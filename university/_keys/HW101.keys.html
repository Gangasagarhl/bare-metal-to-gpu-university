Part C — forensic answer: "The board with two strangers"

Root cause. Two channels have the wrong resistor fitted: channel 2 has 100 Ω and channel 3 has 10 kΩ instead of the 1 kΩ of the schematic; both are the same digit with the multiplier wrong by a factor of ten, which points to parts from the neighbouring drawers (100 Ω and 10 kΩ) mixed into the 1 kΩ box, as in F1-02's forensic lab.

Evidence that proves it (file university/labs/HW101-P/forensic_board.out): every channel shows 7.00 V across the resistor and 2.00 V across the LED (in the ideal model the supply fixes 9 V and the LED fixes 2 V, so the voltages cannot reveal the fault); only the current differs: CH1 and CH4 read 7.00 mA, CH2 70.00 mA (line 3), CH3 0.70 mA (line 4). 7 V ÷ 1 kΩ = 7 mA is the schematic's prediction, so CH1 and CH4 are correct; CH2: 7 V ÷ 0.070 A = 100 Ω; CH3: 7 V ÷ 0.0007 A = 10,000 Ω. Powers: CH2 7 × 0.070 = 0.49 W = 196 % of the pretend 0.25 W rating (the brown mark); CH3 7 × 0.0007 = 0.0049 W (harmless, but the LED gets a tenth of its current: dim); correct channel 0.049 W, a fifth of the rating.

Wrong answers and why. "Faulty LED in CH2 or CH3": ruled out because each LED shows the normal 2.00 V, and the odd currents are explained exactly by a resistor of 100 Ω or 10 kΩ with the same LED voltage; a shorted LED would read 0 V across it and 9 V across the resistor, an open LED would read 0 mA and 9 V across the LED. "Tolerance": ruled out because tolerance is a few percent, not a factor of ten. "Supply fault": ruled out because the supply reads 9.00 V and the correct channels draw exactly 7 mA. "Only CH2 is wrong": CH3's 0.70 mA is a tenth of the prediction, which no tolerance explains.

Fix and verification. Power off; replace CH2's resistor with 1 kΩ and CH3's with 1 kΩ; inspect CH2's LED and the board (the LED carried 70 mA, ten times its design current and above a pretend 20 mA maximum, so it may be damaged) and replace it if in doubt; power on and check that every channel reads 7.00 mA. Prevention: measure each resistor with the meter, power off, before fitting (F1-07), or measure every channel's current at first power-on with a current-limited supply; keep neighbouring values in separated, labelled stores and check a sample of each box on arrival (F1-02).

How the evidence was made. forensic_board.cpp (the same channel model as F1-03's hot_resistor.cpp: ideal 9 V supply, pretend LED voltage 2 V) run with forensic_board.in = CH1 1000, CH2 100, CH3 10000, CH4 1000 Ω; g++ 13.3.0 with the course flags, 2026-10-10, exit code 0. The fault was injected by writing 100 and 10000 in the input file.

Marking (18). F19a (2): the voltages are identical, only the current differs, because the supply and the LED fix the voltages in this model. F19b (2): 7 mA; CH1 and CH4. F19c (3): 100 Ω and 10 kΩ, from I = V ÷ R with 7 V. F19d (3): 0.49 W, 0.0049 W, 0.049 W; CH2 at 196 % explains the brown mark; CH3's tenth of the current explains the dim LED. F19e (4): two hypotheses per channel with the readings that separate them; both fitted values are the label with the multiplier wrong by ten; neighbouring-drawer mix-up. F19f (4): the two replacements, the risk to CH2's LED, the power-off resistance check or first-power-on current check, and the store habit. Forensic rubric of guide 11.2 applies for the write-up: observation, hypotheses, evidence-based conclusion, fix, prevention.
