Story hook

The inspector comes to Amara's house three times. The first visit is before the foundation is poured: she looks at the drawings and the ground. The second visit is before the walls are closed: she wants to see every pipe and cable while they are still visible, because afterwards nobody can. The third visit is before anyone moves in: water, power, stairs, railings, the smoke alarm.

At the second visit, Joon wants to show photos instead of the open wall, because closing the wall early would save a day. The inspector says no, kindly: a photo shows what someone chose to photograph. At the third visit, the stair railing is loose. The inspector writes: "You may move in. The railing is fixed by Friday, by Farah, and I will check it." But when the smoke alarm does not sound, she writes something else: "Not yet. Call me when it works." Some things can wait a few days. Some things cannot.

This chapter is about the five inspections of your mega project, and about being both the builder who prepares for them and the inspector who signs.

What you will be able to do

  • State the purpose of each MP8 gate, R0 to R4, and write its entry criteria at the previous gate.
  • Prepare a review packet in which every claim points to evidence that the reviewer can open or rerun.
  • Review a peer's gate with the questions that belong to that gate, and write the outcome: pass, pass with conditions, or not yet.
  • Write conditions that have an owner, a due date and a check, and refuse conditions on safety items.
  • Diagnose a gate record that should not have passed, using Listing 1 and the incident that followed.
Jargon box
Design-review gate
Simple words: a planned stop where other people check the project before it goes on. Analogy: the building inspector's visit at a fixed stage. Precise: in this university's mega projects, one of the reviews R0 (proposal), R1 (design review), R2 (mid-project review), R3 (readiness review) and R4 (final defence); a gate is passed only with written reviewer approval, by peer learners and, where available, a human mentor; agent reviewers may assist, and the owner decides who signs D1 §11.6.
Entry criteria (gate)
Simple words: the list of things that must be ready before a review can start. Analogy: the walls must still be open for the wiring inspection. Precise: a list of evidence items and acceptance tests, written and approved at the previous gate, that must be present and passing for the gate to be held; fixing them in advance keeps the evidence from being chosen after the result is known; course definition (Layer 3).
Review packet
Simple words: everything the reviewers need, sent before the meeting. Analogy: the drawings and the open walls, ready when the inspector arrives. Precise: the gate record (Listing 1's input), the documents under review, and links to the logs and test outputs named as evidence, sent to reviewers ahead of the meeting so that the meeting is spent on questions, not on reading; course definition.
Gate outcome
Simple words: the result of the review: yes, yes-if, or not yet. Analogy: "move in", "move in, railing fixed by Friday", "call me when the alarm works". Precise: one of PASS (all entry criteria met, independent approvals present), PASS WITH CONDITIONS (as PASS, with recorded conditions that do not touch safety items), or NOT YET (at least one blocking finding; the gate is held again after the fix); course definition implemented by Listing 1.
Review condition
Simple words: a small thing that must be done after a pass, by a named person, by a date. Analogy: the loose railing. Precise: a recorded requirement attached to a PASS WITH CONDITIONS outcome, with an owner, a due date and the evidence that will close it; it may not defer an entry criterion marked as safety; course definition.
Independent reviewer
Simple words: a reviewer who did not do the work. Analogy: the inspector is not the builder. Precise: a reviewer who is not an author of the work under review; the guide applies the same principle to its own pipeline, where author, fact-checker and editor are distinct runs and an agent never marks its own work as checked D1 §3.7 (AH-36), §12.2; Listing 1 does not count the author's own approval.
Readiness review
Simple words: the last check before the risky part. Analogy: the inspection before anyone moves in. Precise: gate R3 of the mega projects; for MP6 and MP7 it is the safety and go/no-go review before any real robot run or flight, with a fixed list of physical checks D1 §11.6; for MP8 it is held before the game-day and before any real-hardware run.

Terms from earlier chapters used here: integration milestone, interface contract, walking skeleton (F12-28); code review (F12-04); threat model (SS401); acceptance test (curriculum section 0).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): the inspector's visits = review gates; "the walls must be open" = entry criteria; photos instead of open walls = claims without evidence; "move in, railing by Friday" = pass with conditions; "call me when the alarm works" = not yet; the smoke alarm = a safety item, which can never be a condition; the inspector who is not the builder = an independent reviewer.

A review is useful only at the moment when changes are still cheap and the evidence is still visible. So each review has a fixed place in the project and a fixed list of what must be ready. The reviewers look at real evidence, not at promises. They can say yes, yes with small fixes by a date, or not yet. Not yet is not a punishment. It means "come back soon".

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The rules from the guide

Every mega project has milestones, deliverables, a grading rubric and review gates: R0 proposal, R1 design review, R2 mid-project review, R3 readiness review, R4 final defence. A gate is passed only with written reviewer approval: peer learners and, where available, a human mentor. Agent reviewers may assist, but the owner decides who signs D1 §11.6. MP8 uses all five gates. The other mega-project cards show what gates are for: MP3's R1 agrees performance targets before coding, never lowered afterwards without owner approval; MP6's R3 must check that the e-stop was tested, speed limits set, the zone clear, supervision arranged and batteries handled per the maker's instructions; MP7's R3 is a go/no-go before any flight, and if regulations or supervision do not allow the outdoor flight, the gate records why D1 §11.6.

Everything else in this section is this course's proposal for how to run the five gates of MP8. It is written as entry criteria and questions, so that your reviewers and you agree on them at the previous gate.

R0 — proposal

Purpose: decide whether this project is worth starting and can be finished. Entry criteria: the one-page proposal of F12-28's lab; the component figure; a plan file with no PROBLEM lines; the cut list; the hardware list with its emulated alternatives. Questions: What is the new behaviour that only integration creates? Which goal areas, from which finished MPs? What is the likely end and the range, and does R4 fit inside the pessimistic end? What will you cut first? Which part needs hardware you do not yet have?

R1 — design review

Purpose: agree the design and the definition of done before the expensive work. Entry criteria: the design document with alternatives (F12-02, F12-03); a contract for every interface; the threat model, since the MP8 card requires a reviewed design document and threat model D1 §11.6; the integration milestones with named acceptance tests; any performance targets as numbers. Questions: For each contract: what happens with a missing field, an unknown field, a newer version, a timeout? Which threat is the most dangerous, and which test shows the mitigation? Which milestone tests each contract? Which targets are fixed now, and who must approve a change? Output beyond the outcome: the entry criteria of R2.

R2 — mid-project review

Purpose: find out whether the project is on a path to R4, and change course early if not. Entry criteria: the walking skeleton test green; contract tests passing on both sides; the plan with actual durations and a re-forecast (F12-28 Listing 1); the risk register updated; any scope change written down. Questions: What does the forecast say, and why? Which risk is now the largest? What was cut, and did anyone weaken a test instead? Which result surprised you?

R3 — readiness review

Purpose: decide whether the system is ready for the risky part: the game-day, and any run on real hardware. Entry criteria: every acceptance test of the plan passing from a clean build, none skipped; the failure-mode tests (for the worked example, the failover matrix); the game-day plan; the threat model updated for what was built; for any real-hardware run, the safety checklist of the matching mega project, signed by the supervisor. Questions: Which test is the closest to the real failure you fear most? What is the stop condition of the game-day? For hardware: who holds the kill switch, and has the bench test with propellers removed been done? A safety item is never a condition: it is either passed before the gate, or the outcome is not yet.

R4 — final defence

Purpose: the public demonstration and oral defence, graded with the MP8 rubric D1 §11.6. Entry criteria: the demonstration script and its recorded fallback run; a claims ledger in which every claim on the slides points to evidence; the post-mortem of the game-day; the retrospective. F12-30 is devoted to it.

The packet and the meeting

Send the packet ahead of the meeting: the gate record, the documents, and the evidence as files the reviewers can open or commands they can rerun. A reviewer who reran one test from a clean checkout has learned more than one who read ten pages of summary. In the meeting, the author answers questions; the reviewers write findings in the style of F12-04 (what, where, why it matters, what would resolve it) and decide the outcome at the end, in writing. The author's job afterwards is the one practised in F12-05: respond to every finding, fix or explain, and close the conditions on time.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why entry criteria are fixed at the previous gate

If the evidence for a gate is chosen when the gate is held, it tends to be the evidence that looks good. This is the same failure as weights written after the scores in a decision matrix (F12-03) or targets lowered after the measurement, which the curriculum forbids without approval and a log entry D2 §13.4, §19.2. Writing R2's entry criteria at R1, R3's at R2 and so on turns each gate into a check of a promise made when the outcome was still unknown. A criterion may still change, but only openly: at a gate, with the reason recorded.

Evidence, not assertions

A gate record line "failover matrix: pass" is an assertion. "logs/m6_failover.log, 4 of 4 tests pass, rerun by Farah from a clean checkout on day 41" is evidence. Two habits make the difference. First, evidence items name files or commands, and the reviewer reruns at least one of them. Second, a summary must agree with its parts: an evidence item that claims "all pass" while one of the tests it covers is skipped is a contradiction in the record, and the contradiction itself is a blocking finding. Listing 1 checks evidence items and tests separately so that such contradictions become visible (the forensic lab).

This is the curriculum's own definition of done applied to a review: all listed acceptance tests pass from a clean checkout and clean build, run by the harness, with output saved; earlier tests still pass D2 §19.3. A skipped test is not a pass, and the runbook says the agent never marks a milestone done with a test skipped, weakened or "to be added later" D2 §0.

Independence and who signs

An author's approval of their own work adds no information. The guide builds its own pipeline on this: the Author, the Fact-Checker and the Editor are separate agent runs, the Fact-Checker sees only artefacts, and agents never mark their own work as checked D1 §3.7, §12.2. For MP8 gates, count only reviewers who did not author the work under review; in a team, a team member may review a part they did not build, but not the gate as a whole. The guide leaves the choice of signers to the owner D1 §11.6; record who decided.

The chapter you are reading is itself inside such a pipeline: it was drafted by an Author agent, its listing was run, and it has not yet passed the independent fact-check gate (G6) of the guide D1 §12.3. The catalogue shows its status. Gates are not a student exercise; they are how this university is built.

Conditions, and what may not be conditional

Conditions let a project keep moving when a small, well-understood item remains. They fail in two ways. A condition without an owner, a date and a check is a wish; it will still be open at R4. A condition on a safety item converts "not safe yet" into "safe, later", which is exactly the inversion the R3 safety checks exist to prevent; MP6's and MP7's R3 lists are checks to be done before the run, not after D1 §11.6. Listing 1 blocks both. Treat every open condition as an entry criterion of the next gate.

The cost of a gate

Gates cost time: preparing the packet, the reviewers' reading, the meeting, the follow-up. A gate that finds nothing is not wasted if its evidence was real; a gate that is skipped to save a week often costs more later, because the finding moves to a moment when the walls are closed. Keep gates light: a packet of what already exists (plan, logs, documents), one hour of meeting, outcomes in writing. If preparing a gate takes days, that is a finding about the project's record-keeping, not about the gate.

How the hardware actually does it

Hardware changes what a gate can see. In an emulator, a reviewer can rerun the test: the evidence is reproducible. On real hardware, the evidence is a run that happened once, on one board, with one battery, in one room; the reviewer cannot rerun it and must judge the record instead. That is why the guide treats real-hardware results as extra evidence recorded separately, never as a replacement for the emulated test, and why the curriculum keeps the QEMU acceptance test as the oracle D2 §0.

For R3, hardware brings items that only a physical check can close: the e-stop or kill switch pressed and observed; speed limits configured and tested; the clear zone marked; supervision present; batteries in the condition their maker documents; for drones, the national aviation authority's current rules recorded, location permission, weather, failsafe configuration and bench tests with propellers removed D1 §11.6. A gate record lists each item with the person who checked it and when.

Safety

Any MP8 that includes a robot, a drone, firmware on real hardware or a real disk applies the university's safety rules (Safety) and the R3 checks of MP6 or MP7 before any real run. Robots and drones can hurt people; propellers cut; LiPo batteries can catch fire; firmware can brick a machine. These items are entry criteria marked safety in the gate record, are never deferred as conditions, and are checked by the supervisor in person.

Watch out

Untested on hardware in this build: the build container has no robot, drone, board or GPU. The R3 hardware items above are taken from the guide's mega-project cards; this chapter could not exercise any of them. Listing 1 checks that a record contains them and that they are not deferred; it cannot check that the physical check was done.

Worked example

Task. Hold R2 for the ground-station MP8 of F12-28, after milestone M4.

Step 1 — the entry criteria from R1. At R1 the reviewers wrote R2's criteria: contract tests pass, skeleton end to end, store suite on three replicas, risk register updated, plan re-forecast. Two reviewers needed. These are the five require lines.

Step 2 — the packet. Amara (author) sends the gate record with five evidence items as log files and five named tests. Joon reran the store suite from a clean checkout; Farah read the re-forecast and the risk register.

Listing 2 — gatecheck.in: the R2 gate record.



Step 3 — the meeting. Joon asks why the SITL telemetry replay (M5) shows one sequence gap in a recorded flight log: is it in the log, or in the ingest? Nobody knows yet. That is not a reason to stop the project: the contract says gaps must be reported, and the skeleton reports it. Joon approves with a condition: a written analysis of the gap, owned by Amara, by day 38. Farah approves.


Step 4 — run Listing 1 and record the outcome. All five evidence items pass, 5 of 5 tests pass, two independent approvals, one condition with owner and due date, no safety item affected: PASS WITH CONDITIONS (1 open) R1. The condition becomes an entry criterion of R3.


Step 5 — write R3's entry criteria now. Failover matrix all pass (none skipped); threat model updated for what was built; game-day plan reviewed; the gap analysis (C1) closed; and, because the team plans one supervised hardware-in-the-loop bench run of the flight controller with the ground station, the bench safety checklist, marked safety.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The inspector enforces codes the builder did not write. A building inspector checks against rules set by an authority. In MP8, you write most of your own entry criteria, and your peers approve them at the previous gate. The analogy hides the hardest part: the criteria themselves must be reviewed.
    • 

  • Software gates can be held again cheaply. A closed wall must be opened to re-inspect; a software gate that says "not yet" can be held again two days later on the same evidence plus the fix. The analogy makes "not yet" sound more costly than it is, which tempts people to pass things that should wait.
    • 

  • Reviewers also learn and teach. An inspector only checks. Gate reviewers are peers who learn your design, spread knowledge and are reviewed by you in turn, the same point SE301 made for code review (F12-04).
    •
