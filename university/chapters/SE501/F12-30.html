Story hook

The house is finished, and the family who will live in it comes for the walk-through. Amara has planned the route: front door, kitchen taps, the switch by the stairs, the fuse box, the heating. At each stop someone turns something on, and everyone sees it work. Farah carries a folder with the inspection records, in case a question needs more than a demonstration.

In the kitchen, a child asks: "What happens if the power goes off?" Joon shows the switch for the emergency light and then says: "In March, the cellar flooded during a test. Here is what happened, what we changed, and the test we ran afterwards." The family's faces relax. A house that never had a problem is a house where nobody looked.

At the end the grandmother asks about the attic. Amara says: "It is not insulated yet. That was on our list of things to cut, and we cut it. Here is what it would take." Nobody is disappointed by an honest answer. They would have been, a winter later, by a dishonest one.

This chapter is about the last gate of MP8: showing what you built, proving what you claim, telling the story of what went wrong, and answering questions.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a deterministic demonstration script with acceptance checks, run from a clean checkout, and keep a recorded run as fallback.
  • Build a claims ledger in which every claim of the defence points to evidence, and reword claims until their scope matches their evidence (Listing 2).
  • Present an incident from your own MP8 as a blameless post-mortem, from evidence to root cause to prevention.
  • Answer questions with evidence, separate what you measured from what you believe, and say "I do not know, and this is how I would find out" when that is the truth.
  • Write the retrospective the MP8 card requires: what you would do differently, with reasons.
Jargon box
Final defence
Simple words: the last review, where you show your project and answer questions in public. Analogy: the walk-through when the house is handed over. Precise: gate R4 of MP8 and the exam of SE501: a public, oral defence with a demonstration, graded with the MP8 rubric (technical depth 25 %, integration quality 20 %, evidence and testing 20 %, leadership and communication 20 %, reflection 15 %) D1 §11.6, §5.17.
Demonstration script
Simple words: the exact list of steps you will show, written in advance, that checks itself. Analogy: the walk-through route, stop by stop. Precise: a versioned, deterministic input (scenario, configuration, fault injections) that drives the system from a clean build through the behaviour being defended and prints machine-checkable acceptance results; course definition (Listing 1 and gsdemo.in).
Recorded fallback
Simple words: a saved earlier run of the same demonstration, used if the live one cannot run. Analogy: the folder of inspection records. Precise: the output and run record (toolchain, command, date, machine, exit code) of the demonstration script from a clean build, made before the defence and labelled as a recording, never presented as live; the same rule the curriculum applies to real-hardware runs, which are recorded as evidence and never replace the emulated test D2 §0; course definition.
Claims ledger
Simple words: a list of everything you will claim, each with the evidence that backs it. Analogy: the list of promises to the buyers, each with its certificate. Precise: a table of the defence's claims, each typed as a claim or a limitation and linked to evidence items (test logs with results, measurements with machine, date and run count, sources with whether they were opened); Listing 2 rejects claims without usable evidence; course definition, applying the guide's claim tagging (AH-15) to a defence D1 §3.3.
Stated limitation
Simple words: something you say your system does not do, or was not tested for. Analogy: "the attic is not insulated yet". Precise: a claim about the boundary of the evidence (untested conditions, cut scope, known faults with their status), stated by the author so that no listener extends a claim beyond what was shown; needs no evidence that the system works, only an honest boundary; course definition.
Acknowledged write
Simple words: a save that the system has told you is done. Analogy: the builder says "the pipe is connected", and you close the wall. Precise: in the worked example's store, a write for which the ground station received ACK; the contract C-store must state on how many replicas an acknowledged write is stored, and acceptance check A1 requires every acknowledged write to be readable after failover; the store is this university's teaching model in Listing 1, not a real product or the Raft algorithm.
Retrospective
Simple words: looking back at the whole project to decide what you would do differently next time. Analogy: after handing over the house, the team asks what they would change on the next one. Precise: an MP8 deliverable: a written reflection on what the learner would do differently D1 §11.6, built in this course from evidence (plan against actual, gate findings, incidents) rather than memory; graded under reflection (15 %).

Terms from earlier chapters used here: interface contract, walking skeleton, re-forecast (F12-28); design-review gate, readiness review (F12-29); blameless post-mortem and game-day (SE402, F12-18); median and percentile (MA202).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): handing over the house = the final defence; the walk-through route = the demonstration script; turning each tap on = a live acceptance check; the folder of inspection records = the recorded fallback; the promises with certificates = the claims ledger; the flooded cellar story = the incident post-mortem; "the attic is not insulated" = a stated limitation; the team's talk afterwards = the retrospective.

At the end of a big project you show it, you prove what you say about it, and you tell the truth about what went wrong and what is not finished. Listeners trust a team that shows its problems and how it fixed them. They stop trusting a team the moment they find a problem it did not mention.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What the defence is

The SE501 exam is the final defence, oral and demonstration, and it is R4 of MP8 D1 §5.17, §11.6. The MP8 card asks for a public defence and lists as deliverables everything from the earlier milestones plus a retrospective; the rubric's five criteria are those in the Jargon box D1 §11.6. The SE501 card also names the course's forensic work: the learner's own incidents during MP8, written up D1 §5.17. So the defence is graded on three things at once: what the system does, how well you know why, and how honestly you report what happened.

R4's entry criteria (written at R3, F12-29) are: the demonstration script and its recorded fallback; the claims ledger with no unbacked claims; the post-mortem of the game-day; the retrospective; all open conditions closed.

The demonstration script

A live demonstration that is improvised shows what happened to work today. A scripted one shows what you claim, and checks it in front of everyone. Write the demonstration as an input file to your system's own harness, as the worked example does with gsdemo.in: each step is an event, the system's output is the oracle, and acceptance checks print PASS or FAIL. This is the curriculum's rule for kernel tests, "serial output is the oracle", applied to the whole system D2 §0.

Four rules make a script defensible:

  • Clean build, tagged commit. Run it from a fresh checkout of the commit you defend, with the build command you will name.
  • Deterministic. Fixed inputs, fixed fault injections, no dependence on timing you do not control. If the real system has nondeterminism, the script fixes seeds and the defence says which.
  • Shows a fault, not only success. The most convincing step of the worked example is the crash of the primary followed by a passing check. A defence that shows only the happy path invites the question "and if a replica crashes?"; answer it before it is asked.
  • Recorded fallback. Run the script the day before, keep its output and run record. If the live run cannot start (a projector, a cable, a battery), show the recording and say clearly that it is a recording, with its date and machine.

The claims ledger

Every sentence on your slides that states a fact about your system is a claim. Before the defence, list them all and link each to evidence: a test log with its result, a measurement with machine, date and number of runs, or a source and whether you opened it. This is the guide's claim tagging (every technical statement carries a source tag) applied to a talk D1 §3.3. Listing 2 checks the ledger. Most problems it finds are not missing evidence but claims that are larger than their evidence: "failover never loses data" where the evidence is one script with two of three replicas alive. The fix is to reword the claim to the scope of the evidence, and to add a stated limitation for the rest.

Measurements follow the rules you have used since SP302: label each with machine, tool, version and date, never present it as a product property D1 §3.5 (AH-23), and use the protocol you fixed at R1, for example the median of at least 20 timed runs after warm-up that the curriculum uses for GPU performance tests D2 §13.4.

The incident, written up

Every MP8 has at least one incident: the game-day is required D1 §11.6, and most projects have unplanned ones too. Present one in the defence as a blameless post-mortem in the form SE402 taught (F12-18): what was observed, the timeline from evidence, the impact, the root cause and contributing factors, what went well, the fix and how it was verified, and the actions that prevent recurrence, each with an owner. "Blameless" means the analysis looks for the conditions that made the fault possible, not for a person to blame D4. In MP8 the person is usually you; the habit is the same.

Answering questions

Questions in a defence come in three kinds. Clarifying ("what does the store acknowledge?"): answer briefly, point to the contract. Challenging ("how do you know failover keeps writes?"): answer with evidence, by name ("check A1 in the game-day script, rerun on the fixed build; here is the log"), and with its scope. Extending ("would this work with ten drones?"): separate what you know from what you think. A good answer to an extending question you have not tested is: "I have not tested it. I expect the store to be fine and the ground station's single ingest loop to be the limit, because each telemetry message is handled in turn. To find out, I would extend the script with ten simulated sources and measure." That answer earns more than a confident guess, and it is honest.

Three answers to avoid: an invented number; "it should work"; a long answer to a short question. If you do not understand a question, ask for it again in other words.

The retrospective

Build the retrospective from records, not memory: the plan against actual from F12-28's tracking (where were your estimates furthest off, and why?), the findings of each gate (which ones repeated?), the incidents (which ones a different early decision would have prevented?). Then write what you would do differently, each item with the evidence that motivates it and the first step you would take next time. Three specific, evidenced items are worth more than ten general ones.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The scope of a claim

Every claim has a scope: the conditions under which the evidence was gathered. "No acknowledged waypoint is lost" from the worked example's script is true for: the teaching model in Listing 1, three replicas, the acknowledge-after-majority rule, the crash sequences in the script, at most one replica down when the check runs, one simulated drone. Each of these is a boundary. A defensible claim names the boundaries that a listener would otherwise assume away, and the limitations list names the rest. Listing 2's rule that a failing test cannot back a claim (only a limitation) is one mechanical piece of this; reading every claim with "under which conditions?" is the rest.

What the demonstration model does and does not show

Listing 1 is a deterministic teaching model, and the defence must say what it abstracts. In its majority mode it stores a write on every live replica before acknowledging, synchronously; there is no network, no message delay, no replica that recovers with stale data, no concurrent writer and no election. A real replicated store acknowledges after a majority while slower replicas catch up, and must then make sure that a new primary is chosen only among replicas that hold every acknowledged write. How a real consensus protocol guarantees that is the subject of DS302 and of the Raft paper D5; this model does not implement it.

Not verified — check before relying on this

This chapter does not state how Raft or any real protocol elects a leader or decides when an entry is committed: the Raft paper was not opened during this build. A learner whose MP8 uses MP2's Raft library must defend failover from that library's own tests (MP2's deterministic fault-injection tests and linearizability check D1 §11.6) and cite the paper's sections from their own reading.

Reproducibility as part of the defence

A reviewer should be able to reproduce the demonstration after the defence. Publish, with the defence: the commit, the build command, the toolchain versions as printed by the tools, the script, its output and run record, and the claims ledger. This is what every chapter of this university does with its own listings (the run records under each output) D1 §3.6. It also protects you: if a reviewer later finds a fault, it can be located in a specific commit rather than argued about.

How the rubric reads a defence

The rubric gives 20 % to evidence and testing and 15 % to reflection D1 §11.6. An incident presented honestly, with evidence and a verified fix, scores on both, and on leadership and communication. A defence that hides the incident loses those marks, and risks the rest when a reviewer finds it in the logs. Technical depth (25 %) is shown less by the size of the system than by your answers about why each design choice was made and what you would see if it were wrong.

The oral part

The oral examination checks that the knowledge is yours. The curriculum's definition of done already asked you to explain every hardware register a milestone touches without notes, and to answer three short questions about each milestone D2 §0, §19.3. The defence asks the same about the whole system: any line of the design, any contract, any test. Prepare by doing what the curriculum asks of its agent: for each milestone, write three questions you would ask a learner about it, and answer them aloud.

How the hardware actually does it

Live demonstrations on hardware fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the design: a flat battery, a loose cable, a busy radio channel, a laptop that sleeps. Plan for them the way the curriculum plans for hardware in general: the emulated or simulated run is the reference, the hardware run is extra evidence recorded separately D2 §0. For the defence that means: the demonstration script runs on the simulator or emulator live; if a hardware run is part of the defence, it is either done live under the safety rules, or shown as a recording with its date, place and supervisor.

Safety

A defence room is not a test area. No drone flight indoors in a defence; propellers stay off for any powered bench demonstration; a robot demonstration needs a clear zone, speed limits and an e-stop in the hand of a supervisor; batteries are handled per their maker's instructions; nothing runs on mains-adjacent equipment. If the hardware part cannot be shown safely, show the recording of the supervised run from R3 onwards (Safety; MP6 and MP7 R3 checks D1 §11.6).

Watch out

Untested on hardware in this build: the build container has no robot, drone, board or GPU. Both listings of this chapter are host programs and were run for real; no hardware demonstration was performed or recorded by this university.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The buyers judge the house; the reviewers also judge the builder. A family wants a house that works. A defence grades your understanding and your honesty as well (reflection is 15 % of MP8); a flawless system defended without understanding scores less than the analogy suggests.
    • 

  • Software can fail safely on stage. Nobody floods a cellar during the walk-through. In software you can inject the fault live (crash the primary) and show the system handling it; the analogy makes demonstrations sound limited to the happy path, when the strongest software demonstrations are of failures.
    • 

  • Evidence in software can be rerun by anyone. An inspection certificate must be trusted. A test log with its commit and command can be reproduced by the reviewer the next day; the analogy undersells how much stronger reproducible evidence is, and so how little excuse there is for unbacked claims.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. gameday.in is the worked example's demonstration script with the acknowledgement rule of the faulty build (ack one) and one replicate step where the background replicator would have caught up; it was run through Listing 1 by run.sh R2. The fix was verified with the same script and ack majority R3.


Timeline. t4–t5: wp1, wp2 acknowledged with 1 copy each. t6: the replicator delivers 4 queued copies (two writes to two backups). t9: wp3 acknowledged with 1 copy. t10: replica 0 crashes, losing 2 queued copies of wp3. t11: replica 1 becomes primary with the first 2 of 3 writes. t12: wp3 NOT FOUND. t13: wp4 acknowledged with 1 copy on replica 1. t14: A1 FAIL, 3 of 4. t15: replica 1 crashes, losing 1 queued copy of wp4. t16: replica 2 primary with 2 of 4. t17: wp5 acknowledged with 1 of 3 and only one replica alive. t18: wp4 NOT FOUND. t19: A1 FAIL, 3 of 5; wp3 and wp4 lost.


Root cause. In code: the one-copy branch (Listing 1, lines 91–100) stores the write on the primary only and the acknowledgement (lines 101–104) follows immediately; copies wait in a queue that dies with the primary (lines 117–123). In the contract: C-store v1 says ACK "when the value is stored" without saying on how many replicas, so the one-copy implementation met the contract as written. wp1 and wp2 survived only because the replicator happened to run (t6) before the crash; wp3 and wp4 were each acknowledged while their copies were still queued.


Second problem. At t17 the store acknowledges with one live replica: any further crash loses everything written since. Even without lost writes, the store cannot keep its promise in that state; the fixed build refuses the write instead (R1, t16).


Fix and verification. Contract C-store v2: "ACK means the value is stored on a majority of the three replicas; with fewer than two replicas alive, writes are refused." Code: acknowledge only after a majority holds the write (the majority branch, lines 79–90). Verification: the same game-day script passes A1 on the fixed build (R3); the skipped R3 test failover_leader_crash_during_write is now run and passes; a contract test checks the refusal with one replica.


Contributing factors (blameless). The acknowledgement rule was ambiguous in the contract; the test that exercises a crash between write and replication was skipped at R3, inside an evidence item that still said "all pass"; the author approved her own gate; the replicator's timing made earlier manual tests pass by luck. Actions: contract v2 with its test (owner Farah); gate records list tests individually and refuse self-approval (owner Amara); a CI job runs the failover matrix and Listing 1 of F12-29 on every gate record (owner Joon). Wrong answer to rule out: "the crash was too early; the replicator should run more often" — faster replication narrows the window but any crash inside it still loses an acknowledged write.
