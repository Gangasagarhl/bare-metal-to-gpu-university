Story hook

The two buildings across town each hold four kitchen halls. On Monday, Bilal's team ran the ring of F8-02 through all eight halls: bowls went round the four halls of building A, out onto the road, round the four halls of building B and back across the road. Every one of the fourteen steps of the ring waited for the slowest link, and one link in each step was the road. The fast corridors inside each building sat mostly idle.

On Tuesday Ayasha proposed a plan in three moves. First, inside each building, the four halls do a quick ring among themselves so that each hall ends up holding one quarter of the building's total. Second, hall 1 of building A trades its quarter with hall 1 of building B, hall 2 with hall 2, and so on: four small deliveries over the road instead of one big parade. Third, inside each building, the halls share their finished quarters so that everyone has the whole result. The road now carries each quarter once, in each direction.

It worked beautifully, until Friday, when one hall in building B was closed for repairs and another flooded. Building B had only two halls open. The plan still ran, nobody complained, and half the soup that came out was wrong.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the three phases of a hierarchical all-reduce (intra-node reduce-scatter, inter-node all-reduce of shards, intra-node all-gather) and why it sends fewer bytes over the network than a flat ring.
  • Build a hierarchical all-reduce from MPI communicators with MPI_Comm_split, and test it exactly against the library's flat all-reduce.
  • Predict flat and hierarchical times with the alpha-beta model for N nodes of G GPUs, and say when the hierarchy helps and when it does not.
  • Explain why a correct hierarchical all-reduce on floating-point data can differ in the last bits from a flat one, and what "reproducible" means for a collective.
  • Diagnose a hierarchical all-reduce that silently produces wrong results when nodes have unequal numbers of ranks.
Jargon box
Hierarchical all-reduce
Simple words: an all-reduce done in levels: first inside each machine, then between machines, then inside each machine again. Analogy: each building totals its own soup into quarters, matching halls in different buildings trade quarters by road, then each building shares the finished quarters. Precise: an all-reduce over P = N × G ranks composed of a reduce-scatter on each node's communicator, an all-reduce of each shard on the communicator of ranks with the same local rank (one per node), and an all-gather on each node's communicator; the network then carries 2(N − 1)/N · S bytes per node instead of the flat ring's 2(P − 1)/P · S per network crossing. D2 R3
Node-local rank
Simple words: a process's number among the processes on its own machine. Analogy: the station number of a hall inside its building; every building has a station 1. Precise: the rank of a process in a communicator that contains only the processes of its node; in MPI it is obtained by splitting MPI_COMM_WORLD by node (with MPI_Comm_split_type(…, MPI_COMM_TYPE_SHARED, …) on real nodes) and calling MPI_Comm_rank on the result. D1
Communicator split
Simple words: making smaller groups out of a big group of processes, each group with its own numbering. Analogy: the delivery service drawing up a separate phone list per building, and another per station number. Precise: MPI_Comm_split(comm, color, key, &newcomm) is collective over comm; processes passing the same color end up in the same new communicator, ordered by key. Listing 1 calls it twice: color = node gives the node communicator, color = local rank gives the cross-node communicator. D1
Summation order (reproducibility)
Simple words: the order in which numbers are added; with floating-point numbers, a different order can give a slightly different answer. Analogy: pouring the same soups into the pot in a different order and getting a pot that weighs a few grains different. Precise: floating-point addition is not associative, so two correct all-reduce algorithms that combine contributions in different orders can produce results differing in the last bits; a collective is reproducible if it returns bit-identical results for identical inputs and process layout, which some libraries offer as an option at a cost in speed. R1 R4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When halls in two buildings must agree on one pot of soup, you can pass bowls in one big circle through every hall, crossing the road twice per lap. Or you can be clever. Inside each building, total up the soup quickly through the corridors. Split the total into equal parts, one part per hall. Then each hall phones only its twin in the other building and adds its part to the twin's part. Finally, inside each building, everybody shares their finished part with everybody else.

The road is slow, so you want it to carry as little as possible. With the clever plan, each building sends its soup over the road only once in each direction, split into small parts carried at the same time.

The plan has one quiet assumption: every building has the same number of halls, so that every hall has a twin. If one building has fewer halls, some parts have no twin to phone. A good plan checks for that first.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Three collectives on two communicators

Number the P = N × G ranks so that node k holds ranks kG to kG + G − 1, and let each rank's local rank be its position inside its node. Listing 1 builds two communicators with MPI_Comm_split (lines 34 and 37): the node communicator (color = node) and the cross communicator (color = local rank), which holds one rank per node. D1 Then it runs three collectives (lines 70, 71 and 73):

  1. MPI_Reduce_scatter_block on the node communicator: the S-byte vector is split into G shards; local rank j receives shard j of the node's sum.
  2. MPI_Allreduce on the cross communicator: the N ranks holding shard j add their shards; each now holds shard j of the global sum.
  3. MPI_Allgather on the node communicator: the G shards are collected, so every rank holds the full global sum.

This is the structure milestone F5 asks for, "a hierarchical version of your F2 all-reduce (intra-node ring, inter-node exchange) over MPI or verbs", with MPI collectives standing in for your own ring of F8-05 in each phase. C1 On GPUs you would replace phases 1 and 3 by your ring (or NCCL/RCCL) on the node's GPUs, and phase 2 by GPU-aware MPI or verbs (F8-08, F8-09).

Testing it exactly

A collective is tested with data whose correct answer is known exactly. Listing 1 lets rank r contribute (r + 1)(i mod 7 + 1) at element i, as 64-bit integers. The sum over P ranks is P(P + 1)/2 · (i mod 7 + 1), with no rounding at all. Every element is compared and the wrong ones are counted per quarter of the vector, which tells you where a bug strikes. The sizes 1, 7, 1,000 and 220 cover a vector shorter than the number of shards, one that does not divide evenly, and a large one. R1

All four checked configurations pass: 4 ranks as 2 nodes of 2, 8 ranks as 2 of 4, 8 ranks as 4 of 2, and 6 ranks with "4 per node" (4 + 2), which the checked program detects as unequal and handles with a fallback (R1). The "nodes" are simulated: MPI_COMM_TYPE_SHARED reports all ranks on one real node, as the first line of each run says.



Correct, and still different


The same runs compare a float all-reduce through the hierarchy with the library's flat MPI_Allreduce. With 4 ranks as 2 × 2, every value is bit-identical. With 8 ranks as 2 × 4, 1,577,072 of 8,388,608 values differ in at least one bit, by at most 2.25 × 10−7 relative (R1). Both answers are correct sums; they add the eight contributions in a different order, and float addition is not associative. R1 Open MPI's own hierarchical component knows this: its parameter coll_han_reproducible is described as "whether we need reproducible results (enabling this disables optimisations using topology)" (R4). R4 So test hierarchical collectives on integers for exactness, and on floats with a stated tolerance.


When does the hierarchy pay?


Listing 2 compares a flat ring over all P ranks with the hierarchy, using the alpha-beta model of F8-03. Its assumptions are written at the top of the listing: one network interface per node shared by concurrent flows; a flat ring ordered node by node whose every step is limited by a network link; equal intra-node links. The parameters are invented teaching values labelled TN-2 (AH-22): inside a node α 2 µs and 80 GB/s, between nodes α 4 µs and 20 GB/s per node. R3


    

  • flat: t = 2(P − 1)(αe + S/(P βe));
    • 

  • intra (phases 1 + 3): t = 2(G − 1)(αi + S/(G βi));
    • 

  • inter (phase 2): t = 2(N − 1)(αe + S/(N βe)) (G rings of S/(GN)-byte steps sharing the interface cost the same as one ring of S/N-byte steps);
    • 

  • hier = intra + inter; "overlap" = max(intra, inter), the bound if the phases were pipelined over chunks.
    • 





Three lessons from R3. Small messages: the hierarchy has far fewer α terms on the network (2(N − 1) instead of 2(P − 1)), so it wins by large factors (2.76 to 5.95 at 8 KiB). Two nodes, large messages: each node sends S instead of 1.75 S (G = 4) or 1.875 S (G = 8), and the intra-node phases are cheap, so hier wins by about 1.27 to 1.31. Many nodes, large messages: the byte saving shrinks (1.969 S against 1.750 S at 8 × 8), while the intra-node phases still add 23,516.1 µs at 1 GiB; run one after the other, the hierarchy loses (flat/hier 0.90). The "overlap" column shows the way out: 94,008.4 µs if the intra-node and inter-node phases are pipelined, below the flat 106,200.5. R3 Real libraries pipeline the phases and choose algorithms per size and topology; this model only tells you which terms matter. D4



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


One analogy sentence: twins must exist in every building, and the phone calls between twins must not queue at a single switchboard. In engineering terms: the hierarchy requires a regular process layout, and its inter-node phase is G concurrent flows that must spread over the node's network interfaces.


Layouts and their assumptions


The reduce-scatter/all-gather form needs the vector split into the same number of shards on every node, and a partner with the same local rank on every other node. Both hold only if every node has the same number of ranks. With unequal nodes, ranks whose local rank exists only on the larger nodes have a cross communicator with fewer members, and the shards no longer line up. Listing 1 handles this in two ways that a production library must also choose between: detect the irregular layout with two MPI_Allreduce calls (min and max of node sizes, lines 38–42) and fall back to a leader-based algorithm (reduce to local rank 0, all-reduce among leaders, broadcast; lines 52–62), which is correct for any layout but puts all inter-node traffic through one rank per node. R1 The forensic lab shows what happens without the check.


On real nodes the node communicator comes from MPI_Comm_split_type(MPI_COMM_WORLD, MPI_COMM_TYPE_SHARED, rank, MPI_INFO_NULL, &node), which groups processes that can share memory, normally one group per node (line 91 shows the call; here it returns one group of all ranks). D1 Process placement then decides which ranks share a node: the launcher's mapping options (by node, by slot, by socket) change the layout, so print it at start-up.


Multiple network interfaces and rails


Listing 2 assumes one network interface per node. Many GPU nodes have several, one close to each GPU or pair of GPUs (F8-04). Then the inter-node phase has a natural mapping: the G concurrent cross-node all-reduces, one per local rank, each use the interface nearest to their GPU, and the network carries G independent flows ("rails"). That turns the shared βe of the model into G times a per-interface β, if the switches give each rail its own path. D4 The hierarchy and the topology fit together: local rank j's GPU, its nearest interface and its cross communicator form one rail.

How the hardware actually does it


On a GPU node the three phases run on different hardware. Phases 1 and 3 move data between GPUs over the node's GPU links (NVLink or xGMI where present, otherwise PCIe through switches or the CPU, F8-04), and the reduction in phase 1 is GPU kernel work. Phase 2 moves shards from GPU memory to the network interface (by GPUDirect RDMA or by staging, F8-09), across the network, and into the remote GPU, plus another reduction. D4 Because the links are different, the phases can run at the same time on different chunks: while chunk k is on the network, chunk k + 1 is being reduce-scattered inside the node. That is the "overlap" column of Listing 2, and it is how the hierarchy stays ahead for large N. In this build all "nodes" are processes on one CPU and every phase is a memory copy in shared memory; Listing 1 shows correctness, not speed.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Halls do not split soup into quarters. The shards are byte ranges of a vector, and the split must be identical on every rank or the arithmetic is wrong. The story's "quarter" sounds approximate; the real one is exact to the element.
    • 

  • Twins phoning each other sounds like one call. Phase 2 is itself an all-reduce among N ranks: for N > 2 it is a ring or tree of its own with 2(N − 1) steps.
    • 

  • "The road carries less" hides the cost inside the buildings. The intra-node phases are not free; at 8 × 8 and 1 GiB they cost more than the network bytes they save (worked example). Corridors are fast, not infinitely fast.
    • 

  • The same soup is not always the same soup. In the kitchen, adding soups in a different order gives the same pot. With floating-point numbers it does not (R1); the analogy cannot show bit-level differences.
    •

Summary


    

  • Hierarchical all-reduce = reduce-scatter in node, all-reduce of each shard across nodes, all-gather in node; two MPI_Comm_split calls give the communicators.
    • 

  • It cuts network α terms from 2(P − 1) to 2(N − 1), and network bytes per node from 2(P − 1)/P · S to 2(N − 1)/N · S (R3).
    • 

  • It wins clearly for small messages and for two nodes; for many nodes and large messages the intra-node phases must be pipelined with the network phase, or it can lose (R3, invented TN-2 values).
    • 

  • Correctness depends on the process layout: with unequal nodes the reduce-scatter form silently gives wrong results unless the layout is checked (R2).
    • 

  • Float results depend on summation order; test exactly with integers and with a tolerance on floats; Open MPI's han offers a reproducible option at the cost of topology optimisations (R1, R4).
    •
