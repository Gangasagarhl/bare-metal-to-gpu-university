Story hook

Every evening the kitchen halls in both buildings cook the same menu, course by course, from dessert back to starter, and at the end every hall must have the combined tasting notes of all halls for every course. Until now, the halls cooked all eight courses, then packed eight crates of notes, then waited while the vans drove them across town and back. The cooks stood idle for the whole delivery.

Ravi had the obvious idea: as soon as dessert is finished, pack its crate and send the van; cook the next course while the van is on the road. By the time the starter is done, most crates are already back. Only the last crate still has to make the trip while the kitchen waits.

The first evening they tried it, nothing changed. The crates were packed early and left by the door, but nobody waved the vans on; they sat there until the kitchen was done and someone finally went outside. The second evening a cook went out between every dish to wave the vans on. The crates flowed, but that cook's dishes took longer. And whatever they did, the starter's crate, the biggest of all, could only leave at the very end.

Jargon box
Communication-computation overlap
Simple words: moving data at the same time as calculating, so the total time is less than the two added up. Analogy: the van drives across town while the kitchen cooks the next course. Precise: running communication operations concurrently with computation that does not depend on them, so that step time approaches max(compute, communication) rather than their sum; it needs independent work, hardware that can do both at once, and a software path that lets the communication advance while the computation runs. D2 C1
Non-blocking collective (MPI)
Simple words: a group operation that you start with one call and finish with another, with other work in between. Analogy: handing the crate to the depot and getting a ticket; you collect the result later with the ticket. Precise: MPI's MPI_I… collectives (here MPI_Iallreduce) return a request immediately; the operation is complete only after a successful MPI_Test… or MPI_Wait… on it, and the buffer must not be touched until then; all ranks must start collectives on a communicator in the same order. D1
MPI progress
Simple words: the library actually doing the steps of an operation you have started. Analogy: someone has to go out and wave the van on. Precise: the execution of the internal steps (sends, receives, reductions) of pending non-blocking operations; depending on the library, transport and configuration, it happens only inside MPI calls on the calling thread, or also in a progress thread or in network hardware. Listing 1's notest schedule shows a case where it happened only in the final wait (R1). D1 R1
Exposed communication
Simple words: the part of the communication time that the computation does not cover, so it adds to the step time. Analogy: the time the kitchen stands idle waiting for the last van. Precise: step time minus the time of the computation the communication could have overlapped (here the backward pass); hidden communication is total communication minus exposed communication. Listing 3 prints both. R3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

If you wait until all the cooking is done before you send any crates, the cooks stand around while the vans drive. If you send each crate as soon as its course is ready, the vans drive while the cooks keep cooking, and most of the waiting disappears.

Two things can still go wrong. First, a crate left by the door goes nowhere until someone waves the van on. If everyone is busy cooking and nobody goes out, the crates all leave at the very end, and you gained nothing. Second, the last course cooked is always followed by its own delivery: nothing is left to cook while that van is on the road. If the last course has the biggest crate, that waiting time cannot be hidden.

So a good plan sends crates early, makes sure someone waves the vans on without slowing the cooking much, and thinks about which crate comes last.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why the gradients can travel early

In a data-parallel step (F8-06) every replica computes gradients in the backward pass, from the last layer to the first, and then all-reduces them. Layer 7's gradient is final as soon as layer 7's backward is done; nothing computed later changes it. So its all-reduce can start then, while layers 6 to 0 are still being computed. Collecting the gradients of one or several layers into a buffer that is all-reduced as a unit is gradient bucketing (F8-06); milestone F6 asks for "Gradient buckets launched as soon as they are ready, on a separate communication stream." C1 D2

The timeline model

Listing 3 schedules one step on two lanes: compute (backward layers in order) and one communication channel that serves buckets first-come, first-served. Each all-reduce costs α + bytes/β. Serial starts all communication at the end of the backward pass; overlap starts bucket k when its layer is done and the channel is free. Its parameters are invented teaching values labelled TN-2 (AH-22): α = 30 µs, 10 GB/s effective all-reduce bandwidth, eight layers whose backward times and gradient sizes are in timeline_model.in, with a 64 MiB bucket for layer 0 (think of an embedding table at the input). Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


One analogy sentence: a van only saves time if it is driven by someone other than a cook, and someone must wave it on. Engineering version: overlap needs separate hardware for the communication and a progress mechanism for it.


Progress


The MPI Standard defines when a non-blocking operation is complete, not which thread does its work. D1 In this build, a non-blocking all-reduce through Open MPI's libnbc-style schedule advanced only when the process called into MPI: notest shows every bucket completing in the final MPI_Waitsome (R1). overlap calls MPI_Testsome 16 times per layer, which gave the library chances to advance the schedules; seven buckets finished during the backward pass. R1 Other ways to get progress: a progress thread inside the library (when built and enabled), a dedicated communication thread in your program that calls MPI_Test in a loop, or offload to network hardware. On GPUs, NCCL and RCCL run collectives as GPU kernels on their own stream, and the GPU executes them concurrently with the compute stream's kernels if resources allow; the host does not drive each step. The GPU schedule


Listing 2 is the overlapped schedule for GPUs with an MPI that is not GPU-aware (this build's, F8-08). The compute stream runs backward kernels, last layer first, and records an event after each. A second stream, the copy stream, waits on each event (cudaStreamWaitEvent) and copies the bucket to pinned host memory, then records a second event. The host thread polls those events with cudaEventQuery; when a bucket is on the host it starts MPI_Iallreduce, and it keeps calling MPI_Testsome; when a reduction is complete it copies the result back on the copy stream. Both streams are created with cudaStreamNonBlocking (0x01 in the CUDA 12.0 header) and the events with cudaEventDisableTiming (0x02), which makes them cheaper to record and query. H1 With a GPU-aware MPI or with NCCL/RCCL, the copies disappear and the collective takes device pointers on a communication stream (F8-06). Three rules hold in every version:


    

  1. The communication must wait for the gradient: an event recorded after the producing kernel, waited on by the communication stream (or queried by the host).
    2. 

  2. Every rank starts the bucket collectives in the same order; Listing 2 keeps a single counter next from the last bucket to the first.
    3. 

  3. Nothing reads or writes a bucket between the start and the completion of its collective; the optimiser step waits for all of them.

Bucket size and order


Small buckets start early and leave little exposed at the end, but each pays α and has its own start and completion overhead. Large buckets amortise α but start late and leave a large tail. Training frameworks use a bucket size of a few megabytes to tens of megabytes as a configurable parameter and fill buckets in reverse layer order; the PyTorch DDP paper describes the design and measures the trade-off. D2 In Listing 3's model the exposed time is at least the last-ready bucket's all-reduce: splitting layer 0's 64 MiB bucket into smaller pieces does not help, because none of them is ready before the backward pass ends. What helps is less data in the last bucket, or work to overlap it with (for example the next step's forward pass, if the framework allows it).


Two nodes


On two nodes the all-reduce of each bucket is the hierarchical one of F8-10, and its cost is dominated by the network for large buckets. The overlapped schedule's floor, backward + last bucket, grows with the network's α and β. The non-overlapped baseline for the milestone is the data-parallel step of F8-06 (milestone F3), run on 2 nodes. Measure both with a profiler that shows all processes on one time axis (Nsight Systems or the ROCm Systems Profiler) and read the timeline, not only the step time. D4



How the hardware actually does it


On a GPU node, overlap is possible because different parts of the hardware do different jobs. The backward kernels run on the GPU's streaming multiprocessors (CUs on AMD). Copies between GPU and host run on the GPU's copy engines (DMA engines), which work independently of the multiprocessors. Network transfers are done by the network adapter's DMA, reading and writing host or (with GPUDirect RDMA, F8-09) GPU memory. The reduction arithmetic of an all-reduce runs on the GPU's multiprocessors (NCCL/RCCL kernels) or on the CPU (staged MPI). D3 D4 So on a GPU, overlap is close to free for the data movement and costs a share of the GPU's compute and memory bandwidth for the reductions; on the CPU of this build, every part of the communication ran on the same cores as the computation, which is what R1 shows. Interference is real on GPUs too: the communication kernels and the backward kernels compete for multiprocessors and memory bandwidth, so the profiler timeline, not the model, decides how much was hidden.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Vans have their own drivers; on a CPU, communication does not. In this build the "van" was driven by the same cores as the cooking (R1). The analogy suggests overlap is always free; it is free only when separate hardware moves the data.
    • 

  • Waving the van on is not one act. A non-blocking all-reduce is many internal steps, each needing progress; a single test call may advance only one of them.
    • 

  • One road. The model has one communication channel; real libraries can run several collectives concurrently on several channels or adapters, and the timeline may show them overlapping each other too.
    • 

  • Courses are not independent. In a real network, a layer's backward needs the activations and weights of that layer; the order of computation is fixed, and the gradient of the first layer is always the last one ready. The kitchen could reorder courses; training cannot.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Ayasha's buckets started at 3.1, 6.5, 10.8, 15.1, 19.5, 24.0, 28.3 and 33.0 ms and completed at 42.7 (seven) and 44.4 ms; none before the backward pass ended at 34.28 ms.


Difference. The colleague calls MPI_Testsome after every compute slice (E3). With it, each bucket completes 1.6 to 3.7 ms after it started, and 7 of 8 before the backward pass ends.


Hypotheses. (a) Slow network: in the colleague's run the same all-reduces finished in a few ms; and all ranks share one machine here; rejected. (b) No progress: the completion row shows everything finishing inside the final wait; confirmed. (c) Wrong order: the hashes are identical and there was no hang; rejected. (d) Slower compute: compute times were 31.89 (serial), 26.85 (overlap) and 25.17 ms (notest); compute was not slower in Ayasha's version.


Root cause. In this build the non-blocking all-reduces advance only inside MPI calls on the calling process. Ayasha's loop made none between starting a bucket and the final wait, so all eight all-reduces ran in the final MPI_Waitsome, after the backward pass, with the extra costs of eight non-blocking schedules on top (10.13 ms after the backward pass against 8.38 ms serially), and the backward pass itself ended 9.11 ms after its compute time. The cause of that 9.11 ms (the start calls themselves, or interference on the shared CPUs) was not separated in this build; lab step 2 measures it.


Why the colleague did not save 8 ms. On a CPU, progress means the same cores copy and add the buckets inside MPI_Testsome; the backward pass ended 6.97 ms after its 26.85 ms of compute. About 7 ms were hidden and about 7 ms were added to the backward pass. E4's model assumes overlap costs nothing; on this machine it does not.


Fix and verification. On the CPU machine: provide progress (tests in the loop or a progress thread) and accept that the gain is limited by shared cores; verify with the completion trace. On a GPU node: use a communication stream with NCCL/RCCL (or Listing 2's pattern), so copy engines and the network move the data; verify with a profiler timeline showing communication under backward kernels, and a step-time saving close to the hidden time.


Prevention. Record per-bucket start and completion times and the compute time in every overlapped implementation; fail a test if no bucket completes before the backward pass ends; compare hashes with the serial schedule. How the evidence was produced: real runs of Listing 1 (three schedules) and Listing 3 on one shared CPU machine (R1, R3).
