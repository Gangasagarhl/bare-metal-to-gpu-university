Story hook

Leila has rehearsed for weeks. In the hallway, with plastic cups, while Tomás narrated the room (the simulator). With the real tray and real shoes, the narration coming over a phone line (hardware in the loop). With empty glasses, in the kitchen, the head waiter watching. Now the restaurant has a garden banquet, and the head waiter says she may carry trays there, on three conditions: the owner of the garden has agreed, he is standing at the door, and the weather is calm.

Leila has also added something of her own to the job. After the evening when a waiter walked straight into the wrong room because his sense of direction was fooled by a mirrored wall, she learned to notice when her own senses disagree with each other, and to stop and put the tray down instead of walking on. Nobody taught her that rule. She designed it, practised it in the hallway, showed that it works, and showed that without it the old accident happens again.

This mega project is that path for a real drone: your own feature inside a real flight stack, proven in software-in-the-loop, then on the bench flight controller, then with propellers off, then, only if the rules, the place and a supervisor allow it, in a supervised outdoor flight whose logs you analyse afterwards.

What you will be able to do

  • Design and build a feature as your own module inside PX4 or ArduPilot (an estimator health check, a mission behaviour, or a companion-computer interface), with unit tests and SITL scenarios whose expectations are written before they run.
  • Prove each failsafe (RC loss, low battery, geofence, estimator failure) with a fault-injection test matrix in SITL, including control runs that show each fault is really harmful when the protection is off.
  • Carry the same build to the bench flight controller in HITL, compare SITL and HITL results row by row, and explain every difference.
  • Run propeller-off bench tests and, where available, a tethered or netted hover, under a written safety case and a signed go/no-go review.
  • Lead, only where legal and supervised, an outdoor test that follows a field-test checklist, and analyse its flight logs against the SITL prediction.
  • Defend the work at five review gates (R0–R4) with evidence a reviewer can open or rerun, and write an incident report when something goes wrong.
Jargon box
Estimator health guard
Simple words: a small program that watches whether the drone's sensors still agree with each other, and raises an alarm when they stop agreeing. Analogy: Leila noticing that what her eyes say and what her feet feel no longer match. Precise: in this handbook, the learner's module that computes consistency measures of the inputs the navigation trusts (here: compass field strength against its value on the ground, GNSS position against a prediction from GNSS velocity, and GNSS freshness), applies a gate and a persistence time to each, and publishes a latched fault that the vehicle's failsafe logic turns into an action. R1 C3
Detection latency
Simple words: how long it takes from the moment something goes wrong to the moment the alarm sounds. Analogy: the steps Leila takes between the floor tilting and her noticing it. Precise: the time between the injection of a fault (known exactly in a simulator) and the first fault published by the monitor; it is at least the persistence time and at most persistence plus the loop period plus any filter delay; a test states its limit before the run, and the report records the margin to that limit. R2
Control run (feature off)
Simple words: the same test with your protection switched off, to show that the fault really does harm. Analogy: showing the head waiter that without the new rule, the old accident happens again. Precise: a scenario in a test matrix that injects the same fault with the feature disabled and must reproduce a stated harm (for example "vehicle more than 100 m from home"); without it, a passing test could mean that the injection never reached the vehicle. It plays the role of a negative control in a numerical test. R2
Known gap (test matrix)
Simple words: a problem your feature does not catch, written down on purpose with a test that shows it. Analogy: Leila admitting, in writing, "my rule does not help if the whole room is tilted". Precise: a row of the test matrix that injects a fault outside the feature's claimed coverage, records what happens, and is reported as an open item for the next review gate; it is not counted as a failure, but if its result changes (the gap closes or worsens) the row flags it. R2 R4
Degraded landing
Simple words: landing straight down without trusting where the drone thinks it is. Analogy: putting the tray down where you stand when you no longer trust your sense of direction. Precise: in this handbook's starter lab, the failsafe action taken on a guard fault: switch to land and zero the horizontal command, so that the vehicle descends without position or heading feedback and drifts with the wind; it trades a bounded drift for not steering on bad data. Real flight stacks have their own degraded modes, which you must look up. R2 D1 D2
Tethered hover
Simple words: the first real hover, with the drone tied down so that it cannot fly away. Analogy: the first walk with a full tray along a rope handrail. Precise: a hover test in which a tether (or a net enclosure) physically limits where the vehicle can go; it reduces some hazards (flyaway) and adds others (the tether snagging or pulling the vehicle over), so it is planned in the safety case and held only after gate R3. C1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

The banquet (F10, registered world: carrying a tray of drinks while walking). Hallway rehearsal with Tomás narrating = SITL; real tray and shoes with the narration over a phone line = HITL; empty glasses in the kitchen = propeller-off bench tests; a walk along a rope handrail = tethered hover; the garden banquet with the head waiter at the door = supervised outdoor test; Leila's own "stop when my senses disagree" rule = your module; the head waiter's three conditions = gate R3. Proposed sub-mappings, listed in NOTES.md.

You are going to teach a real drone one new trick, and then prove, step by step, that the trick works and that the drone is safe while you test it. First you test everything in a pretend world on the computer, many times, including the bad days: the radio stops, the battery gets low, the drone tries to leave its allowed area, a sensor starts lying. Then you put the real flight computer on the desk and let it fly in the pretend world. Then you test the real drone with its propellers taken off. Only then, and only if the rules of your country, the owner of the field and a supervisor all say yes, does it fly outside. Afterwards you read its diary, the flight log, to check that it did what the pretend world predicted.

At every step, other people look at your evidence and sign, or say "not yet". A "not yet" is normal. It is how the project stays safe.

Choosing the path and the feature (before R0)

Flight stack. Take the PX4 path if you did DN302 (modules, uORB, ULog, NuttX: F10-21 to F10-25), or the ArduPilot path if you did DN303 (vehicle code, the HAL, MAVLink, DataFlash: F10-28 to F10-32). Switching stacks in the middle of the project costs most of M1; decide at R0.

Feature. The card gives three examples. This handbook works through the first in detail, because the starter lab can model it honestly and because it closes the story of F10-38 (Team B's flyaway after current-dependent compass interference):

  • A custom estimator check (worked here): an estimator health guard that detects inputs the navigation should no longer trust and triggers a failsafe. The starter lab builds a scaled-down version against the course simulator.
  • A mission behaviour: for example "hold and wait for confirmation at a waypoint" or a new search pattern. Same milestones; the failsafe matrix must show the behaviour gives way to every failsafe.
  • A companion-computer interface: a program on a companion computer that talks to the flight controller (MAVLink on either stack, F10-30, F10-31). The extra hazard is the link itself: what the vehicle does when the companion stops talking must be in the matrix.

Milestone M1 — your own module, with tests in SITL

Entry criteria (agreed at R1). The starter lab of this handbook passes on your machine (run_lab.sh university/labs/MP7, all exit codes as recorded below). The chosen stack builds from source at a recorded commit, and its SITL flies a stock mission (the Part B steps of F10-23 or F10-31). The design document (R1) names the module's inputs, outputs, parameters, failsafe action and claimed coverage.

Tasks.

  1. Port the guard's logic from est_guard.h into the stack's module structure: on PX4, a module with a work-queue item, subscriptions, a published status topic and parameters (F10-24); on ArduPilot, a library or vehicle-code feature scheduled in the main loop, with parameters and a log message (F10-28). Keep the logic in a plain class with no stack dependencies, so that the same unit tests run on the PC.
  2. Connect the fault to the stack's failsafe logic through its documented interface; choose and argue the action (the starter lab's degraded landing is one option; see the worked example).
  3. Port the unit tests (Listing 3's U1–U12) and keep them passing in CI (F12-09).
  4. Write the SITL scenarios: nominal runs with several seeds and winds, one fault-injection test per claimed fault, one control run per fault, one known-gap row per honest limitation. Write each expectation before the first run.
  5. Produce the SITL test report with the configuration identity (stack commit, your module's commit, parameter file hash), margins and open items, as report.py does for the starter lab.

Evidence that proves it done. The test report; every test and control passing; the flight log of every scenario archived and named in the report; a rerun of the suite by a reviewer from a clean checkout. If the real SITL is not bit-for-bit repeatable, the report says so and gives the spread over at least five repeats instead of a single number (F10-33 on lockstep, F12-10 on flaky tests).

Builds on. F10-21–F10-25 (PX4) or F10-28–F10-32 (ArduPilot); F10-33 (SITL, acceptance tests A1–A6, lockstep); F10-38 (the flyaway that motivates the feature); F12-06 and F12-07 (test pyramid, simulation tests).

Milestone M2 — failsafe test matrix in SITL

Entry criteria. M1 evidence complete. The failsafe configuration written as a file: every failsafe-related parameter with its value, unit, and the document or calculation it comes from (the battery reserve calculation of F10-35; the fence from the site plan).

Tasks. Extend the DN401 matrix (F1–F7 of F10-35) to the real stack and your feature. At least one row each for RC loss, low battery (with a too-optimistic capacity setting as in Log B of F10-38), geofence, and estimator failure (your guard and the stack's own checks); at least two combinations (RC loss during a battery return; an estimator fault during a geofence return); escalation checked in both directions. Every row has an injection time, a deadline, an end rule and a control run. Trace every row to a hazard in the hazard log (F11-19, F11-22).

Evidence. The matrix report with configuration identity; every row's flight log; a traceability table hazard → requirement → test → result; every failure investigated with a short incident write-up (template in the forensic section).

Builds on. F10-35, F10-33, F11-21 (safety mechanisms, safe states), F11-22 (traceability).

Milestone M3 — HITL on the bench flight controller

Entry criteria. R2 passed. The flight controller is flashed with the build whose identity matches the SITL report, and the stack's HITL configuration is documented for your release. Propellers are off the airframe, or the controller is on the desk with no motors connected.

Tasks. Run the M1 scenarios (at least the nominal runs, one injection per fault, and the controls) in HITL. Measure what changes: loop timing and the link budget of F10-34, your module's execution time and the CPU load reported by the stack on the real processor (write your own measured numbers, labelled with the board and firmware). Compare row by row with SITL.

Evidence. The HITL report: a table with the SITL and HITL value for each row (verdict, detection latency, drift), every difference above the tolerance agreed at R2 explained, and the board's firmware identity. No unexplained difference.

Builds on. F10-34 (HITL link, delay margin), F10-26 or F10-29 (the board), F12-07; hardware of DN202 (F10-07 to F10-12).

Milestone M4 — bench tests with propellers removed; tethered or netted hover if available

Entry criteria. M3 evidence. The safety case (F10-36, F11-23) drafted, with the hazard log, and checked with the course checker (0 findings). Batteries, charger and storage handled according to the makers' instructions (F10-11).

Tasks (M4a, propellers removed). Arm and disarm; kill switch; motor order and direction from the ground station's motor test (F10-10); RC-loss failsafe shown on the bench (mode change and timing); the battery failsafe shown in the way the safety case allows; your guard's fault shown on the bench through a test input that exists only in test builds and is described in the design document (never by bringing magnets near a powered vehicle or by unplugging connectors on a live system). Pre-arm checks clear. Log every bench run.

Tasks (M4b, only after R3). If a tether or net is available and the safety case covers it: a short hover inside the restraint, with the field-test procedure of F10-37 (roles, abort call, kill switch held by the pilot).

Evidence. The bench test sheet, signed by the supervisor, with the time, firmware identity and result of each item; the logs; for M4b, the flight card and the log.

Builds on. F10-07–F10-12 (DN202), F10-35 Part B, F10-36, F10-37, F11-18–F11-23.

Design arguments a reviewer will probe

Gate and persistence. Each check is a detector with a false-alarm rate and a missed-detection rate (F10-35). The starter guard's position gate is 2.0 m; in five nominal SITL runs the largest innovation was 0.73 m, a ratio of 0.36 to the gate, while a 1 m GNSS jump stayed inside (G3) and a 15 m jump was caught in 0.30 s (G2). These numbers come from the course simulator, whose GNSS noise is invented; real receivers have different, correlated errors, so the gate must be re-justified from real SITL and real logs at R2 and after HITL. R2 R4

Timing from timestamps. A module must compute its time step from the sample timestamps, never from an assumed loop rate: the rate differs between SITL, HITL and the real board, and it jitters. The forensic lab is an incident caused by exactly this, and unit test U11 (200 Hz input) exists to catch it.

The failsafe action. On a position or heading fault, a return home steers with the very data that just failed. The starter lab therefore lands with the horizontal command zeroed (degraded landing). Its cost is drift: in G1 and G2 the vehicle drifted 24.8 m and 24.7 m from the point of detection while it descended, because it was cruising at about 4.4 m/s and nothing braked it. Whether that is acceptable depends on the site (the geofence margin, people, obstacles). A better action for a GNSS-position fault alone might brake on GNSS velocity, if velocity is independent of the failed quantity; that is a design choice to argue at R1, using the stack's own degraded modes, which this build could not check. R2 D1 D2

Latching. The fault stays set until the vehicle is disarmed (test U8). A guard that clears itself would let the failsafe logic return to a mission on data that failed seconds ago; the escalation rule of F10-35 (failsafes only escalate) and the latched safe state of F11-21 say the same thing from two sides.

Coverage, stated honestly. The starter guard does not check heading against anything but the compass's own field strength. Row K1 shows the consequence: with the compass mounted 70° rotated and its field strength normal, the guard saw nothing and the vehicle, still in RTL, had not reached home after 400 s of simulated time. A heading check (for example comparing the commanded and the measured acceleration direction, as the analysis column "cmd→meas" of F10-38 does) is the natural next step, and the gap row turns into a test row when it is added. R2

How the hardware actually does it

The starter lab ran entirely on a PC, in a simulator that is our own code. On a real flight controller, four things change, and each one is a place where SITL evidence can stop being true.

  • Timing. Your module shares a processor with the estimator and controllers, under a real-time operating system (NuttX or ChibiOS, F10-20, F10-27). Its execution time and its place in the schedule are measured on the board in M3, not assumed. D1 D2
  • Sensors. Real IMUs, compasses and GNSS receivers have errors that the course simulator does not model: vibration (F10-09), temperature, magnetic fields from power wiring, correlated GNSS errors, dropouts. The gate values chosen in SITL are re-checked against HITL and bench logs. D5
  • Power. LiPo batteries sag under load and age (F10-11); a capacity setting that does not match the pack flown makes the battery failsafe late (F10-38, Log B). Charging, storage and disposal follow the battery and charger makers' instructions, which this handbook does not quote.
  • People and place. A real vehicle can hurt people. The R3 checks exist because no simulator result can show that the field is clear, that the land owner agreed, or that the rules allow the flight.
Safety

Read the university's safety rules and the F10 faculty safety rules before any hardware step. SITL before HITL, HITL before any flight. Remove every propeller for every bench test. Use an arming switch and a kill switch, and test them before each session. LiPo: a charger made for LiPo, a fire-resistant charging bag or container, never charge unattended, storage and disposal as the battery maker documents. Any flight (tethered, netted or outdoor) only after an R3 GO, where the national aviation authority's current rules allow it, with the land owner's permission, under the supervision the owner of this university has defined. Agents and this handbook never state rule details (heights, weights, distances): read your authority's current documents and record what you read. C1 D4

Worked example

Task. You are a reviewer at R2 for Mei's MP7. Her packet contains the starter-lab version of the SITL report (report.out below). Decide what you would accept, what you would ask, and what you would write as conditions.

Step 1 — is the configuration identified? Yes: the report prints a short SHA-256 of each source tested, including the simulator. If Mei changes est_guard.h after R2, the hash changes and the report no longer covers the new file. R4

Step 2 — do the controls prove harm? M0 (compass interference, guard off) reached 179.6 m from home, the same flyaway as Log A of F10-38; G0 (GNSS frozen, guard off) reached 1,660.3 m and never landed within the 400 s limit. Without these rows, a passing M1 or G1 could mean the injection never happened. R2

Step 3 — read the margins. M1 detected in 0.51 s against a 1.0 s limit. G1 and G2 detected in 0.50 s and 0.30 s against 0.55 s and 0.35 s: margins of 0.05 s each, because the limits were set just above the persistence times. That is fine in a lockstep simulator, where timing is exact; on a board, scheduling jitter can add delay. A reasonable condition: "re-derive the detection limits for HITL from the persistence time plus the measured loop period, before M3; owner Mei; due before R3; checked in the HITL report". R4

Step 4 — the drift. At about 4.4 m/s (the trace of the forensic run shows cruise speeds of 4.3–4.4 m/s), the vehicle travels about 4.4 × 0.30 ≈ 1.3 m during the GNSS-jump persistence alone; the measured drift until touchdown, 24.7 m, is dominated by coasting and wind during the descent, not by the detection. Ask Mei which site constraint the 30 m drift limit comes from, and whether braking on velocity is possible for a position-only fault. R2 R5

Step 5 — the gap. K1 is an honest open item: a 70° compass rotation is not caught, and the vehicle never got home. This is not a failure of M1 as specified, but it is a hazard. Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Leila feels her senses disagree at once and continuously; the guard sees only the numbers it is given, at the rate it is given them, and its "feeling" is a threshold and a timer that someone chose. A sense that fails in a believable way (K1: a rotated compass with a normal field strength) is invisible to it.
    • 

  • Putting a tray down is always safe for Leila. Landing is not always safe for a drone: it may land on a road or a person, and a degraded landing drifts with the wind. The failsafe action is a design decision with its own hazards.
    • 

  • The head waiter can watch the banquet and step in at any moment. A supervisor at a field cannot catch a drone; the safety comes from the preparation (R3, the checklist, the failsafes) far more than from intervention.
    • 

  • Rehearsing in the hallway with plastic cups really is the same walk. A simulator is only as close to reality as its models: the course simulator has no yaw dynamics, invented sensor noise and an invented battery, so "passes in SITL" is evidence about the software's logic, not about the real vehicle.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. est_guard_teamb.h is a copy of est_guard.h with one line changed: the prediction uses a fixed step of 0.02 s ("one step of our 50 Hz loop") instead of the measured dt. The SITL runs at 10 ms steps (lockstep). forensic_mission.cpp ran Team B's own test selection, the hover, and the mission with that header. R5


Observation. Before take-off and in the climb the innovation stays at or below 0.31 m with speed near zero. On the first leg it grows with speed: 1.19 m at 3.77 m/s, 1.73 m at 4.33 m/s, and 2.17 m and 2.31 m at 4.42 and 4.40 m/s; the gate is 2.0 m with 0.3 s persistence, and LAND follows at 10.47 s. After LAND, as the vehicle slows, the innovation falls with the speed (0.96 m at 1.76 m/s at 16.00 s).


Hypotheses. (a) A real GNSS glitch in the simulator: ruled out, because no jump was injected and the innovation is smooth and tracks speed, not a step. (b) Gate too tight for GNSS noise: ruled out, because the hover innovation is far below the gate and the original guard flies the same mission without fault (key K3, max 0.67 m). (c) A bias proportional to speed: confirmed.


Proof. The key's table K2 shows Team B's mean innovation at 100 Hz rising by about 0.5 m per 1 m/s (0.53, 1.02, 1.52, 2.03, 2.53 m from 1 to 5 m/s), while the original stays at 0.19 m; at 50 Hz input, Team B's guard is correct (0.19 m at every speed), because its assumed step then matches. With the filter's gain per step about 0.0198 at 10 ms, the steady innovation is the prediction error per step divided by the gain: 5 m/s × 0.01 s / 0.0198 ≈ 2.5 m, which matches the 2.53 m measured. Our full suite fails U2 (2.40 m at 5 m/s) and U11 (4.56 m at 200 Hz) on Team B's guard; Team B's selection had neither a fast straight line nor a different rate. R5




Fix and verification. Use the measured dt from the timestamps (the original line); rerun U1–U12 and the mission: K3 shows no fault, mission done, landing 1.1 m from home.


Prevention and communication. Keep U2 (top speed) and U11 (another rate) in every fork's suite; make the shared suite, not a selection, the R2 entry criterion; tell the reviewers that the gate must not be widened, because the gate was right and the prediction was wrong. Wrong answers: "widen the gate to 3 m" (hides the bias, which grows with speed and with rate); "GNSS noise in SITL" (the noise is the same in the original's clean run).
