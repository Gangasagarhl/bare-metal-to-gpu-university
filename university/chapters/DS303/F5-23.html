Story hook

Analogy

Lucía's box of chore cards has grown. The friends now write every card the same way: at the top, a list of what the chore needs ("4 friends, ladders, until 6 p.m."); below, the steps. Lucía reads only the top. She sorts the cards: urgent ones first, then the oldest.

On Saturday morning the most urgent card needs all four friends of the river towns, and two of them are busy until noon. Lucía must keep the four free for noon, or the urgent chore will never start. But Yusuf is free now, and his card says "one friend, one hour". Lucía thinks: "If he starts now, he finishes at ten. That is before noon. Nobody waits because of him." So Yusuf goes. Then Mei's card arrives: "one friend, all day". If Mei takes a river-town friend now, the urgent chore cannot start at noon. Mei waits.

And there is a card at the bottom of the box that nobody can do: it asks for a friend with a crane, and none of them owns one. Lucía has learned to read every new card once against her notebook and say "never" straight away. Before she learned that, the crane card sat at the front of the box for a week, and everything behind it waited too.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a batch job script that states its resources (nodes, tasks, CPUs, memory, time) and runs its work, and explain each line.
  • Explain how a batch scheduler orders a queue (priority, age), allocates nodes, enforces time limits, and why accurate time limits matter.
  • Compute by hand the schedule of a small workload under strict priority order and under backfill, and check it against a simulator.
  • Diagnose a queue in which jobs are pending forever, from scheduler logs and node states, and separate the job that can never run from the jobs it blocks.
  • Describe Slurm's parts (controller, node daemons, accounting) and commands, and say which details were verified in this build and which must be checked in the Slurm documentation.
Jargon box
Batch scheduling
Simple words: you hand in work and go away; the cluster runs it when resources are free and keeps the result. Analogy: putting a chore card in Lucía's box instead of standing next to her. Precise: non-interactive execution of jobs from a queue: each job declares its resource needs and time limit at submission, waits until the scheduler allocates them, runs without user interaction, and writes its output to files. D1
Job script
Simple words: a shell script whose top lines tell the scheduler what the job needs. Analogy: the chore card, tools list on top, steps below. Precise: a script submitted to the batch system; scheduler directives in special comment lines (for Slurm, lines beginning #SBATCH) set resources and options, and the rest of the script runs on the first allocated node when the job starts. To the shell, the directive lines are ordinary comments (R3). D1 R3
Partition (Slurm)
Simple words: a named group of nodes with its own rules, like a separate queue. Analogy: the river towns and the mountain towns, each with its own list. Precise: Slurm's grouping of nodes into job queues, each with limits (for example maximum time, allowed users) and its own priority; a job is submitted to one or more partitions. D1
Time limit (job)
Simple words: the longest your job may run; when it is reached, the job is stopped. Analogy: "until 6 p.m." on the chore card. Precise: a wall-clock limit declared at submission; the scheduler uses it to plan (reservations and backfill) and enforces it by terminating the job when it expires. D1 R1
Job priority (cluster)
Simple words: a number that decides which waiting job is considered first. Analogy: Lucía's "urgent first, then oldest". Precise: a value computed per pending job by the scheduler's priority policy; Slurm's multifactor priority combines weighted factors such as age, fair share, job size, partition and quality of service. D2
Head-of-line blocking (queue)
Simple words: the first job in the queue cannot start, and everything behind it waits too. Analogy: the crane card at the front of the box. Precise: in a strictly ordered queue, a job that cannot be scheduled prevents the scheduling of lower-priority jobs that could run; backfill and feasibility checks are the two remedies. R1 R2
Backfill scheduling
Simple words: letting a smaller job jump ahead when it will not delay the jobs in front of it. Analogy: Yusuf's one-hour chore before noon. Precise: a policy that, when the highest-priority pending job cannot start, computes the earliest time it can (a reservation, assuming running jobs end at their time limits) and starts lower-priority jobs now only if they fit and do not delay that reservation, because they end before it or use nodes it does not need. D3 R1
Pending reason
Simple words: the scheduler's short note saying why a job is still waiting. Analogy: Lucía's "not yet" or "never" written on the card. Precise: a code the scheduler attaches to each pending job (for example "resources" or "priority" in this chapter's simulator; Slurm's own reason names are in the unverified box of Layer 3); the first thing to read when a job does not start. D1 R2
Job step (Slurm)
Simple words: one launch of a program inside a job's allocation; a job can have several. Analogy: the separate steps on one chore card, done by the same friends. Precise: a set of tasks launched within an existing allocation (in Slurm by srun inside the job); steps can run one after another or side by side, sharing the job's resources. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A batch scheduler is a queue with rules. You write what your job needs and how long it may take, and you put it in the queue. The scheduler starts jobs in order of importance. When the most important job must wait for space, the scheduler can still start a small job in the meantime, but only if the small job will be finished in time. That is why telling the truth about your job's length helps everybody, including you.

Some jobs ask for something the cluster does not have. A good scheduler says "never" at once. A careless one lets that job wait, and sometimes everything behind it waits too.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What you hand in: the job script

A batch job is a script plus a resource request. In Slurm the request is written as directive lines at the top of the script, each beginning with #SBATCH, and the script is submitted with the sbatch command; Slurm reads the directives, queues the job, and when resources are allocated it runs the script on the first allocated node. Because the directives start with #, the shell treats them as comments: R3 runs this chapter's job script with plain bash, outside any scheduler, and it works, printing "not set" for the variables Slurm would have provided. That is a useful property: you can test the script's logic on your own machine before you queue it. D1 R3

A request states, at least: how many nodes; how many tasks (processes) and how many CPUs per task; how much memory; and a time limit. GPU requests are F5-26's topic. Every number in the request is a promise in both directions: the scheduler guarantees you that much, and enforces that you use no more. Ask for too little memory and the job is killed (F5-24 shows the kernel mechanism); ask for too little time and it is stopped at the limit (job F in R1); ask for too much and you wait longer, because a bigger request fits into fewer gaps. D1 R1

What the scheduler does with it

The scheduler's main loop is simple to state. When something changes (a job is submitted, a job ends, a node changes state, or a timer fires), it builds the list of pending jobs sorted by priority, walks down the list, and starts each job for which it can find an allocation. Listing 1, batchsim.cpp, implements that loop in about 200 lines with two policies:

  • Strict priority order. Walk down the list; stop at the first job that cannot start. Nothing below it may start, so the order of starts always respects priority. The price is idle nodes: in R1, n4 is idle from minute 0 to 50 while C waits for all four nodes.
  • Backfill. When the first job cannot start, compute its reservation: the earliest time and the nodes at which it could start, assuming every running job runs until its time limit. Then keep walking: a later job may start now if it fits and either ends (by its limit) before the reservation, or uses only nodes the reservation does not need. In R1, D and F start in the gap; E, with a 120-minute limit, would still be running at minute 60, so it waits.

Slurm's documentation describes a main scheduler and a backfill scheduler that work in this spirit; its exact algorithm has many parameters (how deep into the queue it looks, how often it runs, how far ahead it plans) that this model does not have. D3 R1

Why the time limit matters twice

The scheduler does not know how long your job will really run, only its limit. It plans reservations from limits (Listing 1, line 82), so a job with an honest, short limit fits into more gaps. In R1, E's real runtime is 30 minutes, but it declared 120 and therefore waited 80 minutes. Lab step 3 changes E's limit to 40 and shows it backfilled at minute 10. The second use of the limit is enforcement: F declared 20 minutes and needed 25, so it was killed at its limit and its work is lost. Good limits are honest and have a margin. R1 D3

Priority

The model takes priorities as given numbers. Real clusters compute them. Slurm's multifactor priority plugin sums weighted factors: how long the job has waited (age), how much the user or account has used recently compared with its share (fair share), the job's size, the partition, and its quality of service. The administrator chooses the weights. Two consequences matter to users: your priority changes while you wait, and heavy recent use lowers it. D2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Slurm's architecture

Slurm has a central controller daemon that holds the queue, the node table and the scheduling logic, with an optional backup controller for failover; a daemon on every compute node that launches and monitors the job's processes and reports the node's state; and an optional database daemon that stores accounting records for all jobs. Users interact through commands that talk to the controller. Nodes are grouped into partitions; nodes have states (idle, allocated, partly allocated, down, drained); jobs have states (pending, running, completed, failed, timed out, cancelled, ended by a node failure). Configuration lives in a cluster-wide configuration file that describes nodes, partitions and plugins; generic resources such as GPUs are described in their own configuration (F5-26). Authentication between daemons uses a shared-key service. D1 D4

Allocation versus steps

A Slurm job is an allocation: a set of nodes and resources held for the job's duration. Inside it, the script launches job steps; each step starts a number of tasks across the allocated nodes. An MPI program like F5-22's pi_mpi is normally launched as one step with one task per rank, either with Slurm's own launcher or with mpirun, which in an MPI library built with Slurm support learns the allocation from the environment. The separation explains several behaviours: the script itself runs on one node only; a step can use less than the whole allocation; several steps can run in sequence (build, run, post-process) without queueing again. D1 D5

The cost of a reservation

Backfill is a trade. It keeps nodes busy that strict ordering would leave idle (n4 in Figure 2), and the reservation protects the first blocked job from starvation by smaller ones. But it makes the schedule depend on time limits, which users overstate, and it plans on a picture that changes: in R1, C's reservation was minute 60, computed from A's limit, and C actually started at 50 because A ended early. When jobs end early, reservations move earlier and some backfill decisions turn out to have been unnecessary; when a backfilled job's limit is wrong (F), the scheduler kills it rather than let it delay the reservation. A feasibility check, in which every new job is compared once with the cluster's node capacities, catches the request that can never run before it reaches the front of any queue. D3 R1 R2

What the model leaves out

Listing 1 has no partitions, no fair share, no limits per user, no node failures, no requeue, and a one-minute clock. It uses first-fit node choice, where real schedulers consider topology (switches, F5-26) and pack jobs to leave large blocks free. It decides with full knowledge of the present; a real controller works from node reports that are seconds old (F5-22). Those omissions are deliberate: the model shows the three ideas of this chapter (queue order, reservations from limits, and the "never" case) in code you can read in one sitting.

How the hardware actually does it

Allocation is only bookkeeping until the node daemon enforces it. On Linux, a node daemon confines each job's processes with the kernel's control groups (CPU sets, memory limits, device access) and starts them under the user's identity; the time limit is enforced by signalling the processes and then killing them. F5-24 shows control groups and an out-of-memory kill for real in this build. Inside the allocated cores, processes still need to be placed on the right sockets and cores for memory locality (HW203's F1-39); Slurm offers binding options for that, which are outside this chapter and must be checked in its documentation. D1 D6

Worked example

Workload batchsim.in: four nodes n1–n4 with 32 CPUs and 128 GiB each; times in minutes. Jobs (id: submit, nodes × CPUs per node, limit, real runtime, priority): A: 0, 2 × 32, 60, 50, 10. B: 0, 1 × 16, 30, 20, 10. C: 5, 4 × 32, 60, 40, 20. D: 10, 1 × 32, 40, 30, 5. E: 10, 1 × 16, 120, 30, 5. F: 15, 1 × 8, 20, 25, 5.

Backfill, by hand.

  1. t = 0: A and B (priority 10) are the only jobs. A takes n1, n2 (32 CPUs each). B takes 16 CPUs of n3.
  2. t = 5: C (priority 20) needs four whole nodes; n3 has only 16 free CPUs: C is blocked. Its reservation: running jobs end at their limits, B at 0 + 30 = 30, A at 0 + 60 = 60. After B, n1 and n2 are still busy; after A, all four are free: reservation at t = 60 on all four nodes.
  3. t = 10: D fits on n4 now and ends by 10 + 40 = 50 ≤ 60: backfilled on n4. E fits on n3 now (16 CPUs free) but 10 + 120 = 130 > 60, and every node is reserved: E waits.
  4. t = 15: F fits on n3 and 15 + 20 = 35 ≤ 60: backfilled on n3. Its real runtime is 25 > 20, so it is killed at 35.
  5. B ends at 20, D at 40, A at 50 (before its limit). At t = 50 all nodes are free and C starts: 10 minutes before its reservation. C ends at 90; E starts on n1 and ends at 120.
  6. Waits: A 0, B 0, C 45, D 0, E 80, F 0; sum 125; 125 / 6 = 20 in integer division. R1 prints "average wait 20 min, last job ended at t=120".

Strict order. C blocks the queue from t = 5 until 50. At 50, C starts; D is now first and waits for resources. At 90 D, E and F start (E and F share n2: 16 + 8 ≤ 32); F is killed at 110, D and E end at 120. Waits: 0, 0, 45, 80, 80, 75, sum 280, 280 / 6 = 46. R1 prints 46. Same finishing time, more than twice the average wait. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lucía sees Yusuf finish at ten and can reconsider. The scheduler plans with the declared limit, not the real duration; when jobs end early, its reservations were too pessimistic, and when they run long, it kills them (job F) instead of asking.
    • 

  • Friends can split a chore halfway. A batch job's allocation is all-or-nothing: a job that needs four nodes does not start on three and wait for the fourth.
    • 

  • Lucía's "urgent" is a judgement. A scheduler's priority is a formula with administrator-chosen weights that changes while the job waits; two identical jobs can have different priorities because of their owners' recent usage.
    • 

  • In the story, Lucía reads every card against her notebook. Not every scheduler configuration rejects impossible requests at submission; the forensic lab shows what happens when nothing does.

Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • "My job is pending; the cluster must be full." Symptom: a job pends while other jobs start. Cause: its reason may be priority (others are ahead), resources (nothing fits yet), or a request no node satisfies. How to find it: read the pending reason (R2's log; Slurm's queue display shows a reason field, names unverified) and compare the request with the largest node (F5-22's Listing 1). Fix: depends on the reason; for "never", change the request.
    • 

  • Overstated time limits. Symptom: short jobs wait long. Cause: the limit, not the runtime, decides backfill (E in R1). Fix: measure your job's runtime and set the limit with a margin; lab step 3.
    • 

  • Understated limits. Symptom: the job ends without its result; the log says it was killed at the time limit (F in R1). Fix: a margin, and checkpoints for long jobs (F5-22).
    • 

  • Heavy work in the script instead of in a step. Symptom: only the first node is busy. Cause: the script runs on one node; other nodes are used only by launched steps. Fix: launch the parallel program with the scheduler's launcher (or an MPI launcher that knows the allocation).
    • 

  • Untested scripts in the queue. Symptom: a job waits hours, then fails in one second with a typo. Fix: run the script's logic locally first (R3) and submit a short test job before the long one.
    •

Summary


    

  • A batch job is a script plus a resource request and a time limit; Slurm reads the request from #SBATCH lines, which bash ignores as comments.
    • 

  • The scheduler orders pending jobs by priority and starts each one it can allocate; under strict order, a blocked job blocks everything behind it.
    • 

  • Backfill reserves the earliest start for the first blocked job (computed from time limits) and lets later jobs run if they do not delay it.
    • 

  • Time limits are used for planning and enforced: honest limits get jobs started sooner; too-short limits get them killed.
    • 

  • Slurm has a controller, a daemon per node, optional accounting, partitions, and multifactor priorities; its exact names and options must be checked in its documentation.
    • 

  • A request that no node set can satisfy must be rejected at submission; otherwise it pends forever and, under strict order, stops the queue.
    •
