Story hook

Analogy

After two years of festivals the friends stop keeping a separate kitchen for every purpose. Everything shares the same four kitchens: the café that must never close, the weekly bread, Mei's slow experiments with sourdough that can wait a week.

Lucía writes three rules. First, each friend has a yearly allowance of oven-hours, and she refuses a request that goes over it, before it ever reaches a kitchen. Second, café chores are urgent and may interrupt hobby chores, never the other way round. Third, she notices that the café books eight ovens "in case of a rush" and uses three; she lets the hobby bakers use the other five, on the understanding that they leave the moment the café needs them.

The kitchens are fuller than they have ever been. But Mei's sourdough, which needs thirty undisturbed hours, has been started five times this month, and every time a café rush threw it out halfway.

What you will be able to do

  • Summarise the architecture of Borg as its 2015 paper describes it: cells, a replicated master, a separate scheduler, node agents, jobs and tasks.
  • Explain admission control by quota, priority bands, and preemption, and trace them in a simulator log.
  • Explain resource reclamation (running lower-priority work in reserved but unused capacity) and compute its effect on utilisation in a small cell.
  • Diagnose starvation by repeated preemption from scheduler logs, and propose fixes in placement, limits and checkpointing.
  • Map Borg's lessons to the Kubernetes design of F5-25 and to Slurm's of F5-23.
  • Read a systems paper critically: separate what it measured, what it claims, and what you can check yourself.
Jargon box
Borg
Simple words: Google's cluster manager, which runs most of the company's programs on shared clusters; the paper about it shaped Kubernetes. Analogy: Lucía's rules for the shared kitchens. Precise: a cluster management system that admits, schedules, starts, restarts and monitors both long-running services and batch jobs on shared cells of machines, described in Verma et al., EuroSys 2015. D1
Cell (Borg)
Simple words: one big shared cluster managed as a unit. Analogy: the four kitchens under one set of rules. Precise: a set of machines, within one data-centre building or cluster, managed by one Borgmaster; jobs are submitted to a cell. D1
Borgmaster and Borglet
Simple words: the central brain of a cell, kept on several machines, and the small agent on every machine. Analogy: Lucía with four deputies who keep identical notebooks; the caretaker in each kitchen. Precise: the Borgmaster is a logically centralised controller replicated over several machines, with its state stored through Paxos; one replica is elected master; a Borglet on each machine starts and stops tasks, manages local resources and reports state when polled. D1
Priority band
Simple words: a class of work, such as "production" or "batch", that decides who may interrupt whom. Analogy: café chores and hobby chores. Precise: a range of job priorities treated alike by policy; in Borg, higher-priority tasks may preempt lower-priority ones, production-band tasks do not preempt each other, and quota is granted per priority. D1 R1
Quota and admission control
Simple words: an allowance of resources per user and priority, checked when work is submitted, not when it is placed. Analogy: the yearly oven-hours allowance. Precise: a per-user, per-priority vector of resource amounts that a user may request; a job whose requests exceed the remaining quota is rejected immediately at submission (admission control), so the scheduler's queue holds only work the cell has promised to run. D1 R1
Preemption (scheduling)
Simple words: stopping a lower-priority task to make room for a higher-priority one. Analogy: the café rush throwing out the sourdough. Precise: the eviction of running tasks chosen by priority so that a pending higher-priority task becomes feasible; the evicted tasks return to the pending queue and lose any progress not saved in a checkpoint. D1 R1
Resource reclamation
Simple words: letting low-priority work use what high-priority work reserved but is not using. Analogy: hobby bakers in the café's spare ovens, ready to leave. Precise: estimating each task's actual needs (its reservation) from observed usage, and scheduling lower-priority work against reservations instead of limits; the reclaimed capacity is revocable when the reserving task's usage grows. D1 R1
Starvation (scheduling)
Simple words: a job that never finishes because it keeps losing its turn. Analogy: the sourdough, started five times and never baked. Precise: indefinite postponement of a task's completion by the scheduling policy, for example by repeated preemption of a task whose work is lost each time. R2

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Google runs almost everything on shared clusters managed by one system, Borg. Borg has a few rules. You may only ask for as much as your allowance. Important work may push aside less important work. And when important work has asked for more than it uses, less important work may borrow the spare space, but must give it back at once.

These rules fill the computers well. But pushed-aside work loses what it had done, so a long job that keeps being pushed aside may never finish. Borg's builders wrote down what worked and what they would change, and Kubernetes was built with those lessons.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Reading the paper

This chapter is built around one source: the paper "Large-scale cluster management at Google with Borg" (Verma, Pedrosa, Korupolu, Oppenheimer, Tune, Wilkes, EuroSys 2015). It describes a system that ran for about a decade before the paper, explains its design, reports measurements from Google's own cells, and ends with lessons. Read it with three questions: what did they measure (and can you check it?), what do they claim follows, and what did they change their minds about? This build did not open the paper; the descriptions below are from memory and are tagged D1 with an unverified box, and the lab models the mechanisms, not Google's numbers. D1

Architecture

A Borg cell is a set of machines managed as one. Users submit jobs, each a set of identical tasks, described in a configuration language. The Borgmaster holds the cell's state; it is replicated over several machines, keeps its state in a Paxos-based store (DS302), and one replica is elected master. Scheduling runs as a separate process that reads the pending queue and writes placements back. A Borglet on each machine starts and stops tasks and reports; the master polls the Borglets. Figure 1 shows the parts. D1

Two workloads share every cell: long-running services that should never go down (user-facing, latency-sensitive) and batch jobs that run for minutes to days and tolerate delays. Sharing is the point: separate clusters for each would need far more machines, because each would be sized for its own peak. The paper argues this with an experiment that repacks workloads into separate cells and counts the extra machines needed; treat the size of that effect as something to read, not quote, until the source is checked. D1

Admission: quota before scheduling

Every job has a priority. Quota is granted per user and per priority, and checked at submission: a job that would exceed its owner's quota is rejected at once. This keeps the scheduler's queue honest: everything in it is work the cell has promised to run. In R1, cara has 8 CPU of production quota; her cache task asks for 12 and is rejected at t = 0, before any machine is considered. Slurm's accounting limits and Kubernetes' resource quotas per namespace play the same role. D1 R1 D5

Priority bands and preemption

Borg groups priorities into bands; this chapter uses two, prod and batch, as the lab does. A pending prod task that does not fit may preempt batch tasks; prod tasks do not preempt each other, which prevents cascades in which preempted tasks preempt others in turn. In R1 (reclamation off), the cell is full by limits when api (prod, 12 CPU) arrives at t = 10; the scheduler finds machine m3, where batch tasks hold 16 CPU, and preempts b1, b2 and b3 until 12 CPU are free. Their work is lost: b1, which had run 9 minutes, restarts from zero at t = 31. D1 R1

Placement: feasibility, then scoring

As in Kubernetes (F5-25), Borg's scheduler first finds the machines that can hold a task (feasibility) and then scores them. The paper discusses the trade-off between spreading load (worst fit, which leaves room on every machine for spikes) and packing tightly (best fit, which leaves whole machines empty for large tasks, F5-26), and describes the hybrid Borg settled on. Listing 1 uses plain best fit: the machine with the least CPU left after placement. D1 R1

Resource reclamation

A task's limit is what it asked for; its usage is usually lower, because owners ask for their peak plus a margin. Borg estimates a reservation for each task from its observed usage, and schedules batch work against reservations rather than limits, while prod work is still placed against limits. The batch work runs in capacity that is reserved but idle, and gives way if the prod task's usage grows. Listing 1 implements the simplest version: with reclamation on, batch tasks are placed against prod tasks' usage (lines 32–35). In R1 the effect is large: with reclamation off, b5 and b6 never start and only 22 of 48 CPU are in use at t = 60 although every CPU is reserved; with reclamation on, all six batch tasks finish by t = 41. D1 R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Reclamation is overcommitment, and overcommitment needs a plan

Figure 2's bottom bar is not free. After reclamation, m1's limits add up to 24 CPU on a 16-CPU machine. If the web tasks' load rises to their limits, someone must lose. For CPU, a compressible resource, the node can throttle the batch tasks (control-group CPU shares and quotas, F5-24) and the prod tasks get their CPU at the cost of batch speed. For memory, which is incompressible, the only way out is to kill a task, and it had better be a batch task: the OOM-kill evidence of F5-24 shows what happens when the kernel chooses alone. The paper describes this split; Listing 1 models placement only and never throttles or kills for usage, which the breaks section lists. D1 R1

Kubernetes encodes the same idea differently: a pod's requests and limits put it in a quality-of-service class (guaranteed when requests equal limits, burstable when requests are lower, best-effort when it has none), the scheduler places by requests, and under memory pressure the node evicts best-effort and burstable pods first. Requests below limits are Kubernetes' version of a reservation below a limit, chosen by the user instead of estimated by the system. D5

Preemption without checkpoints is a loss, repeated

Preemption moves cost from the prod task to the batch task. If the batch task can checkpoint (F5-22), the cost is the work since the last checkpoint plus restart time; if it cannot, it is everything. R2 shows the extreme: a 30-minute simulation is placed on m1 at t = 1 and preempted at 15, 35, 55, 75 and 95 by 10-minute report tasks, losing 14, 19, 19, 19 and 19 minutes of work, and is still running at t = 120. Each decision was reasonable; together they starve the job. The fix is never only "more machines"; Layer 3's questions are: why does the batch task keep landing where prod work arrives (placement), why can prod work not go elsewhere (limits), and why does the task lose everything (no checkpoint)? R2

Centralised, but not a bottleneck

A single logical master for a whole cell looks like a scalability and availability risk. The paper's answers, as remembered: replicate the master's state with Paxos so one replica's failure costs seconds; run the scheduler as a separate process working on a cached copy of state, using optimistic concurrency (placements are checked when written back, like F5-25's resource versions); score machines once per equivalence class of identical tasks; cache scores; and examine machines in random order until enough feasible ones are found, instead of all of them. Running tasks keep running when the master is unavailable: the master is on the control path, not the data path. The Omega paper (D3) explores the alternative of several schedulers sharing state with optimistic concurrency. D1 D3 D4

How the hardware actually does it

Borg's mechanisms end in the same kernel features as F5-24's containers: tasks run in control groups on Linux machines, CPU is shared by weights and limits, memory is enforced by limits with the OOM killer behind them, and isolation between tasks on one machine is imperfect for resources the kernel does not partition (memory bandwidth, caches, network), which is why the paper reports measuring the interference that sharing causes and treating latency-sensitive tasks specially. Reclamation works only because CPU usage can be measured and throttled per group, cheaply and often; F5-24's memory.max_usage_in_bytes and failcnt are the kind of counters a node agent reads to estimate usage. Worked example

R1 with reclamation on, from t = 1 to t = 41. Three machines of 16 CPU. Prod tasks web1–web4 (limit 8, usage 3) are on m1 and m2, two each.

  1. t = 1, b1 (batch, limit 4). Committed CPU, seen by a batch task: m1 3 + 3 = 6, m2 6, m3 0. Left after placing: m1 16 − 6 − 4 = 6, m2 6, m3 12. Best fit: least left, ties to the first: m1.
  2. b2: m1 now 6 + 4 = 10 committed, left 2; m2 6; m3 12: m1. b3: m1 would be −2: infeasible; m2 left 6, m3 12: m2. b4 → m2 (left 2). b5: only m3 (left 12) → m3; b6 → m3 (left 8). All six batch tasks run.
  3. t = 10, api (prod, limit 12). Prod sees limits: m1 8 + 8 + 4 + 4 = 24 committed, m2 24, m3 8. Left: 16 − 24 − 12 < 0 on m1 and m2; 16 − 8 − 12 = −4 on m3. Not feasible.
  4. Preemption. m1: freeable batch 8; 16 − 24 + 8 = 0 < 12: skip. m2 the same. m3: 16 − 8 + 8 = 16 ≥ 12: preempt on m3, in order, until api fits: b5 first (free becomes 16 − 4 = 12, enough), so b6 survives. api → m3.
  5. t = 11, b5 pending. Seen by a batch task, m3 commits api's usage 6 + b6's 4 = 10, left 16 − 10 − 4 = 2: feasible; m1 and m2 have 16 − 14 − 4 < 0. b5 → m3, restarting its 30 minutes: done at t = 41.
  6. The others ran from t = 1 and finish at t = 31. At t = 60: 5 tasks running, limits 4 × 8 + 12 = 44, usage 4 × 3 + 6 = 18 of 48.

Every step is in R1. Compare reclamation off: 6 tasks still running at t = 60 because three batch tasks were preempted without a place to go and two never started; limits 48 of 48 reserved, usage 22. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lucía sees exactly how many ovens the café uses. Borg estimates usage from samples and must leave a safety margin; an estimate that is too low means prod tasks are throttled or batch tasks killed more often.
    • 

  • Hobby bakers "leave the moment the café needs them". In Listing 1 nobody leaves when usage grows: the model only preempts for new prod placements. Real reclamation also has to act on running tasks.
    • 

  • The friends share one set of values. A real cell's users compete; quotas, priorities and pricing are policy decisions with organisational consequences, not only technical ones.
    • 

  • The story's café is one task. A prod service is many tasks spread for fault tolerance; how to spread them (across machines, racks, power domains) is a constraint the model omits.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Running long batch work without checkpoints in a preemptible band. Symptom: jobs restarted many times, never finishing (forensic lab). Fix: checkpoint at intervals shorter than the measured time between preemptions in that cell, or run in a non-preemptible band with quota for it.
    • 

  • Limits sized for the worst day, every day. Symptom: the cell is "full" by reservations with low usage (R1, reclamation off: 48 of 48 reserved, 22 used). Fix: right-size limits from measured usage; let the system reclaim the rest.
    • 

  • Treating preemption counts as noise. Symptom: a team's batch throughput collapses after another team adds periodic prod jobs. Fix: monitor preemptions per task and per cause; alert when a task's count rises.
    • 

  • Reading a paper's numbers as universal. Symptom: design decisions justified by "Google measured X". Fix: separate the mechanism (which you can model and test, as here) from the workload-specific measurement.
    • 

  • Giving everything top priority. Symptom: preemption stops working, because nothing is lower. Fix: priority with quota per band, so high priority is a scarce, accounted resource.
    •

Summary


    

  • Borg manages services and batch work in shared cells: a replicated master with a Paxos-based store, a separate scheduler, an agent per machine (D1, unverified in this build).
    • 

  • Quota per user and priority is checked at submission; the scheduler sees only admitted work (R1: cache rejected).
    • 

  • Priority bands decide preemption: prod may evict batch, prod does not evict prod; evicted work without a checkpoint is lost (R1).
    • 

  • Reclamation places lower-band work against observed usage instead of limits; in R1 it finished all batch tasks instead of leaving two never started and three evicted.
    • 

  • The cost is overcommitment and preemption; without checkpoints and right-sized limits, a batch task can starve (R2: preempted five times, never finished).
    • 

  • Kubernetes adopted Borg's lessons: labels, one IP per pod, pods as allocs, services, and a master built as the kernel of a distributed system.

  2. Cell full: no, 14 of 32 used. sim too large: no, 6 CPU, and it fits on either machine for a batch task. Reports' priority: necessary (prod may preempt batch) but not sufficient. Placement: best fit puts sim on m1 (left 1 CPU after report1, against 6 on m2) every time. Limits: svc2 reserves all 16 CPU of m2 while using 4. No checkpoint: every preemption loses everything.

  3. A report is prod, so it is placed against limits: m1 commits svc1's 10 + sim's 6 = 16, m2 commits svc2's 16; neither fits 6 more, so preemption is needed, and only m1 has a lower-priority task to evict. m2 is unusable for prod because svc2's limit is its whole capacity.

(b) Checkpoint sim every few minutes: progress survives preemption and it finishes within the horizon. Also possible: place batch work away from machines that receive periodic prod work, or limit preemptions per task (a task preempted k times becomes non-preemptible for its next run).
