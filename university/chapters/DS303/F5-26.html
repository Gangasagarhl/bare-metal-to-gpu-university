Story hook

Analogy

For the festival, the friends bake in four town kitchens. Each kitchen has eight ovens on two benches of four. Ovens on the same bench can pass trays to each other directly; to reach the other bench, someone has to carry the tray around the table.

Lucía, the organiser, hands out baking chores as they come in. On Monday morning four friends each ask for two ovens for a day of bread. Lucía wants to be fair to the kitchens, so she puts each friend in a different town. An hour later Yusuf arrives: the wedding cake needs eight ovens in one kitchen, all at once, because the layers must bake together. Every kitchen has six free ovens. Twenty-four ovens are free in total, and the cake waits until evening.

On Tuesday Lucía tries the opposite: she fills one kitchen before opening the next. The four bread bakers share one kitchen, and Yusuf's cake starts the moment he arrives. On Wednesday a friend asks for four ovens and Lucía gives her two on each bench, and the trays spend the day being carried around the table.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how a GPU becomes a schedulable resource in Slurm and in Kubernetes, and how a job is restricted to the GPUs it was given (device rules and CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES).
  • Predict and measure GPU fragmentation: compute, from a placement policy and a workload, which jobs wait although enough GPUs are free in total, and run a simulator to check.
  • Choose between spreading and packing, and add topology awareness, and state the cost of each choice.
  • Explain gang scheduling and the partial-allocation deadlock that occurs without it.
  • Compare ways of sharing one GPU among jobs (partitioning, a multi-process server, time slicing) by isolation and by what is scheduled.
  • Write a GPU job script that checks its GPUs before starting the real work: the first piece of this course's project for the F8 labs.
Jargon box
Generic resource (Slurm GRES)
Simple words: Slurm's way to count things on a node that are not CPUs or memory, such as GPUs. Analogy: the number of ovens in each kitchen, written on Lucía's list. Precise: a named, countable per-node resource (for example GPUs, optionally with a type) declared in Slurm's configuration and requested by jobs; Slurm allocates specific devices to the job and can restrict the job to them. D1
Device plugin (Kubernetes)
Simple words: a helper program on each Kubernetes node that tells the cluster "this node has 8 GPUs" and connects a pod to the GPUs it was given. Analogy: the kitchen's caretaker, who counts the ovens and unlocks the ones assigned to you. Precise: a node-local component that registers with the kubelet, advertises a countable extended resource (for example GPUs of one vendor), and, when a pod is assigned devices, returns what the container needs to use them (device files, environment variables, mounts). D2
Device visibility (GPU)
Simple words: which GPUs a program can see and use; a job should see only the GPUs it was given. Analogy: only your assigned ovens are unlocked. Precise: the set of GPUs a process can open and enumerate, limited from outside by device access rules (control groups, container device lists) and, for CUDA programs, by the CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES environment variable, which restricts and renumbers the devices the CUDA runtime reports. L1 D5
GPU fragmentation
Simple words: free GPUs scattered over many nodes, so that a job needing many GPUs on one node cannot start although enough are free in total. Analogy: 24 free ovens, but never 8 in one kitchen. Precise: the state in which the free capacity of a cluster, summed over nodes, satisfies a request, but no set of nodes satisfies the request's per-node shape; it is caused by placement decisions and job sizes. R1
Packing and spreading (placement policy)
Simple words: packing fills busy nodes first; spreading puts each job on the emptiest node. Analogy: Tuesday's rule and Monday's rule. Precise: node-scoring rules: packing (best fit, most-allocated) chooses the feasible node with the least free capacity, keeping whole nodes free for large jobs; spreading (least-allocated) chooses the node with the most free capacity, balancing load and limiting what one node failure affects. D6 R1
GPU topology (island)
Simple words: how the GPUs inside a node are connected; some pairs talk much faster than others. Analogy: ovens on the same bench pass trays directly. Precise: the interconnect graph of a node's GPUs, network adapters and CPU sockets (direct GPU links, PCIe switches, CPU root complexes); a group of GPUs joined by the fast links is called an island in this chapter; placing a job's GPUs inside one island, near its CPU and network adapter, speeds its communication (DG401). D6 R1
Gang scheduling
Simple words: starting all the parts of a parallel job at the same time, or none of them. Analogy: the cake's layers bake together or not at all. Precise: allocating and starting all processes of a parallel job simultaneously (all-or-nothing); without it, parts of several jobs can hold resources while waiting for their other parts, wasting the resources or deadlocking. D7
GPU sharing (MIG, MPS, time slicing)
Simple words: ways for several jobs to use one GPU. Analogy: splitting a big oven into fixed compartments, letting several bakers use it at once, or taking turns. Precise: Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) partitions supported NVIDIA GPUs into isolated instances with their own memory and compute slices; the Multi-Process Service (MPS) lets kernels from several processes run concurrently through one server; time slicing alternates contexts on the whole GPU. They differ in isolation, in memory protection and in what the scheduler counts. D3 D4 L1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A GPU is a big, expensive helper chip. A cluster has many of them, a few in each computer. When you ask the cluster for GPUs, the scheduler must decide which computer and which GPUs you get, and then make sure your program can use only those.

How the scheduler chooses matters. If it scatters small jobs everywhere, a big job that needs a whole computer's GPUs cannot start, even though many GPUs are free. If it gives a job GPUs that are far apart inside the computer, the job runs slower. And if a job needs several computers, it must get all of them at once, or none.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A GPU as a resource the scheduler counts

To Slurm and Kubernetes, a CPU core and a gigabyte of memory are built-in resources. A GPU is not: it is declared. In Slurm, the administrator declares GPUs per node as a generic resource (GRES), and a job asks for them per node, per task or in total. In Kubernetes, a device plugin on each node advertises a count, and a pod asks for a whole number of them in its resource limits. In both, the scheduler then counts GPUs like any other resource, and the request's shape (how many per node, on how many nodes) matters as much as the total. D1 D2

Making a job see only its GPUs

Counting is not enough: if a job given GPUs 1 and 3 can use GPU 0, it collides with another job. Two mechanisms restrict it (Figure 3). First, the node denies access to the other GPUs' device files: Slurm can do this with control groups, and a container runtime gives a container only the device files it was assigned (F5-24). Second, for CUDA programs, the variable CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES tells the CUDA runtime which devices to enumerate. The CUDA 12.0 header in the build container recommends it "to restrict CUDA to only use those GPUs that have peer-to-peer support" and points to the programming guide's section on CUDA environment variables. Placement: where the GPUs come from

Once a request is counted, the scheduler chooses nodes and GPUs. Listing 1 (gpupack.cpp) models four nodes of eight GPUs and three rules. Spread puts a job on the feasible node with the most free GPUs. Pack puts it on the feasible node with the fewest free GPUs, that is, best fit. Pack+topology also tries to keep a job of up to four GPUs inside one island. Multi-node jobs are placed all-or-nothing. R1 runs the same workload under each rule. R1

Spreading is what F5-25's toy scheduler did with CPUs (most free CPU wins), and for CPU services it has reasons: load balance, memory bandwidth per job, and fewer jobs lost when one node fails. For GPUs, where many jobs want a whole node or a whole island, it manufactures fragmentation. Packing keeps whole nodes free, but it does not by itself respect topology: R1's pack run gave job h GPUs 2, 3, 4 and 5, two on each island. R1

Gangs: all or nothing

Job f needs eight GPUs on each of two nodes. Listing 1 places it only when both nodes are available (lines 81–82). Slurm does the same for a multi-node job: the job starts on all its nodes or waits. Kubernetes, by default, schedules each pod on its own (F5-25). Suppose two training jobs each need two full nodes and two nodes are free: the scheduler may place one pod of each job, and both jobs then hold a node each, waiting forever for a second. That is a deadlock built from reasonable single-pod decisions. Gang scheduling prevents it by admitting a job only when all its pods can be placed. D7 D2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why GPU fragmentation is worse than CPU fragmentation

Three properties of GPU workloads make the "fits in total, not per node" trap of F5-22 more expensive. Requests are coarse: a CPU job asks for 3 of 64 cores, a GPU job asks for 1, 2, 4 or 8 of 8 GPUs, so a single small job can make a whole node infeasible for an 8-GPU job. Durations are long: a training job runs for hours or days, so a bad placement persists. And the hardware is expensive per unit, so idle GPU-minutes are the cost that matters. In R1, from minute 2 to minute 19 under spreading, 24 GPUs stood idle while jobs needing 24 GPUs waited: 18 × 24 = 432 GPU-minutes lost to placement alone in a 4-node toy. R1

Production schedulers attack the problem from several sides: packing scores; separating whole-node jobs into their own partition or node pool; reservations for large jobs so that small ones only backfill into gaps that do not delay them (F5-23's backfill); and, in some clusters, migrating or preempting small jobs to defragment (F5-27). One named study of a multi-tenant GPU cluster for deep-learning training (D8) is remembered as attributing part of the queueing delay to locality constraints and fragmentation; treat that as a direction to read, not as a finding, until the source is opened. D8

Topology inside the node

In real GPU servers, GPUs connect through direct GPU-to-GPU links (NVLink on NVIDIA systems, sometimes through switches that join all GPUs), PCIe switches and the CPU sockets' root complexes; network adapters hang off particular PCIe switches, and each switch is closer to one CPU socket. A data-parallel job's all-reduce runs at the speed of its slowest link (DG401's F8-04 measures this). So the placement question has three parts: which GPUs (inside one fast domain), which CPU cores and memory (on the socket closest to those GPUs, NUMA), and which network adapter (the one on the same PCIe switch, for GPUDirect RDMA in DG402). Slurm can bind CPUs to GPUs and has a topology plugin for the network; Kubernetes has a node-level topology manager that aligns CPU, memory and device allocations. D6

Sharing one GPU

Small jobs (inference services, notebooks, unit tests) waste a whole GPU. Three sharing methods exist on NVIDIA hardware, with different guarantees. Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) partitions a supported GPU into instances, each with its own memory and compute slices and its own fault isolation; the scheduler counts instances as separate devices (the CUDA 12.0 header mentions a "MIG UUID which uniquely identifies the subscribed MIG compute instance"). MPS runs a server through which several processes' kernels execute concurrently; it improves utilisation for small kernels but offers weaker isolation, and the header lists error codes for MPS connection, RPC and server failures, a reminder that clients depend on a shared server. Time slicing lets several containers use the whole GPU in turns, with no memory isolation: one job can exhaust the memory of all. D3 D4 L1

Check the NVIDIA MIG User Guide (D3), the MPS documentation (D4) and the device plugin's documentation (D2).

Failure and health

GPUs fail in ways CPUs rarely do: uncorrectable memory errors, a GPU that falls off the bus, a driver that hangs. A scheduler that keeps placing jobs on a sick GPU turns one fault into a stream of failed jobs. Clusters therefore run health checks before and after jobs and drain bad nodes (Slurm's node states, Kubernetes' node conditions and taints from F5-25). Listing 3 applies the same idea from the job's side: it checks its GPUs first and stops with the check's exit code instead of starting hours of work on a broken allocation. D1 R3

How the hardware actually does it

A GPU appears to Linux as a PCIe device with a driver; user programs reach it through device files (on NVIDIA systems, one file per GPU plus control and unified-memory files), which the CUDA driver library opens. That is why device access rules work as isolation: a process that cannot open GPU 0's device file cannot create a context on it, whatever CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES says. The variable alone is a convenience, not a fence: a program can unset it. The control-group device rules of F5-24's kernel (version 1 has a device controller with allow and deny lists; version 2 uses a small program attached to the group) are the fence. D9 L1

Inside the node, direct GPU links carry peer-to-peer copies without the CPU; PCIe paths through a switch are slower, and paths through the CPU's root complex or between sockets are slower still. The runtime's peer-to-peer checks (DG401) tell you which pairs can access each other directly. None of this was measured in this build: the container has no GPU, and the lab's island model is a stand-in. L1

Worked example

Predict R1's spread run before reading it. Workload (gpupack.in): a, b, c, d arrive at minute 0, need 2 GPUs on one node for 20 minutes; e arrives at 1, needs 8 GPUs on one node for 15 minutes; f arrives at 2, needs 8 GPUs on each of 2 nodes for 15 minutes; g, h, i arrive at 40 and need 2, 4 and 2 GPUs for 10 minutes.

  1. Minute 0, spread. All nodes have 8 free; ties go to the lowest number, so a → n0. Now n0 has 6, the others 8: b → n1 (8 > 6). Likewise c → n2, d → n3. Every node has 6 free.
  2. Minute 1: e needs 8 on one node. No node has 8 free; total free = 4 × 6 = 24 ≥ 8. e waits: a fragmentation wait.
  3. Minute 2: f needs two nodes with 8 free each; none has. Total free 24 ≥ 16. f waits.
  4. e waits at minutes 1 to 19 (19 minute-waits); f at minutes 2 to 19 (18). Sum 37, the "minute-waits" figure in R1.
  5. Minute 20: a–d end and release their GPUs (the release happens before placement in the same minute). e → n0 (all tie, lowest number); f → n1 and n2. Start times e = 20, f = 20; total wait = 19 + 18 = 37 minutes.
  6. Pack instead: a → n0 (tie), b → n0 (6 free is fewer than 8 and fits), c → n0 (4), d → n0 (2). e at minute 1 → n1; f at minute 2 → n2 and n3. Nobody waits.
  7. Minute 40 under pack: g → n0 (GPUs 0, 1). h needs 4: n0 has 6 free, fewer than the others' 8, so n0, and the first free GPUs are 2, 3, 4, 5: across both islands. Under pack+topology, island 0 has only 2 free, island 1 has 4: h gets 4–7.

All seven steps match R1, including the cross-island placement of h at minute 40. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Ovens are identical and independent. GPUs in one cluster differ in model, memory and links; a request may name a type, and a "GPU" in a MIG cluster may be a slice of one.
    • 

  • Lucía can see every oven. A real scheduler sees only what the node agents report; a GPU that is sick but not yet reported is still handed out.
    • 

  • In the story, the cake's waiting costs an afternoon. In a GPU cluster the idle GPU-minutes are the cost, and they add up to the most expensive waste in the building.
    • 

  • A locked oven stays locked. CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES is a request to the runtime, not a lock; the lock is the device rule on the node.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Hard-coding device numbers. Symptom: "invalid device ordinal" or a job using someone else's GPU. Cause: cudaSetDevice(3) in code that runs with renumbered devices. Fix: use indices 0 to count − 1 of what the runtime reports, and log CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES and the PCI bus numbers.
    • 

  • Asking for a total when you need a shape. Symptom: an 8-GPU job lands as 2 GPUs on each of 4 nodes and runs at a fraction of the expected speed. Cause: a total-only request lets the scheduler split it. Fix: request GPUs per node and the node count.
    • 

  • Requesting GPUs but too few CPUs or too little memory. Symptom: GPUs at low utilisation, data loading saturated. Cause: default CPU and memory requests. Fix: request CPUs and memory per GPU, and bind them to the GPUs' socket.
    • 

  • Small jobs scattered over GPU nodes. Symptom: large jobs pend with many GPUs free (the forensic lab). Fix: packing, a separate partition or node pool for small jobs, reservations for large ones.
    • 

  • Gangs without gang scheduling. Symptom: some workers of a distributed job running and waiting at their first collective, others pending. Cause: pods scheduled one by one. Fix: a gang-aware scheduler or queue in Kubernetes; in Slurm, one multi-node job rather than many single-node ones.
    • 

  • Leftover processes. Symptom: a fresh job on a "free" GPU runs out of memory at start. Cause: a previous job's process still holds memory. Fix: the scheduler's cleanup and health check between jobs; report the node.
    •

  • nvcc not found: Listing 2 needs the CUDA toolkit; the placement model does not.
    • 

  • The model never prints a waiting line: the job fits somewhere, or the total free GPUs are fewer than requested (then it is a full cluster, not fragmentation, and nothing is printed).
    • 

  • On a GPU node, the device count is 0 or the error is "no CUDA-capable device": the job was given no GPU (check the request) or the device rules hide all GPUs.
    • 



Extension. Give each node a failure time and make the model drain the node: jobs running there end, and new ones avoid it. Compare how many job-minutes each policy loses (packing puts more jobs on one node).

Summary


    

  • GPUs are declared, countable resources: generic resources in Slurm, extended resources advertised by device plugins in Kubernetes; requests have a shape (per node × nodes), not only a total.
    • 

  • A job is fenced to its GPUs by device rules on the node; CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES restricts and renumbers what the CUDA runtime reports (local CUDA 12.0 header; renumbering unverified here).
    • 

  • Spreading small jobs fragments a GPU cluster: in R1, 24 free GPUs and 37 minute-waits; packing removed all of them.
    • 

  • Topology matters inside a node: packing without topology split a 4-GPU job across islands; a topology-aware rule kept it inside one.
    • 

  • Multi-node jobs need gang scheduling; one-pod-at-a-time scheduling can deadlock gangs.
    • 

  • MIG, MPS and time slicing share a GPU with decreasing isolation.
    • 

  • Jobs should check their GPUs before working and stop with a clear exit code (Listing 3, untested under Slurm and on hardware).
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Device access rules on the node (control-group device rules, the container's device list) and the CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES variable read by the CUDA runtime. The device rules are the fence; the variable can be changed by the program.
    2. 

  2. GPU requests are coarse fractions of a node (1–8 of 8) and jobs run long, so one small job on each node makes every node infeasible for a whole-node job for a long time; CPU requests are small fractions of many cores, so leftover capacity on a node is usable by more job sizes.
    3. 

  3. Spread: 3 → n0, 3 → n1, 3 → n2 (each emptiest); the 8-GPU job finds 5, 5, 5 free and waits. Pack: 3 → n0 (tie, lowest), 3 → n0 (5 free is fewest that fits), then n0 has 2 free, so 3 → n1 (n1 and n2 tie at 8; lowest); n2 is empty: the 8-GPU job starts on n2.
    4. 

  4. At minute 41: under spread, g, h, i went to n0, n1, n2 at minute 40 (each the emptiest at its turn), leaving n3 empty, so j starts on n3. Under pack and pack+topology, g, h, i all went to n0, so n1 is empty and j starts there. j starts at 41 under all three policies. (Checked by running the modified input.)
    5. 

  5. Each job gets one node and waits forever for its second: a deadlock, with 16 GPUs idle. Prevent it with gang scheduling (admit a job only when all its pods fit), or with a queue that admits whole jobs against quota before their pods are created; in Slurm, the multi-node job is placed all at once.
