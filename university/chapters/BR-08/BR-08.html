Story hook

Rafael has ridden the school's bicycle on the smooth playground all year. Today his own bicycle arrives, and he wants to ride it straight down the hill to his friend's house. His aunt Nadia says yes, but not like that.

First she squeezes the brakes with the bicycle standing still, and lets Rafael do it too. She measures the saddle against his leg. Then he rides slowly round the empty yard while she walks beside him, one hand near the back of the saddle. The new bicycle pulls a little to the left, the tyres are softer, and the brakes bite later than the school's. After each lap she writes one line in a little notebook: what they changed, how it felt, what the cycle computer said. When the saddle feels too low, they raise it, and change nothing else until the next lap. Only when the yard is boring do they try the quiet street.

Rafael's riding skill came with him; nothing he learned on the playground was wrong. What changed was everything around the skill, and his aunt's plan was built to find those changes one at a time, safely, and in writing.

This bridge does the same for a controller that leaves the simulator for a real robot or a drone on the bench. The code comes with you unchanged. The world around it does not, and the logs are how you find out how.

What you will be able to do

  • List what carries over from the simulator to the hardware (software stack, interfaces, tests, logs, controller structure) and what changes (noise, delays, friction, vibration, battery sag, calibration errors, unmodelled dynamics, timing jitter, physical danger), most dangerous first.
  • Run a paired run: the same controller, bench sequence and log format in the simulator and on the supervised kit, with the conditions of each run recorded.
  • Explain every difference between the two logs from a column of the log (time stamps, supply voltage, external reference, step-test fit, spectrum), and identify the kit's parameters from them.
  • Close the gap one effect at a time with an updated simulator, and tell a parameter error from a structural error of the model.
  • Diagnose the four traps of the bridge from real runs: simulation gains trusted on hardware, a skipped bench test, testing without an e-stop or with propellers on, and two changes made at once.
  • Prepare a first hardware session that is safe: bench first, propellers off, e-stop in hand, supervision below L3, local drone rules read and recorded.
Jargon box
Paired run
Simple words: running exactly the same program, the same test and the same recording once in the simulator and once on the real machine, so that the two recordings can be laid side by side. Analogy: riding the same route on the trainer and on the road with the same cycle computer recording both rides. Precise: two executions of one controller build and one test plan (sequence, gains, limits, scenario) against two worlds behind the same hardware interface, each producing a log with identical columns and a run record; only the world and its conditions may differ, and the comparison tool checks that (Listing 4, section 1 of its output). R1 R2
Run record
Simple words: the lines at the top of a log that say how the test was done: which machine, which program, which settings, in what conditions. Analogy: the line in the ride diary that says which bicycle, which tyre pressure and what the weather was. Precise: the header of every log written by bench.hpp: world, conditions, controller, gains, period, calibration parameter, filter settings, scenario and how the run ended; a measurement without it cannot be compared or repeated (the rule F9-48 applies to latency). R4 C2
Unmodelled dynamics
Simple words: things the real machine does that the simulator does not contain at all, not even with a wrong number. Analogy: the school bicycle never pulled to the left, so no amount of practice on it taught Rafael to correct for that. Precise: states, nonlinearities or couplings of the physical system that have no counterpart in the model's structure (for example dry friction modelled as an input deadband, a driver's current limit, a once-per-revolution disturbance, compliance or resonance); they cannot be fixed by identifying parameters, only by changing the model's structure, and they show up as a residual that stays after the parameters are right (Listing 5). D2 R3
Bring-up gains
Simple words: deliberately gentle controller settings for the first runs on new hardware. Analogy: riding the first laps at walking pace. Precise: controller gains chosen for a large stability margin (here the closed-loop time constant 0.05 s of F9-22, which leaves a delay margin several times the loop's delay), used until the hardware's own delays and parameters have been measured; faster gains come later, from the identified model, one step at a time. R5 R9 D1
Reference check
Simple words: checking the robot's own speed reading against a separate instrument before trusting it. Analogy: riding next to a friend whose cycle computer you know is right. Precise: a short closed-loop hold at a low setpoint on the bench, during which the controller's measured speed is compared with an external reference that does not depend on the software's calibration parameters (a tachometer, a timed distance); the sequence continues only if the ratio is within a tolerance decided beforehand (Listing 7 uses 5 %). R6
Arming interlock
Simple words: a rule in the software that refuses to drive the motors until the safety checklist says it may. Analogy: the aunt's hand on the saddle: no riding until she is there. Precise: a decision function between the request to drive motors (motor test, closed loop on the bench, flight) and the outputs, evaluated against the signed bench record (propellers off, e-stop tested, supervision, rules recorded); it complements the hardware e-stop and never replaces it. Flight stacks have their own arming checks, which are a separate thing (see the unverified box in Layer 3). R7 C1
Stand-in model (of the kit)
Simple words: a pretend kit, written by the university, that plays the real kit in this build because there is no real one. Analogy: a practice partner who imitates the road's bumps, so the plan can be rehearsed. Precise: kit_model.hpp: a motor, battery, driver, encoder and clock model with invented exercise values, used wherever this chapter says "kit"; it shows the method honestly but proves nothing about any real kit, and on real hardware it is deleted. R1

Terms from earlier chapters used here: sim-to-real gap (F9-22) and reality gap (F9-71), bench test, sign test and hardware interface (F9-22), emergency stop, clear zone and supervision (RB101), system identification, calibration parameter and delay margin (F9-71), jitter (F9-45), voltage sag (F10-11), ground truth (F9-44), Coulomb (dry) friction (MA301), hardware in the loop (HITL) and test ladder (DN401), motor test and vibration level (DN202).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

In the simulator, the robot lives in a perfect world. Its clock ticks exactly on time, its battery never gets tired, its wheel never sticks, and its sensor tells the exact truth. A real robot lives in our world. Its messages arrive a little late, its battery gets weaker when the motor pulls hard, its wheel sticks a bit before it starts, and its sensor counts in small steps and sometimes miscounts.

The good news: the robot's program does not need to change to go from one world to the other. It talks to the wheel through the same small set of buttons in both worlds. So we run the very same program twice, once in each world, and write down everything in the same notebook format. Then we put the two notebooks side by side and ask, for every difference: which line in the real notebook explains it? A late clock? A tired battery? A wrong number in the settings?

And because the real wheel can pinch fingers and a real propeller can cut, the first real runs happen on a table, with the wheel in the air or the propellers taken off, with a big stop button in a grown-up's hand, changing only one thing at a time. A drone learner can think of carrying a tray: you rehearse the route first with empty cups, and only then with real drinks.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Every section of this layer opens the same way as a ride on the new bicycle: what you bring with you, then what is different. The numbers come from one paired run of this build (Listing 3), in which the "kit" is the stand-in model of Listing 2, because the build container has no hardware. The stand-in's numbers are invented; the method is the point.

What carries over

The software stack and the interface. The control program talks to hardware only through MotorIo (F9-22): read the encoder counter, set the duty cycle, let time pass. Listing 1 keeps that interface and adds three readings every world must supply for the logs: the time stamp of each sample, the supply voltage, and a reference speed measured outside the control path (on the bench a tachometer; in the simulator the true speed). Nothing in the controller knows which world it runs in. R1

The controller structure. The F9-21 PID library, unchanged, inside the F9-22 safety layers: sign test, setpoint limit, ramp, stall watchdog, an overspeed stop on the measured speed, and "every exit leaves the motor commanded off". The gains are the ones identified on the simulator in F9-22 (kp = 0.0482, ki = 0.2474). R1

The tests. The bench sequence is a test plan: rest (0.3 s), sign test (0.2 s), coast, open-loop step test at duty 0.25 (1 s), coast, then the closed-loop scenario 150 rad/s, 250 rad/s, 0 rad/s with a 600 rad/s² ramp. The same plan runs in both worlds, and the build that runs it is the same build that passed in simulation. R4

The logs. One format: a run record, then one row per period with phase, nominal time, sample time, setpoint, measured speed, duty, supply voltage and reference speed. The comparison tool refuses to call two logs a pair unless their run records agree on everything except the world and its conditions; in this build they did ("different (other than world and conditions): none"). R2

The first contact: the plan itself needed re-tuning

The first kit run did not reach the controller at all. It stopped at the sign test: duty 0.1 gave "+0.0 rad/s", and the last logged rows show the measured speed jittering between −0.61, 0.00 and +0.61 rad/s while the supply read 11.12 V. In the simulator the same duty gave +25.6 rad/s (F9-22, run R3). Duty 0.1 is about 1.1 V on this battery, and the kit's wheel did not break free. The sign test did its job (it refused to close the loop on a wheel it could not see turning), and the fix was one change, written down before the next run: sign-test duty 0.1 → 0.2. Both worlds then ran the changed plan again, so that the pair stayed a pair. Parameters that worked in simulation need re-tuning on hardware, and that includes the parameters of your tests. R1

What changes, most dangerous first

1. Physical danger. A simulated wheel cannot hurt anyone; a real one can pinch, a real propeller can cut skin and eyes, and a battery can burn (guide "Honesty first"). Software limits only act on what the software measures and only while the software runs: trap 2 shows a wheel that reached 528 rad/s while its own log showed the 250 rad/s it was asked for, and trap 3 shows a hung program leaving a motor at full drive. That is why the first runs happen on the bench, with an e-stop in the power path. C1 R6 R7

2. Delays. On hardware the command passes through a bus, a driver board and the driver's own update before the motor sees it. The step-test fit of Listing 4 found a dead time of 12.0 ms in the kit's log and 0.0 ms in the simulator's. Delay is the effect a feedback loop tolerates least, because the controller corrects an error that is already old; trap 1 shows gains that were stable in simulation oscillating on the kit for that reason. R2 D1

3. Timing jitter. The simulator's samples are exactly 10.00 ms apart. The kit's t_sample column shows intervals between 9.60 and 10.40 ms (each sample 0 to 0.50 ms late). The controller still divides the counts by the nominal 10 ms, so a 10.4 ms interval reads 4 % fast. In the holds, the difference between measured and reference speed correlates with the interval error at 0.98: most of the kit's speed "noise" is not the encoder at all, it is the clock. R2 D3

4. Calibration errors. During the holds the kit's measured speed sat on the setpoint (150.19 rad/s) while the bench reference read 153.83 rad/s: a ratio of 0.9769 in both holds. A constant ratio is the signature of a scale error in the measurement path; 1024 × 0.9769 ≈ 1000 suggests that the encoder fitted has 1000 counts per revolution while the software says 1024. The loop cannot see this error: it makes the measured speed right. Only the external reference shows it. R2

5. Friction and unmodelled dynamics. The kit needs more voltage for the same speed (duty 0.215 at 150 rad/s against the simulator's 0.169), and its wheel stops by itself when the drive stops, while the simulated wheel was still turning at about 12 rad/s when the loop began (Figure 3). The simulator models friction as a deadband on the input; the kit has friction as a torque that always opposes motion. That is a difference of structure, not of a number, and Layer 3 shows that no parameter can fix it. The kit also has a driver current limit, which this scenario never reached: a log cannot reveal what the scenario never excited. R2 R3

6. Battery sag. The kit's supply read 11.16 V at rest and fell to 10.86 V at the lowest; during the step test it went from 11.16 to 11.10 V. The controller converts volts to duty with a nominal 12 V, so every command delivers less than it was computed for; the integral term makes up the difference, which is why the kit's duty is higher. On a bench with the wheel in the air the motor draws little current, so the sag here is small; on the floor, carrying the robot, it will not be (Layer 3). R1 R2 D8

7. Noise. At rest, with duty 0, the kit's measured speed took the values −0.61, 0.00 and +0.61 rad/s (standard deviation 0.45 rad/s): one count of encoder edge noise per period, since one count in 10 ms is 2π/(1024 × 0.01) = 0.61 rad/s. The simulator's was exactly 0. R2

8. Vibration. The spectrum of the kit's reference speed in the 150 rad/s hold has its largest peak at 25.0 Hz (amplitude 0.89 rad/s), and in the 250 rad/s hold at 41.2 Hz, while one revolution takes 24.7 Hz and 40.9 Hz at those speeds. A disturbance whose frequency follows the wheel's speed, once per revolution, points at the wheel itself (here an eccentric wheel in the stand-in; on a real robot an unbalanced or bent part). On a drone, vibration comes from motors and propellers and reaches the IMU (F10-09); Part B of trap 1 shows what it does to a derivative gain. R2 R5

9. And therefore: the gains. Put together, the kit is slower (time constant 0.322 s against 0.196 s), later (12 ms), noisier and slightly mis-calibrated. The bring-up gains still worked, with a small overshoot: the reference peaked 4.0 % above its final value. Faster gains tuned in the simulator did not (trap 1). Parameters that worked in simulation need re-tuning, from the hardware's identified numbers. R2 R5

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

A bicycle shop does not argue about why a new bicycle feels different; it measures the frame and the wheels. This layer turns the list of Layer 2 into measurements, then into an updated model, then into a decision about what still differs.

Identifying the kit from its own log

Dead time and time constant from the step test. An encoder does not report the speed at an instant; each sample is the average speed over the interval since the previous sample. Listing 4 therefore fits a first-order-plus-dead-time model ω(t) = A(1 − e−(t−t₀−L)/τ) to the period averages, using the logged sample times as the interval edges, with a grid over L (0 to 40 ms in 0.5 ms steps) and τ, and the least-squares A for each pair. The simulator's log gives τ = 0.196 s, L = 0.0 ms (fit residual 0.34 rad/s); the kit's gives τ = 0.322 s, L = 12.0 ms (residual 2.54 rad/s, larger because of the kit's noise and its non-linear friction). Estimating a model's parameters from recorded inputs and outputs is system identification; the textbook treatment, including the choice of model structure, is Ljung's. R2 D2

Gain and friction from two holds. In steady state the first-order model with a deadband says ω = K(V − Vd), where V is the voltage actually applied: duty × logged supply, not duty × 12. Two holds give two equations: with the reference speeds 153.83 and 256.13 rad/s and applied voltages 2.388 and 3.440 V, K = 97.2 rad/s per volt and Vd = 0.81 V (worked example C). Using the measured supply matters: F9-22 warned that identifying with the nominal 12 V makes the gain look smaller. R2 R9

Calibration from the reference. The ratio of mean measured to mean reference speed in each hold (0.9764 and 0.9775) estimates CPRtrue/CPRparam. Two holds at different speeds giving the same ratio rule out an offset (an offset would change the ratio with speed) and point to a scale. R2

Jitter from the time stamps, and proof that it is the noise. The prediction "measured error = reference × (interval / period − 1)" can be computed for every sample from two logged columns. Its correlation with the actual measured-minus-reference error is 0.98 in both holds. A hypothesis that predicts the noise sample by sample is much stronger evidence than one that only matches its size. R2

Vibration from the spectrum. A Hann-windowed FFT of the reference speed over the last 0.8 s of each hold (80 samples at 100 Hz, so bins 1.25 Hz apart) finds the once-per-revolution line. The sampling rate limits what you can see: at 100 Hz nothing above 50 Hz is visible, and a real drone's motor vibration lies far above any rate you could log this way (Layer 3 of F10-09). R2

Closing the gap one effect at a time

Listing 5 builds an updated simulator from the identified numbers only (never from the stand-in's source) and runs the unchanged bench sequence on it. It scores each run against the kit's log with the root-mean-square distance between the two reference traces (5-sample averages, so that noise does not dominate), split by part of the loop: the 150 rad/s part, the 250 rad/s part, and the ramp down. Figure 4 shows the cumulative ladder. R3

Three things in this table are worth more than the table itself. R3

The calibration explains the 250 rad/s part almost completely (its distance falls from 5.67 to 0.34 rad/s when the 1000-count encoder is added), because in a steady hold the only thing that matters is where the loop holds the true speed.

A correct parameter can make the match worse. Adding the measured supply (11.16 V) raised the total distance from 8.08 to 8.59 rad/s, and the ramp-down distance from 10.44 to 12.21. The supply value is right; something else in the model was wrong in a way that the wrong supply had partly hidden. Errors compensate each other. That is one more reason to add one effect at a time, and to look at the distance by part of the run, not only in total.

Some of the gap is structure, not numbers. After all seven identified effects the ramp-down distance was still 13.28 rad/s and the 150 rad/s part 6.87 rad/s. Both are the places where the wheel starts or stops, where friction matters most. Replacing the deadband with a friction torque that always opposes motion (one structural change, with the same identified numbers) brought the total to 1.64 rad/s and every part to about 2 rad/s or below. No amount of parameter tuning on the deadband model would have done that: unmodelled dynamics need a new model. D2

Two honest limits. First, the stand-in is itself a model, so the updated simulator can match it far better than it could ever match a real kit; on hardware the residual will not fall to the noise floor, and you decide which remaining differences matter for the task. Second, the score has noise of its own: the "overshoot" column moved between 5.1 % and 5.9 % with effects that cannot change the true overshoot, because the peak of a noisy measured signal is partly noise. Read every metric together with its noise.

Why simulation gains fail first through delay

A pure delay Td removes ωTd radians of phase at frequency ω without changing the gain, so a loop with crossover frequency ωc and phase margin φm tolerates at most φm/ωc of added delay (F9-71 derives and checks this). Faster gains raise ωc and shrink the tolerable delay in proportion. Worked example D applies the integrator approximation to this chapter's loop: the bring-up gains have a crossover of 13.2 rad/s on the kit, where 22.2 ms of loop delay costs 0.29 rad; the simulator-tuned gains have 82.4 rad/s, a delay margin of about 18.3 ms, and a loop delay of 22.2 ms, beyond it. Trap 1's kit run oscillates, as predicted. R9 R5 D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The delay-margin formula and the continuous-time integrator approximation are standard control theory recalled by the author (as in F9-71); "Feedback Systems" (D1) was not opened in this build. The approximation is poor when the crossover approaches the sampling rate: for the simulator-tuned gains in the simulator it gives ωc × delay = 1.51 rad, close to the limit, yet the simulator run is stable with 9.0 % overshoot (R5). Trust the run, not the approximation, and use the approximation only to decide where to look.

The drone path: what the props-off bench can and cannot show

Carrying a tray of drinks, you can rehearse the route with empty cups, but you cannot learn how full glasses slosh. A multirotor on the bench with its propellers removed makes no thrust, so its attitude and rate loops cannot be closed there. What the props-off bench checks is the signal chain: that the flight controller boots the build that passed SITL and HITL, that sensors read sensible values in the right axes, that each output drives the right motor in the right direction (the motor test of F10-07 to F10-10), that the kill switch or e-stop works, and that the logs carry the same fields as the SITL logs. The first test of the closed loop with thrust is restrained, tethered or a supervised first hover, after the go/no-go review of F10-37. C1 D5 D6

Two of the bridge's "changes" depend on thrust and are therefore almost absent from a props-off bench log: battery sag, which grows with current, and propeller-driven vibration. A bench log that shows little of either says nothing about flight. Part B of trap 1 (a model, not a bench test) shows why that matters for gains: a derivative gain that is harmless without vibration turns a 160 Hz vibration on the gyro into motor commands that are saturated 60.3 % of the time. R5

The statement that sag and propeller vibration grow with thrust is physical reasoning (sag is current times internal resistance, F10-11); confirm the size on your vehicle from its own logs.

Measurement discipline: what makes two logs comparable

A difference between two logs is evidence only if the two runs differ in nothing you did not record. The run record therefore names the world, the conditions, the controller, the gains, the period, the calibration parameter, the filter settings, the scenario and how the run ended (Listing 1, lines 225–250). Listing 4 checks the pair before comparing it. The same rule governs the latency measurements of F9-48 and the flight-log analysis of F10-38, and it is the "conditions" part of the curriculum's log-entry template (19.4). On real hardware add what the simulator never needs: the kit's serial number or name, the battery's identity and charge state as measured before the run, the firmware build, the room temperature if your parts are sensitive to it, and who supervised. R4 C2

How the hardware actually does it

On the kit, readEncoder reads a hardware counter that a timer in encoder mode (or an interrupt routine) advances on every edge of the encoder's two square waves; setDuty writes a PWM compare register that an H-bridge driver turns into an average motor voltage (F9-22, HW302). The three new readings come from hardware too: the time stamp from a monotonic hardware timer read at the moment of the counter read; the supply from an ADC channel behind a resistor divider (HW302); the reference from an instrument that does not pass through the robot's calibration parameters, such as a hand-held or clamped tachometer, or a timed distance on the floor. The delay that the step test measured is the sum of the bus message to the driver board, the driver's own update and the electrical response of the motor; F9-66 shows how to budget it. The e-stop is a switch in the motor power path, so it works when the program does not (trap 3); a command timeout on the driver board, where the board has one, is the electronic version of the same idea (F9-69: drive enable and host heartbeat). D8 R7

On a drone the same roles are played by the flight controller's IMU and its sampling clock, the ESCs and their protocol (F10-10), the power module that measures voltage and current (F10-11), and an RC kill switch or arming switch; the external reference is a motion-capture system or, outdoors, only the logs themselves. D5 D6

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. No motor, encoder, driver, battery, tachometer, flight controller or drone was used in this build. Every "kit" in this chapter is kit_model.hpp, a teaching model with invented values (12 ms delay, 0 to 0.5 ms lateness, 1000-count encoder, 11.6 V battery with 0.45 Ω, 2.5 A current limit, dry friction, a once-per-revolution torque). Real kits have more effects (inductance and the driver's current loop, gear backlash, compliance, temperature drift) and different numbers. Whether your driver has a command timeout, how your ADC divider is scaled, and what instrument can serve as the speed reference must come from the kit's own documents (D8), recorded in the dossier.

Safety

Bench first: the wheel off the ground or the motor clamped, nothing on the shaft that the plan does not name. Propellers off for every drone bench step, checked by a second person. E-stop in the motor power path, tested with the motor powered and commanded to zero before the first run, and in the supervisor's hand for the whole session. Supervision for every learner below L3. Speed and output limits set before the session and not raised during it. Battery handling as the battery and charger makers' instructions say (no numbers from memory). Stop at once on any unexpected sound, smell, heat or motion. Before any outdoor flight: read the current rules of your national aviation authority and record them in the lab sheet, with the land owner's permission and the go/no-go review of F10-37. See also the university's safety rules. C1 D7 D8

Worked example

Every number here is computed by Listing 10, whose inputs are copied from the outputs of Listings 3 and 4 and the forensic evidence. R9

A. The calibration error. If the encoder has 1000 counts per revolution and the software assumes 1024, every measured speed is 1000/1024 = 0.9766 times the true one. When the loop holds a measured 150 rad/s, the wheel turns at 153.6 rad/s; at 250, at 256.0 rad/s: 2.4 % fast. The kit's log gave a ratio of 0.9769 and references of 153.83 and 256.13 rad/s, within the noise of these values.

B. The clock. The counts in an interval are proportional to its real length, but the program divides by 10 ms. An interval of 10.4 ms makes a true 150 rad/s read 156 rad/s. In the forensic afternoon log, a 14.4 ms interval followed by a 5.6 ms one would read 216 and then 84 rad/s; the evidence shows 204.3 and 91.4 (the true speed and the exact lateness differ slightly).

C. Gain and deadband from two holds. K = (256.13 − 153.83)/(3.440 − 2.388) = 97.2 rad/s per V, and Vd = 2.388 − 153.83/97.2 = 0.81 V.

D. Delay against margin (integrator approximation, see the unverified box in Layer 3). Crossover ωc = kp × K × (supply/12) × (measured/true) / τ; margin = (π/2 − PI lag)/ωc. Bring-up gains: simulator 24.1 rad/s, margin 56.5 ms, loop delay 10.0 ms; kit 13.2 rad/s, margin 91.1 ms, loop delay 22.2 ms (half a period of sampling, half a period of averaging, 12 ms dead time, 0.25 ms mean lateness). Simulator-tuned gains: kit 82.4 rad/s, margin 18.3 ms against 22.2 ms of delay: beyond the margin, so sustained oscillation is expected, and trap 1 shows it.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Rafael feels the new bicycle pull to the left and corrects without thinking. A controller with fixed gains does not adapt: it keeps doing what its gains say, and the difference shows up only in a log that someone reads.
    • 

  • Aunt Nadia's hand on the saddle works as long as she is there. A software stop works only as long as the software runs and measures correctly: trap 3's hung program never ran its own watchdog, and trap 2's mis-calibrated wheel was over its speed limit while every software check passed. The real "hand on the saddle" is a switch in the power path.
    • 

  • Rafael's cycle computer and his friend's agree unless one is set up wrongly, and you can see both. A robot has no friend riding beside it unless you bring one: the external reference has to be planned, mounted and logged.
    • 

  • A slightly wrong bicycle is merely uncomfortable. A slightly late controller can be unstable: below the delay margin it is a little slower, above it the wheel shakes (trap 1). Small causes can have large, sudden effects.
    • 

  • For drone learners: rehearsing with empty cups teaches the route, but no sloshing. A props-off bench checks the drone's signal chain and nothing about its flight.
    •

The four traps of the bridge, each reproduced by a program that really ran in this build. All four run against the stand-in kit; trap 1 Part B is a flight-condition model. R5 R6 R7 R8



Trap 1: trusting simulation gains on hardware


Symptom: a loop that was crisp in simulation oscillates, overshoots or saturates on the first hardware run. Cause: the faster the gains, the less delay the loop tolerates, and the hardware has delays, slower dynamics and noise that the simulator lacked. How to find it: run the same plan in both worlds and compare the hold's spread and the saturation; check the identified dead time against the gains' margin. Fix: start with bring-up gains, identify the hardware, then raise the gains in steps, one per run.


Listing 6 — trap_gains.cpp






The simulator-tuned gains (closed-loop time constant 0.008 s by the F9-22 rule, line 51) gave 9.0 % overshoot and a quiet hold (0.52 rad/s) in the simulator; on the kit the hold's standard deviation was 16.67 rad/s and the duty saturated in 9.3 % of the loop: a sustained oscillation, visible in the printed trace as swings between about 130 and 176 rad/s, roughly every 80 ms. The bring-up gains had 5.0 % overshoot and a 0.97 rad/s spread on the same kit. In Part B, the derivative gain of 0.002 tuned without vibration saturates the motor command 60.3 % of the time once a 160 Hz vibration reaches the gyro; a smaller derivative gain with a 5 ms derivative filter keeps it at 0.0 % (lines 154–155). All values are invented teaching values. R5

Trap 2: skipping the bench test


Symptom: the first powered run is on the floor (or in the air), and the machine does something no simulation run did. Cause: wiring, sign and calibration faults do not exist in the simulator, and software limits act on the measured quantity, which is exactly what these faults corrupt. How to find it: the bench sequence: sign test, step test, reference check, before the real setpoint. Fix: make the bench sequence a gate that the program enforces and the supervisor signs.


Listing 7 — trap_bench.cpp






With the encoder channels swapped, the floor-first loop saw a negative speed, pushed harder, and was stopped by the overspeed check only when the measured speed reached −455.9 rad/s; the wheel had reached 468.5 rad/s. The bench sequence stopped at the sign test, with the wheel never above 55.8 rad/s. With a 512-count encoder and a 1024 parameter, nothing in software noticed anything on the floor: the run "completed", with the wheel at up to 528.2 rad/s, 1.55 s above the 400 rad/s limit, while its own measurement said 250. Only the reference check, comparing with the bench tachometer, stopped it (ratio 0.500). Note what the reference check did not catch: the correct kit's 2.4 % error passed the 5 % tolerance. Trap 3: testing without an e-stop, or without propellers removed


Symptom: a fault in the program leaves the motor running, and there is nothing to stop it. Cause: drivers hold the last command; a watchdog inside the same program stops with the program. How to find it: before the session, ask "if the program froze now, what would stop the motor?" and test that answer. Fix: a hardware e-stop in the power path, tested before the first run; where the driver offers it, a command timeout; propellers off for every drone bench step; an arming interlock that refuses to drive motors until the bench record is complete.


Listing 8 — trap_estop.cpp






The program froze 0.15 s into an acceleration, while commanding duty 0.79. With nothing else (A) or with a watchdog inside the same program (B, which last ran at 0.14 s), the wheel ran up to 759 rad/s and was still at 758 rad/s when the run ended: nearly three times the setpoint and far above the plan's limit. A command timeout in the driver (C) stopped the drive 0.05 s after the last command, and the wheel was below 5 rad/s at 0.62 s. The supervisor's e-stop (D), pressed after an assumed 1.0 s (an exercise value; measure your own), cut the power at 726 rad/s, and the wheel still coasted until 3.37 s: an e-stop removes power, not momentum, so hands stay clear until the wheel has stopped. Trap 4: changing two things at once


Symptom: after a session of changes the behaviour is "about the same", or better or worse, and nobody can say why. Cause: two changes whose effects add, cancel or interact. How to find it: the run record shows more than one difference between consecutive runs. Fix: one change per run, written in the run record before the run.


Listing 9 — trap_two_changes.cpp






The kit oscillated with the simulator-tuned gains (hold spread 16.67 rad/s). In one session an engineer halved the gains (A) and averaged the speed over 4 samples "to remove the noise" (B). The combined run still oscillated (10.71 rad/s, 18.0 % overshoot), and the tempting conclusion is "neither helped; it must be the hardware". One change at a time tells the truth: A alone fixed it (1.42 rad/s, 7.0 %), and B alone made it much worse (34.72 rad/s, 24.1 %), because a 4-sample average delays the measurement by about 1.5 periods and the loop was already short of delay margin. Summary


    

  • What carries over: the software stack and its interface, the controller structure and its safety layers, the tests, and the logs, unchanged. Make that literal: the same files, built once.

  • A paired run (same build, same plan, same log format, run records that differ only in world and conditions) turns "it behaves differently" into a list of differences.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. forensic_gen.cpp ran the same plan (bench sequence, gains 0.1207 and 0.6188, the team's gains) on two stand-in kits. The afternoon kit had three changes: battery open-circuit voltage 10.6 V instead of 11.6 V; command delay 30 ms instead of 12 ms (firmware 1.4 sends telemetry before the command); and every tenth sample 4 ms late (firmware 1.4's logging task). R10


Observation. Afternoon: 60 of 609 intervals longer than 12 ms (maximum 14.40 ms, none in the morning); supply 10.16 V at rest (11.16 in the morning), lowest 9.02 V; measured peak 236.8 rad/s against a reference peak of 178.1; measured spread 27.52 rad/s against a reference spread of 5.98. The measured signal is far worse than the wheel itself.


The excerpt. At 3.90420 the sample came 4.2 ms late; the measured speed reads 204.3 while the reference reads 152.0: the counts of a 14.2 ms interval divided by 10 ms (worked example B). The controller answers with duty −0.3573, and the next sample, only 6.2 ms later, reads 91.4, so the duty jumps to +0.8085. The same at 4.00410. The voltage dips (9.80 and 9.24 V, then 9.64 V) come four to five samples after these kicks: they are caused by the current the kicks draw, not the other way round.


Listing 4 on the pair (R11): section 2 shows intervals from 5.60 to 14.40 ms in the afternoon; section 4 fits a dead time of 34.0 ms against 12.0 ms (with a poor fit, 19.94 rad/s residual, because of the kicks); section 7 links the measured error to the interval error at 0.99 and 1.00. Do not trust the afternoon's static identification (147.8 rad/s per V, 1.19 V) or its overshoot from measured speed (57.9 %): both are computed from a measurement corrupted by the late samples.




Replay, one change at a time (R11): the battery alone changes almost nothing (reference spread 1.00 rad/s against the morning's 1.06); the late samples alone corrupt the measurement (measured spread 26.94); the 30 ms delay alone makes the wheel itself oscillate (reference spread 6.86, peak 183.6). Computing speed from the logged interval removes the kicks (measured spread 2.41) but not the effect of the delay (peak 175.9); with firmware 1.3's timing and the interval fix, even on the low battery, the wheel is calm (0.85).




Root cause. Firmware 1.4: telemetry sent before the command added delay, and the logging task made samples late; the speed calculation, dividing by the nominal period, turned late samples into speed spikes. The battery is a contributing condition at most (it lowers the loop gain slightly), not the cause; charging it would not have fixed anything, and would have been a second change in the next run.


Fix and verification. Send the command first and the telemetry after it, run logging outside the control task, and compute speed from the logged interval. Verify with a paired run against the morning log: intervals back within 0.5 ms, dead time back near 12 ms, measured and reference spreads back near the morning's.


Prevention. A firmware change is a change: it gets its own run, with the version in the run record, before anything else changes. Add a timing gate to the bench sequence (maximum lateness and the step-test dead time within stated limits) so that the program itself refuses a build whose timing moved. Wrong answers and why: "the battery" (the replay shows it alone changes almost nothing; the dips follow the kicks); "the gains are too high" (they were fine in the morning, and nothing in the run record changed them); "encoder noise" (the rest noise is the same in both logs, 0.46 and 0.43 rad/s).
