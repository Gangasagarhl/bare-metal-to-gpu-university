Story hook

For two years the team of one kitchen hall has worked like a clock. Every helper knows the recipe cards, the order queues at the pass run side by side, and Priya's stopwatch says how long each order takes. Everybody knows the hall's limit: when helpers wait, it is the pantry that is too slow, never the cooking.

Then the restaurant grows. Three more halls open in the same building, and next spring four more open in a second building across town. Tonight's dish is one giant soup: every hall cooks its share, but the seasoning must come from the taste of all the pots, so the halls must share before anyone can continue.

Three things go wrong in the first week. Tomás, who plans the deliveries, assumes that every road between two halls takes the same time; some roads are short corridors and some go through the lobby, and one goes across town. Priya times one small bowl on one road and announces "deliveries are fast"; a full pot on the same road tells a different story. And on Friday the hall across town never sends its bowl. Nobody had said what to do then, so all eight halls stand at their tables, waiting, until closing time.

This chapter is the walk from one hall to many: what you already know that still works, what is new, and the three mistakes that everybody makes on the first day.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain which one-GPU skills carry over unchanged (kernels, streams, events, the measurement protocol, roofline thinking) and which five things change when work spreads over several GPUs and machines.
  • Measure a peer latency and bandwidth matrix with a size sweep, repeat it, and decide from the data whether a slow entry is topology or noise.
  • Draw the link roofline of a link from its α and β, find its ridge (the half-bandwidth size), and show why a benchmark at one message size can rank links the wrong way round.
  • Predict the time of a ring all-reduce with the alpha–beta model before running it, run it, and explain every gap between prediction and measurement.
  • Diagnose a job that hangs or crashes because ranks called collectives in different orders, and a job that stops because one process died.
Jargon box
Scale-up and scale-out (GPU communication)
Simple words: scale-up means more GPUs inside one computer; scale-out means more computers joined by a network. Analogy: more halls in the same building, connected by corridors; more buildings, connected by roads across town. Precise: in this university, scale-up communication is between GPUs of one node over PCIe or GPU-to-GPU links (NVLink, xGMI), and scale-out communication is between nodes over network cards (Ethernet with TCP, InfiniBand or RoCE with RDMA); the two have different α and β and are usually combined in hierarchical algorithms (F8-10). C1 D6
Link roofline
Simple words: a picture of the best speed a link can give you, for every message size. Analogy: a small parcel is limited by the time it takes to start a delivery; a full truck is limited by the road's capacity. Precise: attainable bandwidth = min(β, n/α) for a message of n bytes over a link with start-up time α and bandwidth β; the two lines meet at the ridge n = αβ, where the alpha–beta model n/(α + n/β) gives exactly β/2. It is the roofline of F6-33 with message size in place of arithmetic intensity. D9 D4 R1
Message-size sweep
Simple words: measuring the same transfer at many sizes, from tiny to huge, instead of at one size. Analogy: timing a bowl, a pot and a full cart on the same road. Precise: a benchmark that times one operation for sizes spaced by a constant factor (here ×4 or ×8) over the whole range a program uses, so that both α (from the small end) and β (from the large end) can be read, and the size at which one dominates the other is visible. C1 R3
Collective mismatch
Simple words: the members of a group job do not make the same group calls in the same order. Analogy: half the table passes bowls of soup while the other half passes bowls of salad; nobody gets what they expect. Precise: an erroneous program in which the ranks of a communicator call collectives in different orders or with arguments that do not match (count, type, root, operation); the MPI Standard requires matching calls, and what happens otherwise is not defined: the run in this build first corrupted memory in two ranks and then hung. D2 R9
Collective, all-reduce, ring all-reduce (from F8-01, F8-02)
Simple words: a group operation every member must join; the one that adds everybody's data and gives everyone the sum; and a way of doing that by passing pieces around a circle. Analogy: passing bowls around a round table, each person adding a spoon. Precise: as defined in F8-01: an all-reduce leaves the element-wise reduction of all ranks' buffers on every rank; the ring version is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather, 2(N−1) steps of S/N bytes each. D2 D3
Alpha–beta model, topology (from F1-62, F6-37)
Simple words: a transfer costs a fixed start-up time plus its size divided by the speed; the topology is the map of which device is wired to which. Analogy: loading the cart, then driving it; the building plan of corridors and lobbies. Precise: T(n) = α + n/β per message; the topology is the graph of links (PCIe switches, root complexes, inter-socket links, NVLink or NVSwitch, xGMI, network cards) that decides the α and β of every path. D4 D6 D7

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

One kitchen hall is easy to understand: the helpers cook, the pantry is the slow part, and a stopwatch tells you how long things take. When there are many halls, the halls must send bowls to each other. Some roads between halls are short and some are long. A bowl takes time to send even if it is tiny, and a big pot takes time because it is heavy. So you time every road, with small bowls and with big pots. When all the halls must share, they sit around a round table and pass bowls, and everyone must play the same game at the same moment. If one hall plays a different game, or does not come, everybody else waits.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What carries over, unchanged

In one sentence of analogy: every hall is still the hall you know, with its helpers, order queues and stopwatch; the new part is the roads between halls.

Kernels, streams and events. Each GPU in a multi-GPU program is still driven exactly as in CU303: kernels are launched into streams, operations in one stream run in order, operations in different streams may overlap, and events mark points in a stream that can be waited on and timed. D1 H1 What is new is only that every stream, event and allocation belongs to one device: in CUDA, to the device made current with cudaSetDevice when it was created (F6-37). Listing 8 shows this: lines 40–47 are one GPU's recipe from F6-28, repeated once per device; the communication libraries of DG401 take a stream argument too, so their transfers queue behind your kernels like any other work. H1 D8

The measurement protocol. Curriculum 13.4 says: "warm-up runs, then the median of at least 20 timed runs using device events". C1 Every timing in this chapter follows it (Listings 3 and 4: five warm-up runs, median of 20). Two rules are added for groups: synchronise all ranks before each timed run, and take the slowest rank's time, because a collective is finished only when its last participant is finished (Listing 4, line 103). R7

Roofline thinking. In F6-09 and F6-33 you asked of every kernel: which roof limits it, memory or arithmetic, and how far below the roof is it? The same question works for transfers, with α and β in place of the two roofs: a message of n bytes can never move faster than β, and never faster than one message per α. Below the ridge n = αβ the start-up cost limits you ("latency-bound"); above it, the link's bandwidth does ("bandwidth-bound"). Figure 2 and Listing 1 draw it. D9 D4 R1

What changes, most dangerous first

In one sentence of analogy: the halls are fine; it is the roads, the shared table and the empty chair that are new.

  1. Data must move between memories over links of very different kinds. A GPU cannot simply read another GPU's memory as its own: data move over a path, which may be PCIe through a switch, PCIe through the CPU's root complex, a GPU-to-GPU link (NVLink, possibly through NVSwitch, on NVIDIA; xGMI on AMD Instinct), or, between machines, a network card. C1 D6 D7 Each path has its own α and β; this chapter states no speed for any of them, because none was measured here (see the unverified box in "How the hardware actually does it").
  2. Topology decides performance. "The GPU-to-GPU bandwidth" is not one number but a matrix, one entry per ordered pair, and a group operation that uses many pairs at once is limited by the slowest path it crosses and by paths that several transfers share. Listing 2 shows a ring that takes 3.99 times longer than another ring on the same invented machine, only because of the order of its GPUs. R2
  3. Collectives replace point-to-point thinking. On one GPU you thought "copy this buffer there". With many GPUs you think "every rank contributes, every rank receives": all-reduce, all-gather, reduce-scatter, broadcast (F8-01). A collective is defined by its result, called by every rank of a communicator, and the library chooses how the bytes travel. D2 D8
  4. A failure of one process stops everyone. On one GPU, a crash is your process's own. In a collective job, the others wait for the missing rank's data. In this build, when one of four ranks was killed, Open MPI's mpirun stopped the whole job (Listing 6, R11); a library without such a launcher may simply wait (F8-16). R11
  5. Time is dominated by communication unless overlapped. Splitting a fixed amount of work over more GPUs shrinks each GPU's compute, but the all-reduce does not shrink: its bandwidth term tends to 2S/β. Listing 7's invented example goes from 6 % communication on 2 GPUs to 90 % on 64. Overlapping the communication with computation (on a separate stream, F6-29) is the only way to hide it. R12 D10

The alpha–beta model of a ring all-reduce

The prediction of the lab needs one formula, and you can derive it by counting. N ranks each hold S bytes. The ring cuts each buffer into N segments of S/N bytes. In the reduce-scatter, each of N−1 steps sends one segment to the right neighbour and adds the segment arriving from the left; in the all-gather, N−1 more steps pass the finished segments around. D3 All ranks send at the same time, so a step costs one message of S/N bytes:

Tring(S) = 2(N−1) · (α + S/(Nβ)) = 2(N−1)α + 2(N−1)/N · S/β

The first term grows with the number of ranks and dominates small messages; the second tends to 2S/β and dominates large ones. The model leaves out the time of the additions and assumes that every hop has the same α and β and that hops do not disturb each other. D4 Each of those assumptions failed in this build's runs in a way you can see (Layer 3). F8-03 compares this formula with trees and recursive doubling and defines bus bandwidth, busbw = (S/t) · 2(N−1)/N, which for a ring that runs at the model's speed approaches β. D5

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

One analogy sentence: the delivery planner's three classic mistakes are trusting the building plan to be uniform, timing one bowl, and letting halls pass different dishes; each is shown here with a real run.

Trap 1: assuming all GPU pairs are equally connected

The model. Listing 2 takes two invented nodes. TN-4 is the four-GPU node of F6-37 (two PCIe switches, two GPUs below each). TN-8 has two islands of four GPUs with fast links inside an island and one slower path between the islands. Listing 2 assumes, and states, that all ring hops crossing between the islands in the same direction share that one path. It prints the naive prediction ("every pair as fast as the best link"), two chosen ring orders, and the best and worst of all ring orders: R2



On TN-8, the natural ring 0→1→…→7→0 crosses between the islands twice, once in each direction, and takes 47,116.2 µs for 256 MiB, 3.99 times the naive prediction: the ring is set by its slowest hop, and the naive model never sees that hop. The interleaved ring 0→4→1→5→… crosses eight times, four hops share each direction of the slow path, and it takes 188,044.8 µs, 15.92 times the naive number. Of all 5,040 rings starting at GPU 0, the worst is 3.99 times slower than the best. At 64 KiB the gap is only 1.23: small messages are dominated by α, which sharing does not divide. R2 The same trap therefore costs a factor of four at one size and a quarter at another; this is where traps 1 and 2 meet.

Trap 3: deadlocks from mismatched collective calls


Listing 5 is the smallest realistic version of a bug that data-parallel training code can have: gradients are all-reduced bucket by bucket "as soon as a bucket is ready", and readiness can differ between ranks. D10 In ready mode, odd ranks reduce layer2 (250,000 floats) first while even ranks reduce layer1 (1,000 floats) first. Each all-reduce is matched with the other ranks' all-reduce of a different bucket of a different size. The run, under a 10 s limit: R9




It did not just hang. Two processes reported "Read -1, expected 500000, errno = 14", then "malloc(): corrupted top size", then died with signal 6 (abort); the job then made no progress until the time limit stopped it (exit code 124). Error number 14 is EFAULT, "Bad address", in this build's C library. R9 R14 500,000 bytes is half of the 1,000,000-byte layer2 bucket, far more than the 4,000-byte layer1 buffer of the processes that crashed would hold; a plausible reading is that the library, believing all ranks passed 250,000 floats, tried to move half of that buffer into or out of a 1,000-float one. That reading is an inference from the numbers, not something this build verified inside Open MPI. What the MPI Standard does say is that collectives must be called by all processes in the same order with matching arguments; a program that does not is erroneous, so a hang, a crash, silent memory corruption or a silently wrong sum are all possible outcomes, and you must not rely on any of them. D2 With every rank using the same agreed bucket order (the fixed mode), all four ranks finish and every gradient equals 4: R10




The rule to carry: the sequence of collectives must be the same on every rank, decided by something all ranks agree on (a fixed bucket order, a sorted list of names), never by timing or by data only one rank sees. F8-01's forensic lab shows the other classic form, a rank that skips a collective.

How the hardware actually does it


Inside one machine, every GPU is a PCIe endpoint in a tree that starts at the CPU's root complex and may pass through PCIe switches (F1-62, HW204). A transfer from one GPU's memory to another's is executed by a copy engine (DMA) or by a kernel that loads or stores across the link; if both GPUs sit below one switch, the switch forwards the packets, and if the path crosses the root complex, the CPU's PCIe controller does. D6 D1 GPU-to-GPU links (NVLink and NVSwitch on NVIDIA, xGMI on AMD Instinct) are separate connections between GPUs that bypass PCIe for peer traffic. D6 D7 Between machines, the data leave through a network card: with TCP the CPU and the kernel's network stack handle every message; with RDMA (InfiniBand or RoCE) the card reads and writes registered memory directly, and with GPUDirect RDMA that memory can be GPU memory (HW205, DG402). C1


The runs of this chapter used neither GPUs nor network cards. Open MPI's shared-memory transport moves messages through memory shared between processes, and its TCP transport over the loopback device goes through the operating system's network stack without any wire; both are real transports with real α and β, which is why they can stand in for "a fast path" and "a slower path" in the lab, but their numbers say nothing about GPU links. R3 R5 Every MPI run used mpi_yield_when_idle 1, which Open MPI describes as "Yield the processor when waiting for MPI communication"; without it, early runs of this lab on the shared container (not kept as logs) showed one-way times of milliseconds whenever a waiting process held its CPU. H2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Roads do not carry one bowl at a time. A delivery road suggests one cart after another. A link carries many packets in flight, both directions can be busy at once, and a large transfer is limited by bandwidth, not by "trip time"; the ring cuts each buffer into segments so that all links work at once. D3
    • 

  • A hall can see an empty chair; a rank cannot. A cook notices the missing hall and asks why. A rank waiting in a collective has no idea whether the others are slow, dead or calling something else; it only waits, or crashes, as Listings 5 and 6 showed.
    • 

  • Two dishes on the table are noticed. Halls passing soup and halls passing salad would notice the difference at once. Mismatched collectives are matched by order alone; the library cannot tell your layer1 from your layer2, and the first sign may be a corrupted heap.
    • 

  • Road speed is not printed on a sign. A building plan tells you which corridor is shorter. A topology view tells you which path a transfer takes, but the speed of that path, and how much it slows when shared, must be measured, and on a shared machine it changes from run to run (R3, R4).
    •

Summary


    

  • Kernels, streams, events, the measurement protocol and roofline thinking carry over; they are now used once per device and across ranks.
    • 

  • Every link has its own α and β; a group operation is limited by the slowest and the most shared path it crosses, so topology and ring order decide performance.
    • 

  • The link roofline: below αβ start-up cost dominates, above it bandwidth; one message size can rank links in the wrong order.
    • 

  • A ring all-reduce takes 2(N−1)(α + S/(Nβ)); write the prediction down before you measure, then explain every gap.
    • 

  • Collectives must be called by every rank in the same order with matching arguments; a mismatch is undefined and in this build corrupted memory and hung.
    • 

  • One dead process stops the whole job; plan for it.
    • 

  • Communication's share grows as compute is split; overlap hides it only up to the compute time.
    • 

  • On a shared machine, measurements move: repeat before you call anything topology.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. In R5's sweep, 8 B took 74.67 µs and 32 B 25.35 µs, while 128 B to 8 KiB all took between 14.88 and 16.44 µs, flat as an α-dominated range should be. The first two sizes are outliers: the first runs of a new size pattern were disturbed (the warm-up of five round trips was not enough on this shared machine). The fit took α from the 8-byte point: 74.67 µs.


Root cause (small sizes). The ring's small-message time is almost all α: the PREDICTION table shows an α part of 448.00 µs = 6 × 74.67 and a β part of 0.01 µs at 16 B (R8). With α from the flat range, about 15.5 µs, the prediction is 6 × 15.5 = 93 µs, against the measured 92.01 µs at 16 B and 88.63–100.83 µs up to 16 KiB. The model was fine; one measurement point was not.


16 MiB. Hypothesis 1: β is too optimistic for four simultaneous flows. The fit's β (2.285 GB/s) came from one pair alone, while in the ring four TCP connections and their four processes share the same CPUs and the same memory; R5's bandwidth matrix also varies from 0.847 to 3.440 GB/s between pairs measured one at a time. Hypothesis 2: β itself is noisy. The sweep gave 4.006 GB/s at 512 KiB but 2.240 at 8 MiB. Both are supported; a test separates them: rerun the ring with 2 processes (less sharing) and repeat the sweep several times. Reduction time (γ) adds a third, smaller part. Wrong answer: "the formula is wrong for rings". Mid sizes, 256 KiB to 4 MiB, came within 0.71–1.02 of the prediction, so the formula's structure holds.


Fix and prevention. Fit α as the median of several small sizes (for example 128 B–2 KiB) and β from several large sizes, after enough warm-up for each size. Reject a fit when the small-size times are not flat within some tolerance, or when two repetitions of the sweep disagree. Plot the sweep before predicting. Then let the model and the measurement disagree only where the hardware disagrees with the model's assumptions, and name which assumption. How the evidence was produced: the unmodified run.sh of this lab; nothing was injected. The outlier happened by itself on the shared container, which is why it makes a fair forensic case.
