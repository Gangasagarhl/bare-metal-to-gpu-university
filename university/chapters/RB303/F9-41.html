Story hook

Analogy

The club's map of the season's routes changes rarely. Rafael, who plans the long rides, set his subscription to the "map" board with a note to the post office: "When I join, send me the current map at once, even if it was posted before I joined." That is sensible: a planner without a map is useless.

But Ines, who posts the map, uses the cheapest service: each map is sent once, to whoever is subscribed at that moment, and no copy is kept at the post office. When Rafael's request reached the post office, the clerk compared the two cards. Rafael wanted "keep a copy for latecomers"; Ines offered "no copies". The clerk did not deliver half a service. He did not deliver at all, and he did not tell Rafael either. He only made a small note in a book nobody reads.

Rafael waited a week for a map. Meanwhile Laila, who also reads the map board but never asked for old copies, got every new map Ines posted. Same board, same sender, two readers: one served, one in silence. That is a quality-of-service mismatch, the most common way a ROS 2 subscriber hears nothing.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the main QoS policies used by ROS 2 (reliability, durability, history and depth, deadline, liveliness, lifespan) and what each one promises.
  • Apply the request-versus-offer rule to decide whether a publisher and a subscription are compatible, for each policy.
  • Choose QoS settings for sensor streams, commands, maps and status topics, and justify the choice.
  • Predict what a late-joining subscriber receives for given durability and history settings.
  • Diagnose a subscriber that hears nothing from the QoS settings of the endpoints on a topic (the course's forensic lab).
Jargon box
QoS (quality of service) policy
Simple words: a setting that says how a topic's messages should be delivered and kept. Analogy: the service you tick on the post-office form. Precise: in DDS, a named, typed setting attached to an entity (participant, topic, data writer, data reader…) that controls an aspect of its behaviour; ROS 2 exposes a subset of the DDS policies for publishers, subscriptions, services and actions. D1 D2
QoS profile
Simple words: a ready-made set of QoS settings for a typical kind of data. Analogy: the post office's "parcel" or "postcard" package. Precise: a named combination of policy values (for example a default profile and a sensor-data profile in ROS 2) used when creating an endpoint, from which individual policies may be changed. D1
Reliability (QoS)
Simple words: whether lost messages are sent again. Analogy: registered post versus ordinary post. Precise: BEST_EFFORT: samples are sent once and may be lost; RELIABLE: the writer repairs losses for samples still in its history (F9-40). D2
Durability (QoS)
Simple words: whether old messages are kept for subscribers that join later. Analogy: whether the post office keeps a copy for latecomers. Precise: VOLATILE: no samples are kept for late-joining readers; TRANSIENT_LOCAL: the writer keeps its history and delivers it to readers that match later (DDS also defines TRANSIENT and PERSISTENT, which ROS 2 does not use by default). D2
History and depth (QoS)
Simple words: how many recent messages are kept in a queue. Analogy: how many copies the sender keeps in the drawer. Precise: KEEP_LAST with a depth n keeps the newest n samples per instance and drops older ones; KEEP_ALL keeps all samples, up to resource limits. D2
Deadline (QoS)
Simple words: the longest allowed time between two messages. Analogy: "I promise a new route note at least every week." Precise: a period within which a new sample is expected; a writer offers a deadline it commits to meet, a reader requests one; if no sample arrives within the period, a "deadline missed" event is raised on that side. D2
Liveliness (QoS)
Simple words: how a subscriber can tell that a publisher is still alive, even when it has nothing to say. Analogy: a sender's regular "still here" postcard. Precise: a policy (AUTOMATIC, or MANUAL_BY_TOPIC, where the application must assert it) with a lease duration; if the writer does not assert liveliness within the lease, readers see it as not alive. D2
Lifespan (QoS)
Simple words: how long a message stays valid before it is thrown away undelivered. Analogy: a "use by" date on a note. Precise: the maximum time between publication and delivery; samples older than this are removed from caches and not delivered. D2
Request versus offered (RxO) compatibility
Simple words: the rule that a subscriber may not ask for more than the publisher offers. Analogy: the clerk comparing Rafael's and Ines's cards. Precise: for policies marked request/offered in DDS, a data writer's offered value must be at least as strong as the data reader's requested value; otherwise the two are not matched and both sides receive an "incompatible QoS" status. D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When you post a message in ROS 2, you also choose how it should travel. Should lost messages be sent again? Should a copy be kept for someone who joins later? How many recent messages should wait in the queue? The receiver chooses too. The postal service compares the two choices. If the receiver asks for more than the sender offers, they are not connected at all. Nothing is delivered, and the receiver gets no error message. So when a robot's program hears nothing, check these choices first.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why QoS exists

Different data needs different delivery. A laser scan at a high rate is only useful while it is fresh: losing one is fine, waiting for a repair is not. A velocity command must not be skipped. A map changes rarely and every planner that starts later still needs the latest one. One fixed delivery rule cannot serve all three, so DDS attaches quality-of-service policies to each writer and reader, and ROS 2 exposes the most important ones on publishers and subscriptions. Request versus offer

Several policies follow the request-versus-offered rule: a publisher offers a level of service, a subscription requests one, and the two are matched only if the offer is at least as strong as the request (Figure 1). A reliable publisher can serve a best-effort subscription (it simply does more than needed); a best-effort publisher cannot serve a reliable subscription. A transient-local publisher can serve a volatile subscription; a volatile publisher cannot serve a transient-local one. For deadline, the publisher must promise a period no longer than the subscription expects. For liveliness, the publisher's kind must be at least as strict and its lease no longer. History depth is not part of the rule: a depth-1 publisher and a depth-100 subscription match. Listing 1's incompatibilities function (lines 29–43) implements these four comparisons, and R1's first lines print the reliability and durability tables. D2 R1

The consequence that makes QoS a debugging topic: an incompatible pair is simply not matched. The subscription exists, the publisher publishes, both appear in the graph on the same topic with the same type, and no data flows. DDS raises an "incompatible QoS" status on both endpoints, which ROS 2 can report as an event, but a node that does not listen for it, and does not log it, shows nothing. D1 D2

Durability and history for late joiners

With durability TRANSIENT_LOCAL on both sides, the publisher keeps its last n samples (its history depth) and delivers them to a subscription that matches later; the subscription keeps at most its own depth of them. R1's experiment 2 shows three cases: a map server with depth 1 that published three maps gives a late planner the newest one (3..3); the same pair with VOLATILE gives the late planner nothing until the next publication; a status publisher with depth 5 that published 8 samples, read by a dashboard with depth 3, gives the dashboard samples 6..8. D2 R1

Deadline as a watchdog

A deadline turns "the sensor stopped" from silence into an event. R1's experiment 3 has a teleop node publishing every 100 ms against a base that requests a 50 ms deadline: the pair is incompatible (the publisher promises less than requested). Separately, a subscription with a 40 ms deadline on a topic that actually arrives every 100 ms would see two missed deadlines per gap: 20 events per second in R1's arithmetic. In a robot this is how a controller notices that its command stream has stopped and brings the robot to a safe stop, instead of driving on with the last command. D2 R1

Profiles

ROS 2 provides named profiles so that most code does not set policies one by one: a default profile for ordinary topics, a sensor-data profile for high-rate sensor streams, and profiles for services and parameters. The important practical fact is that the default and the sensor-data profiles differ in reliability: a driver that publishes with the sensor-data profile and a tool or node that subscribes with the default profile form exactly experiment 1's incompatible pair. The values of each profile are in the unverified box of Layer 3. D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Which policies are request/offered

The DDS specification marks, for each policy, whether it must be consistent between the writer and reader ("RxO") and whether it can be changed after the entity is created. Reliability, durability, deadline, liveliness and several others not exposed by ROS 2 (latency budget, ownership, destination order, presentation) are RxO; history, resource limits and lifespan are not, they act on one side only. Reading the specification's table for the exact list and the order of values is the first task of this chapter's dossier. D2

Choosing QoS: a method

  1. Ask what the consumer does with a late sample. If a newer one makes it worthless (pose estimates, scans, camera frames), prefer BEST_EFFORT and a small depth; if every sample matters (commands, events, goals), RELIABLE.
  2. Ask whether a consumer that starts later needs the past. If yes (maps, static configuration, the robot's description), TRANSIENT_LOCAL with depth 1 (the current value), on both sides.
  3. Ask how bursts and losses interact with memory: a reliable topic with depth 1 at a high rate is effectively "the newest, repaired if it is still the newest" (F9-40, scenario 3); KEEP_ALL must be bounded by resource limits.
  4. Make silence visible: a deadline on streams the robot depends on, with a callback that logs and triggers a safe state; liveliness for nodes whose crash must be noticed.
  5. Publish with the strongest QoS any consumer needs, and subscribe with the weakest you can accept. Offering more is always compatible; requesting more is what breaks matches.

Rule 5 is the one that prevents the forensic lab's failure. It also shows a tension: a publisher that offers RELIABLE to satisfy one consumer pays the repair traffic for all of them. D2 R1

QoS of services, actions and tools

Services and actions are built on topics (F9-37), so they have QoS too, normally reliable and volatile; a service request sent before the server is discovered is not kept for it. Command-line tools that subscribe for you choose a QoS as well; a tool that requests RELIABLE on a BEST_EFFORT sensor topic shows nothing, which is a common way to conclude wrongly that a driver is broken. Check what QoS your tools request (Layer 3's box). D1

How the hardware actually does it

QoS policies become concrete mechanisms in the DDS implementation and the network. Reliability becomes HEARTBEAT, ACKNACK and resend traffic (F9-40), which uses link capacity exactly when the link is losing packets, the worst moment on Wi-Fi. History depth becomes memory: each writer and reader keeps up to depth samples per instance, so depth × message size × number of endpoints is RAM that a small robot computer must have (a depth of 100 camera frames is a great deal of memory). Durability TRANSIENT_LOCAL keeps samples in the writer's memory as long as the writer exists; if the writer process dies, late joiners get nothing. Deadline and liveliness need timers in the DDS implementation and, for liveliness, periodic messages on the network. D2 D3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A post-office clerk who refused service would usually tell you. DDS does raise an "incompatible QoS" status, but nothing reaches your node's normal output unless it subscribes to the event or ROS 2 logs a warning; silence is the default experience.
    • 

  • Registered post costs the sender money once per letter. RELIABLE costs network traffic in proportion to the losses, and memory for the history on both sides, all the time.
    • 

  • The post office keeps "copies for latecomers" centrally. TRANSIENT_LOCAL keeps them in the publishing process: when that process stops, the copies are gone.
    • 

  • In the story, only Rafael's request mattered. In DDS, both sides must ask: a TRANSIENT_LOCAL publisher gives no history to a VOLATILE subscription.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Reliable subscription on a best-effort sensor topic. Symptom: no messages; the topic exists with the right type. Cause: incompatible reliability. How to find it: list the endpoints with their QoS (the forensic lab's evidence format; in ROS 2 the verbose topic info, unverified); look for a requested value above the offered one. Fix: subscribe with the sensor-data profile.
    • 

  • Transient-local subscription on a volatile publisher. Symptom: a node that starts late never gets the map. Fix: make the publisher transient local (it is the one with the data), or make the subscriber volatile and wait for the next publication.
    • 

  • Expecting history without durability on both sides. Symptom: a late joiner gets nothing despite a large depth. Cause: depth controls the queue; durability controls late delivery. Fix: TRANSIENT_LOCAL on both sides.
    • 

  • Large depth on large messages. Symptom: memory grows with the number of subscribers. Fix: depth 1–5 for images and point clouds.
    • 

  • Changing QoS on one side only. Symptom: "it worked yesterday". Cause: someone made a subscription stricter. Fix: publish with the strongest needed, subscribe with the weakest acceptable, and log incompatibility events.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F9-40: discovery, matching by topic and type, reliable delivery bounded by history. What carries over: matching happens at discovery; a writer's history bounds what can be repaired or replayed; silence is the failure mode of a failed match. D3 What changes: reliability is now one of several policies, all chosen per endpoint by your code; a third matching condition (QoS compatibility) joins name and type, and it is asymmetric: the publisher must offer at least what the subscription requests. D2 What comes next: F9-42 moves configuration out of code: launch files start nodes with parameters and remappings, and lifecycle nodes give the system a controlled start-up order.

Summary


    

  • QoS policies say how each topic's samples are delivered and kept: reliability, durability, history and depth, deadline, lifespan, liveliness.
    • 

  • A publisher offers, a subscription requests; they match only if every request/offered policy's offer is at least as strong as the request.
    • 

  • An incompatible pair is not matched: no data, and no error in normal output unless events are handled or logged.
    • 

  • Late joiners get old samples only with TRANSIENT_LOCAL on both sides, bounded by both depths.
    • 

  • Choose QoS by what a late sample is worth; publish with the strongest needed, subscribe with the weakest acceptable.
    • 

  • Deadline and liveliness turn silence into events a robot can act on.

With an offer of 20 ms the pair is compatible, but if the data really comes every 100 ms the subscription's 30 ms deadline expires 3 times per gap ((100 − 1) ÷ 30 = 3 in the model): 30 events per second, and the publisher breaks its own offered deadline too.
    8. 

  8. Every policy you request above the minimum is a new way to be incompatible with some publisher; a change on the publisher's side that is harmless for others silences you.

    4.

Fix 2: make the planner request VOLATILE again; it then matches but only receives a map if one is published after it starts, which fails when the planner starts late.
