Story hook

Analogy

The cycling club meets on Saturdays for a long ride. Before they leave, Amara pins a note to the club's notice board marked "route": the roads for today. Kenji, who rides at the back with the spare tubes, never talks to Amara. He reads the "route" board. So does Lena, who leads the slow group, and so does the volunteer who drives the support van. Amara does not know who reads her note, and she does not need to.

Some things do not fit on a board. When Lena wants to know whether the café on the route is open, she walks to the desk and asks Tomás, who keeps the phone list. She waits for the answer: yes or no. And when the van driver is sent to fetch a rider with a broken chain twenty kilometres away, he does not disappear for an hour in silence. He calls in: "ten kilometres to go", "five to go", "found her". If the rider fixes the chain herself, the club calls the van back.

Three ways to work together: a board anyone may read, a question at the desk with one answer, and a long errand with progress calls that can be cancelled. ROS 2 has exactly these three, and a fourth idea underneath them: the postal service that carries every note, which is the topic of F9-40.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain a ROS 2 system as a graph of nodes that communicate through topics, services and actions, and choose the right one of the three for a given interaction.
  • Resolve relative, absolute and private names in a namespace, and predict which topic two nodes will really meet on.
  • Read a graph listing (publishers and subscribers per topic, servers per service) and find a node that is talking to nobody.
  • Build and run the university's in-process graph model, and map each part of it to the ROS 2 concept it imitates.
  • Design a node graph from a written specification (practice for the course's practical exam).
Jargon box
ROS 2
Simple words: a collection of libraries and tools for writing robot software as many small programs that talk to each other. Analogy: the club's way of working: boards, desk, errands. Precise: the Robot Operating System version 2, an open-source robotics middleware framework and tool set; despite the name it is not an operating system but runs on one (Linux, among others), providing client libraries (for C++, rclcpp; for Python, rclpy), a communication layer built on DDS, and command-line tools. D1
Node (ROS 2)
Simple words: one part of the robot's software with one job, such as "drive the wheels" or "read the laser". Analogy: one club member with a role. Precise: a participant in the ROS 2 graph, created through a client library, that owns publishers, subscriptions, service servers and clients, action servers and clients, timers and parameters; several nodes may live in one operating-system process. D1
Topic (ROS 2)
Simple words: a named channel of messages of one type, such as "cmd_vel" carrying speed commands. Analogy: one notice board with a label. Precise: a named bus for anonymous, asynchronous publish–subscribe communication of messages of a single interface type; publishers and subscribers do not know of each other, and there may be any number of each. D1
Publish–subscribe
Simple words: senders post messages under a name; whoever is interested in that name receives a copy. Analogy: pinning a note; reading the board. Precise: a messaging pattern in which producers (publishers) send data to a named topic without addressing receivers, and consumers (subscribers) register interest in the topic; the middleware matches them and delivers. D1 D3
Message type (interface)
Simple words: the agreed layout of a message: which fields, of which types. Analogy: the club's printed form for a route note. Precise: a ROS 2 interface definition (in a .msg file for messages, .srv for services, .action for actions) from which code for each client library is generated; a topic carries exactly one message type. D1
Service (ROS 2)
Simple words: a question that one node asks another and gets one answer to. Analogy: Lena asking Tomás at the desk. Precise: remote-procedure-call style communication: a client sends a request message to a named service, one server processes it and returns a response message; intended for quick operations. D1
Action (ROS 2)
Simple words: a long task you ask for, with progress reports on the way and a final result, which you may cancel. Analogy: the van's errand with phone calls. Precise: a long-running goal-oriented interaction with three parts (goal, feedback, result) and the ability to cancel; in ROS 2 it is built from services and topics underneath. D1 D2
Computation graph (ROS 2)
Simple words: the picture of all running nodes and the topics, services and actions that connect them. Analogy: the club's whole web of boards and desks on a Saturday. Precise: the network of ROS 2 entities processing data together at a given time: nodes as vertices, communication endpoints matched by name and type as edges. D1
Namespace and name resolution (ROS 2)
Simple words: a prefix such as /robot1 that keeps the names of two robots apart, and the rules that turn a short name into a full one. Analogy: two clubs in the same hall, each with its own "route" board. Precise: names are hierarchical; a name starting with / is absolute, a name without it is relative and is prefixed by the node's namespace, and a name starting with ~ is private to the node; the fully resolved name is what endpoints are matched on. D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot's software is not one big program. It is a team of small programs, each with one job. One reads the laser. One drives the wheels. One decides where to go. The team members talk in three ways. They pin notes on named boards for anyone to read. They ask one member a quick question and wait for the answer. Or they give one member a long errand, get progress reports, and may call the errand off. Every note travels through a postal service that finds the right readers, even on another computer.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Nodes: one job each

In the club, each member has a role and nobody does everything. A ROS 2 node is the same idea in software: a unit with one purpose, such as a driver for a laser scanner, a node that turns wheel commands into motor signals, or a planner. Nodes are created through a client library; this course uses the C++ one, rclcpp (F9-38). A node owns its communication endpoints: publishers and subscriptions for topics, servers and clients for services and actions, timers, and parameters (F9-42). Several nodes can run in one operating-system process, or each in its own; that choice does not change the graph, only how the work is scheduled (F9-39). D1

Why split a robot into nodes at all? Because the pieces can then be developed, tested, replaced and restarted separately, and because a node written for one robot (a laser driver) can be reused on another. The price is that data must cross from node to node, and that crossing has costs and failure modes the rest of this course studies. D1

Topics: the notice boards

A topic is a named channel that carries messages of one type. A node that has data to share creates a publisher on a topic; a node that wants the data creates a subscription on the same topic name with the same message type. Publishers do not know who, if anyone, is subscribed; subscribers do not know who publishes. This is publish–subscribe, and it is the right choice for streams: sensor readings, velocity commands, the robot's estimated position, which are produced continuously and may be needed by several consumers. D1

Two things must agree for a publisher and a subscriber to be connected: the fully resolved topic name, and the message type. In Listing 1, the teleop node and the driver node both publish to /cmd_vel with type Twist, and the base node subscribes to it; R1's graph listing shows "publishers=2 subscribers=1". A third condition, compatible quality-of-service settings, is F9-41's topic. D1 R1

Message types

A message type is a fixed layout of fields. In ROS 2 it is written in a small interface language (a .msg file) and the build generates matching C++ and Python types. A velocity command, for example, has a linear and an angular part; a laser scan has an array of ranges and the angles they belong to. Agreeing on types is what lets a node written by one team read data from a node written by another. The exact standard types used on real robots (their package names and fields) are listed in the unverified box of Layer 3; Listing 1 uses its own two small structs, Twist and Odom. D1

Services: a question at the desk

A service is a request and a response between a client and a single server, addressed by a service name. It suits operations that are short and have one answer: "reset the odometry", "what is the map's resolution?", "switch the camera to mode B". The ROS 2 documentation warns against using services for long-running work, because the client has no progress information and cannot cancel. In Listing 1, the base node offers /reset_odom; R1 shows that the client's request is queued, the server's callback runs, and the response arrives as a separate callback afterwards. D1 R1

Actions: a long errand

An action is for goals that take time: "drive 3 m", "move the arm to this pose", "navigate to the kitchen". The client sends a goal; the server accepts or rejects it, sends feedback while it works, and finally sends a result; the client may cancel. Listing 1's drive_distance is a model of this pattern: the goal is a distance, the feedback is the distance travelled so far (read from /odom), and the result is reported when the goal is reached. In ROS 2 an action is not a new transport: it is built from services (to send goals, cancel, and get results) and topics (for feedback and status). D1 D2 R1

Names and namespaces

Two robots in one building may both have a topic called cmd_vel. A namespace keeps them apart: started in namespace /robot1, a node that uses the relative name cmd_vel really publishes to /robot1/cmd_vel. A name starting with / is absolute and is not changed; a name starting with ~ is private and is put under the node's own name. Listing 1's resolve function implements these three rules. That is enough to explain R2, this chapter's forensic evidence, where one node was started in a namespace and the others were not. Remapping, which renames a topic from outside the node at start-up, is F9-42's topic. D2 R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Anonymous, asynchronous, many-to-many

Three properties of topics shape every design decision. Anonymous: a publisher cannot know whether a message was received, and adding a new subscriber (a logger, a visualiser) changes nothing for the existing nodes. Asynchronous: publish returns without waiting for any subscriber's callback; the callback runs later, in the subscriber's executor (F9-39). Listing 1 makes that visible: Graph::publish (lines 39–48) only queues a callback, and nothing runs until spinSome. Many-to-many: two publishers on one topic are legal and sometimes intended (teleop and driver on /cmd_vel), but the subscriber then receives an interleaving of both streams, and with velocity commands that is a classic source of a robot that "twitches". Real systems arbitrate with a dedicated node (a multiplexer that forwards one source at a time) rather than letting two writers fight. D1 R1

How the graph is formed: no master

The first ROS needed a central master process that every node registered with. ROS 2 has no such process: nodes discover each other through the DDS discovery protocol, each participant announcing itself and its endpoints to the others in the same DDS domain. The graph listing you see with ROS 2's tools is therefore each participant's own view, built from discovery traffic, and a node that cannot be discovered (another domain, a blocked network) is simply absent from it. F9-40 opens this layer; Listing 1 keeps a single in-process list of endpoints instead, which is the main simplification of the model. D1 D3

Mapping ROS 2 names onto DDS

Because DDS has its own topics and types, ROS 2 must map its names and interfaces onto DDS ones. The ROS 2 design articles describe a mapping in which ROS topic names receive a prefix in DDS, and services become a pair of DDS topics, one for requests and one for responses. You do not need this to write nodes, but you will see the DDS-level names when you use DDS tools or read a packet capture. The exact prefixes are in the unverified box below. D2

Services are not function calls

A service looks like a function call and is not one. The request may be lost if the server is not running; the server's callback runs in the server's executor; the response arrives in the client's executor as another callback. A client that sends a request and then blocks its only executor thread waiting for the response can wait forever, because the thread that would deliver the response is the one that is blocked. R1 shows the two-step nature even in a one-thread model: after the request, "queue holds 1 callback(s)"; the response line appears only after spinSome runs it. F9-39 returns to this deadlock with callback groups. D1 R1

Choosing message granularity

Every topic is a contract. Messages that bundle unrelated data force every subscriber to receive all of it; messages that are too fine-grained force subscribers to reassemble state from several topics whose samples may not line up in time. The usual compromise is one topic per physical stream (one laser, one camera, one IMU), each message stamped with the time its data was measured, which F9-43 needs for replay and F9-44 for simulation. D1

How the hardware actually does it

A topic is an abstraction over very different hardware paths. Between two nodes in one process, a message can be handed over in memory. Between two processes on one machine, it goes through the operating system, normally as UDP datagrams on the loopback interface (DS201's F5-04) or through shared memory, depending on the DDS implementation and its configuration. Between two machines, it leaves through the network interface as UDP packets; discovery uses multicast where the network allows it. Each path has its own cost: a copy, a system call, a packet, a context switch to the receiving thread. When a ROS 2 system is slow, the first question is which of these paths each heavy topic takes. D1 D3

Worked example

Specification. "A teleop node sends speed commands. A base node turns commands into motion and reports odometry. An operator can reset odometry. A driver node can be asked to drive a given distance and must report progress." Build the graph.

  1. Streams become topics. Speed commands and odometry are continuous: topics cmd_vel (type Twist: speed v, turn rate w) and odom (type Odom: x, y, heading).
  2. One-shot request with an answer becomes a service. "Reset odometry" is short and needs a yes/no: service reset_odom, server in base, because base owns the pose.
  3. Long goal with progress becomes an action. "Drive a distance and report progress": action drive_distance in driver, which needs odom (to measure progress) and must publish cmd_vel (to move).
  4. Resolve names. All nodes in namespace /, relative names: /cmd_vel, /odom, /reset_odom. R1's listing agrees.
  5. Predict phase 1. Teleop sends v = 0.5 m/s ten times; after each command the base integrates 0.1 s: 10 × 0.5 × 0.1 = 0.50 m; then one stop command, so the base counts 11 commands. R1: "base received 11 commands; pose x=0.50".
  6. Predict phase 3. After the reset, the first odometry message only sets the start (feedback 0.00, then driver publishes 0.5 m/s). Each 0.1 s step then adds 0.05 m: feedback 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25, and at 0.30 the result. Commands: 6 non-zero plus 1 stop = 7, so 11 + 7 = 18 in total. R1 prints six feedback lines from 0.00 to 0.25, "result SUCCEEDED, travelled 0.30 m" and "commands received in total 18". R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A notice board keeps old notes until someone takes them down. A ROS 2 topic keeps nothing by default: a subscriber that starts late misses earlier messages unless the publisher's quality-of-service settings keep a history for late joiners (F9-41).
    • 

  • Everyone in the club sees the same board. In ROS 2 each participant builds its own view of the graph from discovery messages; two machines can disagree about which nodes exist, and a node in another DDS domain is invisible (F9-40).
    • 

  • Lena at the desk can see whether Tomás is there. A service client cannot see a server that has not been discovered yet; the client must wait for the service to appear, and it can still disappear between the check and the call.
    • 

  • Pinning a note costs the club nothing. Every message costs copying, serialising and, between processes, a trip through the operating system; a large image published at a high rate is not free (Layer 3 and F9-43).
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Nodes talk past each other because of a namespace. Symptom: the subscriber's callback never runs; the publisher seems fine. Cause: the two endpoints resolve to different names (/robot1/cmd_vel versus /cmd_vel). How to find it: list topics with their publisher and subscriber counts; a topic with publishers=1 subscribers=0 next to a similar name with the opposite counts is the signature (R2). Fix: start both in the same namespace, use the same absolute name, or remap (F9-42).
    • 

  • Same name, different type. Symptom: as above. Cause: the endpoints use different message types. How to find it: list topics with their types. Fix: agree on one interface; never use two types on one topic name.
    • 

  • Using a service for long work. Symptom: callers freeze; no way to stop a running job. Cause: a service has no feedback and no cancel. Fix: make it an action.
    • 

  • Two command sources on one topic. Symptom: the robot jerks between two behaviours. Cause: two publishers on /cmd_vel (R1 shows both); the subscriber obeys whichever came last. Fix: a single arbitrating node.
    • 

  • Blocking for a service response inside a callback. Symptom: the node hangs. Cause: the response needs the executor that is blocked. Fix: asynchronous calls with a response callback, or a separate callback group and thread (F9-39).
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: RB201 gave the robot its sensors, actuators and buses; SP203 gave you threads and queues; DS201 gave you UDP between processes and machines. What carries over: producer–consumer queues, message framing, and the fact that a message crossing a process or a network can be late or lost; the robot's parts (laser, wheels, IMU) become nodes. R1 What changes: you no longer open sockets or address receivers; you name topics and let the middleware discover and match endpoints by name and type, which means a typo, a namespace or a type mismatch fails silently instead of with an error; calls become asynchronous callbacks. D1 What comes next: F9-38 writes these nodes in the shape of real rclcpp code, with classes, shared pointers, timers and callbacks, and shows the lifetime mistake that makes a subscription disappear.

Summary


    

  • A ROS 2 system is a graph of nodes; each node has one job and owns its endpoints.
    • 

  • Topics carry streams anonymously and asynchronously; publisher and subscriber must agree on the resolved name and the message type.
    • 

  • Services are one request and one response from a single server, for short work; they are asynchronous underneath.
    • 

  • Actions are long goals with feedback, a result and cancel, built from services and topics.
    • 

  • Names resolve against namespaces: absolute, relative, private; a namespace mismatch silently splits the graph.
    •
