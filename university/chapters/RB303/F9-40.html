Story hook

Analogy

The cycling club now has two branches, one in each of two towns, and they share route notes. Nobody carries notes by hand: the club uses the post. When a branch opens, it sends a short letter to every club address in its own town: "We are the river branch; we have a 'route' board and a 'weather' board." Every branch that receives it writes back, so after a day each branch knows who exists and which boards they share.

Amara numbers every route note she posts: 1, 2, 3… Every few notes she adds a postcard: "I have sent route notes 1 to 8." Kenji, at the other branch, compares it with his pile. "I have 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. Number 3 is missing," he writes back, and Amara sends 3 again. But Amara keeps copies of only her last few notes. When Kenji once asked for a note from months ago, she could only reply: "That one is gone; stop waiting for it."

And one spring the hill branch opened in the next town. Its letters went to the next town's post office, which never delivers to this one. The hill branch posted notes for weeks and nobody read them, and nobody got an error. This chapter is that postal service: DDS, and the protocol on the wire, RTPS.

What you will be able to do

  • Name the layers between an rclcpp publisher and the network (rcl, rmw, DDS, RTPS, UDP/IP) and say what each adds.
  • Explain DDS's data-centric model: domains, participants, topics, data writers and data readers, matched by topic, type and QoS.
  • Explain RTPS discovery (participants, then endpoints) and reliable delivery with sequence numbers, HEARTBEAT, ACKNACK, resend and GAP.
  • Predict with the university's RTPS model which samples a reader receives under loss, best effort or reliable, and with a short writer history.
  • Serialise a small message into bytes with alignment and read the bytes back in hexadecimal.
  • Diagnose two machines that cannot see each other's topics because they are in different DDS domains.
Jargon box
DDS (Data Distribution Service)
Simple words: a standard for programs to share data by topic name, with rules about how reliably and how long the data is kept. Analogy: the postal service behind the notice boards. Precise: an Object Management Group specification of a data-centric publish–subscribe middleware: applications in a domain read and write typed data samples on topics through data readers and data writers, with quality-of-service policies governing delivery, and with discovery of peers built in. D2
RTPS (Real-Time Publish-Subscribe protocol)
Simple words: the agreed format and rules of the messages DDS sends over the network, so that DDS products from different makers understand each other. Analogy: the post office's rules for envelopes, numbering and receipts. Precise: the wire protocol defined by the OMG specification "DDS Interoperability Wire Protocol" (DDSI-RTPS): messages made of submessages carried over a transport such as UDP/IP, entities identified by GUIDs, a discovery protocol, and best-effort and reliable protocols between writers and readers. D3
Domain (DDS) and domain ID
Simple words: a numbered "world"; programs only find each other if they use the same number. Analogy: a town with its own post office; letters never cross towns. Precise: a DDS domain is a communication plane identified by an integer domain ID; only participants in the same domain discover each other and exchange data; in RTPS the domain ID also selects the network ports used. D2 D3
Domain participant
Simple words: one program's (or one ROS 2 context's) membership in a domain. Analogy: one branch of the club, registered at the post office. Precise: the DDS entity that represents an application's presence in a domain; it owns publishers, subscribers, topics, data writers and data readers, and is the unit of participant discovery. D2
DataWriter and DataReader
Simple words: the sending and receiving ends of one topic inside DDS. Analogy: Amara's numbered note pad and Kenji's pigeonhole. Precise: typed endpoints for one topic: a data writer publishes samples of the topic's type and keeps a history cache of them; a data reader receives samples into its own history cache and gives them to the application; writers and readers are matched by topic name, type and QoS compatibility. D2
Discovery (DDS)
Simple words: how DDS programs find each other without a central list. Analogy: the opening letter to every address in town, and the replies. Precise: in RTPS, a two-stage process: a participant discovery protocol, in which participants periodically announce themselves (typically by multicast), followed by an endpoint discovery protocol, in which participants that know each other exchange descriptions of their writers and readers (topic, type, QoS) reliably; matching follows. D3
Sequence number (RTPS)
Simple words: the number a writer puts on each sample, 1, 2, 3… Analogy: the number on each of Amara's notes. Precise: a writer-assigned, strictly increasing number identifying each change in the writer's history; readers use it to detect loss, order samples and request repairs. D3
HEARTBEAT, ACKNACK and GAP (RTPS)
Simple words: "I have sent 1 to 8", "I am missing 3", and "3 will never come". Analogy: Amara's postcard, Kenji's reply, and "that one is gone". Precise: RTPS submessages of the reliable protocol: a HEARTBEAT announces the range of sequence numbers available in the writer's history; an ACKNACK from a reader acknowledges what it has and lists what it is missing; a GAP tells a reader that a range of sequence numbers is not available and should not be waited for. D3
RMW (ROS middleware interface)
Simple words: the plug that lets ROS 2 use DDS products from different makers. Analogy: the club's standard way of handing letters to whichever post company it uses. Precise: the ROS 2 abstraction layer (a C API) between the client libraries' common layer (rcl) and a middleware implementation; each supported DDS (or other) implementation provides an rmw implementation, chosen at run time. D1
CDR (Common Data Representation)
Simple words: the rules for turning a message's fields into a row of bytes. Analogy: how to fill in the post office's form, field by field. Precise: a binary serialisation format used by DDS for data samples, defining byte order and the alignment of primitive types; DDS's extensible-types specification defines versions of it. D2 D3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the stack under a ROS 2 publisher. Figure 2 is the reliable exchange of R1's scenario 3, where one lost sample can be resent and another cannot.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Under every ROS 2 notice board there is a postal service called DDS. When a program starts, it introduces itself to every other program in its "town", which is a number called the domain. Programs then tell each other which boards they read and write. Each message gets a number. If the connection is "reliable", the sender sometimes says "I have sent up to number 8", and the reader answers "I am missing number 3", and number 3 is sent again. But the sender keeps only a few old messages. A message that is too old cannot be sent again. And programs in different towns never find each other at all.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From publish to the network

When your node publishes, the message passes through several layers (Figure 1). rclcpp hands it to rcl, the common C layer that all ROS 2 client libraries share. rcl hands it to the ROS middleware interface, rmw, whose implementation for a particular DDS product serialises the message and writes it through a DDS data writer. DDS keeps the sample in the writer's history and, using the RTPS protocol, sends it to every matched reader, normally as UDP datagrams. On the other side the same layers run upwards, ending with your subscription callback run by an executor (F9-39). ROS 2 can use several DDS implementations; which one is used is chosen when the program starts, and all of them speak RTPS, so nodes using different DDS products can, in principle, talk to each other. D1 D3

DDS: a data space, not a set of connections

DDS describes itself as data-centric. Applications do not open connections to each other; they join a domain through a domain participant, declare topics with a name and a type, and create data writers and data readers for them. DDS finds the matching endpoints and moves the data, keeping copies in history caches according to quality-of-service policies (F9-41). Seen from the application, there is one shared space of typed data per domain; who produced a sample and over which network path it came are details the middleware handles. ROS 2's anonymous topics (F9-37) are this model with ROS names on top. D2

Discovery in two stages

Nobody configures the list of peers. RTPS discovery runs in two stages. First, participant discovery: each participant periodically announces itself (its identity and the addresses where it can be reached) to a well-known address of its domain, normally a multicast group; participants that hear each other record each other. Second, endpoint discovery: participants that know each other exchange, reliably, the descriptions of their data writers and data readers: topic name, type name and QoS. Each side then matches its own endpoints with the remote ones. Listing 1 models both stages in a few lines: a participant knows the others in its own domain only (lines 36–46), and a writer matches a reader only if they know each other and agree on topic, type and reliability (lines 47–52). D3 R1

Best effort and reliable

RTPS offers two delivery protocols per writer–reader pair. Best effort: the writer sends each sample once; a lost UDP datagram is a lost sample, and the reader passes on what arrives. Reliable: every sample carries a sequence number; the writer periodically sends a HEARTBEAT stating the range of sequence numbers it still holds; the reader answers with an ACKNACK that acknowledges what it has and lists what it is missing; the writer resends missing samples from its history, or sends a GAP for samples it no longer has. R1 shows all three cases with the same two losses: best effort delivers 6 of 8; reliable with a history of 8 recovers both losses with two resends; reliable with a history of 2 recovers one and must send GAP for the other. D3 R1

Scenario 3 is the important one. "Reliable" in DDS does not mean "every sample, whatever happens". It means "repair what the writer still keeps", and how much it keeps is the history QoS (F9-41). A fast publisher with a short history on a lossy link will lose samples on a reliable connection, and that is by design: it keeps a slow reader from holding the writer's memory hostage. D2 R1

Domains

A domain is identified by an integer. Participants in different domains never discover each other: in RTPS the domain ID determines the UDP port numbers used for discovery and data, so their packets do not even arrive at the same ports. In ROS 2 the domain ID is set for a whole program, normally through an environment variable, and the default is domain 0. Domains are how a lab with several robots on one network keeps them apart, and they are also a classic cause of "I can see nothing" (the forensic lab). D1 D3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

RTPS entities and messages

In RTPS every participant, writer and reader has a globally unique identifier, a GUID, made of a prefix shared by all entities of a participant and an entity ID within it. An RTPS message is a header (protocol identification, version, the vendor and the sender's GUID prefix) followed by a sequence of submessages. Submessages of interest here: DATA carries one serialised sample with its writer's ID and sequence number; DATA_FRAG carries a fragment of a sample too large for one datagram; HEARTBEAT, ACKNACK and GAP drive reliability; INFO_TS gives a source timestamp to the submessages that follow it; INFO_DST addresses a participant. Each submessage carries a flag for its byte order, so a little-endian and a big-endian machine can interoperate without agreeing in advance. D3

Writer and reader state

A reliable RTPS writer keeps, per matched reader, what that reader has acknowledged. It may discard a sample from its history once every reliable reader has acknowledged it, or earlier if the history QoS says to keep only the last n; this is exactly the trade between memory and completeness in R1's scenario 3. A reliable reader keeps samples that arrived out of order until the gap before them is filled or declared a GAP, then delivers in order; Listing 1 models that with deliverInOrder (lines 23–30). Best-effort readers deliver on arrival and drop samples older than one already delivered. D3 R1

The cost of reliability

Reliability is not free: HEARTBEATs and ACKNACKs are extra packets; resends add traffic exactly when the network is already losing packets; and in-order delivery means one lost sample delays every later sample until it is repaired (head-of-line blocking, the same effect as in TCP, DS201's F5-04). For a sensor stream where only the newest sample matters (a laser scan at a high rate), best effort with a short history is often the better choice: a late scan is worse than a missing one. For commands and state that must not be skipped (a map, a goal, a parameter change), reliable delivery with enough history, and sometimes durable storage for late joiners, is right. F9-41 turns this into QoS profiles. D2 D3

What the model leaves out

Listing 1 is not RTPS. It has no wire format, no GUIDs, no timers (HEARTBEATs are sent after every fourth sample, and immediately answered), no loss of HEARTBEATs, ACKNACKs or resends, no fragmentation, no multicast, and discovery is one loop over a list. It keeps the four ideas this chapter needs: domains separate participants; matching needs topic, type and compatible reliability; sequence numbers let a reader detect loss; and repair is bounded by the writer's history. R1

How the hardware actually does it

At the bottom of Figure 1 every RTPS message is one UDP datagram (or several, for fragments), so everything DS201 taught about packets applies: a datagram larger than the path's MTU is fragmented by IP or must be split by RTPS; loopback traffic between processes on one machine never touches a network card but still costs system calls and copies; multicast discovery needs a network that forwards multicast, which many Wi-Fi networks and some cloud networks do not, and then participants on different machines cannot find each other unless discovery is configured with explicit peer addresses. Bytes are bytes: a sample is serialised in a defined byte order with defined alignment (Listing 2), so a receiver on a machine with a different byte order can decode it from the flag in the submessage. Worked example

Predict R1's scenario 3 by hand: writer and reader reliable, writer history depth 2, eight samples, the first transmissions of 2 and 3 lost, HEARTBEAT after every fourth sample.

  1. Sample 1 arrives and is delivered (next expected becomes 2). History: [1].
  2. Sample 2 is sent and lost. History: [1, 2]. Sample 3 is sent and lost. History keeps the newest 2: [2, 3].
  3. Sample 4 arrives. The reader holds it, because 2 and 3 are missing and delivery is in order. History: [3, 4].
  4. HEARTBEAT after 4: "first=3 last=4". The reader's ACKNACK lists what it has not received from its next expected (2) up to last (4): missing 2 and 3.
  5. The writer checks its history [3, 4]: 3 is there, so it resends 3; 2 is not, so it sends GAP 2. The reader now has 3 and 4 and knows 2 will never come: it delivers 3 and 4.
  6. Samples 5–8 arrive and are delivered. HEARTBEAT after 8: "first=7 last=8"; nothing missing.
  7. Delivered: 1 3 4 5 6 7 8 (7 of 8). Packets: 8 first transmissions + 1 resend = 9 (the model does not count HEARTBEAT, ACKNACK or GAP). R1 prints exactly this. R1

Serialisation by hand (Listing 2). String "laser": a 4-byte length 6 (five letters and the NUL) = 06 00 00 00 little-endian, then 6c 61 73 65 72 00: 10 bytes. A 2-byte value at offset 10: already aligned (10 is even): 07 00, now 12 bytes. A double needs alignment 8: pad 4 zero bytes to offset 16, then 1.25 in IEEE 754 binary64 is 0x3FF4000000000000, little-endian 00 00 00 00 00 00 f4 3f. Total 24 bytes, 4 of padding. R2 prints exactly these bytes. R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A postal service delivers each letter to an address. DDS on a multicast network may send one datagram that every reader in the group receives at once; "delivery" is often one packet for many readers.
    • 

  • Kenji's "I am missing 3" is sent when he notices. In RTPS a reader typically reports only in answer to a HEARTBEAT, so repair latency depends on the writer's HEARTBEAT period, a configuration detail of the DDS implementation.
    • 

  • Towns are geographic. A DDS domain is only a number; two domains share the same cables and machines, and a participant can even join several domains with several participants.
    • 

  • Letters arrive or not. UDP datagrams can also arrive duplicated or out of order; sequence numbers handle those cases too, which the model does not show.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Different domain IDs. Symptom: each machine sees only its own nodes and topics. Cause: participants in different domains never discover each other (R3). How to find it: print the domain setting in every shell and service file; compare. Fix: one domain ID for the whole robot system.
    • 

  • Multicast blocked. Symptom: nodes on one machine see each other, nodes on two machines do not, even with the same domain. Cause: participant discovery relies on multicast, which the network does not forward. How to find it: a packet capture shows outgoing discovery and nothing incoming. Fix: a network that forwards multicast, or the DDS implementation's configuration of explicit peers or a discovery server (check its documentation).
    • 

  • Believing "reliable" means "complete". Symptom: sporadic missing samples on a reliable topic under load or loss. Cause: the writer's history was shorter than the burst of losses (R1, scenario 3). Fix: larger history, or KEEP_ALL with resource limits, or accept and design for loss (F9-41).
    • 

  • Reliable sensor streams on a bad link. Symptom: growing delay of a high-rate sensor topic. Cause: repairs and in-order delivery hold back newer samples. Fix: best effort for streams where only the newest sample matters.
    • 

  • Large messages on Wi-Fi. Symptom: images arrive rarely or never. Cause: one lost fragment loses the whole sample. Fix: compress, lower the rate, or keep large data on wired links.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F9-37 to F9-39 treated a topic as a channel that "just works"; DS201 taught UDP, ports, MTU and byte order; DS301 taught marshalling. What carries over: every RTPS message is a UDP datagram, with all the loss, size and port rules of DS201; serialisation is marshalling with a defined byte order and alignment; sequence numbers and acknowledgements work like TCP's. D3 What changes: there are no connections or addresses to configure; discovery finds peers by domain, topic, type and QoS, so a mismatch produces silence, not an error; and "reliable" is bounded by the writer's history instead of guaranteed. D2 R1 What comes next: F9-41 makes the QoS policies explicit (reliability, durability, history, deadline, liveliness), shows the request-versus-offer rule, and diagnoses the course's classic "subscriber hears nothing".

Summary


    

  • Under rclcpp are rcl, the rmw interface and a DDS implementation that speaks RTPS over UDP/IP.
    • 

  • DDS is data-centric: participants in a domain write and read typed samples on topics through data writers and readers, with QoS.
    • 

  • RTPS discovery has two stages: participants announce themselves in their domain, then exchange endpoint descriptions; matching needs topic, type and compatible QoS.
    • 

  • Reliable delivery uses sequence numbers, HEARTBEAT, ACKNACK, resends and GAP; it repairs only what the writer's history still holds.
    • 

  • Participants in different domains never see each other, silently.
    • 

  • Samples are serialised with defined byte order and alignment; padding is real bytes on the wire.
