Story hook

Analogy

Every ten minutes during the club's long ride, somebody must check the pace and call "faster" or "slower" to the group. It takes ten seconds. Once an hour, somebody must also write the ride report: who is here, how far, any punctures. That takes several minutes.

At first the club had one helper, Nadia, riding next to the leader. When she started the report, the pace calls stopped. The group spread out over a kilometre, and by the time Nadia looked up, two pace calls were already overdue. "Get a second helper," said someone. So Omar joined. But the club kept one notebook for both jobs, and there was only one pencil. When Nadia was writing the report, Omar had to wait for the pencil before he could note the pace. Two helpers, and the pace calls were just as late as before.

What finally worked was a second notebook and pencil just for the pace. Omar called the pace every ten minutes, on time, while Nadia wrote the report. In ROS 2 the helpers are an executor's threads and the shared notebook is a callback group. This chapter shows, with a model and with real threads, why more threads alone did not help.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what an executor does: wait for ready timers, messages and requests, and run their callbacks on one or more threads.
  • Explain mutually exclusive and reentrant callback groups, and predict which callbacks may run at the same time under a given configuration.
  • Measure in a model how long each callback waits and how many timer periods are missed, and choose groups and threads so that a control timer keeps its period.
  • Show with real threads and ThreadSanitizer that an exclusive group protects plain shared data and a reentrant group does not.
  • Diagnose a late control loop from an executor trace.
Jargon box
Executor (ROS 2)
Simple words: the part of ROS 2 that decides which of your callbacks to run next, and runs it. Analogy: the club's helpers taking the next waiting job. Precise: an object to which nodes are added; when spun, it waits until timers, subscriptions, service servers and clients or other waitables are ready, and executes their callbacks on its thread (single-threaded executor) or on a pool of threads (multi-threaded executor), subject to callback groups. D1 D2
Ready (callback)
Simple words: a callback has something to do: its timer is due, or a message or request has arrived. Analogy: a job card waiting on the desk. Precise: an entity whose underlying wait condition is satisfied, so that the executor may take and execute its work. D1
Callback group
Simple words: a label you put on callbacks that tells the executor which of them may run at the same time. Analogy: which jobs share one notebook and pencil. Precise: a set of a node's callbacks with a type: in a mutually exclusive group at most one callback of the group executes at any time; in a reentrant group callbacks (including several instances of the same one) may execute in parallel; callbacks in different groups may always execute in parallel on a multi-threaded executor. D1
Waiting time (callback latency)
Simple words: how long a callback was ready before a thread started it. Analogy: how long a pace call was overdue. Precise: start time minus ready time; in this chapter's model "wait" in the summary tables; one part of end-to-end latency (F9-43 measures the rest). R1
Missed period (timer)
Simple words: a timer tick that never got its own run because the previous one had not run yet. Analogy: a pace call that was skipped because the previous one was still overdue. Precise: in this chapter's model, a deadline that arrives while the timer's previous instance is still waiting or running; it is counted and dropped, not queued. How rclcpp handles overdue timers must be checked (unverified box). R1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Your node has several small jobs: tick a clock, read a message, write a report. Something must decide which job runs now. In ROS 2 that is the executor. It has one or more helpers, called threads. Each helper takes the next job that is waiting. But some jobs share tools, like one notebook and one pencil. Then only one helper can do any of those jobs at a time, however many helpers there are. If a quick, important job shares tools with a slow one, it waits. Give the important job its own tools, and a free helper can do it on time.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What spin really does

F9-38's spin handed the thread to the library. Underneath, spin runs an executor's loop: wait until at least one entity of the added nodes is ready (a timer is due, a message or request has arrived), take ready work, execute the callback, repeat. Nodes do not run themselves; an executor runs them, and one executor can run many nodes. That is why a program can put several nodes in one process and still decide, separately, how many threads serve them. D1 D2

Single-threaded and multi-threaded executors

A single-threaded executor runs every callback on the thread that called spin, one after another. It is simple and needs no locking inside the node, but a long callback delays every other callback of every node it serves. A multi-threaded executor runs callbacks on a pool of threads (SP203's F2-41), so independent callbacks can run in parallel on several cores. "Independent" is the key word: the multi-threaded executor will only run two callbacks at the same time if their callback groups allow it. D1

Callback groups

Every callback belongs to a callback group. In a mutually exclusive group, at most one of the group's callbacks runs at any time, whatever the number of threads; in exchange, the callbacks of that group may share plain member data without locks. In a reentrant group there is no such limit: different callbacks, and even two runs of the same subscription callback, may overlap, so any shared data needs a mutex or atomics (SP203). Callbacks in different groups may always run in parallel on a multi-threaded executor. A node that creates no groups of its own puts everything into a default group, which the ROS 2 documentation describes as mutually exclusive. D1

That last sentence explains Nadia and Omar. R1's scenario B uses two threads and one exclusive group (the default): its summary table is identical to the one-thread scenario A, and the second thread reports "busy 0 of 1000 ms". More threads helped nobody, because the group allowed only one callback at a time. R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

How rclcpp chooses the next callback

Listing 1's rule ("oldest ready first") is a model. The real executors do not keep a global first-in, first-out queue of ready callbacks: they repeatedly wait on a set of entities, collect those that are ready, and then execute them in an order determined by the executor's implementation and by the kind of entity. One consequence documented for ROS 2 is that a busy executor does not give strict time-ordered fairness: a callback that is always ready can delay others in ways a FIFO model does not show. Treat the model's waiting times as an illustration of the group mechanism, not as predictions for rclcpp. D1 D2

The service-call deadlock

F9-37 warned that a service call is two callbacks. Here is the full mechanism. A node's timer callback calls a service and waits for the response. The response is delivered through the client's callback, which belongs to some callback group. If the client is in the same mutually exclusive group as the waiting timer, the response callback cannot start until the timer callback ends, and the timer callback is waiting for the response: deadlock, whatever the number of threads. With a single-threaded executor it deadlocks even with different groups, because the only thread is busy waiting. The fix is either not to wait inside a callback (handle the response in its own callback), or to put the client in a different group and use a multi-threaded executor. D1

Choosing a configuration

A practical procedure, which the lab and forensic lab apply: (1) List each callback with its period or arrival rate and its cost (measure it, F9-43). (2) Mark which callbacks touch the same data. (3) Give each set of callbacks that share data its own mutually exclusive group; give time-critical callbacks a group of their own and keep slow work out of it. (4) Choose a multi-threaded executor with enough threads for the groups that may run together, or several single-threaded executors on separate threads, one per group of nodes. (5) Check the total load: the sum of cost ÷ period over all callbacks must be well below the number of threads, or waits grow without bound (F9-43's trace shows that growth). In R1, control, scan and logger together use 0.2 + 0.15 + 0.35 = 0.70 of one thread, which the "busy 606 of 1000 ms" line of A reflects: 700 ms of demanded work, less the 27 missed control runs (54 ms) and the three deadlines at t = 1000 that fall outside the run (40 ms). R1

Groups are not priorities

Isolation by groups lets a free thread take the control callback, but it does not make the control callback more important than anything else: if all threads are busy with slow callbacks from other groups, control still waits. Real-time robots therefore go further: a dedicated thread (or executor) for the control loop, with an operating-system priority and a real-time scheduling policy, on a core that slow work does not use. That is RB304's F9-49; this chapter's job is to make sure the executor configuration does not defeat it. D1

How the hardware actually does it

An executor thread is an ordinary operating-system thread (SP203's F2-34). The operating system's scheduler decides on which core it runs and when; a multi-threaded executor with two threads gives parallelism only if two cores are free. The exclusive group's guarantee is implemented in software (Listing 2 uses a mutex; rclcpp tracks whether a group is in use) and costs synchronisation between cores: a lock word moving between caches (SP203's F2-38). A callback's "cost" in Listing 1 is its CPU time; on real hardware it varies with cache misses, page faults, memory allocation and interrupts, which is why RB304 measures distributions, not single numbers. D1 R3

Worked example

Predict scenario A's control line of R1 by hand: "control … runs 72, max wait 30 ms, missed 27".

  1. Deadlines: control is due at 10, 20, …, 990 ms: 99 deadlines in the 1,000 ms run. Logger is due at 100, 200, …, 900: 9 runs. Scan messages arrive at 20, 40, …, 980.
  2. At t = 100, three things are ready at once: control (due 100), scan (arrived 100), logger (due 100). With equal ready times the model takes them in declaration order: control 100–102, scan 102–105, logger 105–140.
  3. Control becomes ready at 110 but the only thread is busy until 140. At 120 and at 130 the next deadlines arrive while the 110 instance is still waiting: two missed periods. At 140 the deadline arrives in the same step, before the thread is free to choose: a third missed period. Then control runs at 140, having waited 140 − 110 = 30 ms.
  4. Each of the 9 logger runs causes the same pattern: 9 × 3 = 27 missed periods. Runs: 99 − 27 = 72.
  5. R1 agrees: 72 runs, max wait 30 ms, 27 missed. The trace lines "t= 140 thread 0 start control (released t=110, waited 30 ms)" show step 3 directly. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Omar could see that Nadia had the pencil and choose something else to do. The model's threads do that too, but rclcpp's executor decides in rounds over all ready entities; it is not a person scanning the desk (Layer 3).
    • 

  • A pace call that is late is still made. A timer instance that is overdue by more than a period is skipped in this model, not queued; the data a control loop would have used at the missed tick is simply not used.
    • 

  • In the club, a second notebook costs nothing. A separate group lets callbacks run in parallel, which means any data they share now needs a mutex or atomics: the "pencil" moves into your code.
    • 

  • Nadia and Omar each had one task at a time and the club had two riders free. In a computer, the threads also compete for cores with every other program; a free thread does not mean a free core.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Adding threads without adding groups. Symptom: a multi-threaded executor and still the same latencies; one thread nearly idle (R1 B). Cause: everything in the default mutually exclusive group. Fix: separate groups for independent activities.
    • 

  • Making everything reentrant to "fix" latency. Symptom: rare wrong values, crashes under load. Cause: callbacks now overlap and share unprotected data. How to find it: ThreadSanitizer (R3 shows a clean run; lab step 4 shows a race). Fix: exclusive groups for callbacks that share data, or proper synchronisation.
    • 

  • Waiting for a service response inside a callback. Symptom: the node hangs. Cause: the response callback cannot run (same exclusive group, or the only thread is waiting). Fix: asynchronous handling, or a separate group plus a multi-threaded executor.
    • 

  • Slow work in the control loop's group. Symptom: control is late at a regular interval, equal to the slow callback's period (the forensic lab). Fix: move the slow callback to another group or another executor.
    • 

  • Overload. Symptom: waits that grow over the run instead of repeating. Cause: total cost per second exceeds what the threads can do. Fix: reduce rates or costs, or add threads and groups; check the arithmetic of Layer 3 step 5.
    •

Summary


    

  • An executor waits for ready timers, messages and requests and runs their callbacks, on one thread or a pool of threads.
    • 

  • Callback groups decide which callbacks may overlap: mutually exclusive (at most one at a time) or reentrant (no limit).
    • 

  • The default group is mutually exclusive: more threads alone do not add parallelism (R1, scenario B).
    • 

  • Isolate time-critical callbacks in their own group; keep slow work out of it; check the load arithmetic.
    • 

  • Exclusive groups protect plain shared data; reentrant groups require your own synchronisation (R2, R3).
    • 

  • Never wait for a service response in a callback whose response path is blocked by the same group or thread.
    •
