Story hook

Analogy

In winter the cycling club trains indoors. Each member's bicycle stands on a turbo trainer in front of a screen that shows a road. The trainer resists as if the road climbed; the video moves as fast as the rider pedals. The bicycle, the legs and the bike computer are real; the road is computed. Nobody falls off on ice, and the same hill can be ridden ten times in an evening.

One evening Aiko's old laptop could not keep up: the video played at half speed. Her coach had said "two minutes at this cadence, then rest", and Aiko timed the two minutes on the wall clock. At the end the screen said she had ridden half the distance her plan expected. Her legs had done two minutes of work; the computed world had only moved one minute forward.

A robot simulator is that trainer for software. The robot's nodes are real; the world, the motors and the sensors are computed. This chapter builds a tiny simulator, connects it in thought to Gazebo, the simulator most used with ROS 2, and shows why a slow computer can make a correct robot look wrong.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a robot simulator computes (a world, physics in fixed steps, sensors, a clock) and what it does not reproduce.
  • Run a teleoperated simulated robot with range-sensor and odometry topics, and compare odometry with ground truth.
  • Use simulated time correctly, and predict the effect of a real-time factor below 1 on code that uses wall time.
  • Describe how Gazebo, its world files, its plugins and its bridge to ROS 2 fit together, and which details must be checked in the documentation.
  • Plan the course project: a simulated robot stack with drivers, odometry, an EKF node and teleop.
Jargon box
Robot simulator (physics)
Simple words: a program that pretends to be the robot's body and the world around it, so that the robot's software can run without hardware. Analogy: the turbo trainer and its screen. Precise: software that integrates rigid-body dynamics (and contacts, friction, joints) in time steps, models sensors by computing what they would measure in the simulated world, models actuators, and exchanges commands and sensor data with the robot software through the same interfaces as the hardware. D1 D3
Physics step
Simple words: the small amount of pretend time the simulator moves forward each time it computes the world. Analogy: one frame of the trainer's video. Precise: the fixed time increment of the simulator's numerical integration; smaller steps give more accurate and more stable contacts at more computation per simulated second. D1 R1
Real-time factor
Simple words: how fast simulated time passes compared with real time. Analogy: the video playing at half speed on a slow laptop. Precise: simulated seconds advanced per wall-clock second; 1.0 means real time, below 1 means the simulator cannot keep up (or is slowed on purpose), above 1 means faster than real time. D1 R2
Simulated clock (sim time)
Simple words: the simulator's own clock, which the robot's programs read instead of the wall clock. Analogy: the clock in the video, not the one on the wall. Precise: a time source published by the simulator (in ROS 2 on a clock topic) that nodes configured for simulation time use for their timers, time stamps and durations, so that their behaviour is the same at any real-time factor. D2 R3
Ground truth
Simple words: the true position of the robot, which a simulator knows exactly and a real robot never does. Analogy: the trainer software knowing exactly where you are on the virtual road. Precise: the simulator's internal state of the robot's pose and velocity, used to measure the error of the robot's own estimates (odometry, EKF); on hardware it can only be approximated by an external measurement system. R1
Simulation world
Simple words: the description of everything in the simulation: floor, walls, objects, light, gravity and the robot. Analogy: the road and the scenery on the trainer's screen. Precise: a file (in Gazebo, an SDF file) listing models with their geometry, mass, inertia, joints, sensors and plugins, and world properties such as gravity and the physics engine's step size. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Before letting a robot drive around a real room, engineers drive it around a pretend room inside a computer. The computer works out, many times a second, where the robot's wheels take it and what its sensors would see. The robot's programs cannot tell the difference: they get sensor readings and send wheel commands just as they would on the real robot. If the program crashes into a wall, nothing breaks. The computer also knows exactly where the robot really is, so you can check how wrong the robot's own guess is. One rule: the programs must read the pretend world's clock, because if the computer is slow, the pretend world runs slower than real life.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What a simulator computes

A robot simulator has four parts. A world: geometry of rooms and objects, the robot's links, masses and joints. A physics engine that advances the world in fixed steps, applying motor torques, friction and contacts. Sensor models that compute what each sensor would measure: a range sensor by casting rays into the geometry, a camera by rendering, an IMU from the simulated motion, encoders from wheel rotation, each with optional noise. And a clock: simulated time advances by one step per physics step, however long the computation took. D1 D3

Listing 1 is the university's tiny version, built for this course: a 4 m by 3 m room with one box, a differential-drive robot (RB201), a 10 ms physics step (line 40), an 8-beam range sensor by ray casting (lines 17–31), odometry, a "real" left wheel that delivers 98 % of its command (line 41), and a teleop script. It is not Gazebo, but every concept below appears in it. R1

The course lab: a teleoperated robot with sensor topics

R1 is the teleoperated run. The robot drives forward at 0.4 m/s for 5 s toward the box. Every 0.5 s of simulated time the output prints what the sensor topics would carry: the pose from odometry, the true pose, and the eight range readings. At t = 4.19 s the front beam reads 0.346 m and the safety check stops the robot ("safety stop: front range 0.346 m < 0.35 m"). The teleop then turns it a quarter turn and drives on. Two things are visible in the numbers. The ranges change as expected: the front beam falls from 1.80 m to 0.35 m as the box approaches. And odometry drifts from ground truth: by t = 4.00 s the true heading is 6 degrees while odometry still says 0, because the slipping left wheel turns the robot slowly left and odometry assumes perfect wheels. At the end the odometry error is 0.152 m. R1

That last number is why simulators are so useful for estimation: ground truth is free. On hardware, measuring the true pose needs a motion-capture system or careful surveying; in simulation it is a variable. The course project's EKF (F9-34, F9-35) is evaluated exactly this way. R1

Simulated time and the real-time factor

A physics step of 10 ms of simulated time may take 2 ms of computer time or 20 ms, depending on the world's complexity and the machine. The ratio of simulated to wall time is the real-time factor (RTF). At RTF 1.0 the simulation keeps pace with reality; at 0.5 it runs at half speed. Listing 1 makes RTF an input (line 42) and keeps both clocks (line 81). D1

Everything in the robot software that involves time must then use simulated time: timers, durations, time stamps, timeouts. ROS 2 supports this with a clock topic published by the simulator and a per-node setting to use it (F9-43's replay used the same mechanism). Listing 1's teleop can time its commands with either clock (lines 43, 61). R2 is a "drive 0.4 m/s for 2 s" at RTF 0.5 timed on the wall clock: at the end, "sim 1.00 s, wall 2.00 s", and the robot moved from x = 0.50 to 0.896, about 0.40 m. R3 is the same command timed on the sim clock: "sim 2.00 s, wall 4.00 s", x = 1.292, about 0.79 m, as on a real robot. Figure 2 draws both. R2 R3

Gazebo

Gazebo is the simulator most used with ROS 2. A Gazebo world is described in SDF, an XML format listing models (geometry, inertia, joints), sensors and plugins. Plugins add behaviour: a differential-drive plugin turns velocity commands into wheel motion and publishes odometry; sensor plugins publish laser scans, images and IMU data. Gazebo has its own transport for messages, so a bridge translates chosen topics between Gazebo and ROS 2, including the clock. The robot's ROS 2 nodes then see the same topics as on hardware (Figure 1). Version pairings between Gazebo and ROS 2 distributions, command names and plugin names have changed in recent years and are listed in the unverified box. D1 D4

Fidelity: what simulation gets wrong

A simulator reproduces what it models and nothing else. Contacts and friction are approximations; wheel slip depends on parameters that are hard to measure; sensor noise models are simple compared with real sensors' failures (reflections, sunlight, dust, motion blur); actuators lack backlash, saturation and heating unless modelled; and timing is not hardware timing (F9-45 covers real-time). Listing 1 adds one realistic error, a 2 % left-wheel deficit, and R1 shows that this alone produces 0.152 m of odometry error in 11 s. The term for the gap between simulated and real behaviour is the "reality gap", and the engineering response is to validate in simulation, then test on hardware with care, then feed hardware measurements back into the simulation's parameters. D3 R1

Step size, stability and determinism

The physics step trades accuracy for speed. With a step too large, fast contacts are missed or bodies interpenetrate; Listing 1's safety stop is checked once per 10 ms step, so at 0.4 m/s the robot can travel 4 mm past the threshold before it reacts, which is why R1 stops at 0.346 m, not exactly 0.35 m. A simulator with a fixed step and no wall-clock dependence is deterministic: Listing 1 at RTF 0.5 with the sim clock gives the same end pose as at RTF 1.0, only slower in wall time (checked during authoring: "end: sim 11.15 s, wall 22.30 s; … odom error 0.152 m"). Determinism is what makes simulation usable for regression tests in continuous integration. Robot software that reads the wall clock, uses unseeded random numbers, or depends on thread timing breaks it. R1 D1

Running faster than real time

If the simulator can compute faster than real time, RTF above 1 runs tests quickly: question 4 runs Listing 1 at RTF 2.0. Robot software using simulated time keeps up as long as its own computation per simulated second fits into the shorter wall time; when it does not, results change (F9-43's overload, now in simulated time). Many test systems therefore run with lockstep, where the simulator waits for the software. D1

How the hardware actually does it

The point of a simulator is that hardware later replaces it without changes to the nodes. That holds only if the simulated drivers publish the same topics, types, units, frames, rates and QoS as the hardware drivers will, and if the simulated sensor behaves enough like the real one: range limits, field of view, noise, dropouts. On hardware, encoder ticks come from a motor controller over a bus (F9-14), the IMU from SPI or I2C, and the laser from Ethernet or USB; each driver stamps its data (F9-43). Moving from simulator to hardware also changes time: there is no /clock, nodes use wall time, and real-time behaviour (F9-45) matters for the first time. Plan that move as a step with its own tests: same launch file with a "simulation or hardware" argument (F9-42), and the same bag-based regression tests. D4 D2

Safety

This faculty's rule is simulator first. Move to a real robot only after the software has passed in simulation, and then with an emergency stop within reach, a clear zone, low speed limits and supervision below L3. A simulated safety stop proves the logic, not the stopping distance of real motors on a real floor.

Worked example

Predict R2 and R3 from the command and the RTF.

  1. The command: 0.4 m/s for 2 s. Intended motion: 0.80 m (on hardware, or with simulated time).
  2. RTF 0.5: one wall second contains 0.5 simulated seconds. Timed on the wall clock, the command lasts 2 wall seconds = 1.0 simulated second: 0.40 m. Start x = 0.50, so the end is near 0.90. R2: truth x = 0.896 (slightly less: the slip turns the robot a little).
  3. Timed on the sim clock, the command lasts 2.0 simulated seconds = 4.0 wall seconds: 0.80 m, end near 1.30. R3: 1.292.
  4. Ratio of motion: 0.396 ÷ 0.792 ≈ 0.5 = RTF. A wall-clock-timed command delivers RTF times its intended motion. R2 R3

Predict R1's first safety stop. The box's near face is at x = 2.5, the robot starts at x = 0.5 heading +x at 0.4 m/s, and stops when the front range is below 0.35 m, at x ≈ 2.15, after about 1.65 m ÷ 0.4 m/s ≈ 4.1 s. R1: t = 4.19 s; slightly later because the robot drifts left (its heading reaches 6 degrees) so its forward speed along x is a little lower, and the beam hits the face at a slant. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A turbo trainer's rider feels the resistance directly. Robot software only sees what the simulator chooses to model; unmodelled effects (a wheel lifting, a reflective wall) do not exist for it.
    • 

  • Aiko could see the video was slow. Robot nodes cannot, unless they read the simulated clock or the RTF; a slow simulator is invisible to code that uses wall time.
    • 

  • The trainer's road is a video; a simulated world is a model that responds: the robot's motion changes what the sensors see, and the safety stop changes the motion.
    • 

  • A cyclist on a trainer still gets tired. Simulated motors never heat, wear or saturate unless someone models it, so a controller tuned only in simulation can ask hardware for more than it can give.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Nodes not using simulated time. Symptom: commands give too little or too much motion when RTF ≠ 1 (R2); time stamps far from the simulator's; transforms rejected as too old. Fix: every node uses simulated time; check the setting per node.
    • 

  • Bridge not carrying a topic. Symptom: the robot does not move; /cmd_vel has a publisher but the simulator receives nothing. Fix: check the bridge's topic list, types and direction (unverified box), with the graph tools of F9-37.
    • 

  • Mismatched versions. Symptom: packages fail to install or plugins do not load. Fix: use a documented pairing of ROS 2 distribution and Gazebo release.
    • 

  • Trusting simulation results on hardware. Symptom: the controller oscillates or the robot stops late on the real floor. Fix: validate parameters (friction, slip, sensor noise) against hardware measurements; retest on hardware with the safety rules.
    • 

  • Using ground truth inside the robot's software. Symptom: excellent results in simulation, failure on hardware. Fix: ground truth only in the evaluator.
    • 

  • Step size too large. Symptom: the robot passes through thin walls or jitters on contact. Fix: smaller steps; check RTF afterwards.
    •

Summary


    

  • A simulator computes a world, physics in fixed steps, sensor readings and a clock; robot nodes use the same topics as on hardware.
    • 

  • Ground truth is free in simulation: use it to measure estimation error, never inside the robot's software.
    • 

  • Real-time factor = simulated seconds per wall second; it depends on the computer.
    • 

  • Nodes must use simulated time; a wall-clock-timed command delivers RTF times its intended motion.
    • 

  • Gazebo uses SDF worlds, plugins for drives and sensors, and a bridge to ROS 2 that must include the clock; versions and names must be checked.
    • 

  • Simulation first, then hardware with the safety rules; the reality gap is closed by measurement.
