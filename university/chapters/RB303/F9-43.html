Story hook

Analogy

After the club's long Sunday ride, Mehmet wanted to check the distance his new bike computer reported. He had a recording of the ride: every few seconds the computer had written down the time and his speed. At home he played the recording into a spreadsheet that added "speed times the time since the last line". To save time he played it at double speed.

The spreadsheet said he had ridden half the distance. His legs disagreed. The recording was fine; the speeds were right. The spreadsheet had measured "the time since the last line" with the kitchen clock, while the lines were arriving twice as fast as they had been written.

Recording, replaying, plotting and timing are how robot engineers find faults after the fact. They are powerful because they let you rerun the past. This chapter builds those tools in miniature, shows what each one tells you, and shows how Mehmet's mistake hides inside a robot's replay.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a bag records (topic, type, time stamps, message data) and what it does not record.
  • Inspect and plot a recording, and read its summary for duration, topics and counts.
  • Replay a recording at a chosen rate, and say which time a node must use so that its results do not depend on the replay rate.
  • Trace callback latency, read its median, 99th percentile and maximum, and recognise an overloaded executor from the numbers.
  • Diagnose a node whose results change with replay speed because it uses arrival time instead of message time.
Jargon box
Bag (ROS 2)
Simple words: a file holding a recording of messages from chosen topics, with their times. Analogy: a photocopy of every notice pinned during the ride, each with its pin time. Precise: a ROS 2 recording made by the rosbag2 tool: for each recorded topic, its name, type and QoS, and for each message its serialised data and a receive time stamp, stored through a storage plugin (for example an SQLite or MCAP file) with a metadata file. D1
Replay rate
Simple words: how fast a recording is played back compared with real time. Analogy: pinning the photocopies again twice as fast. Precise: the factor by which the replay tool compresses the intervals between recorded messages; at rate 2.0, messages recorded 100 ms apart are published 50 ms apart. D1 R1
Timestamp (header stamp)
Simple words: the time written inside a message saying when its data was measured. Analogy: the time printed on the notice, not the time you read it. Precise: the stamp field of a message's std_msgs/Header, set by the producer to the acquisition time of the data; it travels with the message, so it is unchanged by transport delay, recording or replay. D2
Tracing (ROS 2)
Simple words: recording tiny time-stamped events inside the running programs, such as "callback started", to measure where time goes. Analogy: a stopwatch log of when each helper picked up and finished each job. Precise: low-overhead instrumentation of ROS 2 and the application with tracepoints, collected by a tracer (LTTng on Linux in the ros2_tracing project) and analysed offline to obtain callback durations, latencies and message flow. D3
3D visualisation tool (RViz)
Simple words: a program that draws what the robot thinks: its position, sensor readings and plans, in 3D. Analogy: a map on the club-house wall with everyone's position pinned to it. Precise: RViz (RViz2 in ROS 2) subscribes to topics such as laser scans, point clouds, odometry, transforms and markers and renders them in a common coordinate frame; it shows data, it does not simulate. D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot's programs talk by sending messages. You can record all of them into a file while the robot drives, like recording a phone call. Later you can play the file back to your programs, at normal speed or faster, and they behave as if the robot were driving again. You can draw the numbers as a picture to see what happened. And you can put a stopwatch inside the programs to see which jobs are slow. One trap: if a program measures time with the clock on the wall instead of the time written in each message, playing faster changes its answers.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Recording: what a bag holds

The ROS 2 recording tool, rosbag2, subscribes to chosen topics (or all of them) and writes every message it receives into a bag: topic name, message type, the serialised message, and the time it was received. It also stores each topic's QoS, so that replay can offer a compatible QoS (F9-41). D1

What a bag does not hold matters as much. It does not hold parameter values, unless you record them separately; nor the internal state of nodes; nor messages on topics you did not record; nor messages that never reached the recorder, which with best-effort QoS (F9-40) can be some of them. A recorder is a subscriber like any other. D1

Listing 1 models a recording of a 10 s drive: /wheel_speed at 10 Hz (0.5 m/s for 4 s, 0.25 m/s for 4 s, then stopped) and /imu at 50 Hz (lines 15–30). Each message carries a stamp, the time its data was measured.

Inspecting and plotting

The first thing to do with a bag is read its summary: duration, start and end time, topics, types and message counts. R1's summary says "duration 9.980 s … messages 600", with 500 IMU and 100 wheel-speed messages: exactly 50 Hz and 10 Hz. A count lower than expected is the first sign of dropped messages or a stalled publisher. The second thing is to plot. R1's text plot of /wheel_speed shows the two speed levels and the stop. Real tools plot interactively (rqt_plot, PlotJuggler and others, in the unverified box). R1

Replay and the two clocks

Replaying publishes the recorded messages again, with the recorded intervals between them, optionally scaled by a rate: at rate 2.0 a 10 s bag plays in 5 s. Downstream nodes cannot tell a replay from the live robot, which is the point: you can test a new odometry or perception node against yesterday's drive as often as you like. D1

Every replayed message has two times. Its header stamp, written by the original sensor driver, says when the data was measured; replay does not change it. Its arrival time is when the node received it now. Listing 1's replay (lines 55–73) integrates distance with either. With stamps, the result is 3.000 m at rate 1.0 and at rate 2.0. With arrival time, it is 3.000 m at rate 1.0 only: R2 shows dt = 0.050 s instead of 0.100 s, and 1.500 m at rate 2.0 (Figure 1). R1 R2

The rule: a node that computes from sensor data should use the time stamps in the data. Where a node must use "now" (a timer, a timeout), ROS 2 offers a simulated clock: a replay or a simulator publishes the time on a clock topic, and nodes that are told to use simulation time read their clock from it. F9-44 uses the same mechanism for simulators. D1 D2

Visualisation

Plots show numbers over time. A robot also needs a view in space: where the robot thinks it is, what its laser sees, where the map's walls are, which path the planner chose, all drawn in one coordinate frame. RViz does that for ROS 2. It subscribes to topics and draws them; it does not simulate (that is F9-44's Gazebo). The most useful habit with RViz is comparing two things that should agree: the laser scan and the map, the odometry and the ground truth. D4

Tracing

Bags tell you what was published. Tracing tells you where time went inside the nodes. A trace records events such as "message arrived", "callback started" and "callback ended" with precise times, at low overhead, and is analysed afterwards. Listing 1's trace (lines 76–89) models one executor thread (F9-39) handling all 600 messages, each callback costing a fixed time, and reports the latency from arrival to callback start. D3

R1's three traces are the important lesson. At 3 ms per callback, the thread is busy 18 ms of every 100 ms: latency median 0, p99 3,000 µs. At 15 ms: busy 90 % of the time; p99 15,000 µs, still bounded. At 17 ms: busy 102 % of the time. Now median latency is 108,000 µs, p99 209,000 µs and maximum 215,000 µs, and the numbers would keep growing in a longer recording (Figure 2). A small change in cost produced a large change in latency. Utilisation near or above 100 % is the cause to look for when latency percentiles jump. R1

Replay is not reproduction

A replay reproduces inputs, not conditions. The timing between messages is approximately the recorded one, but the replaying machine's scheduling, the replay tool's own timing and the node's processing speed differ, so timing-sensitive faults (F9-39's starvation, F9-41's deadlines) may not reproduce. Messages the recorder missed are missing from the replay too. And if the original nodes are still running during replay, their output mixes with the recorded one on the same topics: record raw sensor topics, and run only the nodes under test. D1

Which time to use where

Three clocks appear in robot software: the sensor's acquisition time (header stamps), the ROS clock of the node (wall time or simulated time), and the steady clock of the operating system for measuring durations (SP102). Integration of sensor data uses stamps; timeouts and periods use the ROS clock so that they scale with simulation and replay; benchmarking and tracing use the steady clock. R2's fault is mixing the first and the second. R2 D2

Reading latency numbers

The median says what usually happens; the p99 and maximum say what happens on bad occasions, and control loops fail on bad occasions. R1's 15 ms case has median 0 and p99 15 ms, a bounded tail caused by queueing behind one other callback. The 17 ms case has a tail that grows with time, the mark of an overloaded resource. The remedy is not a faster thread pool by default: first measure utilisation per callback (the tracing analysis gives durations), then reduce the cost, move work to another executor (F9-39), or lower the input rate. R1 D3

Overhead and the observer effect

Every tool here observes the system and so changes it. A recorder subscribing with reliable QoS adds a reliable reader to every topic it records; RViz receiving large point clouds over Wi-Fi can saturate the link the robot also uses; logging every message to the console costs more than many callbacks. Tracing with in-kernel buffers is designed for low overhead, but it is not zero. Measure with and without the tool when the numbers matter. D3 D1

How the hardware actually does it

A header stamp is only as good as the clock that wrote it. A driver should stamp a measurement with the time the sensor took it, not the time the driver read it from the USB or serial buffer; the difference can be several milliseconds, and for a fast-moving robot that is centimetres. Sensors that carry their own clock need it related to the computer's (RB201 discusses sensor timing). Multiple computers on one robot need synchronised clocks, usually with the Precision Time Protocol or NTP, or their stamps cannot be compared. Recording at high rates is limited by disk write speed: an uncompressed camera stream can exceed what a small embedded board's storage can take, which is why bags are often recorded on a separate machine or compressed. D1 D2

Worked example

Predict R1's replay distance and R2's, from the drive alone.

  1. The drive: 0.5 m/s for 4 s, 0.25 m/s for 4 s, then 0. Distance = 2.0 + 1.0 = 3.0 m.
  2. The node integrates distance += previous speed × dt over 100 messages; the speed changes exactly at a message, so the sum is exact: 3.000 m with stamps (dt = 0.1 s) at any rate (R1).
  3. At rate 2.0, arrival times are stamps ÷ 2, so arrival-based dt = 0.05 s, half of every step: 1.500 m (R2).
  4. In general: arrival-based results scale by 1 ÷ rate. Rate 0.5 would give 6.000 m. (Lab step 2 checks this.) R1 R2

Predict R1's utilisation: per 100 ms there are 6 callbacks. At 15 ms each: 90 ms busy, 90 %. At 17 ms: 102 ms, 102 %: overload.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A photocopy of a notice board catches every notice. A recorder is a subscriber; with best-effort QoS or an overloaded disk it can miss messages, and the bag then lies by omission.
    • 

  • Mehmet's spreadsheet is one program. On a robot, many nodes receive the replay; some use stamps and some use their clock, so a replay can be consistent for one node and wrong for another at the same moment.
    • 

  • A stopwatch held by a person does not slow the helpers. Every recording, display and trace costs CPU, memory and bandwidth on the system it observes.
    • 

  • A map on a wall shows where people are. RViz shows where the robot's software believes things are; it cannot show you the truth, only the agreement or disagreement between the robot's beliefs.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Using arrival time for integration. Symptom: results change with replay rate or CPU load (R2). Fix: use header stamps; check by replaying at two rates and comparing.
    • 

  • Original nodes still running during replay. Symptom: jittery or doubled outputs; two publishers on a topic in the graph listing. Fix: start only the nodes under test.
    • 

  • Forgetting the clock during replay. Symptom: timeouts fire, transforms "from the past" or "the future" are rejected. Fix: publish a simulated clock from the replay and set nodes to use simulation time (unverified box).
    • 

  • Reading only the average latency. Symptom: "average 4 ms" but the controller misses deadlines. Fix: report median, p99 and maximum, and the utilisation.
    • 

  • Recording everything. Symptom: the disk fills; the recorder drops messages; the robot slows. Fix: record the raw sensor topics and the few outputs you need; compress; record on another machine.
    • 

  • Fixed frame missing in RViz. Symptom: nothing drawn, a status error. Fix: choose a frame that exists in the transform tree (F9-23 covers frames and transforms).
    •

Summary


    

  • A bag records topics, types, QoS, serialised messages and receive times; not parameters, internal state, unrecorded topics or missed messages.
    • 

  • Read a bag's summary (duration, counts) and plot it before computing with it.
    • 

  • Replay compresses arrival times by the rate; header stamps are unchanged. Integrate with stamps; use a simulated clock for timers.
    • 

  • RViz shows the robot's beliefs in space; it does not simulate or show truth.
    • 

  • Tracing measures callback timing; report median, p99, maximum and utilisation. Above 100 % utilisation, latency grows without bound.
    • 

  • All observation tools cost resources; measure with and without them when it matters.
