Story hook

The courier service from F5-34 grows. In one valley, the towns build their own courier railway: their own tracks, their own stations, a central timetable office that gives every station a number and tells every junction which way to switch. A train leaves only when the next station has said it has room, so no parcel is ever thrown off a full platform.

In the next valley there is no money for tracks. The couriers drive on the public roads, inside the same envelopes and address labels as everybody else's post. That works, but the public roads were built for traffic where a lost parcel is simply sent again. A courier who loses one parcel must go back and redeliver everything since that parcel, which is slow. So the valley paints one lane on every road: in that lane, traffic lights stop the vans when the road ahead is full, instead of turning parcels away.

The private railway is InfiniBand. The couriers on public roads are RoCE, RDMA over Converged Ethernet. The painted lane with traffic lights is a lossless traffic class. This chapter opens the parcels and reads every label, byte by byte.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the layers of the InfiniBand architecture (physical, link, network, transport) and what a subnet manager, a LID and a GID are.
  • Decode the InfiniBand transport headers (BTH, RETH, AETH) of a captured RoCE v2 frame by hand and check your decoding with tshark.
  • Explain the difference between RoCE v1 and RoCE v2 and what RoCE needs from the Ethernet network: an MTU that fits the RDMA path MTU, and a lossless class or a congestion-control scheme.
  • Calculate per-packet header overhead and the largest RoCE path MTU that fits a given Ethernet MTU.
  • Diagnose from a capture why one sender's RDMA traffic is retransmitted: PSN gaps, NAKs, go-back-N, and a DSCP that puts it in the wrong traffic class.
Jargon box
Subnet manager (SM)
Simple words: a program that discovers an InfiniBand network, gives every port an address and tells every switch where to send each address. Analogy: the railway's timetable office that numbers the stations and sets the junctions. Precise: the InfiniBand management entity that sweeps the subnet, assigns a local identifier to each port, computes routes and programs the switches' forwarding tables; a port learns the SM's own LID (the installed verbs header exposes it as sm_lid in struct ibv_port_attr). D1 D3
LID and GID
Simple words: two kinds of address: a short one used inside one InfiniBand network, and a long one that works everywhere (on RoCE it is built from the IP address). Analogy: a station number on the local railway, and a full postal address. Precise: the local identifier (LID) is a 16-bit address assigned by the subnet manager and used by switches inside a subnet; the global identifier (GID) is a 128-bit address carried in a Global Route Header, required for routing between subnets and, on RoCE, used for every packet. The verbs header declares uint16_t lid and union ibv_gid with 16 raw bytes. D1 D3
Base Transport Header (BTH)
Simple words: the 12-byte label on every RDMA packet: what kind of packet it is, which queue pair it is for, and its sequence number. Analogy: the courier's own label inside the envelope: "parcel 5 for shelf-desk 0x11". Precise: the InfiniBand transport header present in every packet: opcode, solicited-event and migration bits, pad count, header version, partition key, destination queue pair number (24 bits), acknowledge-request bit and packet sequence number (24 bits); decoded field by field by tshark in this chapter's lab. D1 D4
Packet sequence number (PSN)
Simple words: the number of each packet on a connection, so the receiver can spot a missing one. Analogy: numbering parcels 100, 101, 102… so a missing 102 is noticed when 103 arrives. Precise: a 24-bit counter in the BTH; for reliable service the responder expects PSNs in order, acknowledges them, and reports a gap with a negative acknowledgement (NAK, PSN sequence error) carrying the expected PSN. D1 R4
RoCE v1 and RoCE v2
Simple words: two ways of carrying InfiniBand's transport over Ethernet: v1 directly inside Ethernet frames, v2 inside UDP/IP packets so routers can forward it. Analogy: v1 couriers stay on the roads of one town; v2 couriers use full postal addresses and can cross between towns. Precise: RoCE v1 places the GRH and transport headers directly after an Ethernet header with its own EtherType (0x8915 as decoded by tshark); RoCE v2 replaces the GRH by an IPv4 or IPv6 header and a UDP header whose destination port identifies RoCE (4791 in tshark's decode table), so packets are routable at layer 3. D2 D4 R3
Path MTU (RDMA)
Simple words: the largest amount of message data that one RDMA packet carries on a connection. Analogy: the largest parcel the courier's van takes in one trip; longer notes are split. Precise: the InfiniBand MTU chosen per connection from 256, 512, 1024, 2048 or 4096 bytes (the values of enum ibv_mtu in the installed header), counting payload only; on RoCE, the packet with all its headers must also fit the Ethernet MTU of every link on the path. D1 D3 R1
Lossless traffic class and priority flow control (PFC)
Simple words: a setting that makes an Ethernet switch tell the sender "wait" instead of throwing packets away when one class of traffic fills its buffer. Analogy: the painted lane with traffic lights. Precise: Ethernet priority-based flow control pauses transmission of one priority (traffic class) on a link when the receiver's buffer for that priority passes a threshold; packets are mapped to priorities by a VLAN priority field or by the IP DSCP value; RoCE deployments commonly put RDMA traffic in such a class. D5 D6
Go-back-N
Simple words: after a lost packet, resend that packet and every packet after it, even those that arrived. Analogy: the courier who lost parcel 102 redelivers 102, 103, 104 and 105. Precise: a retransmission scheme in which the receiver accepts only in-order packets and discards later ones after a gap, and the sender, on a NAK or timeout, resumes from the first missing sequence number; the reliable-connection recovery assumed in this chapter's forensic lab and in the model of F5-37. D1

Terms from earlier courses: MTU (maximum transmission unit) (DS201), InfiniBand and RoCE and queue pair (HW205), and from F5-34 transport offload.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (proposed for the F5 registry): InfiniBand = a private courier railway with its own tracks, stations and timetable office; the timetable office = the subnet manager; a station number = a LID; a full postal address = a GID; RoCE = couriers on the public roads, using ordinary envelopes (Ethernet, IP and UDP headers) around their own label (the transport headers); the painted lane with traffic lights = a lossless traffic class with priority flow control; "redeliver everything since the lost parcel" = go-back-N.

Every RDMA packet carries a small label that says what it is (a write, a read, a reply), which desk it is for (the queue pair) and its number in the sequence. The label is the same whether the packet rides on InfiniBand or on Ethernet. On InfiniBand the network itself never throws packets away: a link sends only when the other end has said it has room. On Ethernet, switches drop packets when they are full, so a RoCE network must be set up so that RDMA packets get a lane where switches say "wait" instead, or the courier will spend its time redelivering.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The InfiniBand architecture in four layers

The InfiniBand Architecture Specification defines a complete network, not only a protocol D1:

  • Physical layer: its own cables, connectors and signalling (line rates are product facts: read them in the adapter's and switch's datasheets, AH-21).
  • Link layer: packets switched inside one subnet by the destination LID in the Local Route Header; several virtual lanes per link; credit-based flow control: a port transmits on a virtual lane only when the receiver has advertised buffer space, so the fabric does not drop packets for lack of buffer D1.
  • Network layer: the Global Route Header with 128-bit GIDs, for routing between subnets D1.
  • Transport layer: queue pairs and transport services (reliable connection, unreliable connection, unreliable datagram, and reliable datagram), carried by the Base Transport Header and extended headers D1. The installed verbs header lists the queue-pair types IBV_QPT_RC, IBV_QPT_UC, IBV_QPT_UD among others D3.

A subnet manager runs somewhere in the fabric (on a switch or a host). It discovers the topology, assigns LIDs, computes routes and writes forwarding tables into the switches. Until it has done so, ports do not reach the active state D1. The verbs interface shows the result to a program: ibv_query_port fills a struct ibv_port_attr whose fields include the port's lid, the subnet manager's sm_lid, the active_mtu and the link_layer (IBV_LINK_LAYER_INFINIBAND or IBV_LINK_LAYER_ETHERNET) D3.

Two independent artefacts (a header from the RDMA project and a decoder from the Wireshark project) agree with each other and with the bytes; neither is the specification, which remains the authority (D1).

Extended transport headers follow the BTH when the opcode needs them D1 R2:

  • RETH (RDMA Extended Transport Header, 16 bytes) on the first packet of an RDMA WRITE and on an RDMA READ request: Virtual Address (64 bits), Remote Key (32 bits), DMA Length (32 bits). This is where the remote key travels: the responder's adapter checks it against its registered regions (F5-36).
  • AETH (ACK Extended Transport Header, 4 bytes) on acknowledgements: a syndrome byte and a 24-bit message sequence number. tshark decodes syndrome 0x00 as "Ack" with a credit count, and syndrome 0x60 as "Nak", error code "PSN Sequence Error" (frame 14 of the forensic capture) R4.
  • ICRC (invariant CRC, 4 bytes) at the end: a CRC over the parts of the packet that do not change in transit, so the end points can detect corruption that a per-link check would miss D1. The lab writes zeros; tshark shows "Invariant CRC: 0x00000000" without judging it.

RoCE v1 and RoCE v2

RoCE keeps the InfiniBand transport and replaces the InfiniBand link layer with Ethernet D2. In version 1 the GRH (40 bytes, laid out like an IPv6 header with "Next Header" 0x1b for the InfiniBand transport) follows the Ethernet header directly; frame 3 of the lab is such a frame, with EtherType 0x8915, and tshark names that EtherType "RDMA over Converged Ethernet" R2. Because there is no IP header, RoCE v1 cannot cross an IP router. Version 2 replaces the GRH with a real IPv4 or IPv6 header plus a UDP header; the UDP destination port says "this is RoCE". tshark's built-in decode table maps UDP port 4791 and EtherType 35093 (0x8915) to its InfiniBand dissector R3. RoCE v2 packets are ordinary IP packets to every router and switch: they can be routed, hashed across links and classified by their DSCP field.

MTUs: two numbers that must agree

An RDMA connection has a path MTU, one of 256, 512, 1024, 2048 or 4096 bytes (enum ibv_mtu in the installed header, values IBV_MTU_256 = 1 to IBV_MTU_4096 = 5) D3. It counts message payload per packet. On RoCE v2, each packet also carries IPv4 (20), UDP (8), BTH (12), ICRC (4) and, on the first packet of a WRITE, RETH (16) bytes, and the resulting IP packet must not exceed the Ethernet MTU of any link on the path (DS201's MTU counts the IP packet) R1. The lab's table:



With the standard Ethernet MTU of 1500 bytes, 1024 is the largest RDMA path MTU that fits (its first packet is a 1084-byte IP packet; 2048 would need 2108). Raising the Ethernet MTU on every port of the path (a "jumbo" MTU; 9000 is used in the table only as an example, the maximum is in each device's datasheet) allows 4096. The payload share rises from 94.6 % to 98.6 % R1, and, more importantly, a message needs a quarter of the packets, so per-packet costs (adapter processing, acknowledgements, loss recovery) shrink too.


Why RoCE wants a lossless network


InfiniBand's link layer does not drop packets for lack of buffer, and its reliable transport was designed for that world D1. Ethernet switches drop when a queue is full. If an RDMA packet is dropped, the responder sees the next PSN out of order, discards it, and returns a NAK with the PSN it expected; the requester then resends from that PSN, including packets that had arrived (go-back-N) D1. Two families of remedies exist, and real deployments combine them D5 D6:


    

  • Make the class lossless: classify RDMA traffic into one priority (by DSCP or VLAN priority), enable priority flow control on that priority on every port of the path, and give it buffer headroom; the switch pauses the upstream port instead of dropping.
    • 

  • Avoid filling queues: switches mark packets with Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN) when queues grow; the receiver tells the sender, and the sender's adapter lowers its rate.
    • 



Both depend on configuration that must be consistent across hosts and switches. One host that marks its RDMA traffic with the wrong DSCP silently lands in a lossy class: that is the forensic lab.

The cost of making Ethernet lossless


Priority flow control works per link and per priority: a full queue pauses the whole upstream port's traffic in that priority, including flows that were not causing the congestion. Pauses can spread backwards through the network (congestion spreading), and a cycle of pauses between switches can stall a class entirely. A device that keeps sending pauses because it cannot receive (a stuck host) can block every RDMA flow that shares its upstream links. These are reasons to keep the lossless class small, use ECN-based rate control so that pauses are rare, and monitor pause counters on every port D5 D6. There is also a third design point: adapters whose transport tolerates loss better (selective retransmission instead of go-back-N), which some vendors document; check the adapter's documentation before assuming either behaviour.


Two CRCs, and why the lab does not compute the ICRC


On InfiniBand a variant CRC protects each link hop and the invariant CRC protects the packet end to end; on RoCE the Ethernet frame check sequence plays the per-hop role D1 D2. The ICRC for RoCE v2 is computed over the IP, UDP and transport headers with the fields that routers may change (such as the time-to-live, the DSCP/ECN byte and the checksums) replaced by ones. The lab writes zeros instead, because computing it correctly needs the exact mask and CRC parameters from the specification, which this build could not open; a real adapter would discard the lab's frames. That is acceptable for decoding practice and is why the lab's frames are "untested on hardware".


Routing RoCE v2 in a data-centre network


Data-centre networks spread traffic across equal-cost paths by hashing header fields, often including the UDP source port. A RoCE v2 connection keeps its packets in order only if all its packets take the same path, so a connection uses one source port for its life; different connections may use different ports so that the network spreads them. Retransmissions after loss and reordering are both costly for go-back-N; adaptive per-packet routing, which can reorder, interacts badly with it. Treat these as design questions to settle with the network team and the adapter documentation (D6), not as facts this chapter has verified.



How the hardware actually does it


The adapter does in hardware what the lab does in packet.hpp: for every packet of a message it builds Ethernet, IP, UDP and BTH headers from the queue pair's state (addresses, DSCP, destination QP number, next PSN), takes up to one path MTU of payload from host memory by DMA, computes the ICRC and the frame check sequence, and transmits. On receive it checks the CRCs, finds the queue pair from the destination QP field, checks the PSN against the expected one, and for a WRITE checks the remote key and bounds before writing the payload to host memory by DMA D1 D6. Switches look only at the outer headers: Ethernet addresses, IP addresses, DSCP, UDP ports for hashing; to them RoCE v2 is ordinary UDP traffic, which is why classification by DSCP must be configured explicitly.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A railway timetable office is needed for every train. The subnet manager is needed to set the network up and to react to changes; packets flow without asking it each time.
    • 

  • "The network never drops a parcel" is true of InfiniBand only for lack of buffer space. Bit errors, failed links and misconfiguration still lose packets, and the transport still has timeouts and retries D1.
    • 

  • Traffic lights in a painted lane stop only the cars in that lane. A PFC pause stops a whole priority on a whole link, including innocent flows, and pauses can spread backwards through several switches (Layer 3).
    • 

  • Couriers on public roads look like any other van only from outside. To a switch, RoCE v2 is ordinary UDP; nothing in the network "knows" it is RDMA unless someone configures a classification rule.
    • 

  • A courier who loses parcel 102 could redeliver only 102. Whether an adapter resends only the missing packet or everything after it is a property of the transport and the adapter; this chapter assumes go-back-N.
    •

Hardware and software needed. Linux with g++ (C++20) and tshark; no RDMA hardware. The build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 and TShark (Wireshark) 4.2.2. The frames are not sent on any network. Safety: none beyond normal computer use; do not inject hand-built frames into a production network.


Steps.


    

  1. Run university/labs/run_lab.sh university/labs/F5-35 from the repository root; expect exit status 0 and the files roce_packet.out, dissect.out, decodes.out, incast.out, incast_capture.out, nak_detail.out.
    2. 

  2. Compare roce_packet.out's hex with dissect.out for frame 2. Write the AETH bytes and their meaning.
    3. 

  3. Add a frame 4: an RDMA READ Request (look up the opcode in /usr/include/infiniband/opcode.h) with a RETH asking for 64 bytes; and a frame 5: the READ Response Only carrying 64 bytes back, with an AETH. Predict tshark's opcode names, then check.
    4. 

  4. Add a 4-packet RDMA WRITE (First with RETH, two Middle, Last with A=1), path MTU 256, PSNs 40–43. Predict the UDP length of each packet; check with tshark -r roce.pcap -T fields -e udp.length -e infiniband.bth.opcode -e infiniband.bth.psn.
    5. 

  5. Change the payload to 30 bytes and set the pad count so that the payload plus padding is a multiple of 4. What does tshark show?
    6. 

  6. Extend the MTU table with a column "packets for 1 MiB" and check it against the worked example.
    7. 



Expected observations. tshark decodes every lab frame as "InfiniBand" with the opcode names, QP numbers and PSNs you wrote (R2); the Differentiated Services field of frame 1 reads "0x6a (DSCP: AF31, ECN: ECT(0))"; the MTU table matches the worked example.


Verification. For each new frame, the fields printed by tshark equal your predictions; run_lab.sh still exits 0 with no sanitizer messages.


Troubleshooting.


    

  • tshark prints "Malformed Packet" → a length field disagrees with the bytes (IP total length, UDP length or GRH payload length); recompute them from the parts.
    • 

  • tshark shows "Data" instead of "InfiniBand" → wrong UDP destination port or EtherType.
    • 

  • Opcode decoded as a different transport (UC, UD) → the top three bits of the opcode select the transport group (Layer 2 table).
    • 

  • Strange PSNs → a value was written with 16 bits instead of 24; use put24.
    • 



Extension. Write a decoder: read roce.pcap back in C++ and print the BTH fields yourself, then compare with tshark for every frame. Or build an Unreliable Datagram SEND (it needs a DETH extended header; find its layout in the specification, not from memory).

Summary


    

  • InfiniBand is a full network: physical and link layers with credit-based flow control, LIDs from a subnet manager, GIDs for routing, and a transport with queue pairs and several services.
    • 

  • Every RDMA packet carries a 12-byte BTH (opcode, partition key, destination QP, PSN); RETH carries address, remote key and length; AETH carries ACKs and NAKs; the ICRC protects invariant fields.
    • 

  • RoCE v1 puts the GRH and transport straight into Ethernet (EtherType 0x8915); RoCE v2 uses IP and UDP (port 4791) and is routable; tshark 4.2.2 decodes both.
    • 

  • The RDMA path MTU (256–4096) counts payload; on RoCE the IP packet with all headers must fit the Ethernet MTU: 1024 is the largest on a 1500-byte MTU.
    • 

  • Loss is expensive for RoCE (NAK and go-back-N), so RDMA traffic needs a lossless class (PFC) and/or ECN-based rate control, configured consistently on hosts and switches.
    • 

  • Lossless Ethernet has its own failure modes: pauses hit innocent flows and can spread.
    • 

  • Everything in this chapter was built and decoded in software; nothing ran on RDMA hardware.
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Physical (cables, signalling); link (LID switching inside a subnet, virtual lanes, credit-based flow control); network (GRH, GIDs, routing between subnets); transport (queue pairs, BTH and extended headers, reliability). RoCE replaces the physical and link layers with Ethernet (v2 also uses IP and UDP in place of the GRH).
    2. 

  2. It discovers the fabric, assigns LIDs, computes routes and programs switch forwarding tables; sm_lid in struct ibv_port_attr holds the subnet manager's LID (and state shows whether the port became active).
    3. 

  3. Opcode 0x06 = RC RDMA WRITE First; flags 0; P_Key 0xffff; destination QP 0x000123; A = 0; PSN 0x00012c = 300. (Compare with frame 3 of the forensic capture.)
    4. 

  4. RoCE v2 packets are IP packets: routers forward them by the IP header (frame 1 bytes 14–33 and the UDP header at 34–41). RoCE v1 frames have EtherType 0x8915 and a GRH but no IP header (frame 3 bytes 12–13 = 89 15), so an IP router does not forward them.
    5. 

  5. 4096. The largest IP packet at path MTU 4096 is the first packet of a WRITE: 20 + 8 + 12 + 16 + 4096 + 4 = 4156 bytes, which is not more than 4200, so it fits (R1 table). A 64 KiB WRITE then needs 65,536 / 4096 = 16 packets. (Distractor: answering 2048 by reading only the "fits 1500 / fits 9000" columns; compute with the actual MTU.)
    6. 

  6. With go-back-N, one drop makes the responder discard everything after the gap, and the requester resends from the missing PSN, so one drop costs a whole window of packets; TCP with selective acknowledgements resends mostly the missing segments. Remedies: make the RDMA class lossless (PFC with DSCP classification and headroom) and keep queues short with ECN-based rate control.
    7. 

  7. Pauses stop every flow of that priority on a link, including innocent ones, and can spread backwards and stall large parts of the network; traffic that tolerates loss (most TCP) gains nothing and adds pause pressure. Also, more lossless traffic needs more buffer headroom on every switch.
    8. 

  8. 46 is 101110 in binary; with ECN 10 the byte is 1011 1010 = 0xba. A scratch run made while writing this chapter (the same frame with DSCP 46, decoded by the same tshark 4.2.2; not one of the recorded lab runs) printed "Differentiated Services Field: 0xba (DSCP: EF PHB, ECN: ECT(0))" and "Expedited Forwarding (46)". Also the IP header checksum changes, because the second byte changed.
    9. 



Forensic lab answer key


Observation. node-a (10.0.0.1): PSN 100–105 in order, ACK for 105 (frame 16). node-c (10.0.0.3): 200–205 in order, ACK for 205 (frame 18). node-d (10.0.0.4): 300, 301, then 304 (frame 13) — 302 and 303 never reached the port the first time. Frame 14 is from node-b to node-d: an Acknowledge with AETH syndrome 96 = 0x60, which tshark decodes as "Nak", error code "PSN Sequence Error", with PSN 302: "I expected 302" R4. node-d's 305 still arrives (frame 19, discarded by the receiver after the gap), then node-d resends 302, 303, 304, 305 (frames 20–23) and gets an ACK for 305 (frame 24). 304 and 305 were sent twice although they had reached the receiver: go-back-N.


Hypotheses. (1) node-d's link or cable is faulty: possible, but a faulty link would not drop exactly the packets in the middle of the incast burst only; the capture alone cannot rule it out, the switch's error counters could. (2) node-d's traffic is in a different class: the DSCP column shows 0 for every node-d packet and 26 for node-a and node-c, and the configuration says "DSCP 26 -> class 3 lossless; all other DSCP -> class 0 (lossy, PFC off)". During incast, the lossless class pauses senders, while the lossy class drops. (3) node-d's path MTU differs: ruled out, the report shows 1024 for all three. (4) The receiver is slow for node-d's QP: ruled out, the receiver ACKs and NAKs promptly.


Root cause. node-d's queue pairs use traffic class (DSCP) 0, not 26, so its RDMA packets go to the lossy class and are dropped under incast; go-back-N then resends everything after each drop.


Fix and check. Set node-d's RoCE traffic class to DSCP 26 (per QP through the address vector's traffic class, or the adapter's default for RDMA traffic; the exact setting is in the adapter documentation, D6). Prevention: a fleet audit that compares each host's RoCE settings with the switch's classification, and an alert on NAK or retransmission counters per host.


How the evidence was produced. incast.cpp is a scripted replay, not a simulation: it writes the configuration report and a capture in which, for senders whose DSCP is not the lossless one, the 3rd and 4th packets of the burst are left out ("dropped before the mirror port"); the receiver's NAK and the go-back-N resend follow the rules assumed in Layer 2. All frames are built by packet.hpp and decoded by tshark 4.2.2; nothing was sent on a network.
