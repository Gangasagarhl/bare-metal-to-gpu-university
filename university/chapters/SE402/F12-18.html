Story hook

Every month the building crew holds a fire drill. This month the alarm sounded and the crew walked out, and only half of them arrived at the meeting point by the gate. The others gathered at the old meeting point behind the house. Two carpenters did not come out at all for four minutes.

At the first meeting afterwards, someone asked, "Who forgot where the meeting point is?" Faces closed. The carpenters said nothing.

Then Amina, the site manager, asked a different question: "What made it possible for sensible people to go to the wrong place?" The answers came quickly. The meeting-point sign had been moved when the scaffolding went up, and nobody announced it. The alarm could not be heard over the saw, and the carpenters wore ear protection. Nobody had a list to count heads.

None of those answers names a careless person, and every one of them can be fixed. Next month the sign has an arrow and an announcement, the alarm has a flashing light, and there is a headcount list at the gate. That is a blameless post-mortem.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why post-mortems are blameless, and what blamelessness does and does not mean.
  • Build an incident timeline in which every line has a source, from alerts, logs, traces, metrics and chat.
  • Separate trigger, root cause and contributing factors, and find the defences that failed rather than the person who acted last.
  • Write action items that change the system, each typed (prevent, detect, mitigate, process), owned and dated.
  • Review a post-mortem draft for blame, hindsight and missing evidence, with a checker and with judgement.
  • Write the full post-mortem of an incident from its evidence pack.
Jargon box
Post-mortem (incident review)
Simple words: a written story of an incident: what happened, why, and what will change, written after it is over. Analogy: the meeting after the fire drill went wrong. Precise: a document, and the review meeting around it, that records an incident's impact, timeline, causes and contributing factors, detection and response, and action items with owners; written for every incident above an agreed severity and shared so that others can learn from it. D1
Blameless post-mortem
Simple words: a post-mortem that asks what let the problem happen, not who to punish. Analogy: "what made it possible for sensible people to go to the wrong place?" Precise: a post-mortem written on the assumption that every person acted reasonably given what they knew, the tools they had and the pressure they were under; it describes actions and the conditions that shaped them, and directs its fixes at those conditions. Blameless does not mean without responsibility: owners are named for the fixes. D1 D2
Trigger
Simple words: the event that set the problem off. Analogy: the alarm going off is the trigger of the drill; the moved sign is why it went wrong. Precise: the event or change that started the incident (a deploy, a scheduled job, a traffic peak); it activates latent weaknesses but is rarely the only cause, and removing it alone usually leaves the weakness in place. D1
Hindsight bias
Simple words: after you know how a story ended, the warning signs look obvious, although they were not obvious at the time. Analogy: after the drill, everybody "knew" the sign had moved. Precise: the tendency to judge past decisions with information that was only available afterwards; post-mortems counter it by reconstructing what each person could see at each moment. D2
Counterfactual
Simple words: a sentence about what did not happen: "if she had…", "he should have…". Analogy: "the carpenters should have taken off their ear protection". Precise: a statement about an alternative past; in a post-mortem it describes a world that did not exist and hides why the real actions made sense at the time; the useful version asks what would have made the better action easy or automatic. D2
Action item (post-mortem)
Simple words: one concrete change, with a person who owns it and a date. Analogy: "put an arrow on the sign and a light on the alarm by next Monday, Juma". Precise: a tracked work item arising from a post-mortem, typed by what it does (prevent the trigger or weakness, detect it sooner, mitigate its impact, or change a process), with an owner, a due date and a priority; "be more careful" is not an action item. D1
Swiss cheese model
Simple words: a picture of many safety layers, each with holes; an accident happens when the holes line up. Analogy: the sign, the alarm and the headcount were three layers; this month each had a hole in the same place. Precise: a model of accident causation in which defences (technical, procedural, organisational) are imperfect barriers with latent and active failures; an incident passes through when weaknesses in several layers coincide, so prevention strengthens layers rather than searching for one culprit. D3
Five whys
Simple words: asking "why?" again and again to get from the symptom to deeper causes. Analogy: why did people go to the wrong place? Why was the sign moved? Why was there no announcement? Precise: an iterative questioning technique for causal analysis; useful to get past the first answer, but a single chain of whys forces one cause per level and tends to stop at a person, so post-mortems use it as a prompt, not as the method. D4
Near miss
Simple words: something that almost went wrong, and did not, this time. Analogy: the carpenters came out after four minutes; nobody was hurt. Precise: an event, or a period inside an incident, in which a further failure would have caused (more) harm; recorded under "where we got lucky", because it shows a weakness at no cost. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

The fire drill that went wrong. Mapping: the meeting afterwards = the post-mortem; "what made it possible?" instead of "who forgot?" = blamelessness; the alarm going off = the trigger; the moved sign, the quiet alarm and the missing headcount list = contributing factors in three layers of defence; "the carpenters should have…" = a counterfactual; "everybody knew the sign had moved" (after the drill) = hindsight; "arrow on the sign by Monday, Juma" = an action item; the carpenters coming out after four minutes = a near miss.

After something goes wrong, the team writes down what happened, minute by minute, and checks each line against a record. Then it asks why each step made sense to the person who did it. People rarely do silly things on purpose; they do sensible things with the information they have.

If you punish the person who acted last, two things happen. Next time, people hide their mistakes, so the team learns nothing. And the trap that caught them is still there for the next person.

So the fixes change the trap: a better sign, a louder alarm, a list at the gate. Each fix gets a name and a date, and someone checks later that it was done.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Analogy

Amina's meeting had a structure: what happened, in order; what it cost; why each layer let it through; what went well; what will change, by whom, by when.

When to write one, and who

Teams decide in advance which incidents need a post-mortem: for example every incident above a severity level (the SEV1 and SEV2 of F12-16), any data loss, any incident in which the on-call needed outside help, and any near miss that someone asks about. D1 The incident commander names the owner at resolution; the owner is often someone who took part, not necessarily the person whose system failed. A draft within days keeps memories and logs fresh. The curriculum's log template asks for the same core for every working session ("what failed; root cause; fix"), so the habit is not new. D6

A timeline with sources

The timeline is the backbone. Each line has a time, what was observed or done, and the record it comes from: [alerts], [metrics], [log kv-1], [trace], [chat], [change log]. F9-70 built such timelines from robot logs and warned about clocks that disagree; the same rules apply. D7 A line without a source is a memory; memories after an incident are shaped by how it ended (hindsight bias). D2 The scribe's live record (F12-16) makes this step quick; without it, the owner reconstructs the timeline from chat timestamps and logs, slowly.

Trigger, causes and contributing factors

The question "what is the root cause?" has a tempting one-word answer, and it is usually incomplete. A more useful structure separates the trigger (what started it), the latent weaknesses it activated, and the defences that should have stopped or shortened it and why they did not. D1 D3 In the forensic incident: trigger, the nightly compaction; latent weaknesses, compaction needs space equal to what it rewrites and leaves its temporary file when it fails, and a full leader keeps leading; failed defences, the capacity review (absent for the retention change), the disk alert (a daytime ticket), the failover (heartbeats are not health) and the runbook (no disk step). R1 F10-38 used the same distinction between root cause and contributing factors for flight logs.

Writing without blame

Blameless writing is a technique, not a mood. Describe what a person did and what they could see at that moment ("at 02:21 the page was acknowledged; the pager log does not show why it took seven minutes"); never what they "should have" done. Replace "X forgot" by the condition that made forgetting possible ("the disk ticket goes to a queue read in business hours"). Replace "human error" by the question of what made the action reasonable at the time. D2 Listing 2 catches the words; a reviewer must catch the framing, because a draft can blame without a single flagged word ("Rui was slow to respond" passes the checker, R2).

Action items that change the system

Every action item has a type, an owner, a due date and a priority. The type says what kind of protection it adds: prevent (the trigger or weakness cannot recur), detect (we notice sooner), mitigate (the impact is smaller or shorter), process (how people work: runbooks, reviews, rotations). D1 A good set has at least one item per failed layer of Figure 1. Items that ask people to try harder ("be more careful", "set a louder alarm") are not system changes; they are the post-mortem's version of blame.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Analogy

A site manager who punishes the crew after every bad drill soon has perfect drill reports and no information. The reports are only as honest as the meeting is safe.

Why blamelessness works

The argument is about information. An organisation learns from incidents only what people tell it, and people tell it what is safe to tell. If the person closest to the failure expects punishment, the most valuable details (what they saw, what they thought, which tool misled them) stay hidden, and the post-mortem fixes a story instead of the system. D1 D2 A second argument is about replacement: the person who acted last is replaceable by anyone else in the same seat, with the same tools, the same alerts and the same pressure; the conditions stay. Blameless is not the same as unaccountable. Deliberate sabotage or repeated refusal to follow agreed safety rules is a management matter, handled outside the post-mortem; the post-mortem itself still asks what the system allowed. D2

Five whys, and its limits

A five-whys chain for the forensic incident: writes failed (why?) because the leader's disk was full (why?) because the compaction's temporary file filled it (why?) because compaction rewrites before deleting and the baseline was 78 % (why?) because a retention change raised the baseline (why?) because configuration changes have no capacity review. Useful: it got past "the disk was full". D4 Limited: the single chain dropped the detection layer (why did a disk at 96 % not wake anyone?) and the failover layer (why did a full leader keep leading?), each of which would have shortened the outage on its own. Use the whys to go deep on each layer of Figure 1, not to select one layer.

The pattern behind this incident: alive but useless

The most reusable lesson of the forensic incident is general: the leader passed the check that decides leadership (heartbeats) while failing at its actual work (appending to its log). Raft elects a new leader when followers stop hearing heartbeats; a leader that can send heartbeats but cannot persist entries is not removed by that mechanism. D5 R1 Systems engineering literature calls such partial failures, visible to users but not to the failure detector, "gray failures" (see the unverified box). The mitigation action item in the reference post-mortem ("the leader steps down when its WAL cannot append for 30 s") closes exactly this gap; its 30 s is a design choice to be tested in a game day, not a known-good value.

The review meeting and the follow-through

  • Who attends. The responders, the owners of the systems involved, and someone experienced in running reviews who did not take part; the facilitator's job is to stop blame and hindsight in the room, the way Listing 2 stops them on the page.
  • What it decides. Whether the causal analysis covers every failed layer; whether every action item is a system change with an owner; which items are urgent. It does not decide who was wrong.
  • Follow-through. Action items enter the normal work tracker and are reviewed until closed; a post-mortem whose items are never done teaches the team that post-mortems are theatre. D1
  • Sharing. Post-mortems are published widely inside the organisation and searchable, so that other teams find "our leader kept leading with a full disk" before it happens to them. F12-19 extends this to public reports from other organisations. D1

How the hardware actually does it

A full disk fails in a specific place. A log-structured store appends every write to its write-ahead log and makes it durable (on Linux with a call such as fsync) before acknowledging; when the file system cannot allocate another block, the append or the flush returns an error, which POSIX names ENOSPC, "no space left on device". D8 Nothing else on the machine needs to fail: the CPU, memory and network interface keep working, so the process keeps answering heartbeats and reads. That is the hardware reason for the gray failure above: the failure detector tests the network path, the user's write needs the storage path. A compaction that writes the new copy before deleting the old needs, at its peak, room for both; on a disk at 78 % that leaves 22 % of headroom for rewriting, which this model's compaction exceeded at 02:14. R1 Real file systems also reserve space and report usage in ways that differ between file systems; record which one your service uses, and measure its behaviour near full on a disposable disk image (curriculum safety rules for disks). D6

Worked example

Task. From the incident pack (R1), compute the impact and the response intervals for the post-mortem's Impact and Detection sections.

  1. Failed writes. The metrics show 600 write errors per minute from 02:14 to 02:34 inclusive: 02:34 − 02:14 + 1 = 21 minutes. 21 × 600 = 12,600 failed writes. Reads: 0 errors in every row.
  2. Detection. First signal: the ticket at 02:11 (disk above 95 %), 3 minutes before the outage, routed to a business-hours queue. First page: 02:14, in the first failing minute.
  3. Acknowledgement. 02:14 → 02:21: 7 minutes.
  4. Mitigation. Writes succeed from 02:35, 21 minutes after the page; the restart that achieved it was at 02:34, 8 minutes after the SEV2 declaration at 02:26.
  5. Full redundancy. kv-1 rejected appends from 02:37 (rejoin) until the temporary file was deleted at 03:01 and it caught up (log 03:01:48): about 24 minutes on 2 of 3 replicas. That interval is the post-mortem's "where we got lucky".
  6. Budget. If the service has the 99.9 % write SLO of F12-16 at 600 writes per minute, the 30-day budget is 30 × 24 × 60 × 600 × 0.001 = 25,920 failed writes; this incident used 12,600 / 25,920 = 48.6 % of it.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A fire drill is planned; an incident is not. After a drill, everyone knows exactly what the test was and when it started. After an incident, the start itself is a finding: the compaction began at 02:00, the first signal was at 02:11 and the outage at 02:14, and nobody knew any of that at the time.
    • 

  • The drill's evidence is people's memory; an incident's evidence is records. Amina's meeting relied on what people remembered. A software post-mortem has logs, metrics, traces and chat with timestamps, and should prefer them to memory, line by line. It also has to deal with records that disagree, which memories rarely admit.
    • 

  • A drill harms nobody; incidents can. Real users lost 12,600 writes in the model incident. Blamelessness towards the responders does not mean indifference towards the users; the impact section states the harm plainly, and some incidents also have contractual or legal consequences that the post-mortem does not decide.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F12-16 ran the incident with roles and a scribe; F12-17 found causes from evidence; F9-70 and F10-38 built timelines and separated root causes from contributing factors in robot and flight logs. What carries over: sourced timelines, hypotheses tested against evidence, normalised impact numbers, and the scribe's record as the first draft R1. Summary


    

  • A post-mortem records impact, a sourced timeline, causes and contributing factors, detection, response, luck, and action items.
    • 

  • Blameless means describing what people saw and did and fixing the conditions; owners are still named for the fixes.
    • 

  • Separate trigger, latent weaknesses and failed defences; the Swiss cheese picture shows several layers with aligned holes.
    • 

  • Five whys gets past the first answer but tends to follow one chain; map every layer.
    • 

  • Action items are typed (prevent, detect, mitigate, process), owned, dated and tracked; "be more careful" is not one.
    • 

  • A checker finds missing sections, unsourced lines and blame words (20 in the first draft, R2); only a reviewer finds blame without those words.
    • 

  • The kv incident's general lesson: a leader can be alive and useless; health checks must test the work.
