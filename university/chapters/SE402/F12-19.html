Story hook

In another town, the roof of a sports hall sagged after a week of heavy snow. Nobody was hurt; the hall had been closed for the holidays. The town council published the inspector's report: forty pages, with drawings, measurements and a list of findings.

Juma, the foreman, prints it and reads it with his crew at lunch. Someone has heard at the café that "the builder used cheap wood". Juma shakes his head: "We talk about what the report says, with the page number. The café is not a source." The report says something less exciting and more useful: a bracket was rated for a load that assumed the old snow tables, and the drawings did not say which tables they used.

Then Juma asks the only question that matters for his crew: "Do we build roofs like that?" They check their own drawings. Their brackets are the same type. Their drawings do not name the snow tables either.

The hall in the other town paid for that lesson. Juma's crew learned it for the price of a lunch break. This chapter is about reading other people's failures that carefully, and that honestly.

What you will be able to do

  • Find and rank sources about a public failure: the investigating body's own report first, then peer-reviewed analyses; never retellings alone.
  • Read an investigation report: separate recorded facts, analysis, findings and recommendations, and note the report's scope and limits.
  • Reproduce the mechanism of a failure class in a small program you can run, without claiming it is the original system.
  • Transfer a lesson: audit your own code and projects for the same failure class, and turn the result into checks.
  • Diagnose a time-keeping fault from logs that disagree, using the failure classes of this chapter.
Jargon box
Investigation report (primary source)
Simple words: the official report written by the people who investigated a failure. Analogy: the inspector's forty pages, not the café story. Precise: the document published by the body that investigated an accident or failure (an inquiry board, a safety agency, an audit office, the organisation's own published post-mortem); for the facts of that event it is the primary source, ranked above papers about it, which rank above news and retellings (guide tiers 1, 5 and 6 respectively). D7
Finding and recommendation
Simple words: what the investigators concluded, and what they said should change. Analogy: "the bracket was rated for the old snow tables" and "drawings must name the tables they use". Precise: a finding is a conclusion the report draws from its evidence (often numbered and classified as cause or contributing factor); a recommendation is an action the report proposes to an addressee; quoting a report means quoting its findings and recommendations as findings and recommendations, with their numbers. D7
Failure class
Simple words: a kind of mistake that keeps happening in different systems. Analogy: "load rated for old tables" can happen to roofs, floors and bridges. Precise: a mechanism of failure abstracted from particular incidents (a numeric conversion out of range, a unit mismatch at an interface, time accumulated in a finite representation, a race between concurrent activities) for which detection and prevention techniques can be stated independently of any one system. R1 R3 R4
Narrowing conversion
Simple words: putting a number into a smaller box, where big values do not fit. Analogy: pouring a large bucket into a small jug. Precise: a conversion to a type that cannot represent all values of the source type; in C++, converting a floating value whose integer part does not fit the destination integer type is undefined behaviour, and converting an integer to a narrower signed integer type is, since C++20, defined as reduction modulo 2N. D5 R1
Strong type (unit-carrying type)
Simple words: a type that says what a number means, so mixing meanings stops the build. Analogy: a measuring tape labelled "centimetres only". Precise: a distinct type wrapping a value (for example struct NewtonSeconds { double value; };) so that the compiler rejects passing one quantity or unit where another is expected; conversions become explicit functions. R4
Accumulated rounding error
Simple words: a tiny error, repeated millions of times, that grows into a large one. Analogy: a ruler one hair too short, used to measure a long road. Precise: the error that builds up when a value that is not exactly representable (such as 0.1 in binary) is added or stored repeatedly; it grows with the number of operations (for example with uptime) and depends on the representation's precision. D6 R3
Transfer analysis ("could it happen here?")
Simple words: checking your own work for the same kind of mistake someone else made. Analogy: "do we build roofs like that?" Precise: the step that turns a documented failure into local action: name the failure class, search your systems for it with a stated method (a compiler warning, a code search, a test), record what was found, and add a check that keeps it from coming back. D8

This chapter also uses post-mortem, root cause and contributing factor and Swiss cheese model from F12-18.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

The sagging roof in another town. Mapping: the inspector's report = the investigation report (primary source); the café story = a retelling, which points you to the report but is not evidence; "the bracket was rated for the old snow tables" = a finding; "drawings must name the tables" = a recommendation; "load rated for old tables" as something that could happen to any roof = a failure class; "do we build roofs like that?" = transfer analysis.

Other people's failures are the cheapest lessons there are, because someone else already paid for them. But only if you learn what really happened. Stories change each time they are retold; reports with evidence do not.

So you read the report itself. You note what the investigators measured, what they concluded and what they recommended, and you write down the page. Then, separately, you ask: does my system have the same kind of weakness?

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Analogy

Juma's rule at lunch had two halves: talk only about what the report says, with the page; then ask about your own roof.

Sourced only: ranking what you read

The guide's source tiers apply to failures as they apply to hardware. For the facts of a particular event, the primary source is the investigating body's own published report; peer-reviewed papers that analyse it come next; books that teach from it are useful for concepts; news articles, blog posts, slides and forum threads are pointers, never evidence. D7 Famous failures are retold so often that the retellings drift: numbers are rounded, causes simplified, a "bug" becomes a single line of code. The rule for this course is the same as for every chapter of this university: no claim about a real failure without a citation to the report, with its section or page, from a copy you have read.

This build had no access to any of those reports. That is why every statement about a real event in this chapter sits inside an unverified box, and why the chapter's evidence is limited to two things that are true without the reports: the arithmetic and language rules of each failure class, shown by programs run in this build, and the method itself.

Reading a report

Investigation reports differ in format, but most contain the parts of Figure 1. D7 Read them in this order:

  1. Scope and method. Who investigated, with what access (wreckage, logs, source code, interviews), under what mandate. A report can only conclude what its access allowed.
  2. Findings and recommendations. Numbered; usually distinguishing causes from contributing factors. Copy them with their numbers.
  3. Recorded facts. The sequence of events and the data behind each finding. Check that each finding you will cite has facts behind it.
  4. What is not said. Questions the report leaves open, or answers with "probably". Do not close them for the authors.

Then write your own failure-library entry, in your own words: the citation (title, body, version or date as printed, sections), the finding in one sentence with its number, the failure class, the reproduction (if any), the transfer check, and the date you read it.

Four failure classes you can run

1. Numeric conversion out of range. A value that always fitted in an old context does not fit in a new one. Listing 1 converts doubles to 16-bit integers three ways. Wrapping turns 40,000 into −25,536 and 70,000 into 4,464; saturating clamps to 32,767; checked returns "out of range" for the caller to handle. R1 Listing 2 does the conversion directly, which C++ leaves undefined for out-of-range values. Built with this course's default flags (AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer), it printed −25,536 with no diagnostic; built with -fsanitize=float-cast-overflow added, the same program reported "runtime error: 40000 is outside the range of representable values of type 'short int'". D5 R2 The lesson for your build: know which checks your flags actually include.

2. Units at an interface. Two teams agree on a number and disagree on its unit. Listing 4: a producer reports 100 meaning pound-force seconds; a consumer reads 100 as newton seconds and computes a velocity change of 2.000 m/s instead of 8.896 m/s, a factor of 4.4482. R4 The factor is not a measurement: one pound-force is defined as 0.45359237 kg times standard gravity, 9.80665 m/s², which is 4.4482216152605 N. D9 Listing 5 makes the unit part of the type, and the mistake becomes a compile error. R5 F12-06's contract tests are the run-time version of the same defence.

3. Time accumulated in a finite representation. 0.1 has no exact binary form. Listing 3 computes, exactly, what is lost per 0.1 s tick when 0.1 is truncated to k fractional bits, and multiplies it by the ticks in 1, 24 and 100 hours: with 23 bits, 0.000000095 s per tick becomes 0.343 s after 100 hours. Adding 0.1f to a float for 100 hours ends 12,975 s away from the true time; with double, 0.000012 s. R3 The error grows with uptime, which is why such faults appear only in systems that run a long time without restart, and why the fix is to count integer ticks.

4. Races and missing independent interlocks. Two activities share state without a correct protocol, and a software check replaces an independent safety mechanism. This chapter has no new listing for it: F12-10 reproduced a data race and caught it with ThreadSanitizer, and F9-69 built watchdogs, e-stops and safe states that do not depend on the software they protect.

Two cautions. First, a reproduction shows that a mechanism exists; it does not show that it is what happened in the original event. Label every reproduction "a model of the failure class, not of the system". Second, do not overfit: the lesson of a famous failure is rarely "never convert to 16 bits". It is usually about the layers around the mechanism (why reused code was not re-verified for its new context, why an interface had no stated unit, why a long-running mode was never tested), which is the Swiss cheese view of F12-18. D8

The reuse trap, in general form

Classes 1 and 3 share a pattern that deserves its own name in your library: an assumption that was true in the original context. A value range that the old system never exceeded, an operating time that was never longer than a day. The code was correct under the assumption; the assumption was not written next to the code; the context changed. The defence is to make assumptions executable: an assertion or a checked conversion at the point where the assumption is used, a test at the boundary, and a requirement written in the design document (F12-02) that a reviewer can compare with the new context.

Your own failures are documented failures too

Internal post-mortems (F12-18) are the documented failures closest to your work, and they have the same rules: cite the post-mortem, not the hallway version; separate its findings from your transfer. Many organisations publish some post-mortems; the same tier rules apply to them: the organisation's own published post-mortem is the primary source for that event. D8 When you share your own, remove what you may not share (personal data, security details) and keep what others can learn from.

How the hardware actually does it

Each failure class meets the hardware at a specific place. A conversion from floating point to a 16-bit integer is not one instruction on the x86-64 build machine: the compiler emits a conversion to a wider integer and then keeps the low 16 bits, which is why this build printed −25,536 (40,000 − 65,536) for the undefined conversion of Listing 2, the same value the defined wrapping path of Listing 1 gives. R1 R2 Another compiler, flag or processor may give something else, and an embedded processor may raise a hardware exception instead; that is what "undefined" protects the compiler's freedom to do. D5 A float has a fixed number of significand bits (IEEE 754 binary32), so the spacing between representable values doubles each time the value doubles: once a float counter of seconds is large, adding 0.1 is rounded to the nearest representable step, which is why Listing 3's float error grows irregularly (+1.2 s after 1 hour, −12,975 s after 100 hours). D6 R3 And a timer interrupt every 100 ms is an integer event: the hardware counts ticks exactly; only the conversion to seconds introduces the error, so counting ticks in an integer and converting once is exact.

Worked example

Task. Check the 23-bit row of Listing 3 by hand.

  1. 223 = 8,388,608. Then 0.1 × 223 = 838,860.8, so truncation keeps 838,860 / 223 and drops 0.8 / 223.
  2. The program's formula agrees: r = 223 mod 10 = 8, and the loss per tick is r / (10 × 223) = 8 / 83,886,080 = 0.000000095367… s.
  3. 100 hours at 10 ticks per second is 100 × 3,600 × 10 = 3,600,000 ticks.
  4. 3,600,000 × 0.000000095367 = 0.3433 s, matching "0.343323" in the output. R3
  5. For a feel of what 0.34 s means: anything moving at 100 m/s is about 34 m from where a clock with this error says it is. The speed is an example value, not a property of any real system.

Second check, Listing 1: 70,000 − 65,536 = 4,464 and −40,000 + 65,536 = 25,536, matching the wrapping column. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A roof's failure is visible and physical; a software failure's mechanism can be invisible in the report. An inspector can photograph a bent bracket. A report about software often describes the mechanism in words, without the code, so your reproduction is a model of what you understood, not of the original, and must be labelled that way.
    • 

  • Roofs of the same type behave alike; software of the same kind does not. Juma's brackets were the same type as the failed ones, so the lesson transferred directly. Two programs can share a failure class without sharing any code; the transfer needs a search method (warnings, code search, tests), not a parts list.
    • 

  • The inspector's report is final; failure reports can be revised and disputed. Investigations publish updates, errata and later analyses that disagree. Record the version you read, and treat a later disagreement as new evidence to read, not as a reason to pick the more convenient story.
    •

Forensic lab


Scenario. A building site has two gateways that stamp events from sensors. A security review notices that on the second day a door was logged as opening more than a quarter of an hour after the alarm it caused. Gateway B's vendor says the clock hardware "must be faulty". You get the evidence.


Evidence pack. Generated by uptime.cpp (a model; the gateways are fictional): an excerpt of gateway B's time-keeping code, six-hourly sync pulses stamped by both gateways over five days with B's own idea of its uptime, a reboot of B, and the door and alarm stamps.




Questions.


    

  1. Observation: describe how B − A changes with B's uptime, and what happens at the reboot. Plot it if you can.
    2. 

  2. Hypotheses: state the vendor's hypothesis (faulty oscillator) and at least one alternative. What does each predict for the pattern after the reboot?
    3. 

  3. Root cause: which failure class is this, and which line of evidence 1 causes it?
    4. 

  4. Fix and verification: propose the fix and a test that would have caught it before shipping.
    5. 

  5. Prevention and transfer: which documented failure in Figure 2 belongs to the same class, and what would you check in your own projects?
    6. 



Method hints. A hardware clock that runs fast or slow drifts at a roughly constant rate. Compare the pattern before and after the reboot pulse by pulse. Then reread Listing 3, part 2.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. uptime.cpp simulates five days of 100 ms ticks. Gateway A counts integer milliseconds; gateway B adds 0.1f to a float every tick and adds that to its boot time; B reboots at site time 302,400 s. At 30 h the door event is stamped by B and the alarm, 2 s later, by A. Output unedited. R6


Observation. B − A is −46.8 s at 6 h of uptime, then +70.8, +408.3, +745.8, +1,083.3, +1,420.8 s (36 h), then turns and falls to −8,025.2 s at about 78 h. After the reboot it is 0, and pulses 15–20 repeat pulses 1–6 exactly (−46.756, 70.848, … 1,420.844). At 30 h, B stamps the door at 109,083.344 s and A the alarm at 108,002.000 s: the door appears 1,081 s (about 18 minutes) after the alarm it caused.


Hypotheses. H1 (vendor): faulty oscillator; predicts a roughly constant drift rate, different after the reboot only by chance, and not an exact repetition. H2: arithmetic in B's time keeping; predicts a pattern that depends only on uptime and repeats exactly after every reboot. The exact repetition after the reboot, and the changes of direction, refute H1 and support H2.


Root cause. Accumulated rounding error: uptime_s += 0.1f in a float (evidence 1, line 2). As uptime grows, the float spacing grows, and each addition of 0.1 is rounded to a step that is too large or too small; Listing 3, part 2, shows the same effect.


Fix and verification. Count ticks in a 64-bit integer and compute seconds as ticks / 10 when stamping (or count milliseconds, like A). Test: run the tick function for at least the longest expected uptime (simulated, in under a second) and require |stamp − ticks / 10| below a stated bound; run it across a simulated reboot too.


Prevention and transfer. Same class as the third row of Figure 2 (the GAO report on the Patriot system, pending verification of the details). In your projects: search for every variable incremented by a fractional time or distance step (odometry, timers, integrators), and add long-run tests.


Wrong answers and why. "Network delay between the gateways": delay cannot produce 1,400 s, nor an exact repeat after reboot. "Clock hardware": see H1. "Reboot B daily": hides the symptom; the first six hours already show 47 s of error.
