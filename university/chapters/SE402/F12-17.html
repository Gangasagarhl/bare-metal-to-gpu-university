Story hook

The house is nearly finished when a brown stain appears on the living-room ceiling. Ines takes a saw: the stain is here, so the leak must be here. Oren stops her and asks three questions instead. When did the stain first appear? What changed upstairs that week? Does it get bigger when somebody uses the bathroom?

The stain appeared the day after the shower was fitted. So Oren runs the bath tap for ten minutes, alone: the stain stays the same. Then the shower, alone: within minutes the edge of the stain darkens. The leak is at the shower drain, two metres away from the stain. The water had been running along a beam before dripping through.

Ines's saw would have made a hole in the right room and the wrong place. Oren's method cost ten minutes more and found the leak on the first cut. Under pressure, with the owner asking when the ceiling will be fixed, the method is what keeps you from cutting holes. This chapter is about that method, for software.

What you will be able to do

  • Run the evidence loop under time pressure: observe, write down at least two hypotheses, predict, choose the cheapest test that tells them apart, conclude.
  • Find what changed, and compare good requests with bad ones to locate a fault, including when a confounding variable points at the wrong suspect.
  • Bisect a regression across versions with noisy measurements, and predict how many repetitions each step needs.
  • Normalise before concluding: tell a falling error count from a falling error ratio.
  • Coordinate investigation in an incident: read-only helpers in parallel, one pair of hands on production.
Jargon box
Hypothesis (debugging)
Simple words: a guess about the cause, written so that a test can prove it wrong. Analogy: "the leak is at the shower drain": run the shower alone and see. Precise: a statement about the cause of a symptom that predicts an observation not yet made; a useful hypothesis names a test whose result would differ if it were false. D1
Discriminating test
Simple words: a check whose answer is different depending on which guess is right. Analogy: running only the shower: the stain grows if the shower leaks, stays the same if the bath leaks. Precise: an observation or experiment chosen because the competing hypotheses predict different outcomes for it; a test that every hypothesis predicts the same way carries no information, however long it takes. D1
Change correlation ("what changed?")
Simple words: lining up the start of a problem with everything that changed shortly before it. Analogy: the stain appeared the day after the shower was fitted. Precise: comparing the onset time of a symptom with the change record (deployments, configuration, traffic, dependencies, scheduled jobs, certificates and other time-based events) to produce candidate causes; it ranks hypotheses, it does not prove them. D1
Cohort comparison (good versus bad)
Simple words: splitting requests into those that worked and those that failed and asking what the failed ones have in common. Analogy: which rooms have stains, and what is above each of them? Precise: grouping events by attribute values (node, version, region, size, customer) and comparing failure rates between groups, then within groups, to find the smallest description that covers the failures. R1
Confounding variable
Simple words: a hidden third thing that makes an innocent suspect look guilty. Analogy: stains appear under the new bathroom tiles, but the tiles are innocent: the new shower was fitted the same week. Precise: a variable associated with both a candidate cause and the outcome, producing an association between them without causation; controlled by comparing within groups that share the confounder's value. D4 R1
Bisection (regression search)
Simple words: finding which version broke something by testing the middle version, then the middle of the half that is still in doubt, and so on. Analogy: closing half the valves, then half of the remaining ones. Precise: binary search over an ordered history (commits, builds, configuration versions) between a known good and a known bad point; it needs about log2(n) tests and a reliable verdict at every step; git bisect automates the bookkeeping. D3 R2
USE method
Simple words: for every part of the machine, check how busy it is, whether work is queuing for it, and whether it reports errors. Analogy: for every pipe: how much flows, is water backing up, is anything dripping? Precise: a checklist method for performance and resource problems: for each resource (CPUs, memory, disks, network interfaces, locks, thread pools), check utilisation, saturation and errors. D2
Normalised rate
Simple words: "how many out of how many", instead of "how many". Analogy: drops per litre of water used, not drops per hour, because nobody showers at night. Precise: a count divided by its denominator over the same window (errors per request, not errors per minute), so that changes in load do not look like changes in health; the SLI of F5-42 is a normalised rate. R3

This chapter also uses trace and span, structured log and percentile from F5-41, incident commander, operations lead and mitigation from F12-16, and the root cause idea from F0-08.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

The water stain. Mapping: the stain = the symptom you can see; the leak = the cause; "when did it start, what was fitted that week?" = what changed; running one tap at a time = a discriminating test; looking at which rooms have stains and what is above each = comparing good with bad; water running along a beam = the symptom appearing far from the cause; the new tiles that were fitted the same week as the shower = a confounder.

When something breaks and everybody is waiting, the strongest feeling is "do something now". The trouble is that a guess feels exactly like knowledge until it turns out wrong. So good debuggers slow down for one minute and write their guesses down.

Then they look for a test that would come out differently if a guess were wrong. Not a test that would come out the same either way: that one only wastes time. Each test crosses out at least one guess.

Three questions find most problems quickly. What changed just before it started? What do the broken cases have in common that the working cases do not? Is it really getting better, or are there just fewer cases right now?

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Analogy

Oren never cut the ceiling until one guess was left and it explained the whole stain. Each tap he ran was chosen because it would prove one guess wrong.

The loop, written down

The curriculum's runbook already contains the rule in one line: gather evidence first, state a hypothesis before changing code, record the root cause. D5 Under pressure the loop of Figure 1 needs to be written, not only thought, because the incident channel is where the incident commander and the helpers see it. A minimal entry looks like this: "Observed: 15 % of requests failing since 16:20, all three nodes report errors. H1: build b512 (n2, 16:00). H2: client 7.1 (rollout since 09:00). Test: failure rate by node within each client version (read-only, 2 minutes). Prediction: H1 says only n2 fails for both clients; H2 says 7.1 fails on all nodes." D1 R1

What changed?

Most production failures follow a change, so the change record is the first evidence to read: deployments, configuration pushes, feature flags, schema migrations, traffic shifts, dependency releases, and time-based events (certificates, scheduled jobs, calendar boundaries). D1 In Listing 1's scenario there are two candidates: the client library 7.1 rollout from 09:00 and server build b512 on n2 from 16:00, with the alert at 16:20. R1 Change correlation ranks the candidates; it does not decide between them. "It started after the deploy" is also true of everything else that started after the deploy.

Compare good with bad, then split again

Listing 1 holds 6,000 request records with four attributes each. Section 2 of its output splits by one attribute at a time and finds two strong suspects: node n2 (41.7 % failed, "lift" 2.8 times the overall rate) and client 7.1 (25.3 %, lift 1.7). Payloads above 8 KiB also fail often (36.2 %). Region shows nothing (14.8 % in both). R1

The decisive move is to split one suspect by the other. Within client 7.0, only n2 fails (21.4 % against 0.1 %); within client 7.1, again only n2 fails (72.4 % against 0.2 % or less). So the node matters for both clients. Within n2, every request above 8 KiB failed, for both clients, and almost none below. The description "n2 and payload above 8 KiB" covers 882 of 888 failures (99.3 %); the remaining 6 are the model's background rate of 0.2 %. R1

Client 7.1 was a confounder: it sends large payloads more often (70 % against 20 %, line 39 of Listing 1), so it is over-represented among the failures without causing any. R1 An engineer who rolled back the client would have spent an hour and changed nothing, which is the software version of cutting the ceiling under the stain.

Bisection, and why noise breaks it

When the change record does not give a suspect, or gives fifty, bisection does. Between a known good build and a known bad one, test the middle; the half that still contains the change halves at every step; 64 builds need 6 tests. D3 The catch: every verdict must be right. One wrong verdict sends the search into a half that does not contain the change, and the search still ends confidently on some build: the wrong one.

Listing 2 bisects a latency regression (20 ms to 23 ms at build 41) with measurements that vary by up to ±10 %. With one measurement per step it named the right build in 374 of 1,000 trials; with the median of three, 653; with the median of seven, 912. R2 Trial 0 shows the failure mode: build 39 measured 21.69 ms once, was called bad, and the search ended on build 39. With seven measurements its median was 21.28 ms, it was called good, and the search ended on build 41. R2 Measuring seven times costs seven times as long (42 measurements instead of 6); under pressure that feels expensive, and an answer that is wrong six times in ten is more expensive.

Count or ratio?

An error count falls when traffic falls, whatever the health of the service. In the forensic lab of this chapter, a responder restarted two instances and saw errors fall from about 210 to about 180 per minute; the ratio stayed at 8.0 % before and after. R3 Always ask the dashboard for the denominator; the SLI of F5-42 is a ratio for this reason.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Analogy

On a big site with a flooded basement, the foreman does not let five people open five valves. He sends five people to five places to look, and one person to the valve.

Parallel investigation, serial change

In an incident, the operations lead from F12-16 owns the investigation as well as the changes. Investigation parallelises well when it is read-only: one helper reads traces of failed requests, one reads the change record, one checks resources with the USE method, one queries the cohort split. Each reports a finding, with its evidence, to the ops lead, who keeps the list of hypotheses current and proposes actions to the IC. Changes stay serial (F12-16, R2 of that chapter). D1 The useful division of labour is by hypothesis, not by "everyone look at everything".

Systematic sweeps when nothing stands out

When the change record is quiet and the cohort split shows nothing, a checklist beats inspiration. The USE method walks every resource and asks three questions: utilisation (how busy), saturation (is work queuing), errors (does it report failures). D2 Its value under pressure is that it is finite: when the list is done, you have either a finding or a documented absence of one, and the next hypothesis is about something that is not a resource (a logic bug, a dependency, a data change). For a request path, the equivalent sweep is to follow one failed request end to end through its trace (F5-41) and note where its time went and where the error was first raised.

How sure is sure enough?

Every verdict in a debugging session is a measurement with an error rate, and the session's conclusion is only as good as the product of its verdicts. Listing 3 makes that quantitative for the bisection: it computes the probability that one measurement misjudges a good build (0.1249) or a bad build (0.1739) from the noise model, the probability that a median of k is wrong, and the predicted success of the six-step search. R4 The predictions (378, 682 and 922 right in 1,000) are close to the simulation's 374, 653 and 912; with 1,000 trials, a difference of about 15 is one standard deviation of the count, so the gap for k = 3 is large but not alarming. R2 R4

Three practical rules follow. Measure the noise before bisecting (repeat one known-good measurement several times). Choose k from the required confidence, not from patience. And confirm the answer at the end: measure the named build and its predecessor many times; if the difference is not clear, the bisection is not finished. The same reasoning applies to flaky tests (F12-10): a single failing run is a weak verdict. D4

Traps that pressure makes worse

  • The first plausible story. The first hypothesis that fits some of the evidence gets defended instead of tested. Countermeasure: always write a second hypothesis, and choose tests that could refute the favourite. D1
  • Fixing the symptom's location. The stain is not the leak; the node that logs the error is not always the node that causes it (a follower forwarding to a broken leader logs errors too, F12-18).
  • Coincidence after an action. Something changed after your action; something else also changed (traffic, a dependency). Without a control, "after" is not "because" (forensic lab).
  • Destroying evidence. A restart clears the state that would have explained the fault. Capture first (logs, a core dump, the configuration, a trace sample), then mitigate.
  • Changing two things at once. If the system recovers, you do not know which change did it; if it gets worse, you do not know which to undo.

How the hardware actually does it

The noise in Listing 2 is a model, but every source of it on a real machine is physical. Modern CPUs change their clock frequency with temperature and load; caches and branch predictors start cold after a restart and warm up with use; interrupts, other processes and, in a cloud, other tenants on the same host take time at moments you do not control; storage and networks queue. D2 That is why the same build gives different latencies on different runs, and why SE401's benchmarking rules (fixed machine, warm-up, many repetitions, the median, the spread) apply to bisection too. The USE method is the hardware's own checklist: each resource it names (CPU, memory, disk, network interface, bus) has a utilisation that can approach its limit, a queue in front of it, and error counters in the device or driver. D2 Never compare latencies measured on two different machines during a bisection; if you must, measure both builds on both.

Worked example

Task. Predict, before running Listing 2, how often a single-measurement bisection finds build 41.

  1. The path. From good = 0 and bad = 63 the midpoints are 31 (good), 47 (bad), 39 (good), 43 (bad), 41 (bad), 40 (good). To end at 41, all six verdicts must be right: three on good builds, three on bad ones. One wrong verdict moves the culprit out of the search interval.
  2. One good build misjudged. Its true p99 is 20 ms; a measurement is 20 × (1 + n) with n one of 2,001 equally likely values from −0.1 to +0.1. It is called bad if 20 × (1 + n) > 21.5, that is n > 0.075: 250 of the 2,001 values, probability 0.1249.
  3. One bad build misjudged. True 23 ms; called good if 23 × (1 + n) ≤ 21.5, that is n ≤ −0.0652: 348 of 2,001 values, probability 0.1739.
  4. All six right. (1 − 0.1249)3 × (1 − 0.1739)3 = 0.6701 × 0.5638 = 0.3778, so about 378 of 1,000 trials.
  5. Median of seven. The median is wrong only if at least 4 of the 7 measurements are wrong. For the good build: 35 × 0.12494 × 0.87513 + (smaller terms for 5, 6, 7) ≈ 0.0062; Listing 3 computes all terms: 0.0062 (good) and 0.0205 (bad), and 922 right in 1,000.

Listing 3 printed "predicted right in 377.7 of 1000" for k = 1 and 922.3 for k = 7; the simulation found 374 and 912. R2 R4

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A tap can be run alone; a production service cannot be stopped to isolate one path. Oren could turn everything else off. Engineers usually test on live traffic, or on a copy that differs from production in ways that matter (data, load, timing). Cohort comparison exists because you often cannot isolate: you split the traffic you have instead.
    • 

  • Water always flows downhill; software failures can move in any direction. In F12-18 the follower forwards writes to a broken leader and reports the error itself: the symptom appears upstream of the cause, and the logs of the healthy node are full of errors.
    • 

  • A stain stays where it is; software evidence disappears. Logs rotate, metrics are downsampled, a restart clears memory. On the building site you can look at the stain tomorrow; in software you must capture evidence before you act.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F12-16 gave the incident its roles: one decider, one pair of hands. F12-10 taught you that a single failing run is weak evidence; F5-41 gave you logs, metrics and traces. What carries over: mitigation first; one change at a time; measurement discipline and medians from MA202 and SE401 R2. What changes: most dangerous first: you now draw conclusions in minutes from data you did not design, where confounders, noise and falling traffic all produce convincing false stories (R1, R2, R3); and every verdict has an error rate you can calculate. What comes next: F12-18 turns the evidence of an incident into a blameless post-mortem: a timeline with sources, causes and contributing factors, and action items that change the system rather than the people.

Forensic lab


Scenario. The evening on-call engineer, Lena, closed an incident with the note "Root cause: instances needed a restart after 41 days of uptime." The next morning a teammate, Omar, is not convinced. You get the evidence and must decide whether the restart helped.


Evidence pack. Generated by restart.cpp (a model) with the chat of the evening (restart.in, the scenario script) merged in: per-minute requests, errors, error ratio, errors per instance, the top error message, three sampled log lines at 19:12 and 19:33, and the chat.




Questions.


    

  1. Observation: what happened to the error count, the error ratio and the traffic between 19:05 and 19:50? What do the log lines say?
    2. 

  2. Hypotheses: state Lena's hypothesis and at least one alternative. What does each predict for the error ratio after 19:20?
    3. 

  3. Conclusion: did the restart help, hurt, or neither? Give the minutes and numbers that prove it.
    4. 

  4. Root cause and fix: what was the actual cause, and what should the on-call engineer have done at 19:14?
    5. 

  5. Prevention: change one dashboard, one runbook line and one habit so that this does not happen again.
    6. 



Method hints. Normalise first. Then look at the minute of the action itself. Then read the error messages: where does the error come from? Finally, read the last chat line.

Summary


    

  • Under pressure, write hypotheses and predictions down; choose tests that tell hypotheses apart.
    • 

  • "What changed?" ranks suspects; it does not convict them.
    • 

  • Compare failed with working requests, then split each suspect by the others: client 7.1 looked guilty and was a confounder; "n2 and above 8 KiB" covered 99.3 % of failures (R1).
    • 

  • Bisection is fast only if every verdict is right: one measurement per step found the right build 374 times in 1,000, the median of seven 912 times (R2), close to the prediction (R4).
    • 

  • Use ratios, not counts: errors fell after a restart because traffic fell; the ratio stayed at 8 % (R3).
    • 

  • Investigate in parallel, read-only; change serially, through the ops lead; capture evidence before mitigating.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. restart.cpp models a dependency (geo-lookup) that times out for 8 % of calls from 19:05 to 19:49, traffic falling by 30 requests per minute each minute, and the restart of both instances at 19:20, which fails all requests of that minute. The chat lines are the scenario script (restart.in), written to match the computed counts. Output unedited. R3


Observation. Errors start at 19:05 (228 per minute), fall steadily to 122 at 19:49 and stop at 19:50. The ratio is 7.9–8.0 % in every minute from 19:05 to 19:49 except 19:20, when it is 100 % (2,400 failed). Traffic falls from 3,000 to 1,530 over the same period. All error messages before and after the restart are "upstream geo-lookup: timeout after 800 ms"; the log lines show status 503 with elapsed times just above 800 ms on both instances.


Hypotheses. H1 (Lena): the instances were unhealthy after 41 days; prediction: the ratio drops after the restart. H2: the dependency is failing; prediction: the ratio stays the same after the restart, errors are upstream timeouts on both instances, and the errors end when the dependency recovers.


Conclusion. H1 is refuted: the ratio was 8.0 % at 19:19 and 7.9 % at 19:21; the count fell because traffic fell (2,430 to 2,370 requests). The restart hurt: it added one minute of 100 % failure, 2,400 requests, more than ten minutes of the fault. H2 is supported by the error messages and confirmed by the geo-lookup team's message at 19:51 and the stop at 19:50.


Root cause and fix. A bad cache node in the geo-lookup dependency (per its team, 19:05–19:50). At 19:14 the on-call engineer should have read the error message (upstream timeout), checked the dependency's health and contacted its owner, and considered a mitigation on the caller's side (for example serving the route without the geo-lookup enrichment, if the product allows it) rather than restarting.


Prevention. Dashboard: show the error ratio and the traffic next to the count, and errors by upstream dependency. Runbook: "read the top error message and the trace of one failed request before any restart; capture state first." Habit: write the prediction before an action ("if the restart helps, the ratio will drop below 1 %") and check it after.


Wrong answers and why. "The restart reduced errors by 15 %": counts, not ratios. "The incident ended at 19:45": it was resolved at 19:45 while the ratio was still 8.0 %; errors ended at 19:50 for an unrelated reason.
