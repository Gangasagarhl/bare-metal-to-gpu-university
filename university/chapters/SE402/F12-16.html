Story hook

It is Saturday morning on the building site. The house has walls and a roof, and the plumbing went in last week. Then Nadia, walking past the kitchen, hears water. A joint under the sink has burst, and water is spreading across the new floor.

Three people arrive at once. Tomás runs to the main valve in the garden and closes it. Wei, not knowing that, opens the valve in the cellar to "relieve the pressure". Hana starts pulling up floorboards to find the leak. Nobody phones the owner, who arrives an hour later to find a wet floor, a hole in it, and nobody who can say what happened when.

The next time a pipe bursts on that team's site, it goes differently. The foreman, Kwame, says one sentence: "I am running this. Tomás, you own the water: nobody else touches a valve. Lucía, you call the owner and the neighbours every half hour. Yusuf, you write down what happens and when." Kwame himself touches nothing. He watches the whole site, asks questions, and decides.

The water is the same and the people are just as skilled. The only difference is that everyone knows their job. This chapter is about those jobs in a software team, and about practising them before the water comes.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what an on-call rotation is, what a page asks of the person who receives it, and how acknowledgement and escalation work.
  • Run an incident with four named roles: incident commander, operations lead, communications lead and scribe, and say what each one does and does not do.
  • Decide when to declare an incident, how severe it is, and when to hand it over or close it.
  • Prioritise mitigation over diagnosis, and justify every change to production during an incident.
  • Measure an incident response by time to acknowledge, time to mitigate and error budget spent, from a recorded timeline.
  • Plan and run a game day on a model of the MP2 service with a timed response, and review it.
Jargon box
On-call rotation
Simple words: a schedule that says who must answer if the system breaks, today and each day after. Analogy: the list on the site office wall: who has the key and the phone this weekend. Precise: a schedule that assigns, for every period, a primary responder (and usually a secondary) who must acknowledge alerts for a service within an agreed time and has the access and knowledge to act; the load it places on people is part of its design. D1
Acknowledgement and escalation
Simple words: "I have seen the alarm and I am on it"; if nobody says that in time, the alarm goes to the next person. Analogy: if the foreman does not answer the phone, the call goes to the deputy. Precise: acknowledging a page stops its repetition and records who owns the response; an escalation policy sends an unacknowledged page to the next responder after a set time. In this chapter's model the time is 5 minutes. D1 R2
Incident (declared)
Simple words: a problem big enough that the team stops normal work and responds together, with roles. Analogy: the moment the foreman says "this is a burst pipe, everybody listen". Precise: an unplanned event that degrades a service below its objectives (or threatens to), formally declared so that roles, a communication channel and a record start; declaring is a decision with a time stamp, not a feeling. D1
Severity level
Simple words: a number that says how bad the incident is, so everybody reacts the same way. Analogy: a dripping tap, a burst pipe, a flooded house. Precise: a team-defined scale (for example SEV1 to SEV4) whose levels are written in advance in terms of user impact (share of requests failing, data at risk, duration) and that sets the response: who is paged, how often stakeholders are updated, whether a post-mortem is required. The levels and their names are local decisions, not a standard. D1
Incident commander (IC)
Simple words: the one person who runs the response and makes the decisions, without doing the hands-on work. Analogy: the foreman who watches the whole site and holds no tools. Precise: the role that owns an incident from declaration to resolution: assigns the other roles, keeps the current picture, sets priorities (mitigate first), approves changes, decides on escalation and handover, and declares the incident resolved. Every role not delegated stays with the IC. D1 D2
Operations lead
Simple words: the only person allowed to change the broken system during the incident. Analogy: the plumber at the valve. Precise: the role that performs (or directs, if several hands are needed) every change to production during the incident, so that changes are serialised, announced before they happen and recorded. D1
Communications lead
Simple words: the person who tells everyone outside the response what is going on, regularly. Analogy: the person who phones the owner and the neighbours. Precise: the role that publishes status updates to stakeholders (users, support, management, dependent teams) on a promised schedule and shields the responders from interruptions. D1
Scribe (incident)
Simple words: the person who writes down what happened and when, while it happens. Analogy: the site diary. Precise: the role (often called planning or documentation) that keeps the live incident record: timestamps, observations, decisions and who made them, open questions and follow-ups; the record is the first draft of the post-mortem timeline (F12-18). D1
Mitigation (incident)
Simple words: stopping the harm first, even before you know why it happened. Analogy: closing the main valve before looking for the crack. Precise: an action that ends or reduces user impact without necessarily removing the cause (rolling back, failing over, shedding load, disabling a feature); the fix of the cause comes later. Not the same as a security mitigation (F11-13). D1
Runbook (playbook)
Simple words: written steps for a known kind of problem, so the person on call does not have to invent them at 3 a.m. Analogy: the laminated card next to the valve: "if water, close this, then call this number". Precise: a document attached to an alert or a failure mode that lists checks, safe actions, their risks and how to verify them; it is code-reviewed and tested like code, and it ages like code. D1

This chapter also uses terms defined in DS402: page and ticket, SLO, error budget, burn rate (F5-42) and game day (F5-43).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

A burst pipe on the building site. Mapping: the person whose turn it is to carry the site phone this weekend = the on-call engineer; the phone ringing = a page; "I'm on my way" = acknowledgement; the call going to the deputy when nobody answers = escalation; the foreman who runs things and holds no tools = the incident commander; the plumber at the valve = the operations lead; the person who phones the owner every half hour = the communications lead; the site diary = the scribe's record; closing the main valve before hunting for the crack = mitigation; the laminated card next to the valve = the runbook.

When a service that many people use breaks, someone has to answer. A team takes turns: this week it is your turn to carry the phone. If the alarm rings, you say "I've got it" quickly, so nobody else is woken for nothing.

If the problem is big, you say so out loud: "this is an incident". From that moment the team splits the jobs. One person decides. One person, and only one, changes the broken system. One person keeps everyone outside informed. One person writes everything down with the time.

The first job is not to understand the problem. The first job is to stop the harm. You close the valve, then you look for the crack. You will see in the lab what happens when two people each try their own fix at the same time.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Analogy

Before anything else the foreman needs to know three things: is water still coming, where is it going, and who is doing what. The roles exist to keep those three answers current.

From alert to page to person

An alert is a rule over a signal; a page is an alert that needs a human now (F5-42 separated pages from tickets by urgency). D1 In this chapter's model the rule is a fast-burn alert: page if more than 2 % of requests failed over the last five minutes. The service's SLO is 99.9 % successful requests over 30 days, so 2 % failing burns the error budget twenty times faster than the SLO allows. R1 The page goes to the primary on-call. If nobody acknowledges it within five minutes, the model escalates to the secondary. In the uncoordinated run (R2) the primary never answered: the page fired at t+6, escalated at t+11, and was acknowledged at t+12, six minutes after the fault. R2

A rotation has to be humane to work. The person on call needs the access to act, a runbook for the known failure modes, a quiet enough rotation to rest between pages, and the authority to call for help without apology. D1 treats an excessive page load as a problem to fix in the system and the alerts, not something to endure. D1 The rule of F5-42 applies with force here: every page must need a human, now; a page that needs no action trains people to ignore pages.

Declaring an incident

Declaring is a decision with a timestamp: "this is an incident, severity 2, I am the incident commander". It changes how people behave: normal work stops for the people named, a dedicated channel opens, the scribe starts the record, and every production change from now on goes through one person. D1 Teams that wait to be sure before declaring pay for it, because the uncertain first minutes are exactly when uncoordinated actions happen. A useful rule, which D1 also argues for: declare early; closing a small incident costs little. D1

The severity is chosen from a table the team wrote in advance, in terms of user impact. A local example, for the MP2 service:

SeverityUser impact (example definition)Response
SEV1Writes or reads fail for most users, or data may be lostPage primary and secondary; IC from the senior rotation; updates every 15 min; post-mortem required
SEV2A noticeable share of requests fail (the lab's 30 %), no data at riskPage primary; IC named; updates every 30 min; post-mortem required
SEV3Degraded latency or a single replica down, SLO not yet at riskTicket plus chat; no IC needed; post-mortem optional

The levels, their names and their thresholds are this course's example, not a standard. Write your own for your mega project and keep them next to the runbooks.

Four roles and what they do not do

  • Incident commander. Holds the current picture ("30 % of requests failing since t+6; v42 is the only recent change; ops proposes rollback"), sets the priority (mitigate first), approves each change, assigns and reassigns roles, decides when to escalate or hand over, and declares resolution. The IC does not type commands into production: the moment the IC starts debugging, nobody watches the whole incident. The simulator flags an IC who also holds ops for this reason (Listing 1, line 124). D1
  • Operations lead. The only hands on production. Proposes an action with its expected effect and its risk, waits for the IC's approval when time allows, announces "doing it now", does it, and reports the observed effect. Helpers investigate and report to the ops lead; they do not change production. D1
  • Communications lead. Posts status updates on a schedule promised in the previous update ("next update by t+25"), answers support and management so that responders are not interrupted, and keeps the wording factual: what users see, what is being done, when the next update comes. D1
  • Scribe. Records times, observations, hypotheses, decisions and who made them, in the incident channel or document. The scribe's record is the backbone of the post-mortem timeline in F12-18; reconstructing it afterwards from memory is slow and unreliable. D1

In a small team one person can hold several roles, and at the start the first responder holds all four. That is allowed as long as it is explicit; the dangerous case is the implicit one, where nobody knows who decides.

Mitigate first, then understand

During an incident the question is "what is the smallest safe action that stops the harm?", not "why did it happen?". Rolling back the most recent change, failing over to a healthy replica, shedding load or switching a feature off are common mitigations; they may hide evidence, so the scribe records the state before each one. D1 In the coordinated run, the ops lead knew one fact that made the choice easy: v42 was the only change in the last hour. Rollback started at t+12 and errors stopped at t+14, when the rollback completed. R1 The root cause (why v42 broke requests) is a question for later, and for F12-17 and F12-18.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Analogy

A good foreman has decided before the season starts who carries the phone, where the valves are, what counts as an emergency and who phones the owner. On the day, there is nothing to invent.

Why changes must be serialised

The uncoordinated run is a small model of a real failure pattern: two responders, each acting sensibly on their own view, together cause a worse outage than the fault. Ben restarted replica n1 at t+13; the cluster elected n2. Fatima, who could not see that, restarted n2 at t+14 "to be safe". With n1 and n2 both down, one of three replicas remained, so there was no majority: every request failed for t+14 and t+15, and the minutes t+13 and t+16 were lost to leader elections. R2 The quorum arithmetic is Raft's: a three-replica group needs two live members to elect a leader and commit (F5-18). D3

Two properties make serialisation valuable, beyond avoiding collisions. First, attribution: when one change at a time is made and its effect observed, every change becomes an experiment with a result. In R2, two rollbacks were started a minute apart (t+23 and t+24); had the error rate changed, nobody could have said which action did it. R2 Second, reversibility: the ops lead knows what was changed and can undo it. The cost of serialisation is speed when many independent actions are needed; the remedy is delegation by the IC ("Wei: you own n3, nothing else"), not free-for-all.

Handover, escalation and resolution

  • Handover. Long incidents outlast people. The IC hands over explicitly: the current picture, open actions and their owners, the next promised update, and the words "you are now IC", acknowledged by the new IC and written by the scribe. D1
  • Escalating for people. Paging a domain expert or a more senior IC is a normal action, not a confession. The IC decides; the comms lead tells stakeholders who is now involved.
  • Resolution. Resolve when user impact has ended and the system is stable for an agreed time, not when the first good minute appears. In R1 the IC waited 11 clean minutes. In R2 the incident was resolved at t+29, three clean minutes after recovery, by someone who had never declared it. R1 R2 Resolution also names the post-mortem owner (F12-18).

Game days for people, not only for systems

F5-43 used game days to test a system's behaviour under an injected fault. The same exercise tests the people and the process: whether the page reaches someone, whether roles are declared, whether the runbook is right, whether the status page is updated. A game day for the response has a game master who knows the fault, a team that does not, a clock, and a review afterwards. The simulator of this chapter supports it: the game master runs it with the team's actions so far and an argument that hides the future (R3 shows the view at t+10). R3 On a real MP2 deployment, the same game day injects the fault for real; F5-43's safety rules (blast radius, abort condition) apply unchanged.

How the hardware actually does it

Three pieces of hardware sit inside every response, and each sets a time you cannot argue with. The pager is a chain of machines: the monitoring system evaluates the alert rule on a schedule, a notification service sends it over networks to a phone, and the phone has to wake a person; each link adds delay and can fail, which is why escalation exists. A restart is not free: the process stops, the replica reloads its state from disk (in Raft, its log and snapshot), rejoins the group and catches up; the model charges three minutes, a real replica charges whatever your measurement says. D3 And the majority rule is physical: a write is committed when a majority of machines have it on stable storage, so taking two of three machines away at once stops the service no matter how healthy the third is. D3 R2 For a robot (MP6) the hardware adds a fourth clock: a moving machine can hurt someone while you think, so its incident runbook starts with the emergency stop and the safe state of F9-69, before any diagnosis.

Worked example

Task. Compute, by hand, the error budget spent in both runs and check the program's summary.

  1. The budget. 30 days × 24 h × 60 min × 1,000 requests per minute = 43,200,000 requests in the window. The SLO allows 0.1 % to fail: 43,200,000 × 0.001 = 43,200 failed requests.
  2. R1. v42 is active from t+6 until the rollback completes at t+14, so minutes t+6 to t+13 fail at 30 %: 8 minutes × 300 = 2,400 failed requests. 2,400 / 43,200 = 0.0556, or 5.6 % of the month's budget.
  3. R2. Minutes t+6 to t+12 at 30 %: 7 × 300 = 2,100. Minutes t+13 to t+16 at 100 % (election, no quorum, no quorum, election): 4 × 1,000 = 4,000. Minutes t+17 to t+25 at 30 % again: 9 × 300 = 2,700. Total 8,800, or 8,800 / 43,200 = 20.4 %.
  4. Where the difference comes from. R2 lost 6,400 more requests than R1: 4,000 − 4 × 300 = 2,800 of them are the extra damage of the two restarts (the 100 % minutes minus what the fault alone would have cost), and the rest is the 12 extra minutes of the 30 % fault before the rollback.

The program's summaries agree: "failed requests 2400 … 5.6 %" (R1) and "failed requests 8800 … 20.4 %" (R2). R1 R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • You can see a burst pipe; you cannot see a failing service. On the site, everyone can look at the water. In software, every responder sees the system only through dashboards, logs and traces, and two people can look at different graphs and believe different things. That is why the IC's job of keeping one shared picture, said out loud and written down, matters more in software than on a building site.
    • 

  • A valve does the same thing every time; a restart does not. Closing a valve stops water. Restarting a replica can help, do nothing, or take away the majority the service needs, depending on what other people did a minute earlier (R2). Software actions interact through state you cannot see, so "one pair of hands" is a safety rule, not only politeness.
    • 

  • The site has one owner to phone; a service has many stakeholders. Users, support staff, dependent teams and managers need different information at different times, and some of them will not wait to be phoned: they will ask in the incident channel and interrupt the responders (gus in R2). The communications role is larger than the phone call in the story.

Transition box

Where you came from: F5-42 turned SLOs into burn-rate pages, and F5-43 injected faults in game days to test a system; F12-10 (SE302) taught you to treat flaky signals with suspicion; F9-70 (RB403) built incident timelines from logs. What carries over: the SLI as the measure of harm, the error budget as the unit of damage, the page-versus-ticket rule, the fault-injection discipline, and timelines with sources R1. What changes: most dangerous first: the people become part of the system you are testing, and their uncoordinated actions can cause more harm than the fault (R2); time pressure is real; decisions need owners. What comes next: F12-17 is the operations lead's craft: finding out, under pressure and from evidence, what is actually wrong, with methods that work when the first guess is wrong.

Lab


Goal. Run a timed game day on the model of the MP2 service with four roles, measure the response, and review it.


Hardware and software needed. No hardware. g++ 13.3.0 (the build's compiler; any C++20 compiler should work). Folder university/labs/F12-16; run university/labs/run_lab.sh university/labs/F12-16 from the repository root. A group of three to five learners, a game master, and a clock. On a real MP2 deployment (optional): the deployment, its F5-42 alerts and the F5-43 fault-injection tooling; untested in this build.


Safety. None beyond normal computer use for the model. On a real deployment, follow F5-43's rules: a staging system or an agreed blast radius, a written abort condition, and nobody's real data. On MP6 hardware, the emergency stop is tested before the game day starts and a supervisor is present (safety rules).


Steps.


    

  1. Run the lab and read gameday.out and gameday_chaos.out. Check the summary lines against the worked example.
    2. 

  2. Before the game day, write a one-page response plan: the rota line, the severity table you will use, who will be IC, ops, comms and scribe if paged, and the runbook entry "error ratio above 2 %: check what changed in the last hour".
    3. 

  3. The game day (timed: 20 real minutes). The game master changes the fault in gameday.cpp without telling the team (ideas: a fault at a different minute, a different failure share, a fault that a rollback does not fix but a failover does, which needs a new verb). Each real minute is one model minute: the team adds its actions for that minute to a new script, and the game master runs ./gameday <minute> < team.txt and shows the output.
    4. 

  4. After the game day, run the full script and record the summary: page, acknowledgement, declaration, recovery, failed requests, budget share, updates, notes.
    5. 

  5. Hold a 20-minute review: what helped, what slowed you, which runbook line was missing. Write two action items with owners.
    6. 



Expected observations. The runs of this build: R1 recovers at t+14 with 2,400 failed requests (5.6 % of the budget) and 0 coordination notes; R2 recovers at t+26 with 8,800 (20.4 %) and 6 notes. Your game day will differ; what matters is that every interval is measured and explained.


Verification. Your final script runs with exit code 0; the summary has an incident declared, at least one stakeholder update per promised interval, and zero "change by … but the ops lead is …" notes. Your review names at least one improvement in the system and one in the process.


Troubleshooting.


    

  • An action seems to be ignored → the line format is minute actor verb details; a restart needs n1, n2 or n3; lines starting with # are comments.
    • 

  • The declaration shows empty role names → write them as ic=name ops=name comms=name scribe=name, with no spaces around =.
    • 

  • The page escalates although you acknowledged → the acknowledgement was before the page; the model only counts an acknowledgement at or after the page minute.
    • 

  • Your new fault never pages → your failure share may be below the 2 % threshold; that is a finding about the alert, not the fault.
    • 



Extension. Add a failover verb that moves leadership without restarting anything, and a fault that only affects the leader. Does your team's runbook still lead to the right action? Then add a second, unrelated alert in the middle of the incident and see whether your IC notices it.

Forensic lab


Scenario. Monday morning. The weekend's on-call report says only "Saturday: kv errors, restarted nodes, rolled back v42, fine now." The team lead asks you to reconstruct what happened from the recorded timeline before the post-mortem meeting, and to say what the team should change about how it responds.


Evidence pack. The simulator's full record of the weekend (the uncoordinated script gameday_chaos.txt run through Listing 1): per-minute replicas, leader, requests and errors; pages and acknowledgements; chat; changes; the summary.




Questions.


    

  1. Observation: write the timeline of the incident in ten lines or fewer, each line with the minute and its source (page, chat, change, metric).
    2. 

  2. Hypotheses: Ben's restart at t+13 and Fatima's at t+14 could each be "the action that made it worse". State both hypotheses and decide between them with the evidence.
    3. 

  3. Root cause of the extra damage: which property of the response (not of the software) turned a 30 % problem into a 100 % outage?
    4. 

  4. Fix: rewrite the script from t+12 onwards as a coordinated response by the same people, run it, and report the new summary.
    5. 

  5. Prevention: name two changes, one to the on-call setup and one to the team's incident process, and say which interval of the Layer 3 table each would shorten.

Summary


    

  • An on-call rotation gives every alert an owner; acknowledgement and escalation make the ownership visible.
    • 

  • Declare an incident early: it starts the roles, the channel and the record.
    • 

  • Four roles: the IC decides and touches nothing; the ops lead is the only pair of hands on production; the comms lead faces outwards on a schedule; the scribe writes the timeline.
    • 

  • Mitigate first; diagnose after the harm stops.
    • 

  • Uncoordinated sensible actions can be worse than the fault: two restarts took away the majority and cost 4,000 requests in four minutes (R2).
    • 

  • Measure every response: detection, acknowledgement, mitigation, and error budget spent (R1: 5.6 %; R2: 20.4 %).
    • 

  • Game days test people and process as well as systems; a game master reveals the incident minute by minute.

How the evidence was produced. run.sh runs Listing 1 on gameday_chaos.txt, a scripted uncoordinated response to the model's fixed fault (v42 at t+6). The chat lines are part of the script; everything else is computed. Output unedited. R2

Hypotheses. "Ben's restart made it worse": it cost one minute (the t+13 election), but two replicas stayed up. "Fatima's restart made it worse": it left one replica, so t+14 and t+15 had no quorum and t+16 was another election. The evidence (up column: "3" at t+14 and t+15) shows the combination did it: either restart alone would have cost one election minute; together they removed the majority.


Root cause of the extra damage. No declared incident and therefore no single owner of production: two responders changed the same system within one minute without knowing about each other (NOTE lines at t+13, t+14, t+23, t+24). The software fault (v42) caused the 30 %; the response caused the 100 %.


Fix. For example, from t+12: 12 ben ack; 13 ben declare sev2 ic=ben ops=fatima comms=gus scribe=ben (small team: ben holds IC and scribe); 13 ben say fatima: you own production; first question: what changed?; 14 fatima say v42 at t+06 is the only change; 14 gus status investigating failed requests; next update by t+30; 15 fatima rollback; then an update after recovery, and 27 ben resolve with a final update.
