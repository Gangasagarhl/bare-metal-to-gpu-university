Story hook

Behind the kitchen wall, the wiring is almost finished. Bruno notices that a cheaper cable would save a day and some money. It is thinner than the plan says. The wall will be closed tomorrow; the inspector checks only a few places; the family would never know.

Lena, who leads the team, says no. Then she does the harder part: she calls the family. "We are a day behind. We can catch up by working Saturday, or move the painters by a day. We will not use a thinner cable to save time; it would be our problem in ten years, and yours." The family is unhappy for a day and grateful for years.

Later, the team finds out that the cable they installed on another house was from a bad batch. Nobody would ever find out. Lena writes to that family too, and the team replaces it. Responsibility is doing the right thing when nobody is checking, and telling the truth when the truth costs you something.

What you will be able to do

  • Identify everyone affected by an engineering decision, including people who are not in the room.
  • Apply a short ethics check to a decision and name the options it reveals.
  • Report results honestly: claims carried by evidence, measurements labelled, limitations stated, corrections made in the open.
  • Raise a concern or refuse unsafe work constructively, with options and escalation, and keep a record.
  • Respect safety rules, licences, privacy and the limits of your own and your tools' knowledge.
  • Diagnose a report whose headline claim was produced by cherry-picking, and write its correction.
Jargon box
Professional responsibility
Simple words: what you owe to the people who depend on your work because you are the one who knows how it works. Analogy: the electrician who knows what is behind the wall. Precise: the duties that come with specialised knowledge used for others: to the public's safety and well-being, to users, to colleagues and the organisation, and to the profession; professional bodies set them out in codes of conduct (for computing, for example, the ACM and IEEE codes) D1 D2.
Stakeholder without a voice
Simple words: someone your decision affects who is not part of making it. Analogy: the neighbours, the next family to live in the house. Precise: a person or group affected by a system's behaviour or failure who has no part in its design decisions (bystanders near a robot, people recorded by a drone's camera, future maintainers, users of a downstream system); an ethics check names them explicitly because no one else will (course definition).
Cherry-picking (results)
Simple words: showing only the results that support what you want to say. Analogy: showing the family the one straight wall. Precise: selecting a subset of trials, runs, inputs or comparisons for a report because they favour a conclusion, without disclosing the selection; for example comparing the worst run of one version with the best run of another (Listing 2) (course definition).
Corrections log
Simple words: a public list of the mistakes you published and how you fixed them. Analogy: the letter to the family about the bad cable. Precise: in this university, a log attached to each chapter's QA record giving the date, version, what was wrong, what it now says, the source that proves it, who found it and which other chapters were checked; corrections are never silent (guide AH-37 to AH-39) D3.
Escalation (raising a concern)
Simple words: taking a concern to someone with more authority when the people around you do not act on it. Analogy: Lena calling the family, or the building inspector, when the contractor insists on the thin cable. Precise: raising a documented concern through the organisation's defined path, step by step (the person responsible, their lead, the person responsible for safety or ethics, and outside bodies only where the organisation's path fails and the law provides for it), with the facts, the risk and the options; the legal protections for doing so depend on the country (unverified box in Layer 3) (course definition). Not the same as escalation in on-call work (acknowledgement and escalation, SE402), where an unanswered alert is passed to the next responder.
Data minimisation
Simple words: collecting only the personal data you really need, and keeping it only as long as you need it. Analogy: the builders do not photograph the family's living room to measure one window. Precise: the design principle of limiting the collection, retention and access of personal data (images, voices, locations, names in logs) to what a stated purpose requires; its legal form depends on the data-protection law where the data is collected (course definition; legal details unverified).

Terms from earlier chapters used here: functional safety (SS402); threat model (SS401); watermelon status and consequence framing (F12-26); decision quality versus outcome quality (F12-24).

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What the codes say, in outline

Computing has professional codes of conduct. The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct and the IEEE Code of Ethics both place the public's safety and well-being, honesty about work and its limits, competence, fair treatment of others, and respect for privacy and others' work among a professional's duties D1 D2. This chapter does not quote them: read them yourself (lab step 2). They are short, and reading the actual text is the habit this university teaches for every source.

Honest reporting is an engineering skill

This university is built on rules that are, at bottom, ethics of reporting: never write a number about hardware that you did not get from its documentation or measure yourself; label measurements with the machine, tool, version and date; never present a figure as a product's property when it was measured on one machine; no padding that pretends to knowledge ("studies show", "experts say"); and corrections are public, never silent D3. They exist because readers decide with what they read. The same rules apply to a release note, a benchmark slide, a status report (F12-26) or a defence. Listing 1 automates the most mechanical part of the check: it flags padding phrases, numbers used as claims without an evidence tag, and absolute words. On the worked example's draft release note it flags four of five sentences R4.

Safety first, and the right to stop

Robots and drones can hurt people; LiPo batteries can catch fire; firmware can brick a machine someone depends on. The guide's safety rules (simulator first, propellers removed for bench tests, a kill switch, a clear zone, supervised charging, national aviation rules read before any outdoor flight, no mains work) and the curriculum's rule never to flash or write to hardware the learner has not designated are not bureaucracy D3 D4. In MP7 a go/no-go review (R3) comes before any flight D3. Anyone on a team may stop a test that has become unsafe, and a leader's job is to make that easy: no one is ever criticised for pressing the emergency stop. SS402 treats safety-critical engineering formally; here the point is personal: when you see a risk to people, you say so, even if you are the most junior person present.

Other people's work

The curriculum's runbook: reading Linux, EDK II, coreboot, Mesa or vendor code is encouraged; copying code requires checking the licence and recording the origin. Its universal method adds that copying GPL-licensed code brings the GPL's obligations into your project, and asks you to record where every piece of knowledge came from D4. Credit is the same duty applied to people: in a team report, name who did what; in a mentoring relationship, give the mentee the credit for their work (F12-25).

Tools that generate text and code

This university was written with AI agents, and its guide states the honest view: an agent's memory is not a source; it can produce text that sounds right and is wrong; only a process (sources, tags, real runs, independent checks, corrections) makes errors rare D3. The same holds for any generated code or text you use: you are responsible for it as if you had written it. Check it, run it, cite real sources rather than the tool, and say how it was produced where your course or organisation asks.

Privacy

Robots and drones carry cameras, microphones and positioning; their logs can record people who never agreed to it. Apply data minimisation in the design: record only what a stated purpose needs (a SLAM debugging session may need depth data, not face images), blur or drop what you do not need, keep logs only as long as needed, restrict who can read them, and tell people when they may be recorded. A threat model (SS401) should list the logs themselves as assets that can leak.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Using the ethics check in a real decision

The five questions of Figure 1 fit into a decision record (F12-24) as a section, and they change decisions most when asked early. Question 1 adds scenarios and payoffs that the team did not see (the bystander's injury is a payoff too, and usually a constraint rather than a number: F12-24's rule that unacceptable outcomes are removed as options, not weighed). Question 2 checks rules that are not negotiable. Question 3, "would we defend it if it were published tomorrow?", catches decisions made because nobody would see them. Question 4 insists on options, because ethical problems are usually presented as two bad choices ("ship it or miss the date") when a third exists ("ship the safe subset; say what is missing"). Question 5 makes the decision visible and recorded, which protects both the people affected and the engineer.

Raising a concern

Raising a concern well is a skill, and F12-26's structure applies: start with what you need ("I need us to pause the outdoor test until the failsafe is verified"), give the facts and the risk in consequence terms, offer options, and say what you will do next. Write it down (an email, a comment in the decision record) with the date. If the concern is dismissed and you still believe people are at risk, escalate step by step through the organisation's defined path: the person responsible, their lead, the person responsible for safety. In the university, that path ends with the course coordinator and the owner. Most concerns end at the first step, because most people want to hear them; a team where they do not is a team with a problem that a blameless post-mortem should examine.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Legal protection for people who report wrongdoing or danger outside their organisation, the duties of engineers in regulated professions, and the data-protection rules for recording people with robots or drones differ between countries and change over time. This chapter states none of them. Before relying on any of them, read the current law or official guidance of the country where you work, or ask the person responsible for legal or compliance questions in your organisation. Drone rules in particular must come from your national aviation authority's current documents, as the guide requires.

How numbers mislead

Listing 2 generates simulated benchmark trials (the "times" are not measurements: the program draws them from a seeded model with a little noise and occasional interference) and shows how much the summary choice matters. On the same 20 trials, the median ratio is 1.13, the best-against-best ratio is 1.13, and the worst old run against the best new run is 2.61 R2. All three numbers are arithmetically correct; only one comparison is honest without further explanation. The ways numbers mislead are few and recurrent: choosing the summary after seeing the data (best, worst, mean, median), choosing the trials (dropping "outliers" that hurt), choosing the baseline (an old, slow version or a debug build), choosing the axis (a chart that does not start at zero), and leaving out the conditions (machine, input, build). The protection is the same as in DS401, SP302 and F12-03: decide the method before measuring, report the method with the result, publish all trials, and label measurements with their machine and date D3.

When you were wrong

Everyone who publishes results eventually publishes a wrong one. The university's corrections rules show the shape of a good correction: say what was wrong and what is right now, give the evidence, say who found it (with thanks), check where else the same error appears, and do it in the open D3. Silent fixes protect the author's comfort at the reader's expense; readers who decided with the wrong number need to know. In a team, the leader's first job after an error is to make the correction easy and blameless, so the next person reports their mistake early.

Responsibility grows with seniority

Reilly's account of senior technical roles includes being a person others look to for what "good" means, which makes a senior engineer's example part of the team's culture D5. Brooks's book, written about a large project, is in large part about the responsibility of those who lead technical work for the conceptual integrity of what the team builds D6. For an SE404 learner leading an MP8 team, the practical form is: you set the honesty of the status reports, the seriousness of the safety rules, and the tone in which mistakes are reported, mostly by what you do yourself.

How the hardware actually does it

Responsibility for hardware is concrete. An emergency stop must actually cut the motors, which is a hardware fact to verify on the bench (with propellers removed), not a software promise. A battery's charging rules come from its manufacturer's documentation, which the guide requires chapters to quote rather than remember D3. Firmware updates must have a recovery path before they are flashed, and devices that will be used by others need a way to receive security fixes (SS301). A robot that records people carries their data on its storage; that storage must be erased before the robot is lent, sold or thrown away. And every claim about hardware behaviour in a report needs the same evidence as any other claim: "untested on hardware" is an honest and useful phrase, used throughout this university wherever the build had no device.

Situation. An MP6 robot team prepares a demonstration at the university's open day, with visitors, including children, near the robot. The coordinator asks for release notes by Thursday. The team's first draft:

Step 2 — what the tool missed. "Fully tested" is not flagged, and it is the most dangerous phrase: the stopping tests ran in the simulator only. The tool checks habits; a person checks meaning.


Step 3 — the ethics check. (1) Harmed: visitors, especially children, near a moving robot; people recorded by its camera. No voice: the visitors. Step 4 — evidence for each claim. The team finds its own run records: the simulator suite (12 scenarios) passed; the localisation comparison exists for the simulator only; battery life on the route was never measured; a known limitation (glass and mirrors) appears in the simulator logs. They tag each claim with its record ([R7], [R8], [R9] in their project) and remove the "40 %" figure, which nobody can trace.


Step 5 — the final release note.






No flags R5, and, more importantly, every sentence is true and the safety measures are stated as conditions of the demonstration. Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Software has no building code. A house is inspected against written codes by an inspector with authority. Most software is not inspected by anyone outside the team, and in many areas no binding code exists. That moves more of the responsibility onto the engineer than the analogy suggests.
    • 

  • Software defects copy themselves. One thin cable affects one house. One flawed library, model or firmware image is copied into thousands of systems at once, and an error in a widely read document (like a chapter of this university) is learned by every reader. The scale of harm and the urgency of correction are larger than in the analogy.
    • 

  • Software can be changed after delivery, for better and worse. A wall stays as built; software can be fixed remotely, which makes correction possible, and can also be changed without the user's knowledge, which creates duties (telling users, asking consent, keeping updates secure) that a builder does not have.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. The report claim_lint.in and the chat lines were written for this lab; the trials are Listing 2's seeded simulation (no program was timed) R2; Listing 1 was run on the report R1.


1. The 2.6×. Worst old run: trial 9, 218.9. Best new run: trial 5, 83.8. 218.9 / 83.8 = 2.61, printed by Listing 2 as "a cherry-picked comparison" R2. Trial 9 is also one of the slowest new runs (186.9; only trial 18, 190.0, is slower): something slowed both versions in that trial, which a paired comparison would have shown.


2. Honest summaries. Median ratio 1.13; best against best 1.13; new faster in 15 of 20 paired trials; both versions had occasional slow trials, and six of the 20 new trials (7, 9, 10, 14, 16 and 18: 113.5, 186.9, 124.2, 167.8, 163.5 and 190.0) were slower than the old version's median of 102.46. For a non-engineer: "In our 20 trials the new scheduler was usually a little faster, by about an eighth, but it was sometimes much slower; we have not yet tested it against the robot's deadlines."


3. Claims without evidence. "Studies show … industry standard" (no study in the pack); "never missed a deadline" and "100 % safe at full speed" (the chat says deadlines were not tested on the robot); "CPU use fell by 30 %" (no CPU measurement in the log). Listing 1 flagged all four sentences R1. The one that mattered most: "never missed a deadline … 100 % safe", because it turned an untested property into the basis for shipping to all robots; the incident was a missed deadline. Note also that six of the 20 new trials were slower than the old version's median: occasional long delays are exactly what threatens a deadline.


4. Affected. Ring 1: the release meeting, which decided with false information. Ring 2: the operators and maintainers of all the robots. Ring 3: people near the robots during tests and use, who were protected by the watchdog, not by the report. Ring 4: trust in the team's future reports.


5. Decision or outcome. A bad decision: with the evidence in the pack, shipping to all robots could not be justified, and the untested property was claimed as tested. The outcome (a watchdog stop in a supervised test) was mild because the safety layer worked. Blamelessly: the process let a report reach a release meeting without its log, without a method section, and without anyone checking claims against evidence; the chat shows time pressure doing the rest.


6. The correction and prevention. The correction (checked with Listing 1: zero flags R3):






It goes to everyone who received the original, says the earlier report overstated the result and claimed an untested property, and asks the meeting to pause the roll-out until a deadline test on one supervised robot passes. Process changes: (a) release reports must attach the full benchmark log and a method section written before the measurement; checked by the release meeting's agenda refusing reports without both. (b) Safety-relevant properties (deadlines, stopping) may be claimed only with a named test on the target hardware; checked by a review question "where was this tested?" recorded in the minutes. Also good: Listing 1 run on every release report before the meeting.


Wrong answers and why. "The numbers were faked": every number in the slide is computed from real (here simulated) trials; the problem is selection and missing method, which is more common and harder to see. "The watchdog proves the system was safe": the watchdog is the last layer; it worked, but the report claimed the layer above it. "Benchmarks are useless": the same log supports an honest, useful summary.
