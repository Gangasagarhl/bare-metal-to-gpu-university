Story hook

The family asks the builders a simple question: "When can we move in?" Joon, who leads the team, could say "twelve weeks" and see everyone smile. Instead he says: "Most likely fourteen weeks, almost surely by seventeen, and here is what could make it longer." The walls depend on the foundation, the roof depends on the walls, and the painters cannot start until the roof keeps the rain out. Some jobs can happen side by side; most cannot.

Six weeks later the walls are "ninety per cent done". Three weeks after that, they are still "ninety per cent done". Nobody lied: ninety per cent of the bricks were bought and ninety per cent of the planned hours were spent. But nobody had drawn the drain under the kitchen floor, the window frames arrived late, and "done" had never been defined as "a room you can stand in when it rains".

This chapter is about saying "when" honestly: as a range with a probability, built from the work, measured by finished things rather than spent effort, and corrected by your own history.

What you will be able to do

  • Break down a project into tasks small enough to finish and test, with their dependencies, and find the critical path.
  • Estimate each task as a range (three-point estimate) and turn the ranges into a schedule distribution with a seeded simulation.
  • Distinguish an estimate, a target and a commitment, and choose a commitment percentile on purpose.
  • Calibrate your estimates against your own logged history of estimated versus actual time.
  • Measure progress by finished, accepted work, and explain why "percentage of hours spent" is not progress.
  • Diagnose the estimation errors in a plan-versus-actual history (the "always 90 % done" project).
Jargon box
Estimate (project)
Simple words: your best honest guess of how long work will take, with how sure you are. Analogy: "most likely fourteen weeks, almost surely by seventeen". Precise: a statement about the probability distribution of a task's or project's duration or effort, made from the work's content and evidence (history, measurements), independent of what anyone wishes; course definition, separated from target (a desired date set by a need) and commitment (a date a team promises, chosen as a percentile of the estimate).
Three-point estimate
Simple words: three numbers for one task: fast if all goes well, most likely, and slow if things go wrong. Analogy: "the walls take two weeks if the weather is kind, three most likely, five if the bricks are late". Precise: a triple (low, likely, high) used here as the minimum, mode and maximum of a triangular distribution over the task's duration; the mean of that distribution is (low + likely + high) / 3, larger than "likely" whenever the high side is longer.
Critical path (project schedule)
Simple words: the chain of jobs that decides when the whole project ends. Analogy: foundation, walls, roof, painting: a delay to any of them delays moving in. Precise: for given task durations, the longest path through the dependency graph from the first tasks to the last; any delay on it delays the end by the same amount. Not the same as the critical path of a digital circuit (HW102, HW202), which is the slowest signal path between registers.
Monte Carlo schedule simulation
Simple words: pretend to run the project thousands of times with random task durations and look at all the finish days. Analogy: building the house ten thousand times on paper, each time with different weather. Precise: repeated sampling of every task's duration from its distribution, computing the project end for each sample through the dependency graph, and reporting percentiles of the resulting end dates; deterministic when the random generator is seeded. Listing 1 implements it.
Merge bias
Simple words: when two paths must both finish before the next job, the later one wins, so waiting for both takes longer than waiting for a typical one. Analogy: the roofers wait for the walls and for the delivery of the roof tiles, whichever is later. Precise: the end of a task with several predecessors is the maximum of their finish times; the expected maximum of uncertain durations exceeds the maximum of their expected or likely values, so a plan that uses single values underestimates every merge point. R1 shows it.
Earned progress
Simple words: how much of the work is really finished, counted by finished pieces. Analogy: counting rooms you can stand in when it rains, not bricks bought. Precise: the sum of the original estimates of tasks whose acceptance test has passed, divided by the sum of the estimates of all known tasks (course definition, a simplified form of what project-management texts call earned value); a task counts 0 until done and fully when done.
Calibration (estimates)
Simple words: learning how wrong your guesses usually are, and correcting for it. Analogy: a builder who knows "my 'two weeks' usually means three". Precise: the distribution of the ratio actual / estimate over your own finished tasks, used to scale new raw estimates into a range (median ratio for the likely value, a higher percentile for the commitment). Listing 2 implements it. Related to, but not the same as, the calibration of a sensor or instrument (calibration, MA202).

Terms from earlier chapters used here: median and percentile (MA202); decision matrix (F12-03, RB403); acceptance test and definition of done (curriculum section 0) D3.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12, building a house as a team; new mappings proposed in NOTES.md): "how many weeks will the house take" = an estimate; "most likely fourteen, surely by seventeen" = a range with a probability; foundation before walls before roof = dependencies and the critical path; waiting for the walls and the tiles = a merge point; counting bricks bought = measuring effort, not progress; a room you can stand in when it rains = a finished task that passed its acceptance test; the drain nobody drew = discovered work.

Nobody knows exactly how long a job will take, so an honest answer is a range: "probably this, surely by that". If you give only the happy number, you are promising the best day of the year.

Count progress by finished rooms, not by bricks or hours. A room is finished when you can test it: stand in it while it rains. And remember what you learned last time: if your "two weeks" usually becomes three, say three.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Estimate, target, commitment

Three different numbers hide behind "when will it be done?", and confusing them is the first estimation error. The target is what someone needs ("the defence is on the last day of term"). The estimate is what the work will probably take, independent of that need. The commitment is the date the team promises, chosen from the estimate with a stated confidence. If the target is earlier than any reasonable commitment, the honest move is to change the scope, the resources or the target, in the open: not to shrink the estimate until it fits. This course uses these three words with exactly these meanings.

Break the work down until it can be tested

The curriculum asks for one milestone at a time, each finished only when its acceptance tests pass from a clean build, earlier tests still pass and the design note is updated D3. That definition of done is also the best unit of planning. A task is small enough when (1) its acceptance test can be written now, (2) one person can finish it in a few days, and (3) "half done" has no meaning. Tasks of several weeks are not tasks; they are hopes with a name. Write the list of tasks before estimating anything, and add the tasks people forget: integration, tests, the threat model, documentation, the game day and review gates of MP8 D4, and waiting for other people.

Estimate each task as a range

For each task, write three numbers before looking at anyone else's: low (everything goes well), likely (what you would bet on), high (the things you can imagine going wrong do go wrong, but not a disaster). Two estimators writing independently and then comparing find forgotten work: a large disagreement is a signal to check whether the two people imagine different tasks. In the example, every task has a long right tail: its high value is further from likely than its low value is, because the team could imagine more ways for a task to go wrong than to go unexpectedly well. That asymmetry is why the plan built from "likely" values is too early, and it is visible in arithmetic before any simulation: the sum of the likely values in Figure 1 is 41.0 days of effort, the sum of the means is 52.3 R1.

Dependencies and the critical path

With every task taking its likely value, the plan of Figure 1 ends on day 30, along design → proto → ui → integ → sitl → gameday → defence (check: 5 + 6 + 5 + 5 + 4 + 2 + 3 = 30; the store path is 5 + 8 + 5 + 4 + 2 + 3 = 27, three days shorter) R1. People manage what is on the critical path and relax about the rest. The simulation shows why that is not enough: the store path, three days shorter in the plan but with a much longer tail (high 20 against likely 8), was critical in 29.6 % of trials. A path is "off the critical path" only for one set of durations.

More people, more paths

Adding people to a task does not divide its duration. Brooks's argument, in "The Mythical Man-Month", is that tasks with dependencies cannot be perfectly divided, that new people need time to learn the work from the people already doing it, and that communication grows with the team; he concludes that adding people to a late software project can make it later D1. The communication part is arithmetic: n people have n(n − 1)/2 pairs. Four people have 6 pairs, eight have 28, twelve have 66. Not every pair must talk, but every interface between people's work is a place where assumptions differ. For planning this means: add people early or not at all, give each person a task with a clear interface and acceptance test, and count the onboarding and review time of the people already on the team as work.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The model in Listing 1

Each task's duration is drawn from a triangular distribution with minimum a = low, mode m = likely and maximum b = high. Its cumulative distribution reaches F(m) = (m − a)/(b − a) at the mode, and inverting it gives the sampling rule used in the listing (lines 27–37): for a uniform u in (0, 1), the duration is a + √(u (b − a)(m − a)) if u < F(m), and b − √((1 − u)(b − a)(b − m)) otherwise. The distribution's mean is (a + m + b)/3. The triangular shape is a modelling choice, chosen because it needs only the three numbers people can give and has a long tail when b − m is large; other shapes (a beta distribution, a log-normal) are also used in practice, and the conclusions of this chapter do not depend on the exact shape, only on the tail. The project end of one trial is computed by a forward pass in input order (each task starts at the latest finish of its predecessors), which is correct because the input lists tasks after their dependencies (lines 115–125 refuse anything else).

Reading the distribution

The run prints P10 33.2, P50 38.3, P80 42.1 and P95 45.7, and only 1.3 % of trials finish by day 30 R1. Three effects make the single-date plan so optimistic, and each is visible in the output:

  • Skew. Every task's mean is above its likely value (the "mean" column); summed along the critical path, the shift is several days.
  • Merge bias. integ waits for the maximum of three paths. Even when each path is probably short, the probability that all of them are short is smaller, so the expected start of integ is later than the latest likely path. The criticality of store (29.6 %) is the visible symptom.
  • No correlation, which is optimistic too. Listing 1 draws durations independently. Real tasks share causes: a misunderstood requirement or a late board delays several tasks at once. Correlated delays widen the distribution beyond what Listing 1 shows. Treat its P95 as a lower bound on the real P95.

Choosing a commitment

A commitment is a percentile chosen on purpose, and the choice depends on the cost of being late. For an internal milestone that only moves the next milestone, a P50 date with weekly re-planning is reasonable. For the MP8 public defence, which has a fixed date, a commitment near P80–P90 with explicit scope that can be dropped is reasonable. These percentiles are this course's recommendation for learner projects, not a standard; write the chosen percentile next to the date ("day 42, P80 of our model"), so that everyone knows what the date means. If the target is day 35 and the P80 is day 42, show both numbers and the options: drop scope (which tasks?), start earlier, reduce risk on the riskiest task first (a spike on store, F12-24), or move the target.

Calibrating with your own history

A model is only as good as its three-point estimates, and the only way to know whether yours are systematically low is to compare them with what happened. The fix is evidence: the curriculum's log entry template records the date and session length of every working session and the milestone it served D3, so a learner who kept the log already has a table of estimate against actual. Listing 2 turns it into ratios. In the constructed example, 7 of 8 milestones took longer than estimated, the median ratio is 1.40 and the 80th percentile 1.70, so a raw estimate of 16 hours becomes a range of 22.4 to 27.2 hours R2. Two cautions: with fewer than five data points the listing warns that the range is rough, and the ratio of a learner's first project does not carry over unchanged to a team project, where waiting, reviews and integration add work that solo projects do not have. Recalibrate after every MP8 milestone.

Measuring progress honestly

"90 % done" usually means one of three things: 90 % of the planned hours are spent, 90 % of the tasks have started, or the speaker feels close. None of these is progress. Use earned progress: a task contributes its estimate when its acceptance test passes, and nothing before. It is coarse (a big task shows no progress for weeks), which is the point: it forces tasks to be small, and it cannot be argued with. Track two more numbers beside it: scope (the sum of estimates of all known tasks, which grows when work is discovered) and hours spent against the original plan. When hours rise faster than earned progress, or scope keeps growing, the estimate is wrong, and the plan must be redone now, not at the end. The forensic lab shows a project where these numbers told the story for nine weeks while the status said "90 %".

Planning as a leader

Reilly's "The Staff Engineer's Path" treats planning and execution of projects that span several people or teams as a core part of senior technical work, including making the plan and its risks visible to everyone involved and noticing when a project has stalled D2. In this course that means three habits: the plan is a shared written document (a design document section in the sense of F12-02), the estimate is a range with its percentile, and re-planning is a scheduled event (every milestone), not an admission of failure.

How the hardware actually does it

Hardware work brings its own sources of schedule uncertainty, and they belong in the plan as tasks, not as surprises. A real board must be obtained and brought up; the guide's safety rules require the simulator or emulator milestone to pass before any real hardware is used, propeller-off bench tests before any flight, and a recovery path before any firmware is flashed D4; each of those is work with its own estimate. GPU labs need a GPU, which may have to be booked or rented. Datasheets can be wrong or incomplete, which turns an "implement the driver" task into "find out how the device really behaves" (the curriculum's universal method, stage (c)). In a three-point estimate, these are the reasons the high value of a hardware task is far from its likely value; in the dependency graph, "hardware available" is a predecessor of every hardware test. None of this needs numbers about the hardware itself: it needs the plan to name the steps.

Worked example

Task. The MP8 team of Figure 1 must announce a date for its integration milestone review (R2 of MP8 D4) and for the defence. The course coordinator's target for the defence is day 35.

Step 1 — tasks and dependencies. Nine tasks, each with an acceptance test (for example "store: three nodes in QEMU, one killed, no acknowledged write lost, test script exits 0"). The threat model and the game day are tasks because MP8 requires them D4.

Step 2 — independent three-point estimates. Two members estimate separately and compare. They disagree most on store (one wrote high = 12, the other 20); talking it through shows the second person included recovery after a node restart. The team keeps 20 and adds "restart recovery" to store's acceptance test.

Step 3 — the single-date plan, by hand. Critical path 5 + 6 + 5 + 5 + 4 + 2 + 3 = 30 days. The target of 35 looks safe.

Step 4 — the distribution. Listing 1 on schedule.in prints P50 38.3, P80 42.1; 1.3 % of trials finish by day 30 R1. The target of day 35 lies between P10 (33.2) and P50 (38.3): the team's own numbers give it well under even odds.

Step 5 — calibrate. The team's logged ratio from MP2 and MP6 (their own Listing 2 run) has a median above 1. They do not multiply the simulation by it blindly (the three-point estimates already include some pessimism), but they note that their history says their "likely" values are low, which makes P80 more defensible than P50.

Step 6 — options, not a smaller number. They present: "Day 42 at P80 of our model with everything in scope. To meet day 35 we propose: (a) a spike of two days on store restart recovery now, because store's long tail is the largest single risk (F12-24 shows how to value it); (b) ui reduced to a read-only dashboard (high 9 → 5); (c) if store is not passing its acceptance test by day 20, the defence shows the system with the single-node store from MP2 and the replication as future work." The coordinator, not the team, chooses among (a)–(c).

Step 7 — the tracking plan. Every Friday: earned progress, scope, hours against plan, and a re-run of Listing 1 with finished tasks set to their actual durations (low = likely = high = actual). The date is re-announced with its percentile at every milestone.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Software tasks are less alike than house tasks. Builders have built many walls and can estimate the next one from a stable rate. Much software work is done once (a new driver, a new protocol), so the history of "similar" tasks is thinner and the tails are longer. The analogy makes estimation look easier than it is.
    • 

  • Software "done" is invisible. Anyone can see whether a roof keeps the rain out. A half-working replicated store looks exactly like a working one until a node restarts. That is why the definition of done must be an acceptance test written in advance, which the house analogy does not need.
    • 

  • Discovered work behaves differently. In a house, the forgotten drain is one extra job. In software, discovered work can change the design of work already done (the retry logic of the forensic lab touched the parser and the store), so its cost is larger than its own estimate.
    •

Mini-project


The plan defence. The SE404 practical exam is a project plan defence. Prepare for it with your MP8 (or current) plan: a ten-minute presentation to two peers acting as a sceptical coordinator and a sceptical engineer. Summary


    

  • Estimate, target and commitment are different numbers; never shrink the estimate to meet the target.
    • 

  • Break work into tasks with acceptance tests; include integration, tests, reviews and waiting.
    • 

  • Estimate as ranges; long right tails make plans from "likely" values early, and merge points make them earlier still.
    • 

  • Simulate the network (seeded, reproducible) and announce a date with its percentile.
    • 

  • Calibrate with your own logged history; recalibrate after each milestone.
    • 

  • Count progress by accepted work; track scope and hours beside it; re-plan as soon as they diverge.
    • 

  • Adding people costs onboarding and communication; add them early or with clear interfaces.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. history.in was written for this lab as a constructed case (not a real team) and processed by Listing 3 R3; the analysis below is Listing 3 with the argument analyse, run by run.sh R4:





1. What "reported" measured. Hours spent against the original plan: weeks 1–7 report 12, 25, 35, 50, 60, 72, 85 % while hours/plan is 12.5, 25.0, 37.5, 50.0, 62.5, 75.0, 87.5 %. From week 8, hours/plan passes 100 % and the reports stall near 90 %: the team kept the number below 100 because the work was visibly not done.


2. Earned progress. Week 8: parser, ui and docs done = 80 h of 220 h known = 36.4 %. Week 12: plus store and retry = 150 h of 260 h = 57.7 % R4. The honest reports: "about a third done, at the planned end of the project" (week 8) and "a little over half" (week 12).


3. Largest ratio. integration: 110 h against 40 h, a ratio of 2.75 (printed as 2.8). Integration is where everyone's assumptions meet; its work is mostly discovering mismatches, which cannot be estimated from the size of any one component, and it was planned as one task at the end, so its problems surfaced last. Store (1.7) is second: a replicated store's failure handling is the long tail.


4. Discovered work. retry (week 7), migration (week 10), soak (week 12): 60 h estimated, 90 h actual, 21.2 % of all hours R4. Planning questions: "What happens when a call fails?" (retry); "What happens to data written by the old version?" (migration); "How do we know it runs for a day?" (soak test). Each would have appeared if every task had needed an acceptance test covering failure, upgrade and duration.


5. First re-plan. Week 6 at the latest: 75 % of the plan's hours spent, 40 % earned (ratio 0.53). Week 3 already showed parser at 1.5× its estimate. A re-plan at week 6 with the calibrated ratio (planned tasks running at about 1.5×) would have predicted roughly 300 h for the original scope alone, before any discovered work: a finish around week 12 at best, and a clear signal to integrate early. (The analysis shows the true total: 335 h for the planned tasks.)


6. Prevention (blameless). (a) Progress is reported as earned progress plus scope, generated from the tracker, not typed; verify: the status template has no free "percent complete" field. (b) Integration is planned as weekly integration milestones with acceptance tests from week 2; verify: the plan has at least one integration task per phase and the CI runs the integrated system. Also acceptable: three-point estimates with calibration, and a mandatory "failure, upgrade, duration" question per task.


Wrong answers and why. "The team was lazy": the hours were logged at a steady 25 per week; the plan was wrong, not the effort. "Reporting 90 % was a lie": the number was what the team measured (hours); the error was the measure, which is a process fix. "They should have added people at week 10": D1's argument predicts more onboarding and communication load at the moment integration needed the existing team's knowledge D1.
