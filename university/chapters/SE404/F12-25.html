Story hook

Rosa has laid bricks for years. Today Tariq, a new apprentice, joins her on the garden wall. The fastest way to finish the wall is for Rosa to lay every brick while Tariq watches. By evening the wall would be straight, and Tariq would know almost nothing.

So Rosa does something slower. She lays one brick and says what she checks: the level, the gap, the mortar. Then she hands Tariq the trowel. His first bricks are uneven. Rosa does not take the trowel back. She asks: "What does the level say?" Tariq looks, lifts the brick, and tries again. At the end of the day the wall is shorter than it could have been, and Tariq can lay a brick and check it himself.

That evening Rosa writes two lines in her notebook: "Asked before telling; he found the gap himself. Next time: let him mix the mortar." Mentoring is this: helping someone grow by letting them do the work, asking more than telling, and reflecting honestly on how you helped.

What you will be able to do

  • Distinguish mentoring from teaching, coaching, sponsoring and managing, and say which one a situation needs.
  • Run a mentoring session in which the learner sets the goal and holds the keyboard, using questions before answers.
  • Give feedback that is specific, about the work, kind and actionable.
  • Write a short structured reflection after every session and use it to change what you do next.
  • Recognise the signals of mentoring that has drifted into doing the work for the learner, from session records.
  • Follow the safeguarding rules of the peer programme when mentoring younger learners.
Jargon box
Mentoring
Simple words: helping someone with less experience grow, mostly by letting them do the work and helping them think. Analogy: the builder who hands the apprentice the trowel. Precise: a voluntary, ongoing relationship in which a more experienced person supports a less experienced person's own goals with questions, examples, feedback and access to experience; the learner owns the goals and the work (course definition). Reilly discusses mentoring as part of senior technical work D1.
Sponsorship
Simple words: using your position to open doors for someone: recommending them, giving them a visible task. Analogy: the senior builder who tells the site lead "let Tariq build the next wall himself". Precise: actions by a person with influence that create opportunities and recognition for another person, as distinct from advice; discussed alongside mentoring by Reilly D1. In the peer programme, sponsorship means recommending your mentee for a role (a reviewer, a lab assistant) when their work earns it.
Driver (pairing)
Simple words: the person who holds the keyboard. Analogy: the one holding the trowel. Precise: in a pair-working session, the person who types and runs commands while the other observes, asks and suggests; in this course's mentoring sessions the learner drives by default, and a mentor who takes the keyboard says so and gives it back (course definition).
Specific feedback
Simple words: telling someone exactly what you saw in their work, what it caused, and what to try. Analogy: "this joint is wider than the others, so the next row will tilt; try the level after every third brick." Precise: feedback that names an observable piece of work (a line, a command, a sentence), its effect, and a concrete next action, and that is about the work rather than the person; the opposite, vague feedback ("looks good", "be more careful"), cannot be acted on (course definition, consistent with the review practice of F12-04).
Reflection (written)
Simple words: writing down after each session what happened, what you did, what it caused, and what you will change. Analogy: the builder's notebook each evening. Precise: a short structured note with four parts (what happened, what I did, what effect it had, what I will do next), written by the mentor within a day of the session and read before the next one; the evidence base for the course lab (course definition).
Safeguarding
Simple words: the rules that keep young learners safe when they work with older ones. Analogy: the site rules that say an apprentice who is a child never works alone with one adult behind a closed door. Precise: the policies and practices of the programme that protect learners who are children or vulnerable from harm, including where and how sessions happen, who else is informed, how messages are exchanged, and how concerns are reported; their exact content is set by the owner of the programme and by the law where the learner lives (unverified box in the lab).

Terms from earlier chapters used here: code review (F12-04); decision quality versus outcome quality (F12-24).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): the experienced builder = the mentor; the apprentice = the learner; handing over the trowel = letting the learner drive; "what does the level say?" = asking before telling; "this joint is wider than the others" = specific feedback; the builder's notebook = the written reflection; recommending the apprentice for the next wall = sponsorship.

When you help someone learn, the work should stay in their hands. You ask questions that help them see, and you say exactly what you noticed about their work.

After each time together, you write down what you did and whether it helped. Next time you try to help a little better.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Five kinds of help

Before a session, decide which kind of help is needed; each has its place, and mentoring is only one of them (course definitions):

  • Teaching transfers knowledge the learner does not have: "this is what a pointer is". Necessary, but the chapters of the university do it at scale.
  • Mentoring supports the learner's own goals and work over time, mostly through questions, examples and feedback.
  • Coaching is mentoring narrowed to one skill with practice ("read compiler errors from the first one down").
  • Sponsoring opens opportunities: recommending, giving visible work D1.
  • Managing assigns work and evaluates it. A peer mentor never manages a mentee and never grades them; mixing the roles makes honest questions risky for the learner.

The session

Figure 1's five steps are this course's default. The goal comes from the learner ("I want to understand why my loop prints one number too many"), even when the mentor can see a bigger problem; the bigger problem can be raised as a question at step 5. The learner drives: a mentor who types solves the problem in the mentor's head, not the learner's. The mentor asks before telling, climbing a ladder only as far as needed: an open question ("what did you expect it to print?"), a narrower one ("what is i on the last time round?"), a hint ("look at the condition"), and only then showing. The learner explains back what they changed and why; if they cannot, the understanding is not there yet. Feedback closes the session, and the learner writes their own next step.

Feedback that helps

The guide sets the voice of this university: warm, precise, encouraging; praise effort and method, not talent; mistakes are normal and useful D3. In mentoring, that becomes four checks on every piece of feedback:

  • Observable: it names something the learner can find again: "line 12", "the command you ran before the build", "the second sentence of your log entry".
  • About the work: "this function does two things" rather than "you are messy".
  • With its effect: "so when it fails, you cannot tell which of the two failed".
  • With a next action: "try splitting it and writing one test for each half".

Praise follows the same rules: "you read the first error before the others, and it was the real one" teaches more than "good job". Code review taught the same discipline for comments (F12-04); mentoring feedback is spoken and more frequent, and its first purpose is the learner's growth, not the code's correctness.

Reflection is the evidence

The course lab asks for a written reflection after every session. Four parts, five to ten sentences: what happened (facts), what I did (my choices), what effect it had (on the learner's work, questions, confidence: evidence, not guesses), what I will do next. A reflection is not a diary of feelings and not a report on the learner; it is a mentor's post-mortem of their own session, written blamelessly about themselves. Over a semester the reflections show whether the mentor changed, which is what SE404 grades.

Why seniors mentor

Mentoring is not a favour done on the side. Brooks's argument about adding people to projects rests on the cost of new people learning from the people already working D2; mentoring is the deliberate, efficient form of that cost, paid early. Reilly treats raising the skill of the people around you as part of senior technical work D1. And the guide's level table names mentoring junior learners in the university's peer programme among the things a senior-level learner does D3.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The question ladder in practice

The ladder is a time trade-off made explicit. Each rung costs the learner effort and the session time; each rung climbed by the learner rather than given by the mentor leaves more behind. A useful rule: wait at least as long as you would want someone to wait for you (count silently), and climb one rung when the learner is stuck without new ideas, not when they are slow. Questions that are statements in disguise ("don't you think you should check the bounds?") skip rungs and teach the learner to guess what the mentor wants. Questions about evidence teach the method this university is built on: "what does the error say, exactly?", "what did the debugger show?", "how would you test that guess?"

When the mentor takes the keyboard

Sometimes showing is right: a tool the learner has never seen, a dangerous command, a session nearly over with the learner frustrated. The rule is to say so ("I'll show you once, then you do it"), do the smallest demonstration, and give the keyboard back. The session record notes it. A pattern of the mentor driving is the most reliable signal of drift, and Listing 1 flags it (rule F1) because it is the easiest to measure honestly: the mentor estimates the learner's share of keyboard time right after the session.

What the numbers can and cannot say

Listing 1 reads session records and flags five patterns: mentor driving, no mentor questions, goals set by the mentor, missing reflections, more vague than specific feedback. These are crude signals, chosen because a mentor can record them honestly in a minute. They do not measure learning, and they are easy to game: a mentor who asks ten empty questions satisfies F2. If the records become a target, they stop being evidence. Use them the way the course uses them: as a prompt for the written reflection ("F1 flagged three sessions in a row: why did I take the keyboard?"), never as a grade for the mentee, and never shared without the mentee's knowledge.

Boundaries

  • Not a manager, not a grader. A mentor does not assess the mentee's coursework and does not report on them to teachers, except under the safeguarding rules.
  • Not a therapist. If a learner raises worries beyond the work (wellbeing, safety, family), the mentor listens, does not investigate, and follows the programme's reporting path.
  • Not the mentee's answer key. The mentor does not do the mentee's graded labs, exams or projects, or give their answers; doing so harms the learner and breaks the university's honesty rules.
  • Honest about not knowing. "I don't know; let's find the source" is the university's method (memory is not a source, AH-3) and the best model a mentor can give D3.

How the hardware actually does it

Mentoring younger learners on hardware labs adds two rules. First, the learner still drives: they hold the probe, connect the wire, run the flash command; the mentor asks what they expect to see before each step. Second, safety rules are not the mentor's to relax. The guide requires adult supervision, low voltages and age-appropriate kits for physical labs at levels L0–L2, no mains work at any level, simulators before real robots and drones, and propellers removed for bench tests D3. A peer mentor is not the supervising adult. If a session would involve physical hardware, it happens only in a supervised lab session with the responsible adult present, or it stays in the simulator. Everything else in this chapter (the question ladder, the explain-back, the reflection) works the same with a multimeter as with a compiler.

Worked example

Situation. Session 3 of the semester. Ana (SE404) mentors Kofi (SP101). Kofi's goal: "My program crashes with an error I don't understand, and I want to fix it myself." The program reads numbers into a std::vector and prints the largest.

Step 1 — goal. Ana repeats the goal back and asks what "fixed" will look like: Kofi says "it prints the largest of my five test numbers and does not crash".

Step 2 — Kofi drives. He runs the program; the sanitizer prints a long report. Kofi starts scrolling to the bottom.

Step 3 — ladder. Open: "Which part of the report do you think matters most?" Kofi: "The last line?" Narrower: "Where does the report first name a line of your own file?" Kofi finds it: line 14, inside his loop. Narrower: "What is i on the last time round the loop?" Kofi traces it on paper and sees i reaches the size of the vector. Ana did not need a hint.

Step 4 — explain back. Kofi: "The loop went one step past the end, because I wrote <=. The sanitizer stopped it and told me the line." He changes it and reruns: the right number, no report.

Step 5 — feedback and next step. Ana: "You found the first line of the report that named your file, instead of reading from the bottom; that is the habit that works on long reports (specific, method). When you traced i on paper you found the cause in two minutes; try that before changing code next time (specific, action)." Kofi writes his next step: "Write a test with an empty vector."

Session record and reflection. Ana records session 3 40 75 3 6 mentee y 2 1 (40 minutes; Kofi drove about 75 % of the time; Kofi asked 3 questions, Ana 6; goal set by Kofi; reflection written; 2 specific and 1 vague feedback point; the vague one was "nice work" at the end). Her reflection: "Happened: sanitizer report on an off-by-one. I did: three questions, no hints, no keyboard. Effect: Kofi found the line himself and could explain it; he asked about empty vectors unprompted. Next: I said 'nice work' out of habit; replace it with what was good. He did not need the hint rung today; next time wait longer before the second question." This is the record of session 3 in mentor_log.in below.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • An apprentice's wall shows its faults; code hides them. Rosa can see a crooked row. A mentor cannot see a misunderstanding in a program that happens to work, which is why the explain-back step matters more in software than on a building site.
    • 

  • The apprentice wants to become a builder; a mentee's goals vary. A Year 1 learner may want to pass SP101, build a robot, or decide whether this field is for them. The analogy suggests one path; mentoring follows the learner's goal, which can change.
    • 

  • Peer mentoring is not a master and an apprentice. The mentor in this programme is a learner too, a few years further on, with no authority over the mentee. The analogy's hierarchy would make it a management relationship, which the programme forbids.
    •

Transition box

Goal. Mentor a learner in Years 1–2 for a semester in the university's peer programme, with a session record and a written reflection after every session, and a final reflection on how your mentoring changed (the SE404 course lab).


Hardware and software needed. For the session-log tool: any computer with g++ supporting C++20 (the build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0). For the sessions: whatever the mentee's course uses; no physical hardware unless inside a supervised lab session.


Safety
Mentees in Years 1–2 may be children. Follow the peer programme's safeguarding rules at all times. At minimum, as this course's own rules until the owner publishes the programme's: meet only in shared, visible places or in online sessions the programme has approved; do not use private messaging channels with a mentee outside the programme's channels; never be the supervising adult for any physical lab (the guide requires adult supervision for L0–L2 physical labs D3); if a mentee tells you something that worries you about their safety or wellbeing, listen, do not investigate, and report it through the programme's named contact the same day. Share session records and reflections only as the programme allows, and tell your mentee what you record.
