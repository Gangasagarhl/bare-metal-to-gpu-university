Story hook

The builders must choose the foundation before anyone knows the soil. A deep foundation costs more now and is safe on any ground. A shallow one is cheaper and quick, and perfect on firm ground, but on soft ground the walls crack and part of the house must be rebuilt. Lindiwe wants the deep one, "to be safe". Arjun wants the shallow one, "because the ground here is usually fine".

Malia, the youngest on the team, asks: "How much would it cost to dig one test hole first?" One day and a little money. If the hole shows firm ground, they build shallow and save weeks. If it shows soft ground, they build deep and avoid the cracks. Either way they decide with more knowledge than they have today.

Later, on another house, they dig the test hole, find firm ground, build shallow, and an unusual storm still damages a corner. Was the decision wrong? This chapter teaches you to tell a bad decision from bad luck, and to know when one more test hole is worth digging.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a decision as options × scenarios with payoffs and probabilities, labelling which numbers are judgements.
  • Compare options by expected value, worst case and maximum regret, and explain when each rule is the right one.
  • Value information: compute the value of perfect information and of an imperfect spike, and decide whether to run the spike first.
  • Use reversibility and sensitivity to decide how much evidence a decision needs.
  • Separate the quality of a decision from the quality of its outcome in a blameless review.
  • Diagnose a decision record that chose an irreversible option on its best case.
Jargon box
Scenario (decision)
Simple words: one way the future could turn out. Analogy: firm ground or soft ground. Precise: one member of a set of mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive states of the world relevant to a decision, each with a probability; the probabilities add up to 1 (Listing 1 refuses anything else).
Payoff (decision)
Simple words: how good or bad an option turns out in one scenario. Analogy: weeks saved or lost with the shallow foundation on firm or soft ground. Precise: the value of the consequence of choosing option o when scenario s happens, in one stated unit (here: net team-days over the project); a judgement unless measured, and labelled as such.
Expected value (decision)
Simple words: the average result if you could make the same choice many times. Analogy: the weeks the shallow foundation saves on average over many houses on this kind of ground. Precise: EV(o) = Σs ps · payoff(o, s), the mean of the payoff distribution (MA202's expected value applied to a choice); it ignores how bad the worst case is.
Regret (decision)
Simple words: how much better you could have done if you had known the future. Analogy: the weeks lost by building deep on ground that turned out firm. Precise: regret(o, s) = maxo′ payoff(o′, s) − payoff(o, s); the minimax-regret rule chooses the option whose largest regret over all scenarios is smallest.
Value of information
Simple words: how much better your decision gets, on average, if you learn something first. Analogy: what the test hole is worth before you dig it. Precise: the expected value of deciding after observing a signal (choosing the best option for each possible signal, using the posterior from Bayes' rule) minus the expected value of deciding now; the value of perfect information (signal = the true scenario) is its upper bound. Worth buying only if it exceeds the cost of getting it.
Spike
Simple words: a small, time-boxed experiment that answers one question before a big decision. Analogy: the test hole. Precise: work whose output is knowledge, not product: a prototype, a load test, a read of the datasheet, a map of callers, with a fixed time budget and a question written in advance; modelled in Listing 1 by its cost and its accuracy (the probability that it reports the true scenario).
Decision quality versus outcome quality
Simple words: a good choice can turn out badly, and a bad choice can turn out well. Analogy: the storm that damaged a well-built house. Precise: a decision is judged by the information, options, values and reasoning available when it was made (and by whether more information was worth getting); the outcome is one draw from the scenarios. A blameless review asks both questions separately (course definition).

Terms from earlier chapters used here: expected value and Bayes' rule (MA202); decision matrix; reversible decision (F12-03); risk matrix (DN401); merge bias and three-point estimate (F12-23).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): firm ground or soft ground = scenarios; weeks saved or lost = payoffs; "how sure are we the ground is firm?" = a probability; the test hole = a spike; what the hole is worth before digging = the value of information; the storm that damaged a good house = a bad outcome from a good decision; where the drain goes = an irreversible decision.

When you don't know what will happen, list the ways it could turn out, and for each choice ask: how good or bad would it be each way? Then you can see which choice is best on average, which is safest if things go badly, and whether a small test could tell you which way it will go.

If the choice is easy to undo, choose quickly and watch. If it is hard to undo, find out more first, as long as finding out costs less than it is worth.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From a matrix to a table of futures

F12-03 scored options against criteria with weights. That matrix silently assumed one future. When the right choice depends on something nobody knows yet (will the load grow? does the old code have hidden callers? will the board arrive?), write the unknown as scenarios and score each option in each scenario in one unit. The worked example uses net team-days over the project: positive means time saved against a baseline, negative means time lost. A single unit forces honest comparisons ("is the complexity of our own replicated store worth 6 days if the load stays small?") and lets the arithmetic of MA202 apply.

Three rules, three attitudes

  • Expected value chooses the best average. Right when the decision is one of many similar ones, when no single outcome is a disaster, and when the probabilities are reasonable. In the worked example: embedded-db, 0.80.
  • Best worst case (maximin) chooses the option whose worst outcome is least bad. Right when one scenario would be unacceptable (a safety failure, a missed defence date, a lost dataset), whatever its probability. Here: embedded-db again (worst −4).
  • Smallest maximum regret chooses the option you would least regret in hindsight. Useful when probabilities are very uncertain and the team wants a choice it can defend whichever future comes. Here: own-raft (max regret 12, against 14 and 25).

When the three rules agree, the decision is robust. When they disagree, as here, the disagreement is the information: it says the decision depends on the team's attitude to risk and on a probability nobody is sure of. That is exactly the situation in which buying information pays.

What is a spike worth?

If an oracle told you the future, you would pick the best option in each scenario. Averaging that over the scenarios gives the expected value with perfect information; subtracting the expected value of deciding now gives the value of perfect information, 6.80 team-days in the example R1. No experiment can be worth more. A real spike is imperfect: the load test of the example reports the true scenario 80 % of the time. Using Bayes' rule, Listing 1 computes the best choice after each possible result and the average over results: 4.16, so the spike is worth 3.36 team-days and costs 2. Run it first.

The value of information is high exactly when the rules disagree and the options are close: the decision is sensitive to the unknown. It is zero when the same option is best in every scenario (nothing the spike could say would change the choice). That gives a quick test before any arithmetic: "Is there a result of this experiment that would make us choose differently?" If not, do not run it.

Reversibility sets the evidence bar

F12-03 introduced reversible and irreversible decisions. Under uncertainty, reversibility is itself an option with value: a choice that can be undone cheaply turns a wrong guess into a small cost. Flat files can be replaced by a database later if the interface hides them; a replicated store's on-disk format and its consistency promises, once other teams depend on them, cannot. The rule this course uses: reversible and close → decide now, write the revisit trigger, watch; irreversible and close → buy information first (a spike, a read of the source, a measurement), or create reversibility (an interface, a feature flag, a migration path) before committing. The curriculum's runbook gives the same instruction to its agent: when a design decision affects later milestones, stop, present options with consequences, and let the learner decide D3.

Deciding as a senior engineer

Reilly describes senior technical work as including the decisions that span a team or several teams, made in the open and written down so others can follow the reasoning D1. For this course that means: a decision record with the scenarios, payoffs, probabilities and their authors; the rule used and why; the value of information considered; the reversibility; the revisit trigger. A decision record of this kind is also what makes a later blameless review possible.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The spike in Bayes' rule

Let the spike's result j be one of the scenarios. Listing 1's model of an imperfect spike: P(j | s) = a if j = s, and (1 − a)/(S − 1) otherwise, where a is the accuracy and S the number of scenarios (lines 157–163). The probability of result j is P(j) = Σs ps P(j | s); the posterior is P(s | j) = ps P(j | s) / P(j). After result j the team chooses the option with the best posterior expected value, so the expected value with the spike is Σj P(j) · maxo Σs P(s | j) payoff(o, s). The model assumes a symmetric error; a real load test may be better at detecting growth than at ruling it out. If that matters, write P(j | s) per pair (the extension of the lab).

Sensitivity to the probability

Probabilities in engineering decisions are judgements, often the weakest numbers in the table. Listing 1 moves the first scenario's probability from 0 to 1 (keeping the others in proportion) and prints where the expected-value choice changes (lines 192–216). For the example, the choice changes at 0.583 and 0.846 R1; the team's 0.6 is 0.017 away from a switch. A decision that close to a boundary is not a decision the team should defend with confidence: either it does not matter much (look at how far apart the expected values are: 0.80 against 0.40) or it is worth buying information. Here both are true, and the spike's value settles it.

What the model leaves out

  • Risk attitude. Expected value treats a 50 % chance of −30 and +20 as worth −5, the same as a certain −5. Teams, coordinators and users are rarely indifferent between those. Where one outcome is unacceptable, treat it as a constraint (remove the option or add a mitigation) rather than as a large negative number.
  • More than one decision. Real projects decide in stages; the option to stop, switch or extend later has value. Decision trees with several decision nodes capture it; Listing 1 handles one decision and one spike.
  • Payoffs are uncertain too. A three-point estimate per payoff (F12-23) and a simulation would show how much the uncertainty in payoffs, not only in scenarios, matters.
  • Correlated judgement. If the same two people wrote every probability and payoff, their shared optimism is in every number. Independent estimates by people with different roles, compared before averaging, are the cheapest correction (F12-03's "weights before scores" rule, applied to probabilities).

Decision quality, outcome quality

A blameless post-mortem (SE402) asks what the system and the process allowed, not who to blame. For decisions it has a precise form: given what the team knew and could have learned at reasonable cost, was the choice reasonable? A team that chose a reasonable option and met the unlucky scenario made a good decision with a bad outcome; changing their process because of it would teach the wrong lesson. A team that skipped a spike worth more than its cost, or chose an irreversible option on its best case, made a bad decision even if the outcome was good. The decision record is the evidence that allows the review to tell these apart; without it, reviews judge by outcome alone.

Rewrites: a recurring case

Whether to rewrite a working system is one of the classic decisions under uncertainty: the payoff is large if the old system's behaviour is understood and small or negative if it hides behaviour nobody wrote down. Brooks's "The Mythical Man-Month" discusses both sides of this ground: planning for a first system to be discarded, and the tendency of a team's second system to be over-designed D2. The forensic lab is a rewrite decision. The general lesson for this chapter is narrower and arithmetic: when the scenario that decides the payoff ("are there hidden callers?") can be investigated cheaply, the value of information is usually large.

How the hardware actually does it

Hardware turns many trade-offs into decisions under uncertainty with irreversible steps. Ordering a board, choosing a GPU vendor for a project's kernels, flashing firmware, fixing a connector: each is costly to undo, and each depends on facts that may not yet be in hand (does the datasheet describe the DMA engine you need? does the ROCm compatibility list include the card? does QEMU model the device?). The university's rules already encode the value of information for these cases: develop in QEMU before flashing; simulate before any robot or drone moves; check the compatibility list before buying a GPU D4. Each of those is a cheap spike before an expensive, sometimes dangerous, irreversible step. In a decision table they appear as spikes with high accuracy and low cost, which is why, by the arithmetic of Layer 2, they pay whenever a wrong irreversible step would cost much more than the check.

Worked example

Task. The MP8 team of F12-23 must decide, before its R1 design review D4, where the ground station keeps its telemetry: its own replicated store from MP2 (own-raft), an embedded database library (embedded-db), or flat files (flat-files). Nobody knows whether the load will stay on one machine until the defence.

Step 1 — scenarios. small (one machine is enough until the defence) and growth (replication across machines is needed before the defence). Two members estimate p(small) independently (0.5 and 0.7) and agree on 0.6, noting the spread.

Step 2 — payoffs in team-days, judged. own-raft: −6 if small (complexity for nothing), +10 if growth (already replicated). embedded-db: +4 if small, −4 if growth (a migration later). flat-files: +6 if small, −15 if growth (a rewrite under deadline). Written in uncertain.in with the authors' names in the decision record.

Step 3 — by hand. EV(own-raft) = 0.6 × (−6) + 0.4 × 10 = 0.4. EV(embedded-db) = 2.4 − 1.6 = 0.8. EV(flat-files) = 3.6 − 6.0 = −2.4. Worst cases −6, −4, −15. Regrets: best in small is 6, best in growth is 10; own-raft max(12, 0) = 12, embedded-db max(2, 14) = 14, flat-files max(0, 25) = 25.

Step 4 — the load-test spike. Two days, accuracy 0.8. P(says small) = 0.6 × 0.8 + 0.4 × 0.2 = 0.56. After "small": P(small) = 0.48/0.56 = 0.857; EV(flat-files) = (0.48 × 6 + 0.08 × (−15))/0.56 = 1.68/0.56 = 3.00, the best. After "growth" (0.44): P(small) = 0.12/0.44; EV(own-raft) = (0.12 × (−6) + 0.32 × 10)/0.44 = 2.48/0.44 = 5.64, the best. With the spike: 1.68 + 2.48 = 4.16; value 4.16 − 0.80 = 3.36 > 2.

Step 5 — run Listing 1 and compare. R1 prints the same numbers, the switching points 0.583 and 0.846, and "worth running first" R1.

Step 6 — the decision record. "Before R1 we run a two-day load test with recorded telemetry at the defence's planned scale. If it indicates one machine is enough, we use flat files behind the TelemetryStore interface (reversible: the interface hides the format; revisit if the soak test exceeds one machine). If it indicates growth, we use our MP2 store. Expected-value, maximin and regret disagree without the test; the team's probability 0.6 is close to a switching point (0.583). Authors of the numbers: the two members named above."

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Software spikes are often cheaper and more accurate than test holes. A load test, a map of callers or a prototype can answer the question almost completely in a day, so the value of information in software can be higher than the house suggests. "Measure, then decide" is affordable in many cases where a builder could not afford it.
    • 

  • The future of software is partly made by the team. The soil does not change because of the foundation; the load on a service can change because of the design (a fast, reliable service attracts users). Scenarios are not always independent of the options, which the simple table assumes.
    • 

  • Reversibility is designed, not given. A house's drain is hard to move by nature. In software, an interface, a feature flag or a migration tool can make an irreversible-looking choice reversible, at a cost the team decides to pay. The analogy hides that reversibility is itself an option to buy.
    •

Summary


    

  • Write uncertain decisions as options × scenarios with payoffs in one unit and probabilities that sum to 1.
    • 

  • Expected value, maximin and minimax regret express different attitudes to risk; their disagreement is information.
    • 

  • The value of perfect information bounds what any experiment is worth; compute a spike's value with Bayes' rule and compare it with its cost.
    • 

  • Ask first: "which result would change our choice?" If none, skip the spike.
    • 

  • Reversible and close: decide and watch. Irreversible and close: buy information or buy reversibility first.
    • 

  • Judge decisions by what was known and knowable, outcomes separately; the decision record makes that possible.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. Both inputs were written for this lab (constructed case) and run through Listing 1 by run.sh R2 R3.


1. The first draft. Probabilities 0.5 + 0.4 + 0.2 = 1.1: the three scenarios were written by three people and overlap ("new-hardware" can happen together with "hidden-deps"). Listing 1 refused them (exit code 2) R3. The team then dropped a scenario instead of making them exclusive, a sign that the scenarios were an afterthought to a choice already made.


2. The rules. With the approved numbers, all three rules choose patch: expected value 2.00 against −5.00 for rewrite; worst case 1 against −30; maximum regret 17 against 31 R2. The record's "saves 20 team-days" is rewrite's payoff in the smooth scenario only: the best case (a maximax rule nobody named).


3. The spike. Value of perfect information 8.50; the map-callers spike (cost 3, accuracy 0.9) is worth 6.10, about twice its cost: "worth running first" R2. After "smooth": rewrite (expected 15.00). After "hidden-deps": patch (expected 1.20). Dropping it "to save time" cost the team, in expectation, 6.10 − 3 = 3.1 team-days by their own numbers; in the event, much more.


4. Distance to the switch. Rewrite becomes the expected-value choice only above p(smooth) = 0.646; the team's own estimate was 0.5 R2. By hand: 20p − 30(1 − p) = 3p + (1 − p) gives 48p = 31, p = 0.6458.


5. Decision or outcome. Both, and the decision part is the lesson. By the team's own numbers the choice was the worst by every rule, irreversible once the old pipeline was removed, and a cheap, high-value spike was skipped. The bad outcome (hidden consumers) was the scenario the team itself rated at 50 %. Blamelessly: the process allowed a record to be approved whose summary quoted the best case and whose table contradicted it; no reviewer was asked to check the arithmetic.


6. Process changes. (a) Decision records must show expected value, worst case and maximum regret, and the summary sentence must name the rule; a reviewer checks by rerunning Listing 1 on the record's input. (b) For irreversible decisions, any spike with a computed value above its cost runs before approval, or the record states why not; a reviewer checks the spike section exists and the value is quoted. Also good: keep the old pipeline running behind an interface until the new one has passed the old one's tests (buying reversibility).


Wrong answers and why. "The team was unlucky": the bad scenario had probability 0.5 by their own estimate; luck is not the main story. "Rewrites are always wrong": after "smooth" the rewrite is the best choice by far; the error was deciding without the spike. "They needed better estimates of the payoffs": the payoffs were adequate to show the answer; the problem was how they were used.
