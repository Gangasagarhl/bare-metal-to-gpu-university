Story hook

The family visits the building site on Saturday. Kenji, the plumber, explains the problem: "The drain fall from the kitchen stack to the inspection chamber is too shallow because the invert level of the sewer connection is higher than the survey said." The family nods politely and understands nothing. They go home worried.

On Monday, Amara, who leads the builders, calls them. "We need you to choose by Wednesday. The pipe under the kitchen can't slope enough to drain, because the street's sewer is higher than the drawings showed. Option one: we raise the kitchen floor by one step, which adds about three days. Option two: we add a small pump, which adds one day but needs electricity and a service every year. We recommend option one. Nothing is damaged, and the rest of the house is on schedule."

The facts were the same. The second message started with what the family must do, gave them choices in their own terms (days, a step, a yearly service), said what the builders recommend and why, and told them what was not wrong. This chapter is about writing the second message.

What you will be able to do

  • Identify your reader, the decision they own, and what they need from you, before writing.
  • Write a status update or decision request that leads with the ask, offers options with consequences in the reader's terms, and states uncertainty as ranges.
  • Translate engineering risk and incidents into consequences for users, money, time and safety, without jargon and without losing accuracy.
  • Report status colours that their data supports, labelled in words, and change them when the data changes.
  • Check a draft with a simple tool and a reader, and fix what both find.
  • Diagnose a run of status reports that stayed green while the project's data said otherwise.
Jargon box
Stakeholder
Simple words: someone who is affected by your work or decides about it, but does not build it. Analogy: the family, the neighbours, the bank that lends the money. Precise: a person or group with an interest in a project's outcome or authority over its scope, time, money or acceptable risk; for a learner's MP8, for example, the course coordinator, the reviewers at the gates, users of the system and, for physical systems, anyone near it (course definition). Reilly treats working with stakeholders as part of senior technical work D1.
Ask-first message
Simple words: a message whose first sentence says what you need from the reader. Analogy: "we need you to choose by Wednesday" before any pipes are mentioned. Precise: a structure for written communication in which the decision, request or conclusion comes first, followed by options with consequences, the reason in the reader's terms, the evidence and confidence, and finally details for readers who want them (course definition; Figure 1).
Status colour (red/amber/green)
Simple words: a quick label for how a project is doing: fine, worried, in trouble. Analogy: the builders' sign at the gate. Precise: a three-level summary of a project's state against its plan, meaningful only if the team agreed in advance which data decides each level; in this course, the ratio of earned to planned progress (green ≥ 0.90, amber ≥ 0.75, red below), always written as a word next to any colour (course convention, Listing 2).
Watermelon status
Simple words: a project reported as green on the outside while the data inside is red. Analogy: a house painted outside with no plumbing inside. Precise: a sequence of status reports whose stated colour is better than the colour the team's own data supports, for example because the colour is chosen by feeling, by hope, or by fear of reporting bad news (informal term; course definition).
Consequence framing
Simple words: describing a technical problem by what it does to the people who care. Analogy: "the kitchen floor goes up one step" instead of "the invert level is too high". Precise: restating a technical fact as its effect on the reader's concerns (users, schedule, money, safety, legal duties), with the technical fact kept available and unchanged underneath, so that the translation can be checked (course definition).

Terms from earlier chapters used here: earned progress and estimate (F12-23); value of information (F12-24); specific feedback (F12-25).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): the family = non-engineer readers who decide; "we need you to choose by Wednesday" = the ask first; "one step up, three days" or "a pump, one day and a yearly service" = options with consequences in their terms; "nothing is damaged" = what is not affected; Kenji's pipe words = jargon; a house painted outside with no plumbing = a watermelon status.

When you explain your work to someone who does not build it, start with what you need from them. Then give choices in words they use every day, say which one you recommend, and say honestly how sure you are.

Keep the hard words for the people who ask for them. Never make things sound better than they are: people decide using what you tell them.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Start with the reader and their decision

Before writing, answer three questions. Who reads this? (A coordinator, a user, a parent of a young learner, a safety officer.) What do they own? (The date, the money, the scope, the acceptable risk, the users' experience.) What do I need from them? (A decision, an approval, information, or nothing: "for your information" is a legitimate message, but say so.) A message without an answer to the third question makes the reader search for it, and many will not. The curriculum's runbook asks its agent to "present options with consequences" when a decision affects later work D3; non-engineers need exactly that, in their units.

Translate consequences, keep the facts

Consequence framing changes the words, not the facts. "Leader election is flaky under restart" becomes "when one of the three computers restarts, the others sometimes disagree for a moment about which one is in charge, and we have not found the cause yet". Check every translation with two questions: would an engineer reading it agree it is still true? would the reader be surprised later by something it hid? If the answer to the second question is yes, the translation has become a simplification that misleads. Keep the technical version in part 5 of the message, so anyone can check the translation.

Uncertainty in numbers, with words

Non-engineers can handle uncertainty when it is given as a range with a meaning: "4 to 9 working days; the longer end if the cause is in the replication code". This is F12-23's estimate, not a weakness. A single number sounds confident and hides the uncertainty the team knows about (F12-23's single-date plan was met in 1.3 % of simulated trials); a range lets the reader plan. Words like "likely", "probably" or "soon" mean different things to different people; this course does not assume they carry a number. Use them only next to a number or a range ("likely, which we put at about 80 %, by Friday"), and in the lab, ask your own readers what number they hear for each word.

Status colours that mean something

A colour is a summary of data, and a summary needs a rule agreed in advance. This course uses the ratio of earned to planned progress from F12-23 (green ≥ 0.90, amber ≥ 0.75, red below); a team may choose another rule, but must choose before the project starts and write it in the status template. The guide forbids meaning by colour alone on every page D2, and the same rule protects status reports: write "Amber (earned 0.83 of plan)" rather than an amber dot. When the data changes the colour, change it the same week. Bad news early is a gift: it arrives while there are still options.

Explaining incidents

SE402 taught incident roles, including the person who communicates with those affected while others debug, and the blameless post-mortem D4. For non-engineers, an incident message answers, in this order: what users experience now; what they should do (or that they need do nothing); what is being done; when the next update comes (a time, and keep it); and, later, what caused it and what will prevent it, in consequence terms. It never guesses a cause before the evidence shows it, and it never blames a person.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What a tool can check, and what it cannot

Listing 1 is a deliberately simple checker. It splits a text into sentences, counts words, looks for an ask in the first two sentences, flags words from a small jargon list and capitalised acronyms not followed by an explanation in brackets, flags vague phrases ("on track", "mostly", "should be fine", "soon"), and checks whether any date or range appears. On the engineer's draft of the worked example it reports 20 issues; on the rewrite, none R1 R2. Its limits are part of the lesson:

  • It matches whole words only, so "races" and "refactoring" in the draft were not flagged although "race" and "refactor" are on its list (compare R1 with the draft).
  • Its range test looks for " to ", "between" or an en dash, so "added to be safe" counts as a range: run on the forensic headlines it reports a range that is not there R4.
  • It cannot tell whether the ask is the right ask, whether the options are honest, or whether the reader will understand. Only a reader can.

Use such a tool as a first pass that catches habits, then give the text to a real reader from the audience and ask them to tell you, in their words, what you need from them and what will happen. If they cannot, rewrite.

Honesty under pressure

The hardest communication is bad news to someone with power over you. The pressure is to soften: "minor issues", "mostly on track", "should be fine". Each softening moves risk from the team to the reader without the reader's knowledge, and the reader then decides with wrong information. The guide's own rules for this university apply to status reports as much as to chapters: no padding that pretends to knowledge, hedging words only when the evidence itself is uncertain, and say when something is hard D2. In practice: report the data, give the colour its rule supports, add your judgement separately and label it ("our judgement: we can still make the defence if option B is chosen this week"). F12-27 treats the ethical side of this.

Charts for non-engineers

The guide's diagram rules are good rules for any chart shown to a decision-maker: no decorative elements, labels in the reader's words, no meaning by colour alone, and proportions that do not suggest numbers they do not have D2. For a progress chart, that means: plot earned progress against plan on the same axis starting at zero, mark the commitment date with its percentile, and show scope changes as events, not as silent changes of the denominator. F12-23's Figure 3 is such a chart.

Communication as senior work

Reilly describes much of a senior engineer's impact as coming through others: aligning people on direction, making a project's state and risks visible, and writing for audiences beyond the team D1. In MP8, communication is graded directly: "leadership and communication" carries 20 % of the rubric, and the project ends with a public defence D2.

How the hardware actually does it

Hardware projects make communication with non-engineers more important, because the consequences are physical. A robot or drone demonstration involves people who did not build it and who may stand near it; the guide's safety rules (simulator first, propellers removed for bench tests, kill switch, clear zone, national aviation rules for outdoor flights) must be explained to them in plain words before the demonstration, not discovered during it D2. Hardware delays (a board that has not arrived, a part that failed a bench test) are also easier to explain than software delays, because they can be shown; use that, and do not hide software risk behind hardware news. When an emulator result stands in for real hardware, say so in words: "tested in QEMU; not yet tested on the board" is exactly the kind of sentence a non-engineer needs and will remember.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The family can see the house; users cannot see software. The family can visit the site and judge for themselves. A coordinator cannot inspect a replicated store, so they depend entirely on what the team writes. That raises the duty of honesty above what the analogy suggests.
    • 

  • Software options are rarely as clean as "a step or a pump". Software choices often have uncertain consequences and interact; a faithful message sometimes needs F12-24's scenarios, not two fixed options. The analogy makes translation look easier than it is.
    • 

  • Non-engineers are not one audience. The family is one group with one interest. A project's readers include a coordinator, users, a safety officer and perhaps parents of young learners, each with different decisions; one message rarely serves all of them.
    •

Transition box

Mini-project


The defence, for a non-engineer. Prepare a three-minute opening for your MP8 defence aimed at a reviewer who is not an engineer in your area: what the system does for whom, the one decision you are proudest of and why (in consequence terms), the one risk that remains, and how sure you are that it works (with the evidence: tests in QEMU or simulation, untested on hardware where true). Present it to two readers from other faculties and record their one-sentence summaries. Rubric: readers' summaries match your intent (30 %); consequence framing that remains technically true (25 %); honest uncertainty with evidence, including what is untested (25 %); structure and time (20 %).

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. watermelon.in was written for this lab from the earned-progress column of F12-23's analysis run and a 200-hour plan at 25 hours per week; the headlines are constructed. Listing 2 and Listing 1 were run on it R3 R4.



1. Weeks lost. The data supported red from week 2 (and unambiguously from week 6, when 75 % of the planned hours had produced 40 % earned); the first amber report was week 13. 13 of 16 reports were rosier than their data R3. From week 6 to week 13 the coordinator lost seven weeks in which scope could have been cut or the demonstration changed.


2. Week 2. Partly unfair: with tasks of 20–50 hours, nothing can be finished in two weeks, so earned progress is 0 by construction. That is itself the finding: tasks too large to show progress weekly make honest status impossible. The fix is smaller tasks with acceptance tests (F12-23), not a softer rule. From week 4 onward the red is fair.


3. Headlines that hid the most. Week 8, "90 % done, final integration under way": hid that 36.4 % of known work was finished and that integration had not started its real work; honest: "About a third of the work is finished at the planned end date. We need to agree this week what to cut." Week 10, "Minor data-migration work found, no impact expected": hid a 25-hour task found at week 10 with the project already past its plan; honest: "We found 25 hours of work to move old data; this adds about a week on top of the delay we reported." Week 11, "Mostly on track": hid everything. (Week 15's "Should be done soon" and week 16's "Basically done" are also good answers.)


4. The range claim. False: Listing 1 matched " to " in "added to be safe" R4; no headline gives a range or a date. The same run flags 12 vague phrases and no ask in any headline.


5. The week-6 message. For example: "We need to agree by Friday how to reduce the scope or move the demonstration. At the planned 75 % of our hours we have finished 40 % of the work, so at this rate the project needs about twice the planned time.
