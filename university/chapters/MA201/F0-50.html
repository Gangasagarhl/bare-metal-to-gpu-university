Story hook

Analogy

Lina's shopping list from F0-49 worked so well that her dad wants one for each day. On Saturday the neighbours come, so the plan is 3 batches of pancakes and 2 cakes. On Sunday it is only the family: 1 batch of pancakes and 1 cake. He writes the plan as a second little table: dishes down the side, days across the top.

Lina sees what to do. Saturday's column of the plan is an order, so the recipe card times that column is Saturday's shopping list, exactly as last week. Sunday's column gives Sunday's list. Put the two lists side by side and you have a new table: ingredients down the side, days across the top. "We multiplied a table by a table," she says.

That is matrix multiplication. Each box of the answer is one row of the first table combined with one column of the second. Graphics cards spend much of their working life doing exactly this, on tables with thousands of rows, under the name GEMM. This chapter teaches it by hand and in C++, and shows why it is the workload GPUs were made for.

What you will be able to do

  • Multiply two matrices by hand, entry by entry, as row-times-column dot products, and decide when a product is defined and what shape it has.
  • Explain the product AB as "first B, then A" for transformations, and show with an example that AB and BA usually differ.
  • Write a C++ matrix multiply and check it against results computed by hand (the MA201 lab).
  • Count the work of a matrix product (m·n·k multiply-adds) and compare it with the amount of data, the reason GEMM suits GPUs.
  • Diagnose a matrix multiply whose results are right only in the top-left entry.
Jargon box
Matrix product
Simple words: combining two tables into a third, where each box of the answer is "a row of the first times a column of the second". Analogy: recipe card times weekend plan gives ingredients per day. Precise: for A of shape m × k and B of shape k × n, C = AB is m × n with cij = Σp aip bpj (p from 0 to k − 1), the dot product of row i of A and column j of B. D1B1
Inner dimension
Simple words: the size that must match for two matrices to be multiplied. Analogy: the plan must list the same dishes that the recipe card has columns for. Precise: in (m × k)(k × n) the shared k; it disappears from the result's shape m × n. D1
Non-commutative
Simple words: swapping the order changes the answer. Analogy: putting on socks then shoes is not shoes then socks. Precise: in general AB ≠ BA; for non-square shapes one of the two may not even be defined. D1B1
GEMM (general matrix multiply)
Simple words: the standard name for "multiply two big matrices" in numerical software. Analogy: the whole kitchen planning a whole month. Precise: the general matrix–matrix multiply routine of numerical libraries; in the BLAS interface it also scales and adds, C = αAB + βC; SGEMM is its single-precision form (SP302 F2-56, CU302). B3
Multiply-add (FMA count)
Simple words: one "multiply two numbers and add to a running total" step. Analogy: one line of Lina's sums, such as "100 × 3 added to the flour total". Precise: the unit of work of a matrix product; C = AB with shapes (m × k)(k × n) needs m·n·k multiply-adds, usually counted as 2mnk floating-point operations (flops). D1R3
Tile (block)
Simple words: a small square piece of a big matrix. Analogy: working on one corner of a big table of numbers at a time. Precise: a submatrix; matrices cut into tiles multiply like matrices of numbers, with tile products in place of number products, which is the basis of cache and GPU tiling (SP302 F2-52, CU302 F6-22). B1R2

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows how one entry of a product is made: the highlighted row of A meets the highlighted column of B, and their dot product is the highlighted entry of C. Figure 2 shows the same rule one level up, with tiles instead of single numbers.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

To multiply two tables, you fill in the answer table one box at a time. For the box in row i and column j, take row i of the first table and column j of the second. Multiply their numbers in pairs, first with first, second with second, and add. That is the dot product from F0-48.

For Lina's weekend: the flour row of the recipe card is (100, 200). Saturday's column of the plan is (3, 2). Flour on Saturday: 100 × 3 + 200 × 2 = 700 g. Sunday's column is (1, 1), so flour on Sunday is 100 + 200 = 300 g. Eggs and milk work the same way. The whole answer is:

ingredientSaturdaySunday
flour (g)700300
eggs94
milk (ml)550200

The row of the first table and the column of the second must be the same length. Here both have 2 numbers, one per dish. If the plan listed three dishes but the card had only two, the multiplication would make no sense.

Order matters. Multiplying the plan by the recipe card, the other way round, is not even possible here: the plan's rows have 2 numbers, the card's columns have 3. And even for two square tables, swapping them usually changes the answer, as you will see.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Let A be m × k and B be k × n. The product C = AB is the m × n matrix with D1B1

c[i][j] = a[i][0]·b[0][j] + a[i][1]·b[1][j] + … + a[i][k−1]·b[k−1][j]

The inner dimension k must match and disappears; the outer dimensions m and n give the shape. Example: (2 × 3)(3 × 2) is 2 × 2 and needs 2 × 2 × 3 = 12 multiply-adds, while (3 × 2)(2 × 3) is 3 × 3 and needs 3 × 3 × 2 = 18. Run R1 prints both: AB is [[58, 64], [139, 154]], BA is a 3 × 3 matrix. D1R1

Three useful views

  • Entry view: cij = (row i of A) · (column j of B). This is Figure 1 and the loop in Listing 1.
  • Column view: column j of C is A times column j of B. Each column of the plan is an order; each column of the answer is that order's shopping list (F0-49).
  • Composition view: (AB)x = A(Bx) for every vector x. Applying B and then A to a vector is the same as applying the single matrix AB. The matrix written on the right acts first. B1R4

Rules that hold, and one that does not

Matrix multiplication is associative, (AB)C = A(BC), and distributes over addition, A(B + C) = AB + AC. It is not commutative. With P = [[1, 2], [3, 4]] and the swap matrix S = [[0, 1], [1, 0]], PS = [[2, 1], [4, 3]] (S on the right swaps P's columns) while SP = [[3, 4], [1, 2]] (S on the left swaps P's rows). Listing 1 checks both against hand results. For transformations this is everyday experience: "turn left, then step forward" ends somewhere else than "step forward, then turn left", and F0-52 to F0-54 depend on getting such orders right. B1R1

Counting the work

Each of the m·n entries of C needs k multiply-adds, so C = AB costs m·n·k multiply-adds, or 2mnk floating-point operations if you count the multiply and the add separately. For square n × n matrices that is n³ multiply-adds on 3n² numbers (A, B and C). Listing 3 prints the table: doubling n multiplies the work by 8 but the data only by 4. The ratio of work to data, 2n³ / 3n² = 2n/3 operations per number, grows with n: for n = 4096 it is about 2,731. A computation that does many operations per number it reads is called compute-bound, and that is exactly what a GPU's thousands of arithmetic units need to stay busy. D1R3B2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Block multiplication. Cut A, B and C into tiles of matching sizes. Then tile (I, J) of C is the sum over P of tile (I, P) of A times tile (P, J) of B, the same formula as for numbers, with matrix products inside (Figure 2). Listing 2 computes a 6 × 6 product in 2 × 2 tiles and in the naive order and finds them exactly equal; with whole-number entries no rounding occurs, so the different order of additions cannot show. With general floating-point data, a different summation order can change the last bits of the result, which is why GEMM tests use tolerances (MA302). B1R2

Why tiles matter. In the naive loop, each number of A and B is read n times from wherever it lives. If a tile of A and a tile of B fit in fast memory (a CPU cache, a GPU block's shared memory), every number loaded is used for a whole tile of results before being dropped. That raises the operations performed per byte moved from slow memory, the quantity the roofline model of CU302 plots against the hardware's limits. The arithmetic is unchanged; only the order of the same multiply-adds changes. B2U1

The library interface. Numerical libraries expose the product as GEMM, which in the BLAS interface computes C ← αAB + βC and takes flags saying whether A or B should be transposed and how the matrices are laid out in memory. SP302 F2-56 builds a tuned CPU SGEMM and curriculum milestone E6 builds GPU versions measured against vendor libraries (Systems Curriculum, Track E). B3U2

Not verified — check before relying on this

The exact BLAS GEMM argument list (names and order of the transpose flags, leading dimensions, α and β) and the storage order each implementation assumes are not reproduced here. Check the BLAS reference documentation, and for GPUs the cuBLAS and rocBLAS documentation of the versions in CU302's dossier, before calling any of them.

How the hardware actually does it

A CPU executes Listing 1's inner line as a load of aip, a load of bpj, a multiply and an add (or one fused multiply-add instruction if the compiler uses one). Nothing in the hardware knows it is doing linear algebra. What the hardware does care about is where the numbers come from: bpj walks down a column of a row-major matrix, jumping a whole row ahead in memory each step, which uses caches poorly for large n (SP302 F2-52 measures this). GPUs and the matrix units of recent processors (HW301 F1-61, tensor cores and matrix cores) are built to perform many multiply-adds per number loaded, which is why the GEMM shape of computation, with its 2n/3 operations per number, runs well on them. This chapter makes no claim about the speed of any particular processor: CU302 measures that on real hardware. B2U1R3

Worked example

Part A — by hand. A = [[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]] (2 × 3), B = [[7, 8], [9, 10], [11, 12]] (3 × 2). The product is 2 × 2.

  • c00 = 1·7 + 2·9 + 3·11 = 7 + 18 + 33 = 58
  • c01 = 1·8 + 2·10 + 3·12 = 8 + 20 + 36 = 64
  • c10 = 4·7 + 5·9 + 6·11 = 28 + 45 + 66 = 139
  • c11 = 4·8 + 5·10 + 6·12 = 32 + 50 + 72 = 154

Part B — order of application. With P = [[1, 2], [3, 4]], S = [[0, 1], [1, 0]] and x = (5, 6): Sx = (6, 5), then P(Sx) = (1·6 + 2·5, 3·6 + 4·5) = (16, 38). Directly, (PS)x = [[2, 1], [4, 3]] (5, 6) = (16, 38). Same answer, as the composition view promises.

Part C — counting. The recipe product (3 × 2)(2 × 2) needs 3 · 2 · 2 = 12 multiply-adds. Two 4096 × 4096 matrices need 2 · 4096³ = 137,438,953,472 floating-point operations on 3 · 4096² = 50,331,648 numbers, about 201 million bytes in 4-byte single precision.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Kitchens do not mind the order; matrices do. You could buy Sunday's milk before Saturday's flour. But the product of the plan and the recipe card in the other order is not defined at all, and for square matrices it usually gives a different answer. B1
    • 

  • Real recipes are not linear. Twice the batches may not mean exactly twice the eggs (you cannot buy half an egg). Matrix products are exactly linear. B1
    • 

  • The recipe table is small; GEMM tables are huge. Lina does 12 multiply-adds by hand. A 4096 × 4096 product is over 68 billion multiply-adds, and how the numbers move through memory, not the arithmetic, then decides how long it takes. R3B2
    • 

  • Tiles are not a kitchen idea. Cutting the tables into blocks gives the same answer but changes nothing for Lina. On a computer it changes what fits in fast memory, which the analogy cannot show. B2
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F0-49's matrix–vector product and F0-48's dot product. What carries over: every entry of AB is a dot product; every column of AB is a matrix–vector product; row-major storage and the index formula are unchanged. What changes: order now matters (AB ≠ BA), shapes must match on the inside, and the cost grows as n³ while the data grow as n², so how the numbers move through memory becomes the main engineering question. What comes next: F0-51 introduces the matrices that do nothing (identity), undo (inverse) and flip (transpose), the tools for undoing a rotation in F0-52. D1B2

Summary


    

  • For A (m × k) and B (k × n), C = AB is m × n and cij = (row i of A) · (column j of B).
    • 

  • The inner dimensions must match; the outer ones give the shape.
    • 

  • Column j of AB is A times column j of B; (AB)x = A(Bx), so the right-hand matrix acts first.
    • 

  • Matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative.
    • 

  • The work is m·n·k multiply-adds (2mnk flops): n³ for square matrices on 3n² numbers.
    • 

  • Tiles multiply like numbers; tiling changes the order of the same work so that loaded numbers are reused.
    • 

  • Always test a multiply on non-square matrices with all-different entries, against hand results.
