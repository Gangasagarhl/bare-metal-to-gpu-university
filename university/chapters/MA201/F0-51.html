Story hook

Analogy

Every morning Amara puts on her socks and then her shoes. Every evening she takes them off. She has never once taken off her socks first: the shoes are in the way. To undo two steps, you undo the last one first.

That evening, Kenji's robot draws a map of the kitchen floor, but the map comes out slanted and stretched, because one wheel is a little bigger than the other and the robot's odometry is off in a known way. Kenji knows the matrix that describes the distortion. "Can I run it backwards?" he asks. Sometimes you can: the backwards matrix is the inverse. Sometimes you cannot: if the distortion squashed the whole floor onto a single line, the map has lost information and nothing can bring it back.

This chapter introduces three special matrices. The identity does nothing at all, like a recipe step that says "leave it as it is". The inverse undoes a matrix, and undoing two of them follows Amara's socks-and-shoes rule. The transpose flips a table across its diagonal. For rotations, the most important matrices of this course, the inverse and the transpose turn out to be the same thing.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the identity matrix of any size and the transpose of any matrix, and use the rule (AB)T = BTAT.
  • Compute the determinant and the inverse of a 2 × 2 matrix, and check the inverse by multiplying.
  • Decide when a matrix has no inverse, and explain why with a picture (area squashed to zero).
  • Undo a product of transformations with (AB)−1 = B−1A−1.
  • Diagnose an inverse formula that passes a symmetric test and fails on real data.
Jargon box
Identity matrix
Simple words: the matrix that leaves every vector as it is. Analogy: a recipe step that says "leave it as it is". Precise: the n × n matrix I with ones on the main diagonal and zeros elsewhere; IA = A and AI = A whenever the shapes fit, and Ix = x. D1B1
Transpose
Simple words: the same table with rows turned into columns. Analogy: flipping the recipe card over its diagonal, so dishes run down the side and ingredients across the top. Precise: for an m × n matrix A, AT is the n × m matrix with (AT)ij = aji. A matrix with AT = A is symmetric. D1B1
Determinant (2 × 2)
Simple words: one number that says how much a matrix scales areas, and whether it flips them. Analogy: how many floor tiles one tile becomes after the robot's distortion. Precise: for [[a, b], [c, d]], det = ad − bc; |det| is the area of the image of the unit square, and det = 0 exactly when the matrix has no inverse. D1B1
Inverse matrix
Simple words: the matrix that undoes another one. Analogy: taking off what you put on. Precise: for a square matrix A, the matrix A−1 with A A−1 = A−1A = I, if it exists; for 2 × 2, A−1 = (1 / det) [[d, −b], [−c, a]]. D1B1
Singular matrix
Simple words: a matrix that cannot be undone. Analogy: a squashed map: two different floor tiles land on the same spot, so you cannot tell which one it was. Precise: a square matrix with determinant 0; it sends some non-zero vector to the zero vector and has no inverse. A matrix that has an inverse is called invertible (or non-singular). D1B1
Orthogonal matrix
Simple words: a square matrix whose transpose undoes it. Analogy: a turn of the whole floor, which you undo by turning back. Precise: a square Q with QTQ = I, so Q−1 = QT; its columns are unit vectors at right angles to each other. Rotation matrices (F0-52) are orthogonal. B1R1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows a matrix with an inverse and one without. The first turns the unit square into a parallelogram of area 10, and its inverse turns it back. The second squashes the whole square onto a line; once that has happened, no matrix can unsquash it.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The matrix that does nothing

The number 1 is special for multiplication: 1 × 7 = 7. Matrices have a "1" too. It is the identity matrix, written I. For 2 × 2 it is [[1, 0], [0, 1]]: ones on the diagonal from top-left to bottom-right, zeros everywhere else. Multiply any vector or matrix by it and nothing changes. Its columns are the basis vectors ex and ey, which by F0-49's rule means "send each axis to itself".

Undoing

For numbers, dividing by 4 undoes multiplying by 4, because 4 × ¼ = 1. The matrix version is the inverse: A−1 is the matrix that brings you back, so A−1 times A is I. If the robot's distortion sends the point (1, 2) to (18, 14), the inverse sends (18, 14) back to (1, 2).

Some matrices cannot be undone. The matrix F = [[2, 4], [1, 2]] sends (1, 0) to (2, 1) and (0, 1) to (4, 2), which is just twice (2, 1). Both axes land on the same line, so the whole floor is squashed onto that line. Many different points end up on the same spot, and from the spot alone you cannot tell where they came from. Such a matrix is singular.

One number that tells you

For a 2 × 2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]], the number ad − bc is called the determinant. It tells you how many times bigger the area of a shape becomes. For A = [[4, 7], [2, 6]] it is 4 × 6 − 7 × 2 = 10: the unit square becomes a parallelogram of area 10 (Figure 1). For F it is 2 × 2 − 4 × 1 = 0: area squashed to nothing, so no inverse.

Flipping a table

The transpose AT swaps rows and columns: the first row becomes the first column, and so on. The recipe card of F0-49 had ingredients down the side; its transpose has dishes down the side. Nothing is added or lost, the table is only read the other way.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Transpose and its rules

For A of shape m × n, AT has shape n × m and (AT)ij = aji: [[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]]T = [[1, 4], [2, 5], [3, 6]]. Transposing twice gives A back. A vector written as a column x has a row version xT, and the dot product is a matrix product: u · v = uTv, a 1 × n times an n × 1 (F0-50 check question 4). The product rule reverses the order: D1B1

(AB)ᵀ = Bᵀ Aᵀ

Shapes force it: if A is m × k and B is k × n, then (AB)T is n × m, and only BT (n × k) times AT (k × m) has that shape. Listing 1 checks it on numbers: both sides print [[4, 2], [15, 10]]. D1R1

The 2 × 2 inverse

For A = [[a, b], [c, d]] with det A = ad − bc ≠ 0: D1B1

A⁻¹ = (1 / (ad − bc)) · [[d, −b], [−c, a]]

Swap the two diagonal entries, change the sign of the other two, divide everything by the determinant. You can check it by multiplying: the top-left entry of A A−1 is (a·d + b·(−c)) / det = (ad − bc) / det = 1, and the top-right is (a·(−b) + b·a) / det = 0. For A = [[4, 7], [2, 6]], det = 10 and A−1 = [[0.6, −0.7], [−0.2, 0.4]]. Listing 1 prints both A A−1 and A−1A as the identity. D1R1

The inverse solves equations. If Ax = b, then x = A−1b. With b = (18, 14): x = (0.6·18 − 0.7·14, −0.2·18 + 0.4·14) = (1, 2), and indeed 4·1 + 7·2 = 18 and 2·1 + 6·2 = 14. For large systems, numerical software solves Ax = b directly instead of forming A−1, which is faster and loses less accuracy (MA302 F0-77). D1B1R2

Undoing two steps

If B is done first and A second, the combined matrix is AB (F0-50). To undo it, undo A first, then B: B1R1

(AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹ A⁻¹

This is the socks-and-shoes rule, and it has the same reversed order as the transpose rule. Listing 1 prints both sides as [[1, −1.5], [−0.2, 0.4]] for B = [[1, 2], [0, 1]]. Determinants multiply, det(AB) = det A · det B (10 · 1 = 10 here), which shows that a product is singular exactly when one of its factors is. B1R2

When the transpose is the inverse

The quarter-turn matrix Q = [[0, −1], [1, 0]] turns ex into (0, 1) and ey into (−1, 0). Listing 1 prints its inverse and its transpose: both are [[0, 1], [−1, 0]]. Matrices with QTQ = I are orthogonal. Their columns are unit vectors at right angles: the entry (i, j) of QTQ is the dot product of column i with column j, which is 1 when i = j and 0 otherwise. Every rotation matrix has this property, so undoing a rotation never needs a division: transpose it. That fact is the core of F0-52 and F0-53, and swapping a rotation for its transpose by mistake is the bug in F0-53's forensic lab. B1R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Larger inverses. For n × n matrices the inverse exists exactly when det A ≠ 0, equivalently when the columns are linearly independent (no column is a combination of the others). It is computed by Gauss–Jordan elimination: row operations that turn [A | I] into [I | A−1]. Formulas like the 2 × 2 one exist for 3 × 3 (via cofactors) but become impractical beyond that. MA302 treats elimination and its numerical stability. B1

"Exactly zero" does not exist in floating point. Run R2 computes the top-right entry of A A−1 from the printed inverse values as 4·(−0.7) + 7·0.4 and gets 4.44089e-16, not 0: 0.7 and 0.4 have no exact binary representation. Listing 1 therefore prints through a small tidy function that shows values below 5 × 10−10 as 0, and its singularity test asks whether |det| is below 10−12 rather than equal to 0. Both thresholds are choices for this lesson, sized for numbers near 1; real code chooses tolerances relative to the size of the data (MA302 F0-75, F0-78). A matrix with a tiny but non-zero determinant is technically invertible but its inverse amplifies rounding errors enormously; this is called being ill-conditioned. B2R2

Returning "no answer" in C++. inverse returns std::optional<Mat2>: either a matrix or nothing. The caller must check before using the value, which makes "this matrix has no inverse" impossible to ignore silently. The forensic lab's version returns a plain matrix and would divide by zero on a singular input. B3

How the hardware actually does it

The transpose does no arithmetic at all: it is a different way of reading the same numbers, so a library can often "transpose" by swapping the row and column strides of F0-49 instead of moving data, which is why BLAS-style interfaces take transpose flags (F0-50). The 2 × 2 inverse needs two multiplications and a subtraction for the determinant, one division, and four multiplications by its reciprocal or four divisions. Floating-point division is typically slower than multiplication on many processors, which is one reason code often computes 1/det once and multiplies; the exact cost is processor-specific and this chapter states no number for it. B2U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The relative cost of floating-point division and multiplication on a particular processor is not stated here. Check the processor vendor's optimisation manual or instruction tables for the exact processor before relying on a "division is slower" argument; measure on your own machine with the benchmark method of MA202 F0-63.

Worked example

Kenji's robot distorts its map with D = [[4, 7], [2, 6]]: a true floor point p appears on the map at Dp. The map shows the charger at (18, 14). Where is it really?

  1. Determinant: 4·6 − 7·2 = 24 − 14 = 10. Not zero, so D can be undone.
  2. Inverse: swap 4 and 6, negate 7 and 2, divide by 10: D−1 = [[0.6, −0.7], [−0.2, 0.4]].
  3. Check: row 0 of D times column 0 of D−1 is 4·0.6 + 7·(−0.2) = 2.4 − 1.4 = 1; row 0 times column 1 is 4·(−0.7) + 7·0.4 = 0 (in exact arithmetic; R2 shows the floating-point value 4.44089e-16).
  4. Undo: p = D−1(18, 14) = (10.8 − 9.8, −3.6 + 5.6) = (1, 2).
  5. Verify: D(1, 2) = (4 + 14, 2 + 12) = (18, 14). The charger is really at (1, 2).
  6. Area: each floor tile appears on the map with area |det D| = 10 tiles.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Socks and shoes are two steps; matrices can be many. The reversal rule extends to any number of factors: (ABC)−1 = C−1B−1A−1. Getting dressed rarely needs that, but a chain of robot frames does (F0-54). B1
    • 

  • Some things in life can be undone in any order. Taking off a hat and a scarf can happen either way round. Matrix inverses always need the reversed order unless the matrices happen to commute. B1
    • 

  • A squashed map is not always useless in real life. A photo of a room loses depth but you can still guess a lot from experience. A singular matrix gives the computer nothing to guess with: the lost direction is gone. D1
    • 

  • Flipping a recipe card changes nothing; transposing a transformation does. AT describes a different transformation from A (unless A is symmetric); only for orthogonal matrices is it the undo. B1
    •

Summary


    

  • The identity I has ones on the diagonal; IA = AI = A.
    • 

  • The transpose swaps rows and columns; (AB)T = BTAT; u · v = uTv.
    • 

  • det [[a, b], [c, d]] = ad − bc is the area scale; det = 0 means singular: no inverse.
    • 

  • A−1 = (1/det)[[d, −b], [−c, a]] for 2 × 2; always check A·A−1 = I.
    • 

  • (AB)−1 = B−1A−1: undo the last step first.
    • 

  • Orthogonal matrices (all rotations) satisfy Q−1 = QT.
    • 

  • In floating point, compare with tolerances and treat "nearly singular" with care.
