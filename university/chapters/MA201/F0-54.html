Story hook

Analogy

Amara phones home from work and asks Kenji to find a spoon. "It's in the second drawer," she says, "of the cupboard that's to the left of the fridge." Kenji does not need a map of the whole kitchen. He finds the fridge, steps to the cupboard on its left, opens the second drawer and finds the spoon. Each instruction is relative to the previous place: the spoon is described relative to the drawer, the drawer relative to the cupboard, the cupboard relative to the fridge.

Robots describe things the same way. A camera sees a cup relative to the camera. The camera is bolted to the robot at a known place and angle. The robot stands somewhere in the room. To put the cup on the room's map, the program must follow the chain: cup in the camera's frame, camera in the robot's frame, robot in the world frame. Each link is a rotation plus a shift (F0-53).

Doing "rotate, then shift" by hand at every link gets messy fast. This chapter shows a trick that packs a rotation and a shift into one matrix, the homogeneous transform, so that following a chain of frames becomes multiplying matrices (F0-50). It is the form used throughout robotics texts and 3D graphics. B2

What you will be able to do

  • Write a 2D pose as a 3 × 3 homogeneous matrix and a 3D pose as a 4 × 4 one, and point out the rotation block, the shift column and the fixed bottom row.
  • Chain transforms with matrix products, TWS = TWR TRS, and check the order by matching frame letters.
  • Invert a transform without a general inverse: [RT, −RTt].
  • Distinguish points (w = 1), which feel the shift, from directions (w = 0), which only turn.
  • Diagnose a chain multiplied in the wrong order from a log in which a still object seems to move.
Jargon box
Homogeneous coordinates
Simple words: a point written with one extra number at the end, a 1. Analogy: a parcel label with an extra tick box saying "this is a place, not a direction". Precise: the 2D point (x, y) is written (x, y, 1) and the 3D point (x, y, z) is written (x, y, z, 1); the extra component w lets a single matrix product perform both a rotation and a translation. D1B2
Homogeneous transform
Simple words: one matrix that holds both a turn and a shift. Analogy: one complete instruction, "from the fridge, turn left and walk two steps". Precise: the (n+1) × (n+1) matrix T = [R t; 0 1], with an n × n rotation R, an n-vector t, and a bottom row of zeros followed by 1; T(p, 1) = (Rp + t, 1). For n = 3 it is a 4 × 4 matrix. D1B2
Composition of transforms
Simple words: following several frame changes in a row by multiplying their matrices. Analogy: "drawer in cupboard, cupboard next to fridge" combined into "from the fridge, here is the spoon". Precise: if TAB maps B coordinates to A coordinates and TBC maps C to B, then TAC = TABTBC; the inner letters must match, and the product does not commute (F0-50). B2R1
Inverse transform
Simple words: the transform that undoes another, going back the opposite way. Analogy: the directions from the spoon back to the fridge. Precise: for T = [R t; 0 1], T−1 = [RT −RTt; 0 1], so TBA = TAB−1. D1R1
Direction vector
Simple words: an arrow that says which way, not where. Analogy: "towards the window" is the same instruction from anywhere in the room. Precise: a vector written with w = 0, such as (x, y, 0); a homogeneous transform rotates it and ignores the shift, because the shift column is multiplied by 0. Displacements (F0-47), velocities and axes are directions. D1R1
Frame tree
Simple words: the family tree of all the frames on a robot, each one attached to a parent. Analogy: spoon in drawer, drawer in cupboard, cupboard in kitchen. Precise: a tree whose nodes are frames and whose edges are transforms; the transform between any two frames is the product of the transforms along the path between them, inverting edges that are walked backwards. B2U1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the chain used in this chapter's lab, and the anatomy of one homogeneous matrix. Each arrow is a transform that turns coordinates in the frame at its tail into coordinates in the frame at its head. The long arrow is their product.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

In F0-53, moving a point from the robot's frame to the room's frame took two steps: turn it, then shift it. Turning is a matrix product; shifting is an addition. A chain of three frames would mean turn, shift, turn, shift, turn, shift, and it is easy to lose track.

Here is the trick. Write every point with an extra 1 at the end: (2, 0) becomes (2, 0, 1). Then build a slightly bigger matrix: put the turning matrix in the top-left corner, the shift in the last column, and a row of 0, 0, 1 at the bottom. When this bigger matrix multiplies (2, 0, 1), the last column is multiplied by that 1 and gets added in. Turning and shifting happen in one multiplication.

Now a chain is a list of matrices, and following the chain is multiplying them together, like the drawer–cupboard–fridge directions combined into one. You can multiply the matrices once and reuse the result for every point the sensor sees.

The extra number does one more job. A place gets a 1 and feels the shift. A direction, such as "forward", gets a 0 and does not, because "forward" does not move when you walk across the room; it only turns when you turn.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

One matrix for rotate-then-shift

For a 2D pose (x, y, θ), the homogeneous transform is D1B2

T = [[cos θ, −sin θ, x], [sin θ, cos θ, y], [0, 0, 1]]

Multiply it by (u, v, 1): the first row gives u cos θ − v sin θ + x, the second u sin θ + v cos θ + y, the third 1. That is exactly R(θ)p + t from F0-53, with a 1 riding along. In 3D the same layout gives a 4 × 4 matrix with a 3 × 3 rotation, a 3-vector shift and the bottom row (0, 0, 0, 1). D1B2

Chains are products; letters must match

Name each transform by its two frames: TWR takes R coordinates and gives W coordinates. Then B2R1

p_W = T_WR p_R = T_WR (T_RS p_S) = (T_WR T_RS) p_S = T_WS p_S

The rule is the one for matrix shapes in F0-50: inner letters must match (R next to R) and the outer letters name the result. Writing TRSTWR puts S next to W, which has no meaning, and the computed number is wrong. Matrix products do not commute, so the compiler cannot catch this; only the letters can. D1

The cheap inverse

F0-53 found pB = RT(pW − t) = RTpW − RTt. Written as a matrix: D1B1

T⁻¹ = [[Rᵀ, −Rᵀ t], [0, 1]]

No determinant and no division are needed (compare F0-51). Run R1 checks that TWSTSW is the identity and that the cup returns to (1.5, 0) in the sensor frame. R1

Points and directions

Multiply T by (u, v, 0): the shift column is multiplied by 0 and disappears, so the result is (R(u, v), 0). This is right for anything that is a direction rather than a place: a displacement (F0-47), a velocity, a sensor's viewing direction. In run R1 the sensor's forward direction (1, 0, 0) becomes (0.7660, 0.6428, 0), a unit vector at 40° (30° robot plus 10° mount), with no shift added. D1R1

Watch out

Feeding a direction with w = 1 adds the robot's position to a velocity or an axis, which is meaningless. Feeding a point with w = 0 forgets the robot's position. Both compile, both print plausible numbers.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why "homogeneous". In projective geometry, (x, y, w) with w ≠ 0 stands for the point (x/w, y/w); all multiples of the vector describe the same point. This course keeps w = 1 for points and w = 0 for directions. Camera models in computer vision use the general w to describe perspective, where far things look smaller; that is beyond MA201. B1B2

The rigid-motion group. Matrices of the form [R t; 0 1] with R a rotation describe all rigid motions (motions that keep distances and handedness). Products and inverses of such matrices have the same form, which is why chains and inverses never leave the family. Robotics texts call this set SE(2) in the plane and SE(3) in space. Floating-point rounding slowly breaks the property after many products; libraries re-orthonormalise R (F0-55 shows the same issue for quaternions). B2

Cost. A general 4 × 4 by 4 × 4 product takes 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 multiply-adds (F0-50) and a 4 × 4 matrix times a point takes 16; exploiting the fixed bottom row saves some of each. Applying one transform to a whole point cloud is a matrix product of the 4 × 4 transform with a 4 × N matrix of points, which is GEMM-shaped work. D1R2

Not verified — check before relying on this

Libraries name and store transforms differently, and this build did not check any of them. Questions to answer from each library's own documentation, for the version you use: does a transform named "from A to B" map A coordinates into B coordinates or describe frame B inside A (the ROS 2 transform library tf2 and its lookup functions are a common source of this confusion)? Is the matrix stored row-major or column-major (F0-49), and are vectors treated as columns or rows (graphics code often multiplies row vectors on the left, which transposes every matrix)? Does a quaternion-plus-translation type compose in the same order as this chapter? RB301 records the answers for its software stack.

How the hardware actually does it

A homogeneous 3D point holds four numbers. In single precision that is 16 bytes, and a 4 × 4 transform is 64 bytes; both are small, fixed-size blocks that suit short-vector (SIMD) units and GPUs, and 3D graphics pipelines are built around 4 × 4 transforms of exactly this kind (CU302 and the graphics-related chapters return to this). On a robot, the transform from the world to each sensor is recomputed every time the pose estimate changes, and then applied to every reading of a sweep or image. The widths of SIMD registers and the throughput of these products depend on the processor and are taught in later courses; this chapter states no hardware numbers. D1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. The robot, its sensor mount and the drone camera in this chapter are numbers in a program. A real sensor mount is measured or calibrated, never exact; a camera has its own optical frame convention, often different from the body frame; and a drone's camera may sit on a moving gimbal, which adds a time-varying link to the chain. RB301 and DN201 repeat this work with real mounts and their documented conventions.

Worked example

A robot stands at (3, 1) facing +y (heading 90°). Its sensor is mounted 0.5 m ahead of the robot origin, not turned. The sensor sees a cup 2 m straight ahead. Where is the cup on the map?

  1. Write the links. cos 90° = 0, sin 90° = 1, so TWR = [[0, −1, 3], [1, 0, 1], [0, 0, 1]]. The mount is a shift only: TRS = [[1, 0, 0.5], [0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 1]].
  2. Multiply, inner letters matching. TWS = TWRTRS = [[0, −1, 3], [1, 0, 1.5], [0, 0, 1]]. The sensor sits at (3, 1.5), half a metre in front of the robot along +y. Good.
  3. Apply to the point. TWS(2, 0, 1) = (0·2 − 1·0 + 3, 1·2 + 0·0 + 1.5, 1) = (3, 3.5, 1). The cup is at (3, 3.5): 2.5 m in front of the robot. Good.
  4. Wrong order, for comparison. TRSTWR = [[0, −1, 3.5], [1, 0, 1], [0, 0, 1]], which puts the cup at (3.5, 3): the mount's 0.5 m was added along the world's x axis, not along the robot's forward direction.
  5. Inverse. RT = [[0, 1], [−1, 0]] and −RTt = −(1.5, −3) = (−1.5, 3), so TSW = [[0, 1, −1.5], [−1, 0, 3], [0, 0, 1]], and TSW(3, 3.5, 1) = (2, 0, 1). Back to "2 m straight ahead".
  6. Direction. The sensor's forward direction (1, 0, 0) becomes (0, 1, 0): it points along +y and no shift was added.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Spoken directions are read forwards; matrix chains are applied from the right. "Fridge, then cupboard, then drawer" is spoken from the outside in, and the matrix product TWRTRS is written the same way, but the point is multiplied by the right-most matrix first. D1
    • 

  • Kenji can recover from a sloppy instruction; a matrix cannot. "Left of the fridge" survives a small misunderstanding; a wrong-order product gives a confident wrong number (forensic lab). R3
    • 

  • Kitchen furniture does not move; robot frames do. In a real frame tree some links change every millisecond (wheels, gimbals, the robot's own pose), so the chain must be recomputed with values from the same moment. B2
    • 

  • The extra "1" has no kitchen meaning. It is bookkeeping that makes the shift a multiplication; the analogy explains chaining, not the w component. D1
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F0-53 (rotate-then-shift between two frames), F0-50 (matrix products, non-commutative, matching inner dimensions) and F0-51 (inverse, socks and shoes). What carries over: R(θ), R−1 = RT, the triple loop. What changes: points gain a w component; one bigger matrix replaces "rotate then add"; chains of frames become products whose order is checked by matching frame letters; points and directions are told apart by w. What comes next: F0-55 replaces the 3 × 3 rotation block with a quaternion, a smaller and more robust way to store and update a drone's attitude. B2

Summary


    

  • A homogeneous transform [R t; 0 1] rotates and shifts in one matrix product; points carry w = 1.
    • 

  • 2D poses give 3 × 3 matrices; 3D poses give 4 × 4.
    • 

  • TAB maps B coordinates to A; chains are products with matching inner letters, TAC = TABTBC.
    • 

  • The order matters; a wrong order can be right at the origin and wrong everywhere else.
    • 

  • T−1 = [RT −RTt; 0 1]: no division needed.
    • 

  • Directions carry w = 0 and only rotate.
    • 

  • Library conventions (naming, storage order, row or column vectors) must be checked in their documentation.
