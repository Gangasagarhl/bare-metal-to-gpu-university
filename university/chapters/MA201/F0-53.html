Story hook

Analogy

Kenji stands in the kitchen with his back to the window. "The cup is two steps in front of me," he calls. Amara, at the kitchen door, looks at the counter, then at Kenji, then at the counter again. In front of Kenji is not in front of her. To find the cup she has to know where Kenji stands and which way he faces, and then turn his "two steps in front" into her own "three steps forward and one to the left".

A robot has the same problem all day long. Its distance sensor reports what it sees "straight ahead, 4.6 m" or "60° to my right, 2 m". Those numbers are in the robot's own frame of reference, which moves and turns with the robot. The map of the room, the goal the robot must reach and the other robots all live in the world's frame, which stays still. Every useful thing a robot does with a sensor reading starts by converting it from one frame into the other.

This chapter makes "my left or your left" precise. A coordinate frame is an origin and a set of axes. The same point has different coordinates in different frames, and a rotation plus a shift converts between them. Get the rotation the wrong way round and the robot, as this chapter's forensic lab shows, turns the wrong way.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe a coordinate frame and a robot's 2D pose (x, y, θ), and say which frame every vector in a program is expressed in.
  • Convert a point from the robot frame to the world frame, pW = R(θ) pB + t, and back, pB = R(θ)T(pW − t).
  • Turn a simulated robot's range readings into world coordinates and verify them against the simulated room (the MA201 lab).
  • Explain why "rotating a point" and "re-expressing a point in a turned frame" use transposed matrices.
  • Diagnose a transposed rotation matrix from a log of poses (the MA201 forensic lab).
Jargon box
Coordinate frame
Simple words: a starting point and a set of directions from which you measure positions. Analogy: "from the kitchen door: forward and left" versus "from where Kenji stands: in front of him and to his left". Precise: an origin point together with orthonormal axes (right-handed in this course); the coordinates of a point in a frame are its components along those axes measured from that origin. D1B2
World frame
Simple words: the frame fixed to the room or the ground, which does not move. Analogy: the kitchen door and its "forward, left". Precise: a frame W chosen as the fixed reference in which maps, goals and poses are expressed. D1B2
Body frame (robot frame)
Simple words: the frame attached to the robot, which moves and turns with it. Analogy: Kenji's "in front of me, to my left". Precise: a frame B with its origin at a chosen point on the robot and its axes fixed to the robot's body; in this course xB points forward and yB to the robot's left. D1B2
Pose (2D)
Simple words: where a robot is and which way it faces. Analogy: "Kenji is three steps in and one to the left, facing the window". Precise: the position t = (x, y) of the body frame's origin and its heading θ (the angle from xW to xB), both expressed in the world frame; together they define the transform from B to W. D1B2
Frame transform (rigid transform)
Simple words: the recipe for turning coordinates in one frame into coordinates in another. Analogy: Amara's rule for turning Kenji's "two steps in front" into her own directions. Precise: the map pW = RWB pB + tWB, where the columns of RWB are B's axes written in W and tWB is B's origin written in W; it keeps distances and angles. B2R1
Bearing and range
Simple words: "which way" and "how far" from the robot to something it senses. Analogy: "60° to my right, two steps away". Precise: for a point pB in the body frame, range = |pB| and bearing = atan2(yB, xB); conversely pB = (range · cos bearing, range · sin bearing). D1R1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A coordinate frame is a "from here, in these directions" agreement. The kitchen door with "forward" and "left" is one frame. Kenji's own body, with "in front of me" and "to my left", is another. A cup on the counter is one cup, but its description depends on the frame: from the door it might be "3 forward, 1 left"; from Kenji it might be "2 in front, 0 to the side".

The robot's sensor speaks in the robot's frame. A reading of "4.619 m straight ahead" means the point (4.619, 0) in robot coordinates. To put that point on the map of the room, the robot needs two things it knows about itself: where it stands, t = (2, 1), and which way it faces, θ = 30°. Its whole description of itself, (x, y, θ), is its pose.

The conversion has two steps. First turn: the robot's "forward" is the room's direction 30°, so rotate the reading by 30° (F0-52). Then shift: the robot is not at the room's corner, so add its position. Turn, then shift. For the forward beam: turning (4.619, 0) by 30° gives about (4, 2.309); adding (2, 1) gives (6, 3.309), a point on the right-hand wall. The simulated room confirms it.

Going the other way is the undo of those two steps, in reverse order (socks and shoes, F0-51): first un-shift (subtract the robot's position), then un-turn (rotate by −30°, which is the transpose).

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From the body frame to the world frame

Let the robot's pose be t = (x, y) and θ. The body axes, written in world coordinates, are xB = (cos θ, sin θ) and yB = (−sin θ, cos θ): exactly the columns of R(θ) from F0-52. A point with body coordinates pB = (u, v) is "u along xB plus v along yB, starting from the robot", so in the world it is D1B2

p_W = t + u·x_B + v·y_B = R(θ) p_B + t

Robotics texts write this rotation as RWB, "the orientation of B expressed in W", and the shift as tWB. The subscripts read right to left: RWB takes B coordinates in and gives W coordinates out, so RWBpB has matching inner letters (B next to B), the same bookkeeping as matching inner dimensions in F0-50. B2

From the world frame to the body frame

Solve for pB: subtract t, then undo R. Since R is a rotation, R−1 = RT (F0-51): B1B2

p_B = R(θ)ᵀ (p_W − t), so RBW = RWBT.

This is the formula a robot uses to ask "where is my goal relative to me?": the goal is stored in world coordinates, the controller needs it in body coordinates, and its bearing atan2(v, u) says whether to turn left (positive) or right (negative). D1

Turning a point versus turning the frame

F0-52 rotated points: the paper turned and the house went with it. Here the point stays still and the observer turns. These are opposite operations. If the robot turns by +30°, a fixed chair appears to the robot to have turned by −30°. So the matrix that re-expresses a fixed world point in the turned robot frame is R(−θ) = R(θ)T, not R(θ). Run R3 shows the difference for the chair at world point (3.7321, 2) seen from the robot at (2, 1), θ = 30°: the correct RT gives (2, 0), "2 m straight ahead"; using R by mistake gives (1, 1.7321), "2 m away at 60° to the left". Both answers have the right distance, which is exactly why the mistake is easy to miss. B2R3

Range readings

A range sensor reports pairs (range r, bearing b) measured in the body frame. The point is pB = (r cos b, r sin b), and then pW = R(θ)pB + t. Listing 1 does exactly this for four beams at three poses of the simulated robot and checks that every world point lies on a wall of the room. D1R1

Watch out

Every vector in a robot program belongs to exactly one frame, and nothing in a Vec2 says which. Name variables with their frame (goalW, pointB) or use a type per frame. Adding a body-frame vector to a world-frame vector is the frame version of adding metres to seconds.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Frames in 3D and frame trees. In 3D the same formula holds with a 3 × 3 rotation RWB (F0-52) and a 3-vector tWB. Real robots have many frames: the world, the robot's base, each wheel, each sensor (a sensor is mounted at some offset and angle on the body, giving another frame S with its own RBS, tBS), the arm's links, the gripper. They form a tree, and converting from one to another means chaining transforms along the tree. F0-54 packs each rotation-plus-shift into one 4 × 4 matrix so that chaining is a matrix product; RB301 uses the ROS 2 transform library for the tree. B2U1

Conventions are not mathematics. Which world axis points north or east, whether z points up or down, and whether a body's y axis points left or right are choices. Ground robots and aircraft communities have different habits, and software packages document theirs. The mathematics of this chapter works for any right-handed choice; the bugs come from mixing two of them. B2B4

Not verified — check before relying on this

This chapter does not state the frame conventions of any real software. Commonly cited ones include an east-north-up world frame with a forward-left-up body frame for ground robots, and a north-east-down world frame with a forward-right-down body frame for aircraft, but which package uses which, and how its messages and logs order and sign their components, must be checked in that package's own documentation for the version you use (for example the ROS 2 documentation's conventions for units and coordinate frames, and the PX4 or ArduPilot documentation on frames). RB301 and DN201 record these in their dossiers.

Uncertainty enters through the pose. The conversion is exact only if the pose is exact. A 1° error in θ moves a point 4.6 m away by about 4.6 × π/180 ≈ 0.08 m sideways, so far-away readings are more sensitive to heading errors than near ones. Estimating the pose and its uncertainty is the subject of RB302 (Kalman filters). D1U1

How the hardware actually does it

A spinning range sensor or a camera produces many readings per sweep, all taken from the same pose (to a first approximation), so a program computes cos θ and sin θ once per sweep and applies the same 2 × 2 or 3 × 3 matrix to every point: a matrix times a long list of vectors, which is a matrix product (F0-50) and maps well onto SIMD units and GPUs. On a real moving robot the pose changes during a sweep, and careful software corrects each reading for the pose at its own time stamp. B2U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. All readings in this chapter come from the university's simulator in Listing 1: a perfect rectangular room, a perfectly known pose and noise-free ranges. A real range sensor has noise, a minimum and maximum range, invalid returns, a mounting offset from the robot's origin, and its own documented frame and angle conventions. Before using real data, read the sensor's datasheet and driver documentation (HW302 F1-67 covers range sensors) and the robot software's frame conventions; RB301 repeats this lab with a simulator named in its dossier and then on the course robot kit.

Worked example

The robot is at t = (2, 1) with θ = 30°. Its sensor reports a chair 2 m straight ahead: pB = (2, 0).

  1. Rotate: R(30°)(2, 0) = (2 cos 30°, 2 sin 30°) = (1.7321, 1).
  2. Shift: pW = (1.7321 + 2, 1 + 1) = (3.7321, 2). That is where the chair is on the room's map.
  3. Back again: pW − t = (1.7321, 1); R(30°)T(1.7321, 1) = (2, 0). "2 m straight ahead" again.
  4. The classic mistake: using R(30°) instead of R(30°)T in step 3 gives (1, 1.7321): still 2 m away, but at a bearing of 60° to the left instead of 0°. The error in bearing is 2θ.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • People convert "my left" to "your left" by intuition; a robot needs exact numbers. Amara can glance at Kenji and guess. A program needs the pose as numbers, and a small heading error grows with distance (Layer 3). D1
    • 

  • Kenji's "in front" is vague; a frame is not. "In front of me" could mean his nose, his feet or his eyes. A robot frame has one exact origin point and exact axes, and two sensors on the same robot have different frames. B2
    • 

  • In a kitchen, up is always up. In 3D, robots and especially drones tilt, so even "up" has to be converted between frames, and different software chooses z up or z down (unverified box). B4
    • 

  • Real frames move during a measurement. The analogy freezes Kenji while he speaks. A moving robot's pose changes while its sensor sweeps (hardware section). B2
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F0-52 (rotation matrices that turn points), F0-51 (R−1 = RT, reversed order of undoing) and F0-47 (positions versus displacements). What carries over: the same R(θ), the same transpose, the same vector addition. What changes: you now track which frame each vector belongs to; re-expressing a fixed point in a turned frame uses RT, the opposite of turning the point; and every conversion is a rotation plus a shift, so the order of the two steps matters. What comes next: F0-54 combines rotation and shift into a single homogeneous matrix, so that chains of frames (world → robot → sensor) become matrix products. B2

Troubleshooting


    

  • A range of inf → the robot is outside the room or exactly on a wall facing out → keep poses strictly inside 0 < x < 6, 0 < y < 4.

  • Points off the walls by exactly the robot's position → the shift was applied before the rotation → rotate pB first, then add t.

  • Points mirrored top-to-bottom → bearings measured clockwise → bearings in this course are anticlockwise (left is positive).

Summary


    

  • A coordinate frame is an origin plus axes; one point has different coordinates in different frames.
    • 

  • A 2D pose (x, y, θ) places the body frame in the world frame.
    • 

  • Body → world: pW = R(θ)pB + t (rotate, then shift). World → body: pB = R(θ)T(pW − t).
    • 

  • Re-expressing a fixed point in a turned frame uses RT; turning the point uses R.
    • 

  • A transposed rotation keeps distances and gives bearing errors of 2θ; it is invisible at θ = 0.
    • 

  • Range readings become points with pB = (r cos b, r sin b).
    • 

  • Track the frame of every vector; state conventions explicitly and test at non-zero headings.
