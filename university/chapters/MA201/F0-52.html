Story hook

Analogy

Amara has drawn a little house on squared paper, with its corner at the bottom-left of the sheet. She pushes a pin through that corner into the kitchen table and turns the sheet a little. The house turns with it. Every dot of the drawing moves, the door and the window move, yet the house does not get bigger, smaller or bent. Only the pin stays where it was.

"If I tell you how far I turned it," she asks Kenji, "can you tell me where the chimney is now, without looking?" Kenji measures the angle with a protractor: 30°. He knows the chimney was 2 squares right and 1 square up. Working out where it went needs exactly one tool: a rotation matrix.

Robots turn on the spot, drones roll and pitch in the air, and every sensor reading has to be turned into the right direction before it can be used. A rotation is the transformation that turns things without stretching them. This chapter builds rotation matrices in 2D from two cosines and two sines, extends them to 3D, proves they keep lengths and can be undone with a transpose, and shows that in 3D the order of rotations matters.

What you will be able to do

  • Build the 2D rotation matrix R(θ) from where it sends the two axes, and rotate points with it by hand and in C++.
  • Check that a matrix is a rotation: RTR = I and det R = +1.
  • Combine and undo rotations: R(α)R(β) = R(α + β) in 2D, R−1 = RT = R(−θ).
  • Write the three basic 3D rotations about x, y and z, and show that 3D rotations do not commute.
  • Implement and test a 3 × 3 rotation utility (the shape of the MA201 practical exam).
  • Diagnose a turning error caused by degrees passed where radians are expected.
Jargon box
Rotation (about the origin)
Simple words: turning everything around a fixed point without stretching or bending it. Analogy: turning the squared paper about the pin. Precise: a linear transformation that keeps lengths and angles and does not mirror; its matrix R satisfies RTR = I and det R = +1. D1B1B2
Sine
Simple words: how far up you are after walking one step along a direction at angle θ. Analogy: the height of a point on the rim of a turning plate of radius 1. Precise: for the point at angle θ on the circle of radius 1 around the origin, measured anticlockwise from the positive x axis, the point is (cos θ, sin θ); so sin 0° = 0, sin 30° = 0.5, sin 90° = 1. D1R3
Rotation matrix (2D)
Simple words: the 2 × 2 table that turns points by an angle θ. Analogy: the rule for where every dot on the paper goes after the turn. Precise: R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]], turning anticlockwise by θ for positive θ when x points right and y points up. D1B1
Right-hand rule
Simple words: a way to tell which turning direction counts as positive in 3D. Analogy: curl the fingers of your right hand around an axis with your thumb along it: the fingers show the positive turn. Precise: in a right-handed coordinate system (x × y = z), a positive rotation about an axis turns x towards y about z, y towards z about x, and z towards x about y. B2
Rotation matrix (3D)
Simple words: the 3 × 3 table that turns points in space. Analogy: turning a whole toy model in your hands. Precise: a 3 × 3 matrix R with RTR = I and det R = +1; the set of all of them is called SO(3), the special orthogonal group. Its columns are the turned x, y and z axes. B1B2
Heading (yaw angle in 2D)
Simple words: which way something points on the floor, as one angle. Analogy: the angle on Kenji's protractor. Precise: for a direction (x, y), the angle atan2(y, x) measured anticlockwise from the x axis, between −180° and 180°; std::atan2 computes it in radians and uses the signs of both arguments to choose the quadrant. B3R1
Note

Maths you need here: sine. F0-48 introduced the cosine. Picture a circle of radius 1 around the origin. Walk from the origin out to the circle at angle θ, measured anticlockwise from the positive x axis. You arrive at the point (cos θ, sin θ): the cosine is how far across, the sine how far up. Some values, printed by run R3: sin 0° = 0, sin 30° = 0.5, sin 45° ≈ 0.7071, sin 60° ≈ 0.8660, sin 90° = 1; and for every angle cos²θ + sin²θ = 1, which is Pythagoras on the circle. As with the cosine, std::sin expects radians. D1R3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1, left, shows how to build a rotation matrix: turn the two axis arrows ex and ey by θ and read off where their tips land. By F0-49's rule, those two landing points are the two columns of the matrix. On the right, the same matrix turns a whole rectangle.

Figure 2 fixes the 3D directions used in this course: a right-handed system and the positive turn about each axis.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When you turn the paper about the pin, two things are easy to follow: the arrow along the bottom edge (the x axis) and the arrow up the left edge (the y axis). Turned by 30°, the bottom arrow of length 1 now points a little upwards. It ends about 0.866 across and 0.5 up: that is (cos 30°, sin 30°). The left-edge arrow turns the same way and now leans left: it ends at (−0.5, 0.866).

Every other dot on the paper is "so much along the bottom arrow plus so much along the left arrow" (F0-47). After the turn it is the same amounts along the turned arrows. So to turn the chimney at (2, 1), take 2 of the turned bottom arrow and 1 of the turned left arrow: 2 × (0.866, 0.5) + 1 × (−0.5, 0.866) = (1.232, 1.866). That is exactly multiplying by the matrix whose columns are the two turned arrows: the rotation matrix.

A rotation never stretches anything. The chimney was √5 ≈ 2.236 squares from the pin before the turn and is still 2.236 squares away after it. And a rotation is easy to undo: turn back by the same angle.

In 3D there are three axes to turn about. A drone can turn its nose left or right (about the up axis), tip its nose up or down, and tilt from side to side. Here something surprising happens: turning about one axis and then another usually ends differently from doing the same two turns in the other order. Try it with a book: lay it flat, turn it a quarter turn about the up axis and then a quarter turn about the left-right axis, and then try the other order.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Rotations in the plane

With x to the right, y up and θ measured anticlockwise, the rotation by θ sends ex to (cos θ, sin θ) and ey to (−sin θ, cos θ). These are its columns, so D1B1

R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]], and R(θ)(x, y) = (x cos θ − y sin θ, x sin θ + y cos θ).

Four properties follow, and Listing 1 prints each of them for θ = 30°: B1R1

  • Lengths are kept: |Rv| = |v|. The columns are unit vectors at right angles, so R is orthogonal (F0-51) and RTR = I; then |Rv|² = (Rv)T(Rv) = vTRTRv = vTv = |v|². Dot products, and therefore angles, are kept too.
  • No mirroring: det R = cos²θ + sin²θ = 1. An orthogonal matrix with determinant −1 is a mirror (it flips the paper over), not a rotation.
  • Angles add: R(α)R(β) = R(α + β). Listing 1 prints R(30°)R(60°) and R(90°) as the same matrix [[0, −1], [1, 0]], the quarter-turn of F0-51. In 2D, rotations therefore commute.
  • Undo by transposing: R(θ)−1 = R(θ)T = R(−θ). Listing 1 prints R(−30°) and R(30°)T as the same matrix.

A heading is the angle of a direction: for (2, 1) it is atan2(1, 2) ≈ 26.5651°, and after turning by 30° it is 56.5651°. Rotating adds to the heading; that is how a robot's yaw angle and its rotation matrix stay in step. std::atan2(y, x) takes the y component first and handles all four quadrants, unlike std::atan(y / x). B3R1

Rotations in space

A rotation about the z axis turns x and y exactly like the 2D case and leaves z alone. Rotations about x and y follow the right-hand rule of Figure 2, cycling x → y → z → x: B1B2

AboutMatrixPositive turn
xrotX(t) = [[1, 0, 0], [0, cos t, −sin t], [0, sin t, cos t]]y towards z
yrotY(t) = [[cos t, 0, sin t], [0, 1, 0], [−sin t, 0, cos t]]z towards x
zrotZ(t) = [[cos t, −sin t, 0], [sin t, cos t, 0], [0, 0, 1]]x towards y

Note where the minus sign sits in rotY: in the bottom-left, not the top-right. It is the same 2D pattern applied to the pair (z, x) in that order, which is how the cycle x → y → z → x places it. Listing 2 tests all three with quarter turns: rotZ(90°) sends ex to ey, rotX(90°) sends ey to ez, rotY(90°) sends ez to ex. B2R2

Every product of rotations is a rotation, and every 3D rotation can be written as such a product. But in 3D, order matters. Listing 2 applies "rotX(90°), then rotZ(90°)" and "rotZ(90°), then rotX(90°)" to ey and gets (0, 0, 1) and (−1, 0, 0): completely different directions. Remember from F0-50 that in rotZ·rotX the right-hand matrix acts first. B1R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

SO(3) as an engineer's checklist. A 3 × 3 matrix is a valid rotation exactly when RTR = I (columns are orthonormal) and det R = +1 (no mirror). Listing 2's isRotation applies both tests with a tolerance. Robotics texts call this set SO(3) and treat it as a space in its own right with three degrees of freedom: nine numbers, six constraints from RTR = I. B2R2

Drift. A program that keeps an orientation by multiplying many small rotations accumulates rounding error, and after many steps its matrix is no longer exactly orthogonal: lengths start to change slightly. Production code re-orthonormalises the matrix from time to time, or stores the orientation as a unit quaternion and renormalises that (F0-55's forensic lab shows the effect for quaternions). B2B4

Angles as parameters. Writing a rotation as three angles about fixed axes (for example rotZ(ψ)·rotY(θ)·rotX(φ), the yaw–pitch–roll sequence used for vehicles) is compact and readable, but there are many such conventions (which axes, which order, rotating the object or the axes), and every one of them has an orientation where two of its angles describe the same motion. F0-55 shows that case and the quaternion alternative. B2B4

Not verified — check before relying on this

Which Euler-angle sequence and sign conventions a particular robotics or flight software package uses (ROS 2 libraries, PX4, ArduPilot) is not stated in this chapter. Check that project's own documentation of frame and angle conventions for the version you use, and record it in the dossier of RB301 or DN201, before converting angles from its logs or messages.

How the hardware actually does it

The processor has no "rotate" instruction in this sense. std::cos and std::sin are computed by the C++ library (from additions, multiplications and stored constants), and their results are rounded approximations of the true values: run R1 prints cos 30° and sin 30° to four decimals, but the stored values are binary approximations, not the exact √3/2 and 1/2 of the textbook. Applying R to a point is then four multiplications and two additions in 2D (nine and six in 3D), the matrix–vector product of F0-49. Because the angle is fixed for many points, a program computes the sine and cosine once and reuses the matrix for every point of a laser scan or a 3D model. B3R1

Worked example

A robot stands at the origin facing along x. Its distance sensor reports a chair at (2, 1) in front-left of it. Then the robot turns on the spot by 30° anticlockwise. Where is the chair now, as seen in the original directions, if the chair turned with the robot? (This is the "turning paper" question; F0-53 asks the opposite question for a chair that stays put.)

  1. cos 30° ≈ 0.8660 and sin 30° = 0.5, so R(30°) = [[0.8660, −0.5], [0.5, 0.8660]].
  2. R(30°)(2, 1) = (2·0.8660 − 1·0.5, 2·0.5 + 1·0.8660) = (1.2321, 1.8660).
  3. Check the length: |(2, 1)| = √5 ≈ 2.2361 and |(1.2321, 1.8660)| ≈ 2.2361.
  4. Check the heading: atan2(1, 2) ≈ 26.5651°, and 26.5651° + 30° = 56.5651°, the heading of the new point.
  5. Undo: R(−30°)(1.2321, 1.8660) = (2, 1) again.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Paper turns about a pin anywhere; a rotation matrix turns about the origin only. Turning about another point needs a shift, a turn and a shift back, which F0-54 packs into one matrix. B2
    • 

  • Paper stays flat on the table; 3D rotations do not. On the table, two turns add up in any order. In space, the order of turns changes the result, which the paper cannot show (use a book instead). R2
    • 

  • Turning the paper 360° brings it back; so does 720°. The matrix forgets how many whole turns were made: R(390°) = R(30°). A robot's controller that needs to know "turned twice" must count turns separately. D1
    • 

  • You can flip paper over; a rotation cannot. Turning the sheet face-down is a mirror image (determinant −1), which Listing 2 rejects as "not a rotation". R2
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F0-48 (cosine, radians), F0-49 (columns are images of the axes), F0-50 (order of products) and F0-51 (orthogonal matrices). What carries over: everything: a rotation is an orthogonal matrix with determinant +1, applied with a matrix–vector product, combined with matrix products and undone with a transpose. What changes: sines and cosines enter, so results are rounded; angle units become a common bug; in 3D the order of rotations matters and sign conventions (right-hand rule, which axis is which) must be stated. What comes next: F0-53 uses rotations to answer "where is that in my frame and in the world's frame?", with the robot's sensor readings as the example. B1B2

Lab


Goal


Check rotation matrices against a real turned sheet of paper, then extend Listing 2 into your own tested 3 × 3 rotation utility (practice for the MA201 practical exam).


Hardware and software needed


Squared paper, a pin or pencil tip, a protractor, a ruler; a computer with a C++ compiler (this build: g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0, flags as in the build command).


Safety


Pins are sharp: push the pin into a piece of cardboard under the paper, not into a table someone uses, and put it away afterwards. Otherwise none beyond normal computer use.


Steps


    

  1. Draw x and y axes on a sheet of squared paper and mark three points, for example (2, 1), (3, 0) and (1, 3), in centimetres. Put a second sheet underneath with the same axes.
    2. 

  2. Pin the top sheet at the origin and turn it 40° anticlockwise (protractor). Prick through each point onto the bottom sheet and measure the new coordinates with a ruler.
    3. 

  3. Compute R(40°) times each point with a small program (copy Listing 1, change the angle and the points). Compare with your measurements.
    4. 

  4. Copy Listing 2. Add two functions: Mat3 fromYawPitchRoll(double yaw, double pitch, double roll) returning rotZ(yaw)·rotY(pitch)·rotX(roll), and double headingDegrees(const Mat3& r) returning atan2(r[1][0], r[0][0]) in degrees.
    5. 

  5. Add tests: fromYawPitchRoll(t, 0, 0) equals rotZ(t); the heading of rotZ(35°) is 35°; fromYawPitchRoll always passes isRotation for five angle triples of your choice.
    6. 

  6. Run until every test passes. Then break one function on purpose (swap a sign in rotY) and confirm that at least one test fails and the exit code is non-zero.
    7. 



Expected observations


Measured and computed coordinates agree to within about 1 mm, the accuracy of a ruler and a hand-turned sheet. All tests pass, as in run R2, and the deliberate sign error makes at least the "rotY(90 deg) turns z into x" test fail.


Verification


Every new test prints PASS and the program exits with code 0; the deliberately broken version exits with a non-zero code; sanitizers print nothing.


Troubleshooting


    

  • Computed points are rotated the wrong way compared with the paper → the sheet was turned clockwise, or the sine signs are swapped → check R(90°)(1, 0) = (0, 1).
    • 

  • Measured points are off by a few millimetres in the same direction → the pin was not exactly at the origin → re-pin and repeat one point.
    • 

  • A test fails by about 10−16 → exact comparison used somewhere → use near with a tolerance.

Method hints


A rotation keeps distances, so a correct-looking distance with a wrong position means the angle is wrong, not the matrix shape. Convert each observed position into an angle first; then look for a single factor or rule that turns every command into its observed angle.

Summary


    

  • R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]]; its columns are the turned axes.
    • 

  • Rotations keep lengths and angles: RTR = I and det R = +1.
    • 

  • In 2D, R(α)R(β) = R(α + β); the inverse is RT = R(−θ).
    • 

  • In 3D, rotX, rotY and rotZ turn about the axes by the right-hand rule; rotY has its minus sign in the bottom-left.
    • 

  • 3D rotations do not commute: the right-hand factor acts first.
    • 

  • std::cos and std::sin take radians; convert once, at the boundary.
    • 

  • Test rotation code with quarter turns of the axes, RTR ≈ I, det ≈ 1, and non-commuting pairs.
