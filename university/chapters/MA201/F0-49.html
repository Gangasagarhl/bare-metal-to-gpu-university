Story hook

Analogy

Every Saturday, Lina and her dad make pancakes and cake for the family. Her dad keeps the recipes on one card, as a table. The columns are the dishes: one batch of pancakes, one cake. The rows are the ingredients: flour, eggs, milk. Each box says how much of that ingredient one batch of that dish needs.

This week the neighbours are coming, so they plan three batches of pancakes and two cakes. Lina writes the shopping list. For flour, she goes along the flour row: 100 g for each pancake batch times three, plus 200 g for each cake times two, which is 700 g. Then eggs, then milk. Her dad does it differently. He takes the whole pancake column three times, the whole cake column twice, and adds the two lists. They get the same shopping list.

The recipe card is a matrix: a table of numbers with rows and columns. Turning "how many batches of each dish" into a shopping list is multiplying the matrix by a vector, and Lina's and her dad's methods are the two ways engineers think about it. The same operation turns a robot's sensor readings into world coordinates (F0-53) and is the inner step of the GPU's most famous workload (F0-50).

What you will be able to do

  • Read a matrix: its size (rows × columns) and the entry in row r, column c.
  • Compute a matrix–vector product in two ways: row by row (dot products) and column by column (a combination of the columns).
  • Explain a 2 × 2 matrix as a transformation of the plane: its columns are where the basis vectors go.
  • Store a matrix in one C++ std::vector in row-major order and compute the index of any entry.
  • Diagnose a wrong index formula that works for square tables and fails for others.
Jargon box
Matrix
Simple words: a rectangular table of numbers. Analogy: the recipe card: ingredients down the side, dishes across the top. Precise: an m × n matrix A is an array of m rows and n columns of numbers; its entry in row r and column c is written arc (or A(r, c)). D1B1
Entry (element)
Simple words: one number in the table. Analogy: one box on the recipe card, such as "milk for one cake". Precise: arc, identified by its row index r and column index c. This course numbers rows and columns from 0, as C++ does; many textbooks number from 1 (a11 is the top-left entry there). D1
Shape (size) of a matrix
Simple words: how many rows and how many columns. Analogy: 3 ingredients by 2 dishes. Precise: the pair m × n, rows first; a 3 × 2 matrix has 6 entries. D1
Matrix–vector product
Simple words: using a table to turn one list of numbers into another. Analogy: batches of each dish in, shopping list out. Precise: for an m × n matrix A and a vector x with n components, Ax is the vector with m components whose entry r is the dot product of row r of A with x; equivalently Ax = x0(column 0) + … + xn−1(column n−1). D1B1
Linear transformation
Simple words: a rule that moves every arrow of the plane (or space) so that straight lines stay straight and the origin stays put. Analogy: the recipe card does the same thing to any order: double the order, double the shopping. Precise: a function T with T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v) and T(kv) = kT(v); every such T from ℝn to ℝm is "multiply by some m × n matrix". B1
Row-major order
Simple words: storing a table in memory one row after another. Analogy: reading the recipe card line by line, left to right. Precise: the layout that places entry (r, c) of an m × n matrix at index r × n + c of a one-dimensional array; column-major order places it at c × m + r instead. D1R1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the recipe card as a matrix, with each entry's row and column number, and the same six numbers as they lie in a computer's memory, one row after another. Figure 2 shows a different use of a matrix: moving every point of the plane.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A matrix is a table of numbers arranged in rows and columns. The recipe card has 3 rows (flour, eggs, milk) and 2 columns (pancakes, cake), so it is a "3 by 2" matrix, written 3 × 2. Rows always come first. Each number in the table is an entry, and you find it by its row and its column, like a seat in a theatre.

The order for the weekend is a vector: (3, 2) means 3 batches of pancakes and 2 cakes. Multiplying the matrix by this vector gives the shopping list. There are two ways to do it, and they always agree.

Lina's way: along each row

For each ingredient, go along its row, multiply each box by the number of batches of that dish, and add. Flour: 100 × 3 + 200 × 2 = 300 + 400 = 700 g. Eggs: 1 × 3 + 3 × 2 = 9. Milk: 150 × 3 + 50 × 2 = 550 ml. Each line is a dot product (F0-48) of a row with the order.

Her dad's way: whole columns

Take the pancake column (100, 1, 150) three times: (300, 3, 450). Take the cake column (200, 3, 50) twice: (400, 6, 100). Add them: (700, 9, 550). Same list. This way says: the answer is a mix of the columns, with the order telling you how much of each.

A matrix can move things

A small 2 × 2 matrix can act on positions instead of recipes. Multiply each corner of a square by it and you get the corners of a new shape (Figure 2). Some matrices stretch, some slant, some turn. The turning ones, rotations, are the heart of this course from F0-52 on.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

An m × n matrix A has m rows and n columns. In this course, rows are numbered r = 0, …, m − 1 and columns c = 0, …, n − 1, and arc is the entry where they meet. Two matrices of the same shape add entry by entry, and a scalar multiplies every entry: [[1, 2], [3, 4]] + [[5, 6], [7, 8]] = [[6, 8], [10, 12]] and 3 × [[1, 2], [3, 4]] = [[3, 6], [9, 12]]. (Inline, this course writes a matrix as a list of its rows in double brackets.) D1B1R2

The matrix–vector product

If x has n components, y = Ax has m components, given by D1B1

y[r] = a[r][0]·x[0] + a[r][1]·x[1] + … + a[r][n−1]·x[n−1] for every row r.

Read it two ways. Row view: yr is the dot product of row r with x. Column view: y = x0 × (column 0) + x1 × (column 1) + …, a linear combination (F0-47) of the columns with the components of x as weights. Both are the same sum, added in a different order; Listing 1 computes both and prints the same shopping list. B1R1

Shapes must fit. The number of columns of A must equal the number of components of x: a 3 × 2 recipe matrix needs an order of 2 numbers, one per dish. Multiplying it by a 3-component vector is meaningless, and code that does not check will read past the end of a row. D1

Matrices as transformations

Matrix–vector multiplication is linear: A(u + v) = Au + Av and A(kv) = k(Av). Double the order, double the shopping list; two separate orders cost the same as one combined order. Conversely, any linear rule on vectors can be written as a matrix. To find the matrix, see where the rule sends each basis vector: Aex is column 0 of A and Aey is column 1, because multiplying by (1, 0) picks out the first column (column view with weights 1 and 0). In Figure 2, M = [[2, 1], [0, 1]] sends ex to (2, 0) and ey to (1, 1), and the point (3, 2) to 3 × (2, 0) + 2 × (1, 1) = (8, 2). This "columns are the images of the axes" rule is how you will build rotation matrices in F0-52 without memorising them. B1R2

Storing a matrix in memory

Memory is one long row of numbered places (SP201 F2-18), so a two-dimensional table must be flattened. Row-major order stores row 0, then row 1, and so on: entry (r, c) of an m × n matrix goes to index r × n + c. In Figure 1, entry (2, 0) is at 2 × 2 + 0 = 4. For a 3 × 4 matrix, entry (2, 1) is at 2 × 4 + 1 = 9. Column-major order stores whole columns instead: (r, c) at c × m + r, so (2, 1) of a 3 × 4 matrix would be at 1 × 3 + 2 = 5. C++ built-in two-dimensional arrays are row-major; some numerical libraries use column-major. Mixing the two conventions silently transposes your matrix. D1B3R2

Not verified — check before relying on this

Which storage order a particular linear-algebra library (a BLAS implementation, a GPU library such as cuBLAS or rocBLAS, or a robotics maths library) uses by default is a fact about that library and its version. This chapter does not state any library's default. Check the library's own documentation for the version you use before passing it a std::vector laid out by Listing 1; CU302's dossier must record it for cuBLAS and rocBLAS.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Strides. Row-major indexing r × n + c is a special case of a general rule: index = r × (row stride) + c × (column stride), with strides (n, 1) for row-major and (1, m) for column-major. A sub-block of a larger matrix, or every second row, is described by the same formula with a different starting index and strides, without copying anything. GPU GEMM libraries describe tiles and layouts in exactly these terms (CU302 F6-27). D1U1

A matrix type should protect its shape. Listing 1's Matrix keeps rows, columns and data together so that at(r, c) always uses the right column count. It does not check that the data has exactly rows × cols entries or that r and c are in range; a library class would enforce that as a class invariant (SP102 F2-09) and offer a checked access for tests. The forensic lab shows what happens when the shape and the index formula disagree. B2

Loop order is a performance decision. The row view reads A along rows (consecutive memory for row-major), the column view jumps by n each step. For tiny matrices this does not matter; for large ones the memory access order decides the speed, which SP302 (F2-52, loop interchange) and CU302 measure. The arithmetic is identical. U1

How the hardware actually does it

The processor has no notion of rows and columns: it sees one array of doubles at consecutive addresses, as Listing 1's "storage order in memory" line shows (100 200 1 3 150 50). The index formula is ordinary integer arithmetic performed by the program before each load. Caches fetch memory in blocks of neighbouring bytes (HW203), so walking along a row of a row-major matrix uses every fetched byte, while walking down a column may use only one number per fetch for a large matrix. On a GPU, each thread usually computes its own (r, c) from its thread and block indexes and then applies the same r × n + c formula (CU201). R1U1

Worked example

Part A — a transformation. Let M = [[2, 1], [0, 1]]. Where do the corners of the unit square go?

  • M(0, 0) = (2·0 + 1·0, 0·0 + 1·0) = (0, 0). The origin never moves under a matrix.
  • M(1, 0) = (2, 0): column 0.
  • M(0, 1) = (1, 1): column 1.
  • M(1, 1) = (2 + 1, 0 + 1) = (3, 1) = column 0 + column 1.

The square becomes the parallelogram of Figure 2. The point (3, 2) goes to 3 × (2, 0) + 2 × (1, 1) = (8, 2), the same as the row view (2·3 + 1·2, 0·3 + 1·2).

Part B — indexes. In a 3 × 4 row-major matrix, entry (2, 1) is at 2 × 4 + 1 = 9. In column-major order it would be at 1 × 3 + 2 = 5.

Part C — the shopping list, by both views: rows give 700, 9, 550; columns give (300, 3, 450) + (400, 6, 100) = (700, 9, 550).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Recipe amounts cannot be negative; matrix entries can. A matrix entry of −1 is common (rotations, F0-52), and an order of −2 cakes would be meaningless in a kitchen but is a perfectly good vector. D1
    • 

  • Real recipes are not linear. Doubling a recipe does not always double the baking time or the salt that tastes right. A matrix is exactly linear: twice the input always gives twice the output. B1
    • 

  • A recipe card is only a table; a matrix is also a motion. Nothing about a recipe card "moves" anything, but the same kind of table can stretch, shear or rotate space (Figure 2), and that is how this course mostly uses matrices. B1
    • 

  • The card's layout on paper does not matter; the layout in memory does. You can read a card by rows or columns at will. A program must know whether the numbers were stored by rows or by columns, or it reads a different matrix. D1
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F0-47 (vectors and linear combinations) and F0-48 (dot products); SP201 showed memory as one long row of numbered places. What carries over: every entry of Ax is a dot product, and Ax as a whole is a linear combination of columns; std::vector still holds the numbers. What changes: you now handle a two-dimensional table, its shape rules and its flat memory layout; one index formula connects the two, and getting it wrong gives plausible-looking wrong answers. A matrix is also a transformation, not only a table. What comes next: F0-50 multiplies two matrices together, the general matrix multiply (GEMM), checked by hand and in C++. D1

Summary


    

  • A matrix is an m × n table; entry arc is in row r, column c; rows come first.
    • 

  • Ax needs as many components in x as A has columns; it has as many components as A has rows.
    • 

  • Row view: each entry of Ax is a dot product of a row with x. Column view: Ax is a combination of the columns.
    • 

  • Matrices are linear transformations; column c is where the basis vector ec goes.
    • 

  • Row-major storage puts (r, c) at r × cols + c; column-major at c × rows + r.
    • 

  • An index formula that uses the wrong dimension can work for square matrices and fail silently for others.
    •
