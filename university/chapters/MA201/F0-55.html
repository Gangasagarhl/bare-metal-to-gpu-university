Story hook

Analogy

Kenji pushes a skewer through a potato and twists it. Whatever he does, the potato's new orientation can be described as "this skewer direction, twisted by this much". Amara tries a second potato and a different description: "turn it left, then tip it forward, then roll it sideways". It works most of the time, but when she tips the potato straight up, "turn left" and "roll sideways" suddenly do the same thing, and she cannot tell them apart.

A drone's flight controller has to know its attitude, which way it is tilted and turned, hundreds of times a second, while the drone spins about all three axes. Amara's description, three angles called yaw, pitch and roll, is easy for people to read but has the same flaw as her potato: at one pitch angle it loses a direction. A rotation matrix (F0-52) has no such flaw but uses nine numbers for three degrees of freedom, and rounding slowly turns it into something that is no longer a rotation.

Kenji's skewer description, written with four numbers, is a quaternion. It has no flawed angle, it is cheap to update from a gyro, and repairing its rounding takes one division by its length. This chapter teaches enough of it to read and write a drone's attitude code.

What you will be able to do

  • Build the unit quaternion for a rotation by angle α about a unit axis n, and read the axis and angle back.
  • Rotate a vector with v′ = q v q*, and compose rotations with the Hamilton product, in the right order.
  • Convert a unit quaternion into a rotation matrix and check it against F0-52.
  • Explain gimbal lock with numbers from a real run, and why q and −q mean the same attitude.
  • Integrate simulated gyro rates into an attitude and keep it normalised; diagnose a drifting attitude caused by a missing normalisation.
Jargon box
Attitude
Simple words: which way a drone or robot is tilted and turned, ignoring where it is. Analogy: how the potato is turned on its skewer, not where it lies on the table. Precise: the orientation of the body frame relative to the world frame, RWB (F0-53), represented by a rotation matrix, a unit quaternion or a set of angles. D1B4
Quaternion
Simple words: a number made of four parts, one plain number and three that act like a vector. Analogy: the skewer's direction (three parts) plus how much it is twisted (one part). Precise: q = w + xi + yj + zk, written (w, x, y, z) in this course, with i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1. D1B5
Unit quaternion
Simple words: a quaternion of length 1, the kind that describes a rotation. Analogy: a skewer twist written down in a standard size. Precise: q with w² + x² + y² + z² = 1; the rotation by α about the unit axis n is q = (cos(α/2), n sin(α/2)), and q and −q give the same rotation. D1R1
Hamilton product
Simple words: the rule for multiplying two quaternions; it combines two rotations into one. Analogy: two twists of the skewer, one after the other, replaced by one twist. Precise: (aw, a)(bw, b) = (awbw − a·b, awb + bwa + a × b); not commutative; q2q1 means "q1 first, then q2" in this course's convention. D1B5
Quaternion conjugate
Simple words: the quaternion with its vector part negated; for a unit quaternion, the opposite rotation. Analogy: the same skewer, twisted back. Precise: q* = (w, −x, −y, −z); q q* = (|q|², 0, 0, 0), so for a unit quaternion q* = q−1, playing the role RT plays for matrices (F0-51). D1
Euler angles (yaw–pitch–roll)
Simple words: three angles, turn left-right, tip nose up-down, roll side to side, that describe an attitude. Analogy: Amara's "turn, tip, roll" instructions. Precise: in this chapter, R = Rz(yaw) Ry(pitch) Rx(roll); other orders and axis choices exist and must be stated. D1B4
Gimbal lock
Simple words: the pose at which two of the three angles do the same thing, so one direction of turning cannot be expressed. Analogy: the potato tipped straight up, where "turn" and "roll" coincide. Precise: for yaw–pitch–roll at pitch ±90°, the matrix depends only on yaw ∓ roll; the angles cannot be recovered uniquely and small attitude changes can need large angle changes. D1R1
Renormalisation
Simple words: dividing a quaternion by its length so it is exactly length 1 again. Analogy: rescaling a slightly stretched skewer drawing back to standard size. Precise: q ← q / |q|, applied after each update to undo rounding and integration errors that change |q|. D1R2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Any way a solid object can be turned, it can also be reached by one single twist about one single line through it. That line is the axis and the amount of twist is the angle. A quaternion is a tidy way of writing down "axis and angle" in four numbers.

The recipe: take the axis as an arrow of length 1, multiply it by the sine of half the angle, and put the cosine of half the angle in front. A quarter turn (90°) about the up axis z becomes (cos 45°, 0, 0, sin 45°) = (0.7071, 0, 0, 0.7071). No turn at all is (1, 0, 0, 0).

The half angle looks strange, but it makes everything else work: with it, there is one multiplication rule that combines two twists into one, and another short rule that turns any arrow. And because the four numbers always have length 1, if rounding stretches them a little, you can shrink them back by dividing by their length. That repair is called renormalisation, and forgetting it is this chapter's forensic case.

Yaw, pitch and roll (turn, tip, roll) are what a pilot reads on a screen. They are fine for display. But if the nose points straight up, turning and rolling become the same motion, and the three numbers can no longer describe every small change. Quaternions never have that problem, so flight software keeps the attitude as a quaternion and converts to angles only for people.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From axis and angle

For a unit axis n = (nx, ny, nz) and an angle α, D1B5

q = (cos(α/2), n_x sin(α/2), n_y sin(α/2), n_z sin(α/2))

Its length is 1 because cos² + sin²·|n|² = 1. Back the other way: α = 2 acos(w), and n is (x, y, z) divided by sin(α/2) (undefined for α = 0, where any axis will do). Because the angle is halved, q and −q describe rotations by α and by α + 360°, which are the same rotation: every attitude has exactly two unit quaternions. Run R1 rotates ex with both and gets (0, 1, 0) twice. D1R1

The Hamilton product

Quaternions multiply with the rules i² = j² = k² = −1, ij = k, jk = i, ki = j, and the reverse orders give minus signs (ji = −k). Expanded, with a = (aw, a) and b = (bw, b): D1B5

a b = (a_w b_w − a·b, a_w b + b_w a + a × b)

The dot product is F0-48's; the cross product a × b is the 3D vector perpendicular to both, and it is the part that makes ab ≠ ba. Listing 1 writes the product out in 16 multiplications and 12 additions, against 27 and 18 for a 3 × 3 matrix product. D1R3

Rotating a vector and composing rotations

Write the vector v as the "pure" quaternion (0, v). Then the rotated vector is the vector part of D1B5

v′ = q (0, v) q*

Applying q1 and then q2 gives q2(q1 v q1*)q2* = (q2q1) v (q2q1)*, so the combined rotation is q2q1: the same right-to-left reading as matrix products (F0-52, F0-54). Run R1: pitch 90° and then yaw 90° sends the nose ex to (0, 0, −1); the other order sends it to (0, 1, 0). R1

To a rotation matrix

For a unit q = (w, x, y, z), D1B5

R = [[1 − 2(y² + z²), 2(xy − wz), 2(xz + wy)], [2(xy + wz), 1 − 2(x² + z²), 2(yz − wx)], [2(xz − wy), 2(yz + wx), 1 − 2(x² + y²)]]

Run R1 checks that this matrix for "pitch 90° then yaw 90°" equals Rz(90°)Ry(90°) from F0-52 to within 2 × 10−16, and that R v and q v q* agree for a 37° rotation about the axis (0.48, 0.6, 0.64). The formula assumes |q| = 1; with a longer q it gives a matrix that also scales, which is the mechanism behind the forensic lab. R1

Gimbal lock, in numbers

With R = Rz(yaw)Ry(pitch)Rx(roll) and pitch = 90°, run R1 builds the matrix for (yaw, roll) = (30°, 0°), (0°, −30°) and (50°, 20°): all three agree with each other to about 10−16, while (30°, 10°) differs by 0.158. Only yaw − roll matters at that pitch; one of the three degrees of freedom has vanished from the angles, though not from the drone. R1

Watch out

The four numbers are not "an axis and an angle"; they are an axis times sin(α/2) and cos(α/2). Putting degrees or the full angle into them gives a valid-looking but wrong quaternion. And the stored order (w first or w last) differs between libraries (unverified box).

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Integrating a gyro. A gyroscope measures the body's angular rate ω in the body frame (HW302). Over a short step dt the body turns by the angle |ω|dt about the axis ω/|ω|, so the attitude is updated by multiplying on the right by that small rotation: q ← q ⊗ q(ω/|ω|, |ω|dt). Right, because the increment is expressed in the body frame (F0-53: body-frame motions compose on the right of RWB). Listing 2 does this and lands on the exact expected attitude to within 10−15. D1B4R2

The first-order shortcut. The rate equation of a quaternion is dq/dt = ½ q ⊗ (0, ω) B4B5; integrating it with one Euler step gives q ← q + ½ dt q ⊗ (0, ω). Each such step multiplies |q| by √(1 + (|ω|dt/2)²), slightly more than 1, so without renormalisation |q| grows steadily. Run R3 computes the factor 1.0000095 per step for 50°/s at dt = 0.01 s, which compounds to |q| = 1.059 after 6,000 steps. D1R3

Why four numbers and not three. Every three-number description of all attitudes (Euler angles in any order, or axis times angle) has a singular point or a jump somewhere; four numbers with one constraint avoid it, at the price of the double cover (q and −q). Estimators such as the Kalman filters of RB302 and DN301 work around the constraint with an error state of three numbers; that is beyond MA201. B2B4

Interpolation. Moving smoothly between two attitudes (camera paths, setpoint smoothing) uses spherical linear interpolation of quaternions, after choosing the sign of one so that their dot product is positive. Its formula is left to DN301. B5

Not verified — check before relying on this

This chapter uses the Hamilton convention (ij = k), stores (w, x, y, z), treats q as rotating body vectors into the world frame, and composes with q2q1 meaning "q1 first". Other conventions are in use: a "JPL" convention with a different multiplication sign, storage with w last, and quaternions that map world to body instead. Which one a given library, message format, flight stack or log file uses (for example Eigen, ROS 2 geometry messages, PX4, ArduPilot) was not checked in this build. Before mixing code or data from two sources, check each one's documentation for the version used, and test with a known rotation such as 90° about z.

How the hardware actually does it

A flight controller reads its IMU's gyroscope at a fixed rate, updates the attitude quaternion with each sample, and corrects the slow drift of pure gyro integration with the accelerometer (gravity direction) and sometimes a magnetometer; that fusion is the estimator taught in DN301. Quaternion updates suit small microcontrollers: a few dozen multiply-adds per step and one square root for renormalisation, with no trigonometric function if a first-order update is used (DN201 and the flight stack's own documentation give the real numbers). This chapter states no sample rates, cycle counts or sensor specifications; none were measured. B4U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. The gyro in Listing 2 and the drone in the forensic lab are perfect, noise-free simulations. A real gyro has bias, noise, scale errors and a mounting orientation relative to the body frame, and its axes and signs are defined in its datasheet and driver (HW302). Nothing in this chapter has been run on an IMU or a drone.

Safety

All work in this chapter is simulation. Attitude code must never be first tried on a drone with propellers fitted. When DN201 moves this work onto hardware, follow that course's safety rules: propellers removed for bench tests, a supervised test area, and a working disarm switch.

Worked example

Rotate ex = (1, 0, 0) by 90° about z with q v q*, by hand.

  1. Build q. α/2 = 45°: q = (cos 45°, 0, 0, sin 45°) = (0.7071, 0, 0, 0.7071). Check: 0.7071² + 0.7071² = 1.
  2. Pure quaternion. v = (0, 1, 0, 0).
  3. q v. With a = q, b = v: w = 0.7071·0 − (0·1 + 0 + 0) = 0; vector = 0.7071·(1, 0, 0) + 0·(0, 0, 0.7071) + (0, 0, 0.7071) × (1, 0, 0) = (0.7071, 0, 0) + (0, 0.7071, 0) = (0.7071, 0.7071, 0). So q v = (0, 0.7071, 0.7071, 0).
  4. Conjugate. q* = (0.7071, 0, 0, −0.7071).
  5. (q v) q*. w = 0·0.7071 − (0.7071, 0.7071, 0)·(0, 0, −0.7071) = 0; vector = 0·(0, 0, −0.7071) + 0.7071·(0.7071, 0.7071, 0) + (0.7071, 0.7071, 0) × (0, 0, −0.7071) = (0.5, 0.5, 0) + (−0.5, 0.5, 0) = (0, 1, 0).
  6. Result. ex → (0, 1, 0), the same as Rz(90°)ex in F0-52. Two quarter turns: q q = (0, 0, 0, 1), the quaternion for 180° about z.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A skewer has no half angle. The analogy explains axis and angle; the half angle and the double cover (q and −q) have no potato meaning. D1
    • 

  • A potato is turned once; a drone is turned continuously. The interesting part in practice is updating q many times a second and keeping it normalised, which the analogy does not show. R2
    • 

  • Amara's angles are not "wrong", only singular. Yaw, pitch and roll are correct away from pitch ±90° and are the right thing to display; the problem is storing and updating them. R1
    • 

  • A real potato on a real skewer can also slide. Attitude ignores position; F0-54's transforms combine attitude with position. D1

Summary


    

  • Attitude is the orientation of the body frame; a unit quaternion stores it in four numbers.
    • 

  • Rotation by α about unit n: q = (cos(α/2), n sin(α/2)); q and −q are the same attitude.
    • 

  • Rotate with v′ = q (0, v) q*; compose with q2q1 = "q1 first"; the conjugate is the inverse.
    • 

  • A unit quaternion converts to a rotation matrix; the formula assumes |q| = 1.
    • 

  • Yaw–pitch–roll lose a degree of freedom at pitch ±90° (gimbal lock); use them for display.
    • 

  • Integrate body rates on the right, and renormalise after every update.
    • 

  • Conventions (Hamilton or JPL, w first or last, direction of mapping) must be checked per library.
