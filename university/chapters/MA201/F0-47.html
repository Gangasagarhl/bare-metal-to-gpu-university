Story hook

Analogy

Kenji has written a tiny program for the toy robot that rolls around his family's kitchen. The kitchen floor is made of square tiles, so he gives every instruction in tiles: "3 tiles towards the window. Then 2 tiles away from the door. Then 1 tile back from the window and 1 more away from the door."

His cousin Amara is watching. "You keep saying two numbers for every move," she says. "Towards the window, away from the door. Why not just one?" Kenji thinks about it. One number would tell the robot how far, but not which way. Two numbers say both. Amara draws each move as an arrow on squared paper, the next arrow starting where the last one ended. "Look," she says, "the three arrows end at the same tile as this single arrow from the start. You could have sent the robot there in one move."

An arrow you can write as a short list of numbers is a vector. Robots use vectors for every position and every movement, drones use them for wind and thrust, and graphics cards push millions of them through every second. This first chapter of MA201 teaches what they are and how to add, subtract and stretch them.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain a vector both as an arrow (a direction and a length) and as a list of numbers called components.
  • Compute the sum and the difference of two vectors, and a vector multiplied by a number, in 2D and 3D, by hand and in C++.
  • Find the vector that points from one position to another, and use the rule "target minus start" without mixing up the order.
  • Write any 2D or 3D vector as a combination of the basis vectors along the axes.
  • Diagnose a robot that drives away from its target because a difference was taken in the wrong order.
Jargon box
Vector (geometric)
Simple words: an arrow that says "this far, in this direction", written as a short list of numbers. Analogy: one robot move on the kitchen floor: so many tiles towards the window, so many away from the door. Precise: an element of the space ℝn, written as an ordered list of n real numbers (v1, …, vn); in 2D and 3D it can be drawn as an arrow whose shift along each axis is one of the numbers. Not the same thing as the C++ container std::vector (KID102 F0-35), which is a resizable list of any type. D1B1
Component
Simple words: one of the numbers inside a vector. Analogy: the "towards the window" count, or the "away from the door" count. Precise: the i-th entry vi of a vector v; in 2D the components are usually called x and y, in 3D x, y and z. D1
Scalar
Simple words: an ordinary single number, used to stretch or shrink a vector. Analogy: "do that move twice as far". Precise: a real number k; multiplying a vector by k multiplies every component by k. D1B1
Position vector
Simple words: the arrow from a fixed starting point (the origin) to a place. Analogy: "the toy is 3 tiles towards the window and 5 tiles away from the door". Precise: the vector from the origin O to a point P; its components are P's coordinates (F0-18). D1
Displacement
Simple words: a move from one place to another, without caring where it started. Analogy: "3 tiles towards the window", which means the same move from any tile. Precise: the vector B − A that takes point A to point B. D1
Linear combination
Simple words: a mix of vectors, each one stretched by its own number, then added. Analogy: "2 big steps east plus 3 big steps north". Precise: a sum a1v1 + a2v2 + … + amvm with scalars ai and vectors vi. D1B1
Basis vectors (standard)
Simple words: the one-unit arrows along each axis that every other vector is built from. Analogy: "one tile towards the window" and "one tile away from the door". Precise: in ℝ3, ex = (1, 0, 0), ey = (0, 1, 0), ez = (0, 0, 1); every vector is exactly one linear combination of them, and the coefficients are its components. D1B1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Some quantities need only one number. The temperature in the kitchen is one number. The weight of a bag of rice is one number. These single numbers are called scalars.

Other quantities need a direction too. "Walk 3 metres" is not enough to find the fridge; you need to know which way. A vector packs both into a short list of numbers. On Kenji's floor, the move (3, 0) means "3 tiles towards the window, 0 tiles away from the door". The numbers inside are the vector's components. The order matters: (3, 0) and (0, 3) are different moves, just like the coordinates of F0-18.

You can draw a vector as an arrow. Its tail is where the move starts and its tip is where it ends. The same move drawn from a different tile is the same vector. "3 tiles towards the window" means the same thing from the door or from the sink.

Adding: one move after another

To add two vectors, add the matching components: (3, 0) + (0, 2) = (3, 2). In the picture, you put the tail of the second arrow on the tip of the first. The sum is the arrow from the very first tail to the very last tip. Figure 1 shows this for three moves: (3, 0) + (0, 2) + (−1, 1) = (2, 3).

The order of the moves does not change where you end up. Going 3 tiles east and then 2 tiles north lands on the same tile as going 2 north and then 3 east. The path is different; the end is the same.

Subtracting: the arrow from here to there

Suppose the robot stands at (3, 5) and the fridge is at (6, 5). Which move takes the robot to the fridge? Take the target and subtract where you are: (6, 5) − (3, 5) = (3, 0). Three tiles towards the window. The rule is always "target minus start". Swap them and you get (−3, 0), which points away from the fridge. That swap is the bug in this chapter's forensic lab.

Stretching: multiplying by a number

"Do the first move again, but only half as far": (3, 0) times 0.5 is (1.5, 0). Multiplying by 2 doubles each component. Multiplying by −1 turns the arrow round: (−1, 1) becomes (1, −1), as Figure 2 shows for another vector.

Three numbers for the air

A robot on the floor needs two numbers. A drone in the air needs three: east, north and up. The rules are the same; there is just one more component to add or stretch.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A vector in n dimensions is an ordered list of n real numbers, v = (v1, v2, …, vn). The set of all of them is written ℝn. In this course n is 2 (a robot on the floor), 3 (a drone in the air), or 4 (homogeneous coordinates in F0-54 and quaternions in F0-55). D1B1

Two operations define how vectors behave, and both work component by component: D1B1

  • Addition: u + v = (u1 + v1, …, un + vn). Example: (2, 1) + (1, 3) = (3, 4).
  • Scalar multiplication: k v = (k v1, …, k vn). Example: 3 × (2, −1) = (6, −3).

Subtraction follows from these: u − v = u + (−1)v. Example: (5, 4) − (2, 1) = (3, 3). Because each component is handled by ordinary addition of numbers, the familiar rules carry over: u + v = v + u (order does not matter), (u + v) + w = u + (v + w) (grouping does not matter), and the zero vector 0 = (0, …, 0) changes nothing when added. Run R2 confirms the order rule on the worked example: leg1 + leg2 and leg2 + leg1 both print (4, 3, 2.5). D1R2

Points versus displacements

Engineers use the same list-of-numbers notation for two different things, and mixing them is a classic source of bugs. A position vector is the arrow from a chosen origin to a point: the robot is "at (3, 5)". A displacement is a move: "go (3, 0)". The difference of two positions is a displacement (B − A takes you from A to B). A position plus a displacement is a new position. But the sum of two positions has no physical meaning: "the fridge plus the sink" is not a place. F0-54 turns this distinction into arithmetic by giving points a fourth component 1 and directions a fourth component 0. D1B2

Building every vector from the axes

The standard basis vectors are one unit along each axis: in 2D, ex = (1, 0) and ey = (0, 1). Any 2D vector is a linear combination of them, and the coefficients are exactly its components: (2, 3) = 2ex + 3ey. This looks too obvious to mention, but it is the seed of the whole course. The components of a vector are "how much of each basis vector". If you choose different basis vectors, for example the robot's own "forward" and "left" instead of the kitchen's "east" and "north", the same arrow gets different components. That is what coordinate frames are (F0-53), and changing between them is what rotation matrices do (F0-52). D1B1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Mathematicians define a vector space by a list of rules (axioms) that addition and scalar multiplication must obey: commutativity and associativity of addition, a zero vector, an opposite for every vector, and distributive rules such as k(u + v) = ku + kv. ℝn with component-wise operations satisfies all of them. Other objects satisfy them too: polynomials, sound signals, the colour channels of an image. The payoff is that every theorem proved from the axioms applies to all of them at once. B1

For engineering code, three practical points matter more than the axioms:

  • Units and meaning live outside the numbers. The list (3, 5) does not say whether it is tiles, metres or pixels, or which way the axes point. Production robotics code attaches a frame name to every vector for this reason (F0-53, RB301). B2
  • Computer numbers are not real numbers. A C++ double holds a finite set of values, so operations round. Small whole numbers and halves such as 2.5 are stored exactly, which is why the outputs in this chapter are exact; the vectors of later chapters (rotations) are not, and tests then compare with a tolerance (SP101 F2-08; MA302). B3R2
  • A vector type should be a small value type. Listing 1 uses a plain struct of two doubles, passed by value. It has no constructor, no heap allocation and no hidden cost, and the compiler can keep it in registers. B3

How the hardware actually does it

A Vec2 is two doubles stored next to each other in memory. On the build machine, Listing 1 printed that one Vec2 uses 16 bytes, which is two 8-byte doubles with no padding; that number is a measurement of this machine and compiler, not a rule of C++ (SP201 F2-18 explains sizeof). R1B3

Adding two vectors is n independent additions: the x sum does not wait for the y sum. Hardware exploits exactly this independence. CPUs have SIMD vector registers that hold several numbers and add them with one instruction (SP302 F2-53, where the word "vector" means "a register of several lanes", a third meaning). GPUs give each of thousands of threads its own element (CU201). The arithmetic of this chapter is the same in all three places; only the number of additions done at once changes. U1

Worked example

A drone takes off from a pad. Distances are in metres, components are (east, north, up). It flies three legs:

  1. leg 1 = (4, 3, 0): 4 m east and 3 m north, at the same height;
  2. leg 2 = (0, 0, 2.5): straight up 2.5 m;
  3. leg 3 = (−1, 2, 0): 1 m west and 2 m north.

Where does it end? Add component by component. East: 4 + 0 + (−1) = 3. North: 3 + 0 + 2 = 5. Up: 0 + 2.5 + 0 = 2.5. The drone ends at (3, 5, 2.5).

Does the order of the first two legs matter for the end point? leg 1 + leg 2 = (4, 3, 2.5) and leg 2 + leg 1 = (4, 3, 2.5). Climbing first and then flying, or flying first and then climbing, ends at the same point (but not along the same path: in a room with a low ceiling lamp that difference could matter).

Which single move takes it straight home? Home is the origin (0, 0, 0). "Target minus position": (0, 0, 0) − (3, 5, 2.5) = (−3, −5, −2.5), which is −1 times the end position.

Halfway along leg 1? 0.5 × (4, 3, 0) = (2, 1.5, 0).

As a combination of basis vectors. The floor point (2, 3, 0) is 2ex + 3ey: two units east plus three units north.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Tiles are whole; components are not. The floor counts whole tiles, but a vector component can be any real number, such as 1.5 or −0.25. The tiles are only a ruler for drawing. D1
    • 

  • A real robot's move is not just its arrow. On the floor, "3 tiles east, then 2 north" and "2 north, then 3 east" end on the same tile, but the robot crosses different tiles and may hit a chair on one path and not the other. The vector sum keeps only the end point and forgets the path. D1
    • 

  • "Towards the window" depends on the room. The kitchen fixes which way the axes point. A vector by itself carries no room: the same three numbers mean different arrows in a different frame. F0-53 makes this precise. B2
    • 

  • Vectors are not only arrows. Lists of numbers that add component by component also describe colours, sound samples or a row of prices. Arrows are the picture for 2D and 3D only. B1
    •

Summary


    

  • A vector is an ordered list of numbers; in 2D and 3D it is drawn as an arrow.
    • 

  • Addition and scalar multiplication work component by component.
    • 

  • Adding vectors means following arrows tip-to-tail; the order of addition does not change the sum.
    • 

  • "Target minus start" gives the displacement from start to target; swapping the order reverses it.
    • 

  • Positions and displacements look alike but behave differently: position + displacement is a position, position − position is a displacement.
    • 

  • Every vector is a linear combination of the basis vectors; its components are the coefficients.
    • 

  • std::vector is a list container, not a geometric vector.
