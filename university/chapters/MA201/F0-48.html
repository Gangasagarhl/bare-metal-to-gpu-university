Story hook

Analogy

Late in the afternoon, the sun shines low through the kitchen window and every chair throws a long shadow across the floor. Amara holds a broom handle at an angle above the floor and watches its shadow. When she holds it flat, along the direction of the sunlight, the shadow is as long as the broom. When she tips it upright, the shadow almost vanishes. When she points it back towards the window, the shadow falls on the other side.

Kenji's robot has a related problem. It faces one way, and it has just spotted its charger somewhere to the front-left. "How far ahead is it?" is not the same question as "how far away is it?". The answer to the first is the shadow of the arrow to the charger on the line the robot faces. If the shadow points backwards, the charger is behind.

Two tools answer these questions. The length of an arrow says how far. The dot product of two arrows says how much of one lies along the other. Together they also give the angle between two directions. Robots use them to decide what is in front, drones use them to split wind into headwind and crosswind, and every entry of a matrix product (F0-50) is one dot product.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute the length of a 2D or 3D vector and the distance between two points with Pythagoras.
  • Normalise a vector to length 1 and explain when that is not possible.
  • Compute a dot product, and use its sign to decide whether something is in front of, beside or behind a robot.
  • Find the angle between two vectors, and the part of one vector along another (projection).
  • Diagnose an angle computation that prints nan because a length was left out.
Jargon box
Length (norm) of a vector
Simple words: how long the arrow is. Analogy: measuring a move on the kitchen floor with a tape measure, straight from tail to tip. Precise: |v| = √(v1² + … + vn²), the Euclidean norm; for (3, 4) it is 5. D1B1
Unit vector
Simple words: an arrow of length exactly 1 that only says "which way". Analogy: pointing your finger in a direction without saying how far. Precise: a vector u with |u| = 1; for any non-zero v, v / |v| is the unit vector in v's direction (normalising). The zero vector has no direction and cannot be normalised. D1
Dot product
Simple words: multiply matching numbers of two vectors and add the results; the answer is one number. Analogy: the shadow of one arrow on the line of the other, times that other arrow's length. Precise: u · v = u1v1 + … + unvn = |u| |v| cos θ, where θ is the angle between u and v. Also called the scalar product or inner product. D1B1
Orthogonal (perpendicular)
Simple words: at right angles. Analogy: the broom held upright, with no shadow along the floor line. Precise: two vectors are orthogonal when u · v = 0. D1B1
Projection
Simple words: the part of an arrow that goes along a chosen direction. Analogy: the shadow on the floor line. Precise: for a unit vector d, the scalar projection of v on d is v · d, and the vector projection is (v · d) d; the rest, v − (v · d) d, is orthogonal to d. D1B1
Radian
Simple words: the unit of angle that maths libraries use; a half turn is π radians. Analogy: measuring a turn by how far a point one metre from the centre travels around the circle. Precise: the angle that cuts an arc equal to the radius; 180° = π rad, so 1 rad ≈ 57.2958°. The C++ functions std::cos and std::acos use radians. D1B3R2
Note

Maths you need here. MA101 did not cover these three tools, so here is the minimum. Square root: √x is the non-negative number whose square is x; √25 = 5 because 5 × 5 = 25. Pythagoras: in a right-angled triangle, the long side c satisfies c² = a² + b². A move of 3 east and 4 north is the long side of a triangle with legs 3 and 4, so its length is √(9 + 16) = 5. Cosine: for an angle θ, cos θ is a number between −1 and 1 that tells how much a unit arrow at angle θ from a line points along that line: cos 0° = 1 (fully along), cos 60° = 0.5, cos 90° = 0 (across), cos 120° = −0.5, cos 180° = −1 (fully backwards). The inverse, arccos (std::acos), turns such a number back into an angle between 0° and 180°. Angles in C++ are in radians: degrees × π / 180. These values are printed by run R2. D1R2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

How long is an arrow?

The move (3, 4) goes 3 tiles east and 4 tiles north. If you stretched a string straight from the start to the end, how long would it be? The east part and the north part meet at a right angle, so they form a right-angled triangle with the string as its long side. Pythagoras says the long side squared equals the sum of the other two squared: 3 × 3 + 4 × 4 = 9 + 16 = 25. The square root of 25 is 5. So the move is 5 tiles long, even though it was 3 + 4 = 7 tiles of walking along the grid lines.

The same rule works in 3D, with one more square: a drone 2 m east, 3 m north and 6 m up from its pad is √(4 + 9 + 36) = √49 = 7 m away. The distance between two points is the length of the arrow from one to the other, "target minus start" from F0-47.

Only the direction, please

Sometimes you only care which way an arrow points. Divide every component by the length and you get an arrow of length 1, a unit vector. (3, 4) divided by 5 is (0.6, 0.8). It points the same way, but its length is exactly 1. The zero vector has no length to divide by and no direction, so it has no unit vector.

How much is ahead?

The robot faces east, so its forward direction is the unit vector (1, 0). The charger is at the end of the arrow (3, 4). To find how far ahead the charger is, multiply the matching numbers of the two vectors and add them: 1 × 3 + 0 × 4 = 3. That is the dot product. The charger is 3 tiles ahead, as the shadow in Figure 1 shows.

The sign of the dot product tells the story at a glance. Positive: the thing is in front. Zero: it is exactly to the side. Negative: it is behind. For a sofa at the end of the arrow (−2, 2), the dot product with (1, 0) is −2. The sofa is behind the robot.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

For vectors u and v in ℝn the dot product is the number u · v = u1v1 + u2v2 + … + unvn. Example: (1, 2, 3) · (4, −5, 6) = 4 − 10 + 18 = 12. It has the rules you would hope for: u · v = v · u, u · (v + w) = u · v + u · w, and (ku) · v = k(u · v). The dot product of a vector with itself is the sum of the squares of its components, which is the square of its length: v · v = |v|². D1B1R2

The angle formula

The same number also equals |u| |v| cos θ, where θ is the angle between the two arrows (0° to 180°). This is the law of cosines from geometry in vector form; Strang's text proves it, and run R2 checks it on this chapter's examples. Rearranged, it gives the angle: B1R2

cos θ = (u · v) / (|u| |v|), so θ = arccos( (u · v) / (|u| |v|) ).

For the robot: forward (1, 0) and to = (3, 4) give cos θ = 3 / (1 × 5) = 0.6 and θ ≈ 53.13°. For the sofa, (1, 0) · (−2, 2) = −2 and |(−2, 2)| = √8 ≈ 2.828, so cos θ ≈ −0.707 and θ = 135°. Because |cos θ| ≤ 1, the quotient must lie between −1 and 1; if your code produces a value outside that range, a length is missing or a vector is zero, and std::acos returns nan ("not a number"). That is the forensic lab. D1R1R3

Two vectors with u · v = 0 are orthogonal: (2, 1) · (−1, 2) = −2 + 2 = 0, so they are at right angles. Checking orthogonality needs no square roots and no angles. D1R2

Projection: splitting a vector in two

If d is a unit vector, v · d is the signed length of v's shadow along d (the scalar projection), and (v · d) d is that shadow as a vector. What is left, v − (v · d) d, is orthogonal to d. So every vector splits into "the part along d" plus "the part across d", and Pythagoras holds between the parts: |v|² = (v · d)² + |rest|². The worked example uses exactly this to split a wind into headwind and crosswind. D1B1

A field-of-view test without angles

A camera that sees 60° to either side of forward sees a thing when the angle to it is at most 60°, that is when cos θ ≥ cos 60° = 0.5. Since cos θ = (forward · to) / |to| for a unit forward vector, the test is one dot product, one length and one comparison. Listing 1 uses it: the charger (cos θ = 0.6) is in view, the door (cos θ = 0, straight to the side) is not. D1R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. |u · v| ≤ |u| |v| for all vectors, with equality exactly when one is a multiple of the other. This is what makes the angle formula meaningful: the quotient can never leave [−1, 1] in exact arithmetic. In floating-point arithmetic it can, by a tiny rounding error, when the two vectors are almost parallel; Listing 1 therefore clamps the quotient into [−1, 1] before calling std::acos. A clamp hides rounding, but it must not hide a real bug: the forensic lab's values are far outside the range, and a clamp there would turn an obvious nan into a silently wrong 0° or 180°. B1B2R3

Angles near 0° are badly conditioned through arccos. Near cos θ = 1 the arccos curve is very steep, so a tiny error in the cosine becomes a large error in the angle. Libraries that need small angles accurately combine the dot product with the 2D cross product, using std::atan2; F0-52 uses atan2 for headings. MA302 studies such conditioning. B2

Length without overflow. Squaring very large components can overflow a double even when the length itself is representable. The C++ standard library offers std::hypot for two or three arguments, which is specified to avoid that intermediate overflow. For the room-sized numbers of robots and drones, std::sqrt(dot(v, v)) is fine. B3

Dot products are the inner loop of linear algebra. One entry of a matrix–vector product (F0-49) or of a matrix–matrix product (F0-50) is a dot product of a row with a column. That is why GPU courses spend so much effort on making dot products fast (CU302). U1

How the hardware actually does it

A dot product of length n is n multiplications and n − 1 additions, done as a running sum: sum = sum + a[i] * b[i]. Many processors provide a fused multiply-add instruction that computes a × b + c with a single rounding at the end, and compilers may use it when allowed. Because floating-point addition is not associative, adding the same products in a different order (for example in parallel, several partial sums at once) can change the last digits of the result. That is why the GPU and SIMD courses compare results with a tolerance instead of exact equality (SP302 F2-53, MA302). The examples in this chapter use small whole numbers and their dot products are exact. B2U1R2

Not verified — check before relying on this

Whether a given compiler contracts a * b + c into a fused multiply-add by default depends on the compiler, its version, the target processor and options such as floating-point contraction flags. This chapter does not state the default for g++ 13.3 on the build machine. Check the GCC manual's section on floating-point optimisation options for the recorded version, and the processor's instruction set reference, before relying on either behaviour.

Worked example

A drone flies north-east in the direction d = (0.6, 0.8, 0) (east, north, up). The wind is w = (2, −1, 0) m/s: 2 m/s towards the east and 1 m/s towards the south. How much of the wind pushes the drone along its path, and how much pushes it sideways?

  1. Check d is a unit vector: |d| = √(0.36 + 0.64 + 0) = √1 = 1.
  2. Along-track part: w · d = 2 × 0.6 + (−1) × 0.8 + 0 × 0 = 1.2 − 0.8 = 0.4 m/s. Positive, so it is a tailwind of 0.4 m/s, pushing the drone forward.
  3. Along-track vector: 0.4 × (0.6, 0.8, 0) = (0.24, 0.32, 0).
  4. Crosswind: w − (0.24, 0.32, 0) = (1.76, −1.32, 0). Its length is √(1.76² + 1.32²) = √(3.0976 + 1.7424) = √4.84 = 2.2 m/s.
  5. Check orthogonality: (1.76, −1.32, 0) · (0.6, 0.8, 0) = 1.056 − 1.056 = 0. The crosswind is exactly across the path.
  6. Check Pythagoras: |w|² = 4 + 1 = 5, and 0.4² + 2.2² = 0.16 + 4.84 = 5.
  7. Angle between wind and path: cos θ = 0.4 / √5 ≈ 0.179, so θ ≈ 79.70°. The wind blows almost across the path, which matches the large crosswind.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A shadow has no sign; a dot product does. A shadow on the floor is just a dark patch. The dot product is negative when the arrow points backwards, which the shadow picture only shows if you say which side of the broom the shadow falls. D1
    • 

  • The shadow equals the dot product only for a unit direction. If "forward" were (2, 0) instead of (1, 0), the dot product would be twice the shadow length. Always normalise the direction first, or divide by its length. D1
    • 

  • Real shadows depend on the sun's height; projection does not. A low sun stretches shadows longer than the object. A projection can never be longer than the vector itself (Cauchy–Schwarz). B1
    • 

  • Dot products work in any number of dimensions. There is no shadow to see in 4 or 1,000 dimensions, but the formula works the same, and F0-50 uses it with long rows and columns. B1
    •
