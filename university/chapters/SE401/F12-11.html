Story hook

Kwame, Ana, Yusuf and Ingrid are building a house for a family. On the first day the family says two things: "We want it warm," and "We want to move in in about two months." Everyone nods. Six weeks later the house is finished, the heating works, and the family is unhappy. On the first cold night the living room reached only 16 °C, because the front door opened every few minutes while the children played outside. And the move-in date slipped by a week, although every team finished "on time on average".

Ana sits down with the family and writes the requirements again, this time so that someone could check them: "With −10 °C outside and the front door opened 30 times an hour, the living room stays at or above 20 °C, measured at the middle of the room, every hour of a winter week." And: "The house is ready in 60 days; the foundation team has 10 days, walls 20, roof 15, interior 15, and each team says how many days it needs in a bad week, not an average one."

The first version was a wish. The second is a requirement: it names the number, the conditions, where it is measured and for how long. This chapter does the same for software: performance stated so that a test can fail it, and split into a budget each part of the system can own.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a performance requirement that names its six parts (statistic, threshold, offered load, load-generator type, measurement point, window), and recognise a requirement that no test can fail.
  • Split an end-to-end latency target into a budget along the request path, knowing that means add up and percentiles do not.
  • Compute how fan-out amplifies the tail (the share 1 − 0.99k of requests that meet at least one slow call) and use it when budgeting.
  • Turn a requirement into a performance acceptance test whose target is written before the work and never lowered silently (curriculum 13.4).
  • Diagnose a release that passed a vague requirement in the lab and failed it in production, from the test report and the dashboard.
Jargon box
Performance requirement
Simple words: a promise about speed that a test can check. Analogy: "20 °C in the living room at −10 °C outside with the door opened 30 times an hour", not "warm". Precise: in this course, a statement that names (1) the statistic (mean, or a percentile such as p99), (2) the threshold with its unit, (3) the offered load at which it must hold, (4) how that load is generated (open or closed loop), (5) the point where time is measured, and (6) the window over which the statistic is computed. A statement missing any part has a verdict that depends on a guess (Listing 2, R2) R2. This six-part checklist is the course's convention, built from the measurement protocol of curriculum 13.4 and the SLO definitions of F5-42 D2 D4.
Offered load
Simple words: how many requests per second arrive at the system, whether it keeps up or not. Analogy: how often the front door opens, decided by the children, not by the heater. Precise: the arrival rate imposed on the system under test, in requests per second, as opposed to the throughput it actually completes. Below saturation the two are equal on average; above it, throughput stops growing while offered load keeps arriving and queues grow.
Open-loop and closed-loop load generator
Simple words: an open-loop tester sends requests on a timetable; a closed-loop tester waits for each answer before sending the next. Analogy: children who open the door whenever they like, versus one child who waits until the room is warm again before going out. Precise: an open-loop generator issues requests at times fixed in advance (for example exponential gaps at rate λ), independent of the system's responses; a closed-loop generator has a fixed number of clients, each sending its next request only after the previous reply. A closed-loop test with one client never builds a queue, so it cannot observe queueing delay (Listing 2, R2; the measurement consequences are the subject of F12-13) R2.
Latency budget
Simple words: the total time a request may take, divided into parts, one per piece of the system. Analogy: 60 days for the house: 10 for the foundation, 20 for walls, 15 for the roof, 15 inside. Precise: an allocation of an end-to-end latency target to the components on the request's critical path, each owned by a team, such that meeting every component's allocation implies meeting the end-to-end target. Allocations of means add exactly; allocations of percentiles do not (Listing 1, R1) R1.
Tail amplification (fan-out)
Simple words: when one request waits for many smaller ones, it is slow whenever any one of them is slow. Analogy: the house is late if any one of ten deliveries is late. Precise: if a request waits for k independent calls and each call exceeds its own p99 with probability 0.01, the request meets at least one such slow call with probability 1 − 0.99k (arithmetic of independent events; checked by simulation in R1) R1; the effect in large services is discussed in D5 D5.
Performance acceptance test
Simple words: a test that fails when the system is too slow. Analogy: the inspector's thermometer in the living room on a cold evening. Precise: an executable check that measures a performance requirement under its stated conditions and compares the result with a target that was written down before the work began; curriculum 13.4 adds that targets are never lowered afterwards without the owner's explicit approval and a log entry D2.

Terms from earlier courses used here: percentile (p50, p99), SLO, SLI and error budget (DS402, F5-41, F5-42); tail latency (DS201, F5-07); median and percentile (nearest-rank, as used in all SE401 listings), benchmark and performance regression (SP302, F2-51, F2-55).

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows a request's path through four stages, each with its own time, and the two ways to give each stage a share of the end-to-end target. On the left, shares of the mean: they add up exactly. On the right, shares of the 99th percentile: the sum of the stages' p99 values is larger than the p99 of the whole request, because the slow 1 % of one stage rarely coincides with the slow 1 % of another.

Figure 2 shows the second effect a budget must respect: fan-out. A request that waits for the slowest of k parallel calls meets at least one call slower than that call's own p99 far more often than 1 % of the time.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; registered: design doc = the house blueprint; proposed in NOTES.md): "warm" = a performance wish; "20 °C in the living room at −10 °C outside with the door opened 30 times an hour, every hour of a winter week" = a performance requirement (threshold, load, measurement point, window); the children opening the door = offered load; the 60-day schedule split into team shares = a latency budget; the inspector's thermometer = the performance acceptance test.

"Fast" is a wish, like "warm". A wish cannot fail, so it cannot guide anyone. A requirement says how fast, for which requests, how busy the system is at the time, where the clock is held, and for how long you watch. Then anyone can test it and get the same answer.

A requirement for the whole request is then split into shares for each part. That way each team knows its own number. But be careful with "bad days": two teams rarely have their worst day on the same day, so adding up everyone's worst day gives a number worse than the house's real worst day. And when the house waits for many deliveries, one late delivery is enough to be late.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The six parts of a performance requirement

Listing 2 (reqcheck.cpp) reads requirement sentences and looks for six parts. Each part exists because leaving it out changes the verdict:

  1. Statistic. Mean or a percentile. The mean hides the tail; users who wait are in the tail. F5-41 showed why services report p50 and p99 rather than averages D4.
  2. Threshold with unit. "≤ 20 ms". Without a number nothing can fail: R0 in Listing 2, "the get API must be fast", is rejected as not testable R2.
  3. Offered load. "At 400 req/s". Latency depends on load; a requirement without load is true at some load and false at another. In R2, "mean latency of get ≤ 5 ms" passes at 100 req/s (2.01 ms) and fails at 400 req/s (10.00 ms) on the same simulated service R2.
  4. Load-generator type. Open loop or closed loop. A closed-loop client waits for each answer, so it never makes requests queue behind each other; it measures service time, not response time under load (F12-13 shows the consequences in detail).
  5. Measurement point. Where the clock starts and stops: at the client, at the gateway, inside the server. Time spent in a queue in front of the server is invisible to a timer inside it.
  6. Window. Over how long the statistic is computed: one minute, ten minutes, a month. A p99 over a quiet hour and a p99 over the busiest ten minutes are different numbers. SLOs in F5-42 always name their window for the same reason D4.

A requirement with all six parts (R2, R3, R4 in reqcheck.in) produces one verdict. Note what the run says about this simulated service: at 400 req/s its p99 is 46.12 ms and fails a 20 ms target, while at 250 req/s it is 18.76 ms and passes R2. A precise requirement does not make the system faster; it makes the truth visible early, while it is cheap to act on.

Where performance requirements come from

Three places, in order of preference:

  • From users and their tasks. A person waiting for a page, a robot control loop that must finish before the next sensor sample, a training step that must not leave GPUs idle. Each task gives a time that matters and a share of cases that must meet it.
  • From the service-level objectives the service already promises (F5-42): an SLO of "99 % of requests within 100 ms at the gateway, over 30 days" is a performance requirement for the whole service; this chapter's job is to break it into parts D4.
  • From a reference. For GPU kernels the curriculum's acceptance tests compare with the vendor library on the same GPU, same data type and sizes, with the target written as a percentage of that reference before work starts D2.

A number "from experience" or "what the old system did" is a starting point for discussion, not a requirement, until someone has written down the six parts and agreed to them.

Budgets: splitting the target

A request crosses several components. If the whole request must meet a target, each component needs a share it can test on its own. Listing 1 shows the arithmetic every budget must respect R1:

  • Means add. The mean of a sum is the sum of the means, whatever the distributions. The four stage means (0.50, 1.00, 3.00, 3.50 ms) add to 8.00 ms, and the end-to-end mean is 8.00 ms.
  • Percentiles do not add. The stage p99 values add to 23.22 ms, but the p99 of the whole request is 16.36 ms. A request in the slowest 1 % of the store stage is usually not also in the slowest 1 % of the replicate stage, so the slow parts rarely line up.

Two practical consequences follow. A team that splits a p99 target by giving each stage a p99 share is conservative (safe but wasteful) when stages vary independently. And when stages are not independent, because they share a cause of slowness (the same overloaded machine, the same lock, the same garbage-collection pause), slow parts do line up, and the end-to-end p99 moves towards the sum. A budget therefore states its assumption: "stages independent, checked by measuring the end-to-end p99 as well as the parts".

Fan-out: when one request waits for many

A search request may ask ten index shards and wait for all of them. Its time is the time of the slowest. If each shard is slower than its own p99 in 1 % of calls, and the calls are independent, the chance that all ten are fast is 0.9910 ≈ 0.904, so about 9.6 % of requests meet at least one slow shard. With 100 shards it is 1 − 0.99100 ≈ 63.4 %. The simulation in Listing 1 measured 9.8 % and 63.3 % R1. The lesson for budgets: a component that is called k times in parallel per user request needs a budget at a much higher percentile than the user-facing target. To keep a fan-out of 100 within its p99 for 99 % of requests, each call must meet its share in all but about 1 in 10,000 calls (because 0.9999100 ≈ 0.99). The practical importance of this effect in large online services is the subject of D5 D5.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Performance requirements as acceptance tests

Curriculum 13.4 describes performance acceptance tests in four rules, and they generalise beyond GPU kernels D2:

  1. Correctness first. A fast wrong answer is a failure; the performance test runs only on outputs that the correctness test accepted.
  2. A fixed measurement protocol. Warm-up runs, then the median of at least 20 timed runs, with the settings recorded (F2-51 built this protocol; F12-13 extends it to whole services).
  3. A reference measured by the same harness. Datasheet peaks are context, not targets.
  4. Targets written before the work, and "the agent never lowers X after the fact without your explicit approval and a log entry". The mega-project rule repeats it: for MP3, targets are agreed at the R1 gate before coding D3.

Rule 4 is the one that makes performance a requirement rather than a report. If the target is chosen after measuring, it describes what the system does, not what it must do. Write it down first; if it later proves wrong, change it in the open, with the reason, like any other requirement.

Keeping requirements true over time

A requirement met at release can be lost by any later change. Two mechanisms keep it:

  • A performance test in continuous integration (SE302, F12-09) that runs the acceptance test, or a cheaper proxy of it, on every change, and fails the build on a performance regression beyond a threshold. The threshold must be larger than the run-to-run noise of the CI machine, or the test becomes flaky (F12-10); F12-13 shows how to estimate that noise with a confidence interval.
  • Production monitoring of the same statistic at the same measurement point (F5-41, F5-42), so that the requirement is checked under real load, not only in the test environment. The forensic lab of this chapter is what happens when the test and production measure different things.

Beyond latency

The same six-part discipline applies to other performance properties. Throughput: "≥ 2,000 req/s sustained for 30 minutes with p99 ≤ 50 ms at the gateway" (a throughput requirement is meaningless without a latency bound, because any system reaches high throughput with unbounded queues). Resource use: "peak resident memory ≤ 2 GiB at 10,000 open sessions". Start-up: "from power-on to the first served request ≤ 30 s, median of 20 boots". Efficiency: "≥ 70 % of the vendor library's throughput on the same GPU at sizes 1024, 4096, 8192" (the curriculum's form) D2. Each names a statistic, a threshold, the conditions, the measurement point and the window.

Choosing the number

Tighter requirements cost more. A p99.9 target needs ten times more samples than a p99 target to measure with the same confidence (nearest-rank p99.9 over 1,000 samples is the single largest value), more machines to provide headroom (F12-15), and engineering effort on rare events. Gregg's book on systems performance discusses how performance goals relate to cost and to the methodology of measuring them D1; this chapter's rule is simpler: every number in a requirement has an owner who can explain which user task needs it.

How the hardware actually does it

Requirements must name load because the hardware underneath is shared, and sharing creates queues. A CPU core runs one thread at a time; a disk serves a limited number of operations at once; a network link carries one packet at a time per direction. When requests arrive faster than a resource finishes them, they wait, and the wait grows sharply as the resource approaches full use. That is why the same service in Listing 2 has a p99 of 18.76 ms at 250 req/s and 46.12 ms at 400 req/s: its single simulated server is busy half the time in the first case and 80 % of the time in the second R2. F12-12 derives the formula behind these numbers; here it is enough to see that "latency" without "at what load" is not a property of the system.

Measurement point matters for a hardware reason too. Time spent waiting in a network card's receive ring, in the kernel's socket buffer, or in a request queue in front of the worker threads is not seen by a timer started when a worker picks the request up. The F12-14 service measures from the intended arrival time for exactly this reason (F12-14).

Worked example

Task. The team behind the simulated service of Listing 2 is asked for a requirement for its "get" API that the product owner can sign. Users notice delays over about 50 ms in the search page; the search page calls get once per keystroke. Traffic forecasts say 250 req/s at the busiest ten minutes of a day for the next quarter.

Step 1 — statistic and threshold. One call per keystroke means users feel individual slow calls, not averages, so the statistic is a percentile. The team chooses p99 ≤ 20 ms for get, leaving the rest of the 50 ms for the network and the page. A second line catches rare long stalls: p99.9 ≤ 40 ms.

Step 2 — load, generator, point, window. At 250 req/s (the forecast busiest ten minutes); open loop, because real users do not wait for each other; measured at the gateway, which is where the SLO is measured too; over 10 minutes, matching the busiest-window forecast.

Step 3 — write and run it. These are R3 and R4 of reqcheck.in. The run: p99 18.76 ms at 250 req/s, PASS; p99.9 28.59 ms, PASS R2.

Step 4 — check the margin. 18.76 ms against 20 ms is a margin of about 6 %. R2 shows the same service at 400 req/s with a p99 of 46.12 ms. So the requirement holds at the forecast load but the service has little headroom; the team records "capacity review needed before load exceeds 250 req/s" as an open item (capacity planning is F12-15).

Step 5 — budget it. Suppose the get call crossed four internal stages like those of Listing 1. The team gives each stage an owner and a p99 share. Using Listing 1's numbers as an example: shares summing to the stages' p99 values (23.22 ms) would over-state the end-to-end p99 (16.36 ms) by about 42 %, so the team budgets with the stage p99 values but verifies with an end-to-end measurement, and records the independence assumption R1.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A house is tested once; a service is tested forever. The inspector measures the living room on one cold evening. A service's load, code and machines change every week, so its performance requirement must be re-checked continuously, in CI and in production monitoring.
    • 

  • Room temperature is one number; latency is a distribution. "20 °C at the middle of the room" is a single reading. Software latency differs for every request, so the requirement must also say which part of the distribution (mean, p99, p99.9) it constrains. The analogy has no natural equivalent of the tail.
    • 

  • Building schedules add up; latency percentiles do not. The 60-day house schedule adds team durations in series and can be planned from each team's worst week. Latency percentiles of stages in series do not add (Listing 1), so budgeting by "everyone's worst day" over-states the whole when stages are independent and under-states it when they share a cause.
    • 

  • The children choose when to open the door, but software load can push back. In a house, the door opens whatever the heater does. Some software systems slow their clients down when overloaded (back-pressure), which changes the offered load itself; a requirement must then say whether the load is offered (open loop) or allowed (closed loop).
    •

Summary


    

  • A performance requirement names six parts: statistic, threshold with unit, offered load, generator type, measurement point and window; without them its verdict depends on a guess.
    • 

  • Prefer percentiles to means for anything users feel request by request.
    • 

  • Latency budgets give each component a share and an owner; means add exactly, percentiles do not, and the gap depends on whether stages share causes of slowness.
    • 

  • Fan-out amplifies the tail: 1 − 0.99k of requests meet at least one call slower than its p99.
    • 

  • Performance acceptance tests follow curriculum 13.4: correctness first, a fixed protocol, a reference measured by the same harness, and targets written before the work and never lowered silently.
    • 

  • Keep requirements true with CI tests whose thresholds exceed measured noise, and with production monitoring at the same measurement point.
    • 

  • Closed-loop tests with one client never build queues; they cannot verify a requirement about latency under load.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. signoff.cpp simulates one server with exponential service time (mean 2 ms). Evidence 1 is a closed-loop test with one client pacing itself to 100 req/s for 60 s, so no request ever waits for another. Evidence 2 is an open-loop simulation of 10-minute windows at the stated rates, each starting with an empty queue; a request over 50 ms counts as a client timeout R3.


Observation. Timeouts appear at 11:30 (51), 12:10 (710) and 12:40 (4,883), and a few at 14:00 (7). As load rises from 120 to 420 req/s the p99 rises from 12.34 to 58.70 ms (about 4.8×), the mean from 2.65 to 12.69 ms, the p50 from 1.83 to 8.65 ms. The p99 crosses the 50 ms client timeout first; it is the statistic users feel.


REQ-7 in production. At 12:40 the mean is 12.69 ms: REQ-7 is violated even on its own terms. At 08:00 the mean is 2.65 ms: met. So REQ-7's verdict depends on the load, which it does not name: the requirement was never a statement about the system, only about the test's conditions.


Missing or wrong parts. Statistic: a mean, while users time out on individual requests (should be p99 or p99.9). Load: none named; the test used 100 req/s, production peaks at 420. Generator: the test was closed loop with one client, so queueing could not occur; production is open loop. Measurement point: not named. Window: not named; the test lasted 60 s at a constant pace, production's problem is the busiest ten minutes.


Why 9.16 ms versus 58.70 ms. (1) No queueing in the test: its p99 is the p99 of the service time alone (2 ms × ln 100 ≈ 9.2 ms for an exponential with mean 2 ms). (2) Production's lunchtime load is 84 % of the simulated server's capacity of 500 req/s, where waiting time dominates (F12-12 derives this).


Rewritten requirement. "p99 latency of get ≤ 40 ms at 420 req/s offered, open loop, measured at the gateway, over 10 minutes" (from the 12:40 window, with margin under the 50 ms client timeout). Evidence 2 shows p99 58.70 ms at 420 req/s, so the release would have failed, as it should: the service needs more capacity or a shorter service time. Wrong answer to rule out: "lower the threshold to 3 ms average" — still a mean, still no load, the closed-loop test still passes it.


Process change. Run every performance requirement through the six-part checklist at the design review (Listing 2 or a reviewer's checklist), and require the acceptance test's load to be at least the forecast busiest-window load, open loop, with the window written down. Add production monitoring of the same p99 at the gateway so the next drift is seen before users report it.
