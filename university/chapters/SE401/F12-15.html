Story hook

Next spring the team will build twelve houses instead of four. Yusuf does the sums: last season they laid on average 1,600 bricks a day; a bricklayer can lay 1,000 a day flat out; with a comfortable 70 % pace, three bricklayers will do, and they hire four "for safety".

The first week of spring is the busiest of the year: all twelve foundations are ready at once, and the client wants walls before the rain. The four bricklayers lay as fast as they can, the walls start leaning, and on Wednesday Kwame is called away for his brother's wedding, as planned since winter. Three bricklayers, twice the work of an average day. Bricks pile up for days.

Ingrid reads Yusuf's plan afterwards. Every number in it was correct. It was the wrong numbers: the average day instead of the busiest week, the flat-out pace instead of the pace that keeps walls straight, and no one off sick. This chapter is about planning for the day that matters.

What you will be able to do

  • Derive a node's safe rate from its requirement and a load test, and explain why it is lower than the rate at which its CPU is fully used.
  • Forecast the peak demand for a planning horizon from a day profile, a peak factor and a growth rate, and state the forecast's assumptions.
  • Size a deployment for the peak with N+1 redundancy, and check it against the case of one node down at peak.
  • Fit the Universal Scalability Law to throughput measurements, read its contention and coherency terms, and judge whether the fit can be trusted.
  • Diagnose a capacity failure from a plan, traffic metrics and a change calendar.
Jargon box
Capacity plan
Simple words: a written answer to "how much hardware do we need, by when, and why?" Analogy: the hiring plan for next season's busiest week, with the sums shown. Precise: a document that turns a requirement (an SLO), a measured capacity per unit (from a load test) and a demand forecast (peak, growth, horizon) into a number of units with redundancy, lists the assumptions, and says when it must be revisited. Its central rule: plan for the peak that must meet the requirement, with the failures you accept, not for the average D1.
Safe rate (of a node)
Simple words: the most work per second one machine can take while still meeting the latency promise. Analogy: the pace at which a bricklayer's walls stay straight, not the fastest pace he can manage. Precise: the highest offered load at which a load test of one unit still meets the latency SLO (here p99 ≤ 25 ms), measured under the production request mix. It is below the throughput limit because queueing delay rises steeply as utilisation approaches 1 (F12-12); in Listing 1's model it is 75 % of the CPU capacity R1.
Peak-to-average ratio
Simple words: how much busier the busiest time is than an average time. Analogy: the busiest week's bricks divided by an average week's. Precise: the ratio of the demand in a chosen peak window (busiest hour, busiest minute) to the average demand over a longer window (a day, a week). Plans built on averages fail by roughly this factor at peak; the right window is the shortest one over which the system cannot absorb an overload in its queues without breaking the SLO.
Headroom
Simple words: spare capacity kept free on purpose. Analogy: the bricklayer you could put on the wall tomorrow if a foundation is ready early. Precise: the difference between the capacity that meets the SLO and the current or forecast demand, kept to absorb forecast errors, bursts shorter than the planning window, failures and the lead time needed to add capacity. Headroom is stated in the same units as demand (req/s) and with the assumptions it covers.
Universal Scalability Law (USL)
Simple words: a formula for how throughput grows, stops growing and can even fall as you add workers. Analogy: more bricklayers help, until they queue at the one mixer (contention) and spend their time telling each other what they changed (coherency). Precise: X(N) = λN / (1 + σ(N − 1) + κN(N − 1)), with λ the throughput of one unit, σ the contention (serialised) fraction and κ the coherency (crosstalk) cost. With κ = 0 it reduces to Amdahl's law in throughput form; with κ > 0 it has a peak at N* = √((1 − σ)/κ) and falls beyond it. Attributed to Neil Gunther D5; fitted in Listing 2.
Planning horizon and lead time
Simple words: how far ahead a plan must look, and how long it takes to get more capacity. Analogy: hiring takes two months, so in March you plan for May. Precise: the horizon is the period a plan must cover; it must be at least the lead time to obtain and bring capacity into service (purchase, delivery, installation, configuration, testing; or quota and region limits in a cloud) plus the interval between plan reviews.

Terms from earlier courses and chapters used here: SLO, percentile, offered load, performance model, utilisation law, saturation of a resource, queueing model (F12-11, F12-12); Little's law, Amdahl's law, utilisation; N+1 redundancy (DS304); scalability (DS301); lock contention (SP203).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): bricklayer = server node; bricks per day = requests per second; comfortable pace = safe rate; flat-out pace = CPU capacity; busiest week = peak demand; next season's twelve houses = growth over the planning horizon; one bricklayer off sick = one node down (N+1); time to hire = lead time; one mortar mixer shared by everyone = contention; bricklayers telling each other what changed = coherency.

How many people do you need for a job? Not enough for an average day: enough for the busiest day, working at a pace where the work stays good, with one person missing. And you must ask early, because new people take time to find.

Adding people helps, but not forever. If everyone needs the same tool, they start waiting for it. If everyone must tell everyone else what they did, they spend more time talking than working.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A capacity plan in four steps

Listing 1 builds a plan for a service of identical nodes from exercise inputs; every number below is from its output R1.

Step 1 — measure the unit against the requirement. The SLO is p99 ≤ 25 ms, fixed before the work as curriculum 13.4 asks D2. A load test of one node (here a simulated node: four workers, exponential service time with mean 4 ms) at increasing open-loop rates gives p99 23.82 ms at 750 req/s and 27.07 ms at 800 req/s. The safe rate is 750 req/s, 75 % of the CPU capacity of 1,000 req/s. The remaining 25 % is not waste; it is what keeps queueing delay inside the SLO (F12-12).

Step 2 — forecast the peak. The day's average is 1,664 req/s, but the busiest hour is 3,150 (peak-to-average 1.89). Within that hour the busiest minute is 1.25 times the hour (a factor from telemetry, exercise data), 3,938 req/s. Growth of 5 % per month for a nine-month horizon multiplies demand by 1.551: the planned peak is 6,108 req/s.

Step 3 — size with redundancy. ⌈6,108 / 750⌉ = 9 nodes carry the planned peak within the SLO. One more node means one can fail or be updated at peak: 10 nodes. Check: with one down, each of the 9 takes 679 req/s, below the safe rate.

Step 4 — compare with the naive plan. Average × growth / (CPU capacity × 70 %) = 2,582 / 700 → 4 nodes. At the planned peak those 4 nodes would each receive 1,527 req/s, above even the CPU capacity of 1,000: an overload, not a slow day.

The two plans differ by a factor of 2.5 in hardware. The naive plan is not "a bit optimistic"; it is wrong by the peak-to-average ratio, the peak-minute factor and the gap between safe rate and CPU capacity, all multiplied.

Which peak?

The right peak window depends on how long the system can absorb an overload. A service whose queues hold a few seconds of work cannot absorb a busy minute; it must be sized for the busiest minute or shorter. A batch system that must finish nightly jobs by morning can be sized on the night's total work. Asking "how long can demand exceed capacity before the requirement breaks?" chooses the window.

When demand exceeds capacity, the backlog grows at the difference. In the forensic lab, each remaining node receives 1,200 req/s against a capacity of 1,000: the backlog grows by 200 requests every second, and after ten minutes holds 120,000 requests, two minutes of work. That is what the gateway's p99 of 117,731 ms (about two minutes) shows R4. Overload does not degrade latency gently; it grows without limit for as long as the overload lasts.

Scaling out is not free

Step 3 assumes that ten nodes carry ten times the load of one. That holds when nodes share nothing on the request path. Shared state — a primary database, a lock service, a replicated log, a cache that must stay consistent — adds contention and coherency costs. The Universal Scalability Law (USL) models both D5: throughput X(N) = λN / (1 + σ(N − 1) + κN(N − 1)). σ is the fraction of work that is serialised (as in Amdahl's law); κ is a cost that grows with the number of pairs of units that must coordinate.

Listing 2 fits the USL to constructed exercise data (made from λ = 950, σ = 0.05, κ = 0.002, then moved by up to ±2 %). It recovers σ = 0.0519 and κ = 0.00199, with fit errors within ±2.3 %, and predicts a peak at N* = 21.8 with X(N*) = 7,015 req/s R2. Beyond about 22 units, adding units reduces throughput. A plan that needs more than that must change the design (partition the shared state), not buy more of the same.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Fitting the USL, and when not to trust it

With λ fixed to X(1), the USL rearranges to N·X(1)/X(N) − 1 = σ(N − 1) + κN(N − 1), which is linear in σ and κ. Listing 2 solves the two normal equations of an ordinary least-squares fit with no constant term. The rearrangement is convenient but not neutral: it weights points by the transformed variable, and an error in X(1) moves every point. Gunther's book discusses fitting practice D5; this chapter gives three checks you can always make:

  1. Residuals. Look at the error column. Small, patternless errors (Listing 2's exercise data: −2.3 % to +1.7 %) support the model. Large or systematic errors mean the model does not describe the system, whatever the coefficients say.
  2. Range. Predictions far beyond the measured N are extrapolations. A peak at N* = 21.8 from data up to N = 32 is inside the range; a peak predicted at 200 from data up to 8 is a guess.
  3. Stable conditions. Each point must be a steady-state measurement of the same workload on the same hardware (F12-13). Points measured at different times on a shared machine mix machine noise into the curve.

Listing 3 measures a real program on the build machine: N threads, each doing private work and then a short update of shared state under one mutex, one second per N, then Listing 2 fits the result R3. The fit fails the first and third checks. X(3) is 4.03 million requests per second but X(4) only 3.52 million; X(8), with twice as many threads as hardware threads, is 4.04 million. The fitted σ = 0.249 and κ = 0.015 give errors from −9.2 % to +22.2 %. The machine is a shared virtual machine with four reported hardware threads and other jobs running; one second per point is short; and above four threads the program measures time slicing as much as scalability. The honest reading: on this machine, throughput rose up to three threads and then stopped growing. The coefficients and the predicted N* = 7.1 should not be used for anything.

Redundancy, failure domains and correlated load

N+1 protects against one node down. Ask what "one" means: if two nodes share a power supply, a rack switch or a cloud zone, one failure takes both down; plan with the failure domain as the unit (N+1 zones, not N+1 nodes). Maintenance is also a "node down", and it is planned, so it can be avoided at peak — but only if the people scheduling maintenance know when the peak is (forensic lab). Finally, failures shift load: when a node fails, its clients retry on the others, briefly raising their load above the share computed in step 3. Admission control and load shedding (refusing some requests quickly) keep an overload from turning into the unbounded queues of the forensic lab.

Forecasts and their errors

A forecast is a model and must be checked like one (F12-12). Write the growth assumption as a number with its source (5 % per month, from the last twelve months), the peak factor with its measurement window, and the date when the forecast will be compared with reality. Compound growth makes errors grow too: 5 % per month for nine months is ×1.55, 7 % is ×1.84. Plan reviews at an interval shorter than the lead time catch a wrong forecast while there is still time to act. Events (launches, marketing campaigns, seasonal days) are not growth; they are separate peaks, and the people who plan them must tell the people who plan capacity.

Elastic capacity does not remove planning

Cloud services can add nodes in minutes, which shortens the lead time but does not remove it. Autoscaling reacts to a metric after demand has risen; if demand rises faster than new nodes start and warm up, the gap is served by the headroom you kept or not at all. Quotas, regional capacity and the budget are also limits. The plan changes form: instead of "buy 10 nodes", it says "keep at least 10 nodes' worth of headroom available at peak; scale on this metric with this lead; never fewer than 4".

How the hardware actually does it

A node's capacity is set by whichever resource saturates first under the production mix: processor cores, memory bandwidth, storage, the network interface, or a software resource such as a lock or a connection pool (F12-14). Listing 1's model has a single resource, the workers; a real node needs a load test with the real mix, and the USE method run during it, to know which resource sets the safe rate.

The USL's two terms have hardware counterparts. Contention (σ) is serialisation: one mutex, one disk queue, one database primary; Listing 3's mutex is one. Coherency (κ) is the cost of keeping shared data consistent: on a multicore processor, a cache line written by one core must be invalidated in or transferred to the others' caches, so a lock and the data it protects move between cores at every hand-over; between machines, replicas exchange messages D3. Both grow with the number of participants; the coherency cost grows with the number of pairs.

On the build machine, the four "hardware threads" reported to Listing 3 are virtual processors of a cloud virtual machine. Whether they are full cores or two threads of a hyperthreaded core, and how much other tenants use them, is not visible from inside; both change how throughput scales from three to four threads R3.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Why Listing 3's throughput fell from three to four threads on the build machine is not known. Hypotheses: the fourth virtual processor shares a physical core with another, other tenants' load during that second, or more lock hand-overs between cores. A test needs a dedicated machine with known topology, several repetitions per N in interleaved order, and hardware counters for cache-line transfers (not available in this build).

Worked example

Task. Re-plan Listing 1's service for a launch event: the marketing team expects the busiest minute of launch day to be 1.6 times the forecast busiest minute, and the service must still meet its SLO with one node down. Use the safe rate and forecast of Listing 1 R1.

Step 1 — the event peak. Forecast busiest minute at the horizon: 6,108 req/s. Launch: 6,108 × 1.6 = 9,773 req/s.

Step 2 — nodes at the safe rate. 9,773 / 750 = 13.03 → 14 nodes. Note how close 13 is: 13 × 750 = 9,750, 23 req/s short. Rounding up is not optional.

Step 3 — redundancy. 14 + 1 = 15 nodes. Check one down: 9,773 / 14 = 698 req/s per node, below 750.

Step 4 — headroom for the forecast error. If the marketing estimate could be 25 % low, the event peak is 12,216 req/s: 12,216 / 14 = 873 per node with one down, which fails the SLO (between the 850 and 900 rows of the load test: p99 33–46 ms) but stays below the CPU capacity of 1,000, so the service degrades rather than collapses. Decide explicitly whether that risk is acceptable; if not, 17 + 1 = 18 nodes cover it (12,216 / 750 = 16.3 → 17).

Step 5 — scalability check. Do 15–18 nodes scale linearly? If the nodes share a database primary whose USL fit gave N* = 21.8 (Listing 2's exercise data), 18 nodes are close to the peak, where each added node adds little. Measure the shared component at the event load before trusting step 2's linear arithmetic.

Step 6 — the plan's text. "Launch: 15 nodes (one spare) from the day before until the day after; 18 if the event estimate is uncertain by more than 15 %; no maintenance on the launch day; load test the shared database at 10,000 req/s by the date the nodes must be ordered; review the plan with marketing's final estimate."

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Bricklayers tire; nodes do not, but they do degrade. A tired person slows gradually; a node near saturation keeps its throughput while its latency explodes. The "comfortable pace" is set by latency, not by fatigue.
    • 

  • A crew can work overtime; a node cannot. When demand exceeds a node's capacity, there is no reserve effort to call on; the excess waits, and the queue grows without limit.
    • 

  • Hiring takes months; cloud capacity takes minutes. The analogy exaggerates lead times for elastic services, but understates them for shared components (databases, licences, quotas) that cannot be added quickly.
    • 

  • One sick bricklayer is independent of the others; nodes often fail together. Shared power, network or software versions make failures correlated, which the "one off sick" picture hides.

Summary


    

  • A capacity plan turns an SLO, a measured safe rate and a demand forecast into a number of units with redundancy, and shows every input.
    • 

  • The safe rate comes from a load test against the SLO; it is below CPU capacity because queueing delay rises steeply near saturation.
    • 

  • Plan on the peak window your queues cannot absorb, after growth over a horizon at least as long as the lead time; never on the average.
    • 

  • N+1 per failure domain, and check one down at peak; schedule maintenance away from the peak.
    • 

  • Above capacity, backlog grows at demand minus capacity: latency grows without limit for as long as the overload lasts.
    • 

  • Scaling out has limits set by shared state; the USL's σ and κ describe them, but only a fit with small residuals over steady measurements should be used.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. outage.cpp simulates 10 minutes of each listed hour on one node of Listing 1's model (4 workers, mean 4 ms, starting with an empty queue), with the hour's total traffic divided by the nodes in service R4.


Q1. The plan used a pilot average (1,900 req/s), CPU capacity with a 70 % target, and no peak factor or redundancy rule. With the chapter's method: the launch day's busiest hour was 3,600 req/s; 3,600 / 750 = 4.8 → 5 nodes, plus one spare = 6 (and more with a peak-minute factor and growth). Four nodes were enough only up to 3,000 req/s at the safe rate, with no node down.


Q2. At 19:00: 3,300 / 4 = 825 req/s per node, above the safe rate of 750; p99 30.5 ms exceeds the 25 ms SLO, though the node is below CPU capacity (82 %). 18:00 at 750 per node was the last hour within the SLO (23.9 ms).


Q3. node-3 was drained for a kernel update in the planned weekly maintenance window, at the busiest hour of launch day. The 3 remaining nodes each received 1,200 req/s, above the CPU capacity of 1,000: the backlog grew by about 200 requests per second. After 5 minutes (the middle of the sample) about 60,000 requests wait, about 60 s of work: p50 59.2 s. After 10 minutes, about 120,000, about 120 s: p99 117.7 s.


Q4. Still 3 nodes until 22:00: 3,100 / 3 = 1,033 req/s per node, still above capacity; the backlog grows by about 33 req/s per node, about 20,000 requests in 10 minutes, about 20 s (p99 19.5 s). Note that the simulation starts each hour with an empty queue; in reality the backlog from 20:00 would add to it, so 21:00 was worse than shown.


Q5. The average over the rows (2,610 req/s) mixes quiet and busy hours; the queues cannot store the excess of the busy hours for the quiet ones. Capacity must cover the peak window, with a node down. The average also counts each sampled hour once although the sample omits the night, so it is not even the day's average.


Q6. Plan errors: average instead of peak; pilot traffic scaled without a growth or launch factor; CPU target instead of a safe rate from the SLO; "deploy 4 for safety" without a one-down check. Process failures: maintenance scheduled months earlier without checking the launch date; no load test against the SLO before launch; no admission control, so overload became minutes of latency instead of fast refusals. Corrected plan: launch peak from the pilot's peak hour × a launch factor × a minute factor, at 750 req/s per node, N+1 (6 or more nodes); freeze maintenance on launch days; load shedding when queues exceed a few seconds of work. Wrong answer to rule out: "the kernel update caused it" — it removed a node the plan had no margin for; at 19:00, with all four nodes, the SLO had already failed.
