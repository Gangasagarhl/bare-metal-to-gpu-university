Story hook

Before the walls go up, Yusuf does arithmetic on the back of an envelope. Twelve thousand bricks, four bricklayers, each lays about 500 bricks a day: six days. Ingrid looks at the envelope and asks one question: "Where does the mortar come from?" There is one mixer. Each bricklayer fetches a fresh bucket every hour, and filling a bucket takes about ten minutes. Four bricklayers, four buckets an hour, forty minutes of mixing per hour: the mixer is busy two thirds of the time.

"Then they will wait," says Ingrid, "and not a little. When the mixer is busy most of the hour, a bricklayer who arrives often finds someone already in front." Yusuf adds a waiting line to the envelope, and a column: "what I expect to see on day one". On day one they count. The walls go a little slower than six days, as the envelope said they would, and the mixer queue is about as long as predicted. Where it is not, they look for the reason before believing either the envelope or the count.

That envelope is a performance model. This chapter is about writing it before you measure: so you know what to measure, so you notice when the measurement is strange, and so the first design is not built on hope.

What you will be able to do

  • Apply the operational laws (utilisation law, Little's law, the bottleneck bound) to predict a system's throughput limit from service demands, before measuring it.
  • Predict mean and p99 latency of a single-server queue from its utilisation with the M/M/1 and M/D/1 formulas, and explain why latency rises steeply near full utilisation.
  • Check a model against a simulation and a real measurement, and classify the gap: a missing mechanism, a wrong assumption, or a noisy measurement.
  • Find a hidden serial resource (a lock, a single log writer) that a CPU-only model misses, from thread-state evidence.
  • Write predictions down before running an experiment, and explain the remaining gap afterwards (curriculum 13.4).
Jargon box
Performance model
Simple words: a small calculation that predicts how fast a system will be, made before you measure it. Analogy: Yusuf's envelope: bricks, bricklayers, the mixer, the expected days. Precise: a simplified quantitative description of a system (its resources, the work each request demands of them, and the arrival pattern) from which throughput and latency can be predicted; it states its assumptions so that a measurement can confirm or refute it. Families used in this university: operational laws, queueing models, the roofline model (F2-56), the alpha–beta model (F1-62) and Amdahl's law (F2-34) D1 D2.
Service time and service demand
Simple words: how long one request keeps a resource busy. Analogy: ten minutes of the mixer per bucket. Precise: the service time S of a resource is the time it is busy with one request (excluding waiting); the service demand of a request at a resource is the total of its service times there. Latency = waiting time + service time.
Utilisation law
Simple words: how busy a resource is equals how many requests per second it finishes times how long each takes. Analogy: four buckets an hour × ten minutes = forty minutes of mixing per hour: busy two thirds of the time. Precise: U = X × S, where X is the completion rate and S the mean service time; it follows from the definitions (busy time / elapsed time = completions × mean service time / elapsed time) and needs no assumption about distributions D5. Compare utilisation of an RTOS task set (OS305).
Bottleneck (of a system)
Simple words: the resource that fills up first as load grows. Analogy: the one mixer, if the bricklayers could otherwise go faster. Precise: the resource with the largest service demand per request, Dmax; because its utilisation cannot exceed 1, the system's throughput is bounded by X ≤ 1 / Dmax (for a resource with c identical servers, X ≤ c / D) D5.
Queueing model (M/M/1, M/D/1)
Simple words: a mathematical picture of requests waiting in line for a server. Analogy: bricklayers arriving at the mixer at random moments and waiting their turn. Precise: in Kendall's notation, A/B/c names the arrival process, the service-time distribution and the number of servers; M means memoryless (Poisson arrivals or exponential service times), D means deterministic (constant); "M/M/1" is Poisson arrivals, exponential service, one server, served first come first served D5.
Saturation (of a resource)
Simple words: work waiting because a resource is already busy. Analogy: the line of bricklayers at the mixer. Precise: the degree to which a resource has more work than it can serve at once, observed as queue length, waiting time or blocked threads; it grows sharply as utilisation approaches 1. Gregg's USE method checks utilisation, saturation and errors for every resource (F12-14) D1. Not the same as sensor saturation (MA202) or actuator saturation (RB202).

Terms from earlier courses used here: Little's law (HW203, F1-32); Amdahl's law (SP203, F2-34); roofline model (SP302, F2-56); alpha–beta model (HW301, F1-62); lock contention (SP203); and from F12-11: offered load, open- and closed-loop generators.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): Yusuf's envelope = the performance model; "six days" written before building = the prediction; the mixer = a resource with one server; ten minutes per bucket = service time; buckets per hour = arrival rate; the share of the hour the mixer is busy = utilisation; bricklayers waiting at the mixer = saturation and queueing delay; counting on day one = the measurement.

When a helper is busy only now and then, you almost never wait for them. When they are busy almost all the time, you almost always wait, and the line can get long, because the helper never catches up after a crowd. So "the mixer is 90 % busy" sounds fine, but it means long waits.

That is why you guess before you measure. Your guess tells you what to count, and when the count is very different from your guess, you have learned something: either your picture of the work was wrong, or your counting was wrong. Both are worth knowing before you build more.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why model first

A measurement without a prediction is a number with no meaning attached: 46 ms is neither good nor bad until you know what you expected. A prediction written before the experiment does three jobs. It tells you which quantities to measure (if the model says the log writer is the bottleneck, measure the log writer's busy time). It tells you whether the measurement is plausible (a result ten times better than the model's bound is more likely a broken benchmark than a miracle). And it tells you, when the two disagree, that a mechanism exists that you have not understood yet. Gregg's book on systems performance describes modelling as one of the methodologies of performance analysis D1; curriculum 13.4 makes the same demand in its own words: set the target first, and end every performance milestone with a profiler-backed explanation of the remaining gap D2.

Operational laws: true by definition

Three relations hold for any system observed over a long enough interval, whatever the distributions, because they follow from the definitions of the quantities D5:

  • Utilisation law: U = X × S. A resource that completes X requests per second, each taking S seconds of its time, is busy X × S of the time.
  • Little's law: N = X × R. The average number of requests inside a system equals the throughput times the average time each spends inside (you met it for memory in F1-32) D3.
  • Bottleneck bound: since no utilisation can exceed 1, the throughput is at most 1 / Dmax, where Dmax is the largest service demand of a request at any single resource. A resource with c parallel servers gives c / D.

These laws are the first model to write, because they need only numbers you can obtain cheaply: the CPU time per request from a profile at low load, the number of disk writes per request, the time the lock is held per request. In the forensic lab, the team computed 4 workers / 4 ms = 1,000 req/s from the CPU alone, and missed a resource with a larger demand: one lock held for half of each request's work, so D = 2 ms at a single server, and X ≤ 500 req/s R3.

Queueing: why latency explodes near full load

Operational laws give limits and averages, not latency under load. For that you need assumptions about how requests arrive and how long service takes. The simplest useful model is M/M/1: arrivals at random (Poisson, so the gaps between them are exponential with mean 1/λ), service times exponential with mean S, one server, first come first served. With U = λS < 1, queueing theory gives D5:

  • mean response time R = S / (1 − U);
  • the response time is itself exponentially distributed with mean R, so its p-th percentile is R × ln(1 / (1 − p)); for p99 that is R × ln 100 ≈ 4.6 R.

For constant service times (M/D/1), the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula for the mean waiting time, W = λ E[S²] / (2 (1 − U)), gives W = U S / (2 (1 − U)), half the M/M/1 wait, so R = S + U S / (2 (1 − U)) D5. The variability of service times matters as much as their mean.

Listing 1 checks these formulas by simulation: one million requests per row, served by Lindley's recursion (a request starts at the later of its arrival and the previous request's finish). The means agree to within about 1.5 % up to U = 0.9, and the p99 to within 2 %; at U = 0.95 the simulated p99 is 5.6 % below the formula, because near saturation even a million requests is a short run for such a slowly settling queue R1. The M/D/1 means agree to within 2 % at every row.

The shape is the lesson: 1 / (1 − U) is 2 at half load, 5 at 80 %, 10 at 90 %, 20 at 95 %. Each halving of the idle fraction doubles the mean latency. A system run "efficiently" at 95 % busy has, by this model, ten times the latency it had at half load.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

A ladder of models

Choose the cheapest model that can answer the question, and climb only when it cannot:

  1. Bounds (operational laws, roofline, Amdahl): need only demands and peaks; answer "what is the best possible?" and "which resource limits it?". The roofline of F2-56 and F6-33 is a bound of this kind for kernels; the alpha–beta model of F1-62 and bridge BR-04 is one for messages D3.
  2. Analytic queueing models (M/M/1, M/D/1, and their multi-server and network forms): need distributional assumptions; answer "what latency at this load?".
  3. Simulation (Listing 1, the forensic lab's lockmodel.cpp): needs a program, not a formula; handles locks, priorities, bursts and any distribution you can sample; answers the same questions for systems no formula covers. The forensic lab's lock is an example: a worker that holds a thread while it waits for a lock is easy to simulate and awkward to solve.
  4. Measurement of the real system: the final judge of the model, and the source of its parameters.

Assumptions that break models

The formulas of Layer 2 assume, among other things: arrivals independent of each other; a stable system (U < 1 over the window); service time independent of load; first-come-first-served order; and one queue in front of one resource. Real systems break each of these:

  • Bursty arrivals (a retry storm, a batch job, a cron at the full hour) wait longer than Poisson arrivals at the same mean rate.
  • Overload windows: if U > 1 for ten minutes, the queue grows for ten minutes; no steady-state formula applies, and latency depends on how long the overload lasts (F12-15's forensic lab).
  • Load-dependent service time: caches shared by more threads, more context switches, lock hand-offs and memory bandwidth make S grow with load. SP302 measured such effects on one machine (F2-52) D3.
  • Hidden serial resources: a lock, a single log writer, one database connection. They form a queue of their own inside the system, often with a larger demand than the CPU (the forensic lab).
  • Overheads the model omits: the hand-off between threads, the wake-up of a sleeping worker. Listing 2 shows them as a gap between model and measurement even at low load.

The real measurement of Listing 2

Listing 2 builds the simplest real queue: a generator thread releases requests at exponentially distributed intended times, one worker thread serves each by spinning for exactly 200 µs, and latency is timed from the intended arrival. The model is M/D/1. In the recorded run, three things are visible R2:

  • At U = 0.02, 0.30 and 0.50 the measured mean is above the model by a similar amount each time (between about 120 and 145 µs), while the model itself changes by 100 µs over those rows: a constant-offset pattern, an overhead the model omits.
  • The measured p99 is several times the measured mean even at U = 0.02, where queueing is negligible: the real system has a tail the model does not have.
  • At U = 0.70 and 0.85 the mean and p99 jump far above both the model and the neighbouring rows (the p99 reaches tens of milliseconds, about a hundred at U = 0.70), while the p50 stays within a few hundred microseconds of the model. A few requests waited a very long time; most did not.
Not verified — check before relying on this

The causes of the gaps in Listing 2's recorded run are hypotheses, not findings. The constant offset is attributed to the hand-off between the generator and the worker (the worker sleeps on a condition variable and must be woken), but no scheduler trace was taken: perf does not work in the build container (SP302 NOTES). The very long waits at U = 0.70 and 0.85 coincide with other build jobs running on the same shared virtual machine; this is an inference about the environment, not a measurement of it. The short ThreadSanitizer run of the same program (R2) does not show jumps of that size, but it is short and its timings are not meaningful. To verify: repeat the run on an otherwise idle machine, and record scheduler events (for example with perf sched on a system where it works); check the tool's documentation for the exact commands.

How the hardware actually does it

Every "server" in a queueing model is a piece of hardware or something that serialises access to it. A CPU core executes one thread at a time; the operating system time-slices runnable threads on it, so threads beyond the number of cores queue for CPU time. A storage device completes a limited number of operations concurrently and each write that must be durable waits for the device to report completion (F12-14 measures fdatasync calls). A lock is a software server built on a memory location that only one core can own at a time. The service time in the model is the time the hardware (or the lock) is held by one request.

That is why S must be measured, not assumed, and measured under the conditions of the prediction: S at low load can be smaller than S at high load when the request's data stays in a core's cache only while the core is not shared (cache behaviour in F2-52) D3. And it is why the bottleneck may not be the CPU: on the build machine, the four-thread runs of SP302 scaled poorly for reasons that were never fully explained (SP302 NOTES), a reminder that the hardware of a shared virtual machine is itself partly unknown D3.

Worked example

Task. Predict, before any measurement, the p99 latency of the one-server service of F12-11 (Listing 2 there: exponential service time with mean S = 2 ms) at 250 and 400 req/s, and the highest load at which p99 ≤ 20 ms.

Step 1 — utilisation. U = λ S: at 250 req/s, U = 250 × 0.002 = 0.5; at 400 req/s, U = 0.8. Both below 1, so the queue is stable.

Step 2 — mean. R = S / (1 − U): 2 / 0.5 = 4 ms at 250 req/s; 2 / 0.2 = 10 ms at 400 req/s. (F12-11's R2 measured a mean of 10.00 ms at 400 req/s in simulation.)

Step 3 — p99. p99 = R × ln 100 = 4.605 R: 4.605 × 4 = 18.42 ms at 250 req/s; 4.605 × 10 = 46.05 ms at 400 req/s. F12-11's run printed 18.76 ms and 46.12 ms R4: the prediction is within 2 % of the simulation.

Step 4 — the load limit. Require 4.605 × S / (1 − U) ≤ 20 ms: 1 − U ≥ 4.605 × 2 / 20 = 0.4605, so U ≤ 0.5395, λ ≤ 0.5395 / 0.002 ≈ 270 req/s. The service can promise its p99 target up to about 270 req/s, a little above the 250 req/s of F12-11's requirement R3: that is the "little headroom" F12-11 found.

Step 5 — what would change it. If the service time were constant at 2 ms (M/D/1), the mean at 400 req/s would be 2 + 0.8 × 2 / (2 × 0.2) = 6 ms instead of 10. Reducing variability helps; Listing 1's last columns show the same factor at every load R1. If the service needs to carry 400 req/s with p99 ≤ 20 ms, the formula says S must fall to (1 − U) × 20 / 4.605, which, with U = 400 S, gives S ≈ 1.48 ms; or the load must be split over two servers (F12-15).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Bricklayers adapt; requests do not. A bricklayer who sees a long line at the mixer waits to fetch mortar later, or mixes by hand. Requests in an open-loop system arrive regardless of the queue, which is why the queue can grow so much faster than intuition from human queues suggests.
    • 

  • The mixer's ten minutes are nearly constant; software service times often are not. The analogy suggests M/D/1. Many software services have service times with long tails, closer to (or worse than) exponential, and Layer 2 shows that variability alone doubles the waiting time.
    • 

  • A house has a few resources you can see; a computer has many you cannot. On a building site you can see the one mixer. In software the serial resource may be a lock, a log writer or a connection pool, invisible until you look at thread states (the forensic lab).
    • 

  • Bricks per day is a rate over days; utilisation near 1 behaves differently in seconds. A site that is 95 % busy over a week can still catch up overnight. A server that is 95 % busy has no night: the formulas describe the steady state of a queue that never rests.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F12-11 stated performance as requirements and budgets; SP302 (F2-56) and CU303 (F6-33) used the roofline bound for single kernels; SP203 (F2-34) used Amdahl's law; HW203 (F1-32) used Little's law for memory. What carries over: bounds from peaks and demands, and the habit of comparing a measurement with a ceiling before believing it D3. What changes: most dangerous first: latency under load is not a property of the code alone but of the code and the load together, and it grows as 1 / (1 − U), so "efficient" high utilisation means long waits R1; the bottleneck may be a lock or a log writer rather than a processor; and a model is now written before the experiment and judged by it. What comes next: F12-13 makes the experiment trustworthy: warm-up, repetitions, intervals, interleaving, and load generators that do not hide the queue.

  • The bottleneck is the resource with the largest demand, which may be a lock or a log writer, not the CPU.
    • 

  • For a single-server queue, mean latency is S / (1 − U) (M/M/1); p99 is about 4.6 times the mean; constant service times halve the waiting time.
    • 

  • Latency rises steeply near full utilisation: 1 / (1 − U) doubles from 0.8 to 0.9 and again to 0.95.
    • 

  • Climb the ladder of models only as far as the question needs: bounds, analytic queues, simulation, measurement.
    • 

  • Classify the gap between model and measurement: constant offset, growing with load, better than the bound, or random; each points to a different next step.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. lockmodel.cpp simulates four workers taking requests in arrival order; each request needs exponential CPU work with mean 4 ms, the first half of it while holding one shared lock (first come first served); a worker waiting for the lock stays occupied. Arrivals are Poisson; 120 simulated seconds per row R3.


Observation. At 400 req/s: running 39 %, blocked on the mutex 28 %, idle 33 %. The workers are occupied 67 % of the time, not 39 %. At 480 req/s they are occupied 92 %, with 44 % blocked. p99 rises from 18.80 ms at 100 req/s (close to the service-time tail alone) to 46.91 ms at 400 and 297.56 ms at 480.


Hypotheses. (1) A serial resource (the lock) saturates before the CPU. (2) The worker pool saturates because blocked workers hold threads. (Both are true and linked; a third, "the CPU is slower under load", is ruled out by the CPU column growing exactly in proportion to the rate.)


Lock arithmetic. Lock demand per request = 0.5 × 4 ms = 2 ms. Ulock = 400 × 0.002 = 0.8 at 400 req/s and 0.96 at 480 req/s. Bound: X ≤ 1 / 0.002 = 500 req/s. By Figure 1, a single server at U = 0.8 has five times its service time as mean response, and at 0.96 twenty-five times: the lock queue explains the explosion.


Corrected model. Resources: CPU with c = 4, D = 4 ms (bound 1,000 req/s, U = 0.4 at 400 req/s); lock with c = 1, D = 2 ms (bound 500 req/s, U = 0.8). The lock is the bottleneck; real capacity is below 500 req/s, and for any reasonable p99 target the usable load is well below that.


Design changes. (a) Shorten the critical section, for example prepare the update outside the lock and hold it only to swap a pointer: if the locked part falls to 10 % of the work, Dlock = 0.4 ms and its bound is 2,500 req/s, so the CPU (1,000 req/s) becomes the bottleneck. (b) Shard the index into 8 independently locked parts: if requests spread evenly, each lock carries 1/8 of the load, its bound per lock is 500 req/s so the system bound from locks is 4,000 req/s, again leaving the CPU as the bottleneck. Wrong answer to rule out: "add more worker threads" — the lock still serves one request at a time; more workers only make more threads wait.


Review question that would have caught it. "List every resource a request uses, including locks, logs, connections and devices, with the demand of each; show the bottleneck bound for each." The review model listed only CPU time.
