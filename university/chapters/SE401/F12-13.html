Story hook

Ana has a new way of laying bricks: she spreads mortar for three bricks at once. Kwame times her on Thursday: a metre of wall in 40 minutes. He timed the old way on Monday: 55 minutes. "Thirty per cent faster!" says Kwame. Ingrid looks out of the window and asks what the weather was on Monday. It rained. Wet bricks, slippery boards, cold hands.

So they run a fair contest. Same wall, same bricks, the two methods alternating morning and afternoon for two weeks, so that rain, tiredness and Monday mornings fall on both. Before they start, they write down how they will decide: the middle time of ten tries each, and "faster" only if the difference is bigger than the spread between tries. After two weeks Ana's method is faster, but by a little, not by thirty per cent.

A week later, Yusuf complains that deliveries are slow. The depot's report says every delivery arrived within an hour of the truck leaving. But during the two days the depot was closed, no trucks left at all, and the orders sat waiting. The report timed the trucks, not the orders. This chapter is about both mistakes: unfair contests and clocks that start in the wrong place.

What you will be able to do

  • Design a benchmark from a question and a prediction: workload, environment record, warm-up, repetitions, statistic and decision rule, all written before running.
  • Compare two variants with interleaved runs and a bootstrap confidence interval for the ratio of medians, and state "faster", "slower" or "no difference shown" from the interval.
  • Explain coordinated omission, recognise it in a load generator's design, and measure latency from the intended send time instead.
  • Diagnose a benchmark result caused by drift or interference rather than by the change under test, from timestamps and machine logs.
  • Report a benchmark so that someone else can rerun it: raw data, protocol, environment and interval, not a single number.
Jargon box
Benchmark protocol
Simple words: the written rules of a speed test, decided before you run it. Analogy: "same wall, alternate mornings and afternoons, ten tries each, the middle time decides". Precise: the specification of a benchmark run: question and prediction, workload, environment and its record, warm-up, number and order of repetitions, statistic, interval method and decision rule. Curriculum 13.4's protocol (warm-up runs, then the median of at least 20 timed runs, settings recorded, reference measured by the same harness) is the minimum used in this university D2.
Steady state (of a benchmark)
Simple words: the point after which the system's speed has stopped changing because it has "settled in". Analogy: after the first wheelbarrow, when the boards are laid and the mortar is mixed. Precise: the regime in which the measured statistic no longer drifts with time: caches, memory pages, just-in-time compilation, connection pools, queue lengths and device clocks have reached their running condition. Warm-up runs (F2-51) aim to reach it; for a service under load, the first minutes of a run are usually discarded for the same reason.
Interleaving (ABAB order)
Simple words: taking turns between the two things you compare, instead of doing all of one and then all of the other. Analogy: alternating the two bricklaying methods morning and afternoon, so the rain falls on both. Precise: an ordering of benchmark runs that alternates variants (here A B, then B A, and so on) so that slow changes of the machine (drift, background jobs, thermal state) affect both variants nearly equally, instead of being confused with the difference between them. It turns a time trend from a common cause (confounder) into noise.
Bootstrap confidence interval
Simple words: a range of plausible values for a result, found by re-drawing your own measurements many times. Analogy: shuffling the ten timing cards of each method, drawing ten with replacement, and seeing how much the middle time moves. Precise: a percentile-bootstrap interval resamples each variant's runs with replacement, recomputes the statistic (here the ratio of medians) for each resample, and reports the 2.5 % and 97.5 % points of those values as an approximate 95 % interval; it needs no assumption that times are normally distributed (Listing 2) D4 R2.
Coordinated omission
Simple words: a load tester that waits for slow answers stops sending, so it never measures how long the requests it did not send would have waited. Analogy: timing deliveries from when the truck left the depot; while the depot is closed no truck leaves, so no delivery looks late. Precise: a measurement error of closed-loop load generators: when the system stalls, the generator delays its next requests (it is "coordinated" with the system) and times them from their late actual send, so the stall appears as one slow sample instead of as every request that should have been sent during it. Fixed by open-loop generation, or by timing from the intended send time (Listing 1) R1.
Benchmark record (provenance)
Simple words: everything someone needs to rerun your test and check your number. Analogy: the contest sheet with dates, weather, bricks used and every try's time. Precise: the raw per-run data, the protocol, the code version, the build command, the toolchain version line, the machine (processor, core count, virtualisation, other load), relevant settings (clock frequency policy where controllable, affinity), date and the statistic with its interval; the run logs of this university record the same fields (AH-23) D3.

Terms from earlier courses used here: benchmark, microbenchmark, warm-up run, run-to-run variation, median and percentile (SP302, F2-51; MA202, F0-63); robust statistic and common cause (confounder) (MA202); open- and closed-loop generators (F12-11); performance model (F12-12).

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows why the order of runs matters. On the left, all runs of A come first and all runs of B after them; a background job that slows the machine during the first half makes A look slow. On the right, the runs alternate; the same job slows A and B alike, and the difference between them survives.

Figure 2 shows coordinated omission. The server stalls for two seconds. The open-loop generator keeps sending every 10 ms and records 200 requests that each waited part of the stall. The closed-loop generator sends nothing during the stall, then sends the overdue requests back to back and times each from its late send: one slow sample.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): the contest between bricklaying methods = an A/B benchmark; the same wall and bricks = the same workload; the weather = the machine's changing condition (drift, other jobs); alternating mornings and afternoons = interleaving; the decision rule written before the contest = the protocol; the spread between tries = run-to-run variation; the delivery report that timed trucks from leaving the depot = coordinated omission.

A fair race needs the same track, the same weather and the rules written before the start. If one runner runs in the rain and the other in the sun, the race measures the weather. Taking turns, many times, makes the weather fall on both.

A fair clock starts when the wish starts, not when the work starts. If you order a pizza at six and the shop opens the order at eight, your waiting began at six. A tester that starts its clock only when it manages to send a request misses all the waiting before that.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A benchmark in nine steps

Benchmarking methodology is treated at length in Gregg's book and in Jain's book on performance analysis D1 D5. This course uses a nine-step checklist built from curriculum 13.4 and the earlier chapters; each step names the failure it prevents:

  1. Question and prediction. "Is B faster than A for our lookup mix, and by how much? The model (F12-12) predicts about 4×." Without a prediction, a broken benchmark looks like a discovery.
  2. Workload. The request mix, data sizes, key distribution, concurrency and duration of the real use. A benchmark of the wrong workload is a precise answer to the wrong question.
  3. Correctness check. Both variants produce the same answer, and the work cannot be optimised away (F2-51's optimisation barrier). Listing 3 prints "same answer from both variants: yes" R3.
  4. Environment control and record. Same machine, same build flags, nothing else heavy running if you can arrange it; record what you cannot control (benchmark record).
  5. Warm-up and steady state. Untimed runs first; for services, discard the start of the run; check that the timed runs show no trend.
  6. Repetitions. At least 20 timed runs (curriculum 13.4) D2; more when the effect is small compared with the spread.
  7. Order. Interleave variants (A B, B A, ...), never all A then all B (Figure 1, forensic lab).
  8. Statistic and interval. The median (robust to rare slow runs) and a bootstrap interval for the ratio of medians (Listing 2); for latency distributions, the percentiles of the requirement (F12-11).
  9. Decision rule and report. Written before running: "B is faster if the whole 95 % interval of B/A is below 1". Report the raw runs, the protocol, the record and the interval.

Medians, intervals and verdicts

Listing 2 applies steps 8 and 9 to constructed exercise data (21 runs per variant, typed into bootstrap.in and marked as not measured). The baseline contains two slow outliers (12.40 and 11.70 ms) among values near 10 ms; its mean is 10.13 ms but its median 9.96 ms, so the median is barely moved by them R2. For patch B the ratio of medians is 0.959 with a 95 % bootstrap interval of [0.939, 0.977]: the whole interval is below 1, so "B is faster, by roughly 2 to 6 %". For patch C the ratio is 0.986 but the interval [0.968, 1.011] contains 1: "no difference shown". C might be a little faster; these 21 runs cannot tell R2.

"No difference shown" is not "no difference". It means the experiment was too small or too noisy to see one of that size. To decide whether a 1.4 % improvement is real, you need more runs or less noise, and you should decide that before running, from the effect size you care about.

Load generators and coordinated omission

For services, the benchmark is a load generator plus a latency recorder, and its design decides what it can see. Listing 1 simulates a server that answers in 1 ms except during a 2-second stall, measured three ways over 60 s at an intended 100 req/s R1:

  • A — closed loop, timed from the actual send: p99 1.00 ms, p99.9 1.00 ms, max 2001 ms, one request over 100 ms.
  • B — the same closed-loop run, timed from the intended send: p99 1461 ms, p99.9 1956 ms, 212 requests over 100 ms.
  • C — open loop, timed from the intended send: identical to B in this simulation.

A's report says the tail is perfect except one odd sample. In reality 212 of 6,000 requests (3.5 %) would have waited over 100 ms. The fix: every request has an intended send time from a schedule that does not depend on the system, and latency is measured from it. In this simulation B equals C because the closed-loop generator sent the overdue requests back to back and the server served them in order; with more realistic clients (several connections, timeouts, retries) the two differ, and open loop (C) is the faithful one for user-facing requirements.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The percentile bootstrap, and its limits

The bootstrap treats the observed runs as a stand-in for the unknown distribution of run times: drawing n runs with replacement from your n runs imitates drawing a new experiment from the real distribution. Repeating that 10,000 times gives 10,000 plausible values of the statistic; their middle 95 % is the interval (Efron and Tibshirani describe the method and its variants) D4. Three limits matter in practice:

  • Runs must be exchangeable. If run times drift during the experiment, resampling mixes early and late runs as if they were alike; the interval is then too narrow around a biased value. The forensic lab's first benchmark has a tight interval [0.707, 0.733] around a ratio that is wrong by about 26 percentage points R4. Statistics cannot repair a confounded design; interleaving can.
  • Small samples give rough intervals. With 21 runs the median moves in steps of single observations; the interval's edges are themselves noisy. Report the interval with its sample size.
  • Fixed seeds make intervals reproducible, not exact. Listing 2 uses a fixed seed so the same data always gives the same interval; another seed gives a slightly different one. The resampling's own noise shrinks with more resamples.

The last row is often forgotten: a load generator is part of the experiment. If it cannot send fast enough or its own thread is delayed, the system looks better than it is. F12-14's service records the generator's CPU time for that reason.

Watch the benchmark while it runs

A benchmark result is a claim that some resource limits performance. Check that claim during the run: which resource is busy, which threads are waiting, whether the generator keeps its schedule. Gregg's book on systems performance recommends analysing a benchmark while it runs rather than only collecting its final number D1; this course's version is concrete: for every benchmark in your report, name the limiting resource and show the evidence (profile, utilisation, thread states) from the same run. If you cannot name it, you do not yet know what you measured. F12-14 gives the tools.

Benchmarks in CI

Regression gates in continuous integration (F12-11) are benchmarks run by a machine for a machine, which makes every rule above stricter: the CI machine is often shared, so interleave the candidate with the baseline build in the same job; use the interval rule, not a fixed percentage; fail only when the whole interval is beyond the allowed regression; and keep the raw runs as an artefact, so a human can inspect a failure. A gate that compares a candidate with yesterday's stored number measures the CI machine's mood as much as the change.

How the hardware actually does it

Run-to-run variation comes from the hardware and the software that shares it. A modern processor changes its clock frequency with load and temperature, so the same instructions can take different times minute to minute; curriculum 13.4 asks for fixed clocks where the driver allows it, and a record of the setting D2. Caches and translation buffers hold the working set only after it has been touched, which is why first runs are slow (F2-51, F2-52) D3. On a virtual machine, the physical cores, caches and memory bandwidth are shared with other tenants whose load you cannot see: SP302's runs on the build machine could not even read hardware performance counters D3.

Listing 3 shows the consequence on the build machine. The std::map lookups follow pointers through a tree far larger than the caches and wait on memory for most nodes, while binary search in a sorted vector touches fewer, more predictable locations (cache behaviour of pointer chasing is measured in F2-52) D3. In the recorded run the map's rounds ranged from about 520 to about 826 ms, while the vector's stayed between about 140 and 166 ms: the memory-bound variant is also the noisier one, plausibly because it depends on a resource the other tenants share R3.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Why the std::map variant is the noisier one on the build machine is a hypothesis (memory bandwidth and caches shared with other tenants). The build had no hardware performance counters (perf unavailable), so cache misses and memory stalls were not measured. On a machine with working counters, compare the two variants' cache-miss counts and their spread across a quiet and a busy period.

Worked example

Task. Decide from bootstrap.in whether patch B and patch C are faster than the baseline, using the decision rule of Layer 2, and check one number by hand.

Step 1 — the medians by hand. Sort the 21 baseline runs: 9.43, 9.44, 9.69, 9.71, 9.71, 9.82, 9.87, 9.90, 9.91, 9.93, 9.96, 10.01, 10.04, 10.05, 10.10, 10.17, 10.17, 10.28, 10.34, 11.70, 12.40. The 11th value is 9.96 ms, matching Listing 2's output R2. The two outliers sit at the end and do not affect it.

Step 2 — the mean. The sum of the 21 values is 212.63 ms, so the mean is 10.13 ms: 0.17 ms above the median, a shift caused almost entirely by the two outliers. A comparison of means would mix "B is faster" with "the baseline happened to have two slow runs".

Step 3 — the ratios. B: 9.55 / 9.96 = 0.959. C: 9.82 / 9.96 = 0.986. These are the point estimates; on their own they say nothing about whether the difference exceeds the noise.

Step 4 — the intervals and verdicts. From Listing 2: B [0.939, 0.977], below 1: faster, by between about 2 % and 6 %. C [0.968, 1.011], contains 1: no difference shown.

Step 5 — what to do about C. If a 1–2 % improvement matters, plan a larger experiment: the interval's width shrinks roughly as one over the square root of the number of runs for well-behaved data, so halving the width needs about four times as many runs (84 instead of 21). Write that plan, with the decision rule, before running it.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Bricklayers get tired and learn; programs do not. In the contest, alternation also protects against learning effects in the people. A program does not improve with practice, but the machine's state (caches, page cache, clock frequency) does change with use, so alternation protects against that instead.
    • 

  • Weather is visible; interference often is not. Ingrid could look out of the window. On a shared virtual machine you usually cannot see the other tenants; you infer them from the data, which is why interleaving must be the default rather than a reaction.
    • 

  • Two weeks of contest is a few dozen tries; computers can run thousands. The analogy suggests small samples are all you can have. In software, cheap repetitions are usually available, so a "no difference shown" verdict is often a reason to run more, not to stop.
    • 

  • Delivery trucks are independent; generator and server share a machine. The depot does not slow down when the truck counter is busy. A load generator on the same machine as the server competes with it for CPU, and can itself become the bottleneck.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: SP302 (F2-51) measured one function: steady clock, warm-up, median of 21 runs; MA202 (F0-63) taught medians and robust statistics; F12-12 taught you to predict before measuring. Summary


    

  • Write the question, prediction, workload, statistic and decision rule before running.
    • 

  • Check correctness in the benchmark itself; a fast wrong answer is a failure.
    • 

  • Record the environment; control what you can (warm-up, quiet machine, fixed clocks where allowed) and record the rest.
    • 

  • Use at least 20 timed runs, the median, and a bootstrap interval for the ratio of medians; "faster" only if the whole interval says so.
    • 

  • Interleave variants: sequential runs confuse drift with difference, and no interval repairs that.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Question and prediction; workload; correctness check; environment control and record; warm-up and steady state; repetitions; order (interleaving); statistic and interval; decision rule and report. Examples: the correctness check prevents measuring optimised-away work; interleaving prevents drift being read as a difference; a decision rule written first prevents choosing the conclusion after seeing the data.
    2. 

  2. The bootstrap resamples the runs as if each were an independent draw from one distribution per variant. In the night run all A runs were slowed by the backup job and all B runs were not; each group is internally consistent (small spread), so resampling finds little variation and a narrow interval. But the interval is around the ratio of "A while slowed" to "B while not slowed", not of A to B. The bias is in the design, which resampling cannot see.
    3. 

  3. Closed loop timed from actual send: each connection has one request in flight when the freeze starts, so about 4 slow samples per freeze (one per connection). Open loop at 400 req/s: about 400 requests arrive during the 1-second freeze, all delayed (by between 1 s and nearly 0 s), so about 400 slow samples.
    4. 

  4. (c). The interval contains 1, so neither "faster" nor "slower" is supported; it is wide enough to include a 5 % gain and a 2 % loss. (a) reads the point estimate as a result; (b) reads "not shown" as "shown not"; (d) has no support.
    5. 

  5. Expected: the half of the runs taken while the CPU-heavy program runs will be slower; if those are mostly B runs, the ratio rises (B looks worse), and the interval stays narrow around a biased value. The interleaved version spreads the slowdown over both variants: a wider interval around a ratio close to the quiet one.
    6. 

  6. Also the harness itself: time with device events as curriculum 13.4 requires.
    7. 

  7. Because a benchmark that compares different answers is invalid, and a warning is often missed in a log or a CI job; a non-zero exit code makes the run fail where people and machines will see it.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. drift.cpp simulates a routine whose true time is 10.0 ms for A and 9.8 ms for B (B 2 % faster), with ±3 % run-to-run noise. In the night run all 20 A runs come first (01:58:00 to 02:02:45), then all 20 B runs (02:03:00 to 02:07:45); a background job slows the machine by a factor of 1.38 until 02:02:50. The next-day run interleaves 21 rounds with no background job R4.


Observation. A's runs all lie between 13.41 and 14.08 ms, B's between 9.53 and 10.09 ms: two flat levels with similar small spreads, and a step exactly where the variant changes. Evidence 2: A median 9.99 ms, B 9.89 ms, ratio 0.989, interval [0.976, 1.008].


Hypotheses. (1) B is faster and the CI machine is different. (2) Something on the night machine slowed the A runs. (3) Something made the B runs faster. Evidence 3 decides: the nightly backup ran from 01:30:00 to 02:02:50; the last A run started at 02:02:45, the first B run at 02:03:00. Every A run happened during the backup and no B run did.


Why the interval was tight. See answer 2 above: each group was consistent, so the bootstrap saw little spread; the confounder (the backup) lined up perfectly with the variant, which no resampling can detect.


Conclusion from evidence 2. No difference shown: the interval contains 1. B might be up to about 2 % faster (the simulation's truth is 2 %), but this experiment cannot claim it. The 28 % claim is withdrawn; the merge itself may stand on other grounds, but its description must be corrected.


Prevention. Interleave variants in every benchmark (the harness enforces it); run benchmarks only on machines whose job schedule is known, or record the machine's other jobs and load next to every run; require a rerun by CI for any claimed improvement above a threshold. Wrong answer to rule out: "use more runs" — 200 sequential runs during the backup would give an even tighter interval around the same wrong ratio.
