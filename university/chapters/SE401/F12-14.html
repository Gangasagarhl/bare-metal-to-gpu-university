Story hook

Most days, a wheelbarrow of bricks takes three minutes from the lorry to the wall. A few times a day it takes twenty-five. Kwame watches the bricklayers: they are fast and never idle while they have bricks. He watches the lorry: it unloads on time. Ana suggests buying faster wheelbarrows.

Ingrid does something different. She gives every wheelbarrow a card and asks each person who touches it to write the time: left the lorry, reached the gate, passed the gate, reached the wall. At the end of the day she sorts the cards by total time and reads the slowest ones. Every slow card has the same gap: thirty minutes between "reached the gate" and "passed the gate". The site has one gate, and twice a day the inspector closes it while he counts everything inside the fence. Nobody was slow. Everybody was waiting.

Faster wheelbarrows would have changed nothing. This chapter teaches Ingrid's method for software: follow requests through the whole system, find where the slow ones waited, and connect that wait to whatever else was happening at the same moment.

What you will be able to do

  • Instrument a service so that every request carries a trace of timed spans, every thread reports its CPU time and context switches, and background tasks log what they did and when.
  • Separate on-CPU time from off-CPU time, and choose the tool that sees each: a sampling profiler for the first, traces, lock and I/O timing and system-call summaries for the second.
  • Apply the USE method (utilisation, saturation, errors) to every resource of a system, including locks, worker pools and the disk, not only the CPU.
  • Attribute a latency tail to its cause: compare the slowest requests with the rest, and correlate them in time with background events, distinguishing cause from coincidence.
  • Plan the next measurement when the evidence does not decide, and recognise the next bottleneck after a fix.
Jargon box
Whole-system profile
Simple words: a record of where every part of the system spent its time, collected together during one run. Analogy: Ingrid's cards on every wheelbarrow, plus the gatekeeper's diary and each worker's timesheet, for the same day. Precise: a set of time-aligned measurements of one run of a system under a defined load: per-request traces (where requests spent their time), per-thread or per-process resource use (CPU time, context switches), per-resource utilisation and saturation (locks, queues, disks, network) and an event log of background activity. It answers "where did the time go?" for the user-visible metric, not only "which function uses the CPU?" D2
On-CPU and off-CPU time
Simple words: time a thread spends running instructions, and time it spends waiting. Analogy: a bricklayer laying bricks, and the same bricklayer standing at the gate. Precise: a thread's wall-clock time divides into time scheduled on a processor (on-CPU) and time blocked or runnable but not running (off-CPU: waiting for a lock, I/O, a condition variable, a timer, or for a processor). A sampling profiler of CPU use sees only on-CPU time; off-CPU time is seen through tracing of blocking events, per-thread CPU time against wall time, context-switch counts and system-call timing D1 R1.
USE method
Simple words: for every resource, ask how busy it is, how much work waits for it, and whether it fails. Analogy: for the gate: how often is it closed or in use, how long is the queue in front of it, does anything get turned away? Precise: a checklist method attributed to Brendan Gregg: for each resource (processors, memory, disks, network, and software resources such as locks, queues and thread pools) check utilisation (fraction of time busy, or fraction of capacity used), saturation (extra work waiting, such as a queue length or wait time) and errors. It finds bottlenecks that function profiles miss because the waiting resource is not a CPU D1.
Critical path (of a request)
Simple words: the chain of steps that decides when a request finishes. Analogy: the wheelbarrow's route: lorry, gate, wall; making the lorry faster does not help if the gate is shut. Precise: the sequence of spans, including waits, on which the request's completion depends; time removed from a span off the critical path does not shorten the request. In a service, waits for queues, locks and durable writes are often on the critical path even when no thread uses the CPU.
Lock hold time
Simple words: how long one thread keeps a lock before letting go. Analogy: how long the inspector keeps the gate closed. Precise: the time between acquiring and releasing a lock in one critical section; every thread that needs the lock during that time waits at least until the release. Long holds by rare operations (snapshots, compaction, resizing) produce tail latency for frequent operations even when the lock's average utilisation is low.
Tail attribution
Simple words: finding out which cause made the slowest requests slow. Analogy: sorting the wheelbarrow cards by total time and checking what the slowest have in common. Precise: the analysis that compares the slowest requests (for example the slowest 1 %) with the rest: which spans grow, which events overlap them in time, and how much more often than for an ordinary request. A cause is supported when the slow requests share it far more often than the others and a change that removes it removes the tail; a single coincidence in time is not enough.

Terms from earlier courses used here: trace and span, metric, percentile, tail latency (DS402); profiler, sampling profiler, instrumentation, wall-clock time (SP202); hot spot, latency histogram (SP302); system call, blocking (system call), context switch, strace, starvation (OS201); mutex, lock contention (SP203); fsync, journaling (write-ahead logging) (OS401); utilisation, head-of-line blocking; utilisation law and saturation of a resource (F12-12).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): wheelbarrow = request; times written on its card = the request's trace; the gate = the store lock; the inspector closing the gate to count = the snapshot task holding the lock; bricklayers standing at the closed gate = workers blocked off-CPU; wheelbarrows piling up at the lorry because nobody is free to push them = requests waiting in the queue; each worker's timesheet = per-thread CPU time and context switches.

When something is slow, people look for the slow worker. But often nobody is slow: somebody is waiting. To find the wait, you follow one job from start to end and write down the time at every step. Then you look at the slowest jobs and ask what they had in common: the same step, the same moment, the same thing happening nearby.

And you check every shared thing, not only the busy people: the gate, the queue, the one mixer. A shared thing that is closed even for a short time makes everyone behind it wait.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why function profiles are not enough

SP302 (F2-55) used profilers that answer "which functions use the CPU?" That is the right question when a program is CPU-bound. A service's tail latency is usually something else: requests that waited. In the recorded run of Listing 1 the three worker threads together used about 212 ms of CPU in about 6,040 ms of wall time each, about 1.2 % of their lives; the whole process used about 1.19 CPU-seconds in 6 seconds on a 4-CPU machine R1. A sampling profile of that process would show the generator's spin loop, the snapshot's formatting and the parse loops — and nothing about the 22-millisecond waits that make the 99th percentile.

The method: seven steps

  1. Start from the requirement. The metric that matters is the one in the requirement (F12-11): here, latency at the service entry, from the intended arrival to the reply, as percentiles. Measure it from the intended send so the generator cannot hide stalls (F12-13).
  2. Trace every request. Record a timestamp at each boundary: entering the queue, leaving it, before and after each lock, before and after each wait for I/O. The differences are spans. Cheap timestamps from a steady clock cost far less than the waits you are looking for D3.
  3. Read the slowest traces first. Sort by total time and read the top few by hand. They show the shape of the problem: which span is large.
  4. Compare the slowest 1 % with the rest. One slow trace is an anecdote. Sum each span over the slowest 1 % and over the fastest 99 %, and compare the shares. The span whose share grows is where the tail lives.
  5. USE every resource. For the CPU, the store lock, the worker pool, the WAL disk and the request queue: utilisation, saturation, errors (Layer 3 has the table for Listing 1).
  6. Correlate with events. Lay the slow requests' times next to the log of background tasks, deployments and other machines' jobs. Count how often the slow requests overlap an event, and how often all requests do: the comparison, not the overlap alone, is evidence.
  7. Test the hypothesis and look for the next bottleneck. Change the suspected cause, rerun with the protocol of F12-13, and profile again: the tail that remains has a new cause.

Where the time shows up is not always where it is caused

Figure 2 shows a trap. Three requests waited on the lock: their traces say "lock wait". The next three waited in the queue: their traces say "queue". All six waited for the same reason — the snapshot held the lock — but the second three never touched the lock. They were stuck behind workers that were. With a fixed-size worker pool, a wait inside the workers becomes queueing in front of them; this is head-of-line blocking on the pool. In the recorded run, the slowest 1 % of requests spent 68.6 % of their time in the queue span and 22.6 % in lock wait R2. Someone reading only the span shares would blame the queue, and might add workers. More workers would also block on the lock; the waits would move, not vanish.

Watch out

A large "queue" span means "no worker was free", not "the queue is slow". Ask what the workers were doing at that moment. The traces of requests that were in the workers at that time answer it.

Correlation, base rates and the second cause

The analysis part of Listing 1 (--key) counts requests whose lifetime overlaps a snapshot lock hold. Of the slowest 120 requests (1 %), 112 overlap one; of all 12,068 requests, 412 do (3.4 %) R2. If snapshot holds had nothing to do with slowness, about 3.4 % of the slowest requests would overlap one by chance, about 4 of 120. Finding 112 is the evidence. Without the base rate, "112 slow requests overlapped a snapshot" could be an artefact of snapshots happening all the time.

Not all of the tail has this cause. The two slowest requests of the run are puts that waited 27.1 and 24.3 ms for the WAL; the background log shows a WAL batch at 3,004.2 ms whose write and fdatasync took 23.0 ms R1. In the slowest 1 %, WAL wait is 8.0 % of the time R2. A real tail usually has several causes with different shares; attribute each, and fix the largest first.

Remember the eight requests that did not overlap a snapshot hold. They are where the next cause hides.

The chunked row is the lesson. Its holds are short, yet its tail is worse. Two things happened in that run. First, its slowest requests (arriving 2,163.9–2,192.7 ms) coincide with three slow WAL flushes logged at 2,129.5, 2,157.5 and 2,187.5 ms (27.3, 29.9 and 25.8 ms) R3: a disk episode, not the snapshot, dominates that run's tail. Second, releasing a lock and taking it again at once does not guarantee that a waiting thread gets in between: the standard mutex promises no fairness, so the snapshot thread may win the lock again and again while workers wait. Whether that happened here was not measured.

The paced mode leaves a gap after each chunk, so waiting workers can take the lock. Its p99 is about half of whole's, and only 17 of the slowest 120 requests overlap a hold, against a base rate of 704 of 12,068 (5.8 %): the snapshot is now a small contributor R3.

Watch out

These are three single runs on a shared virtual machine, and one of them was hit by a disk episode. By F12-13's rules they are not a benchmark: they show what to look at, not by how much a mode is better. A real comparison interleaves several runs of each mode and reports intervals for p99.

(2) That the chunked run's worse tail involved the snapshot thread re-taking the lock before waiting workers: the run did not record lock hand-offs. To check, record for each hold which thread acquired the lock next, or count consecutive snapshot holds without a worker acquisition in between.

What the system calls say

The strace run of a 2-second paced run counted 13,772 futex calls (982 returning errors), 3,251 clock_nanosleep, 355 write and 349 fdatasync calls R4. Futex calls are how waits on mutexes and condition variables reach the kernel on Linux (OS201); their count shows how often threads blocked and woke each other. About 175 fdatasync calls per second against about 200 puts per second (10 % of 2,000 req/s) means one flush served one or two puts on average: group commit was doing little batching at this load, and each put paid nearly a full flush. The time column of a -c summary is harder to read: strace slows every system call it traces, and whether the column is system CPU time or wall time spent in the calls depends on options D4.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The meaning of the time columns of strace -c (system CPU time or wall-clock time, and the effect of the -w option), the meaning of the 982 futex "errors" (expected to be return values such as "try again" or a timeout, not failures), and the exact semantics of fdatasync, getrusage(RUSAGE_THREAD) and CLOCK_THREAD_CPUTIME_ID are written from general knowledge. Check them in the strace manual page and the Linux manual pages for these calls (titles only — not opened during this build; dossier gate G1 open).

On-CPU sampling and off-CPU analysis with real tools

On a machine where hardware and software events can be sampled, a sampling profiler of the whole system (all processes, kernel and user stacks) gives the on-CPU picture, and off-CPU analysis records the stacks of threads when they block and for how long. Both are described in Gregg's book D1. The rule from this chapter stands: for latency tails, start from request traces and resource saturation, and use stack profiles to explain what a resource holder was doing.

How the hardware actually does it

A thread that finds a mutex taken does not stay on the processor for long. After a short attempt in user space, it asks the kernel to put it to sleep on that lock's address (the futex system call on Linux), and the scheduler runs something else; that is a voluntary context switch. When the holder releases the lock, it wakes one or more sleepers through the kernel, and they become runnable; they run again when a processor is free D3. Each worker in the recorded run made over 4,000 voluntary context switches in 6 seconds, about one per request it served, because every wait for the queue, the lock or the WAL put it to sleep R1. Off-CPU time is time spent in exactly these sleeps.

A durable write is a different wait. write copies the data into the kernel's page cache and returns; fdatasync waits until the file's data have been sent to the storage device and the device reports them stored, including flushing the device's own volatile cache where it has one (OS401) D3. On a cloud virtual machine the "device" is a virtual disk whose latency depends on a storage system shared with other tenants: in the recorded runs most batches took less than 5 ms, but single batches took 23.0 ms and, in another run, 25.8–29.9 ms R1 R3. Every put waits for its batch, so the disk's tail becomes the puts' tail.

The snapshot's 23 ms hold is mostly formatting 100,000 entries with snprintf while holding the lock: CPU work done by one thread at a time when nobody else may touch the store. The length of a hold therefore depends on the CPU speed and on whether that thread is preempted while holding the lock — on a shared machine it can be, which may be one reason hold lengths in the background log vary from 7.18 to 23.07 ms R1.

Worked example

Task. After switching to paced snapshots, the p99 is 4.656 ms. Find where the remaining tail comes from, using only the recorded paced report and its analysis R3.

Step 1 — the table. Gets: p99 3.358 ms, max 10.159. Puts: p99 8.452 ms, max 10.450. The tail of all requests is now set by the puts more than the gets: 1,249 puts are about 10 % of requests, so the slowest 1 % (120 requests) can be mostly puts.

Step 2 — span shares. Slowest 1 %: queue 45.2 %, WAL wait 54.5 %, lock wait 0.1 %. Fastest 99 %: queue 44.4 %, WAL wait 52.5 %. Lock wait has nearly vanished from the tail; the shares of the slowest and the fastest are almost the same, so the tail is not one span growing, but everything being longer.

Step 3 — the slowest traces. Four of the eight slowest are puts whose WAL wait is 6.7–8.1 ms. Four are gets that arrived at 5,356.9–5,357.3 ms and spent 9.8–10.2 ms in the queue with no lock wait.

Step 4 — the background log. Around these times the log shows WAL batches that took 5.9 ms (5,317.1 ms) and 5.7 ms (5,409.3 ms), and three others between 5,209 and 5,226 ms. Slow flushes cluster in this second of the run. No snapshot hold is longer than 4.55 ms.

Step 5 — a hypothesis and what is missing. Puts: the disk is the next bottleneck, supported by the WAL-wait span and the log. Gets at 5,357 ms: they waited for a free worker, but the report does not say what the three workers were doing. The most likely candidates are puts waiting for a slow flush (a worker waiting for durability is not available), or the workers being preempted on the shared machine. The evidence does not decide.

Step 6 — the next measurement. Add a per-worker state timeline (what each worker was doing at every moment: idle, parsing, waiting for the lock, waiting for the WAL), and log every WAL batch, not only those above 5 ms. Then decide between the hypotheses. A design change follows from the first: let a worker hand off a put that is waiting for durability and serve other requests (group commit with asynchronous completion), so a slow disk delays only puts.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Cards cost nothing to the wheelbarrow; timestamps cost the request. Each timestamp takes some nanoseconds and memory, and logging inside a lock lengthens the hold. Measure the overhead of the instrumentation, and keep it outside critical sections.
    • 

  • The gate is visible; a lock is not. Anyone on site sees the inspector at the gate. In software, a lock hold leaves no trace unless the code records it; Listing 1 records snapshot holds because the author suspected them. Unsuspected locks need lock profiling or off-CPU stacks.
    • 

  • Bricklayers can see the queue and walk away; threads cannot. A worker blocked in fdatasync cannot decide to serve a get instead. The fix in software is a design change (asynchronous completion), not a decision by the worker.
    • 

  • The inspector's count is slow because counting is slow; the snapshot is slow partly because of the machine. Hold times varied from 7 to 23 ms between snapshots of the same size; on a shared machine, the holder itself can be paused.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: SP302 (F2-55) profiled single programs for on-CPU hot spots; DS402 collected traces, spans and metrics in distributed systems; OS201 explained blocking system calls and context switches; F12-13 made measurements fair. What carries over: timestamps from a steady clock, percentiles, open-loop load from intended times, and the habit of a prediction before a measurement D3. What changes: most dangerous first: the cause of a tail is usually a wait, not a computation, and a CPU profile does not see it R1; the span where time shows up can be far from the cause (queue time caused by a lock) R2; and attribution needs a base rate, not a coincidence. What comes next: F12-15 turns measured limits into plans: how many machines are needed for next year's peak, and why a resource that is fine on average fails at the peak.

Mini-project


A profiling kit for your mega project. Build the instrumentation your project needs for a whole-system profile and keep it in the code: request or step traces with spans, per-thread resource records, a background-event log, and an analysis tool that produces the percentile table, slowest traces, span shares by tail, the USE table and event correlation with base rates. Run it at three load levels (50 %, 80 % and 95 % of the capacity you measured) and write the profile section of the SE401 project report, ending with the current bottleneck and the next one. Rubric: instrumentation complete, spans adding up, overhead measured (25 %); USE table covering software resources (20 %); tail attribution with base rates (25 %); a tested fix or a justified decision not to fix (20 %); clarity of the report (10 %).



Summary


    

  • Profile the whole system against the requirement's metric: traces with spans for requests, per-thread CPU and context switches, resource saturation, and a timestamped event log, all from the same run.
    • 

  • Tail latency is usually off-CPU: waits for locks, I/O and queues, invisible to CPU profiles.
    • 

  • Read the slowest traces, then compare the slowest 1 % with the rest, span by span.
    • 

  • Apply USE to every resource, including locks and worker pools; low average utilisation can hide long blockages.
    • 

  • Time often shows up in a different span from its cause: queue time behind blocked workers.
    • 

  • Attribute with base rates, and test the attribution by changing the cause; one run is not a benchmark.
    • 

  • After a fix, profile again: the tail has a new cause.
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. During the slow periods the waiting threads were asleep (blocked on the store lock or on the queue's condition variable); a sampling profiler of CPU use takes samples only of threads that run, so it sees the snapshot thread formatting and other threads' short bursts, not the waits. The workers used about 1.2 % of their wall time on the CPU.
    2. 

  2. All three workers were occupied: two were blocked on the store lock (6238, 6239) and the third was busy and then blocked (6240). No worker could take a request from the queue until the snapshot released the lock at about 3,148.5 ms. Proof: the traces of the requests in the workers at that moment (lock-wait spans ending at the release) and the snapshot hold at 3,125.4 ms lasting 23.07 ms.
    3. 

  3. Utilisation is an average over the run. Queueing depends on the load during the busy periods: for 23 ms at a time the lock serves no requests at all, so every request that needs it during that interval waits, a queue builds, and it drains only after the release. About 3.4 % of requests overlap a hold, which is enough to set the 99th percentile.
    4. 

  4. With the base rate: 412 of all 12,068 requests (3.4 %) overlap a hold. By chance about 4 of the slowest 120 would; 112 is far more, which supports a causal link. Without the base rate, a high count could come from holds being frequent or long.
    5. 

  5. Expected: p99 drops somewhat (fewer holds, so fewer requests meet one), but p99.9 and the maximum change little, because each hold is as long as before. The run-to-run noise of this machine may hide the p99 change; compare two runs of each.
    6. 

  6. Examples: the data loader not keeping up (gaps on the GPU timeline before each step, host thread busy reading or decoding; evidence: host-side spans and per-thread CPU); host-to-device copies from pageable memory serialising with kernels (copy spans on the timeline, no overlap; evidence: timeline profile showing copies and kernels on one stream).     7. 

  7. Because anything done inside the critical section lengthens the hold and delays all requests waiting for the lock; logging takes another lock and may allocate memory. The listing takes the log lock only after releasing the store lock.
    8. 

  8. (1) A disk episode: three slow WAL flushes (25.8–29.9 ms) around the slowest requests block workers on puts and queue the gets. (2) Unfair re-acquisition: the snapshot thread takes the lock back immediately after each chunk, so workers can wait for much of the 100 chunks. Experiment: record lock hand-offs (which thread acquired after each snapshot release) and repeat the runs several times; if the tail follows WAL episodes and hand-offs to workers are frequent, (1) is supported; if workers rarely acquire between chunks, (2) is.
