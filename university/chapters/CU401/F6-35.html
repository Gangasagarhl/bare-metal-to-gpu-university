Story hook

For three courses, Tomás and his row of helpers have built every dish themselves: their own sum, their own scan, their own tiled matrix stew. Today the hall's manager shows them a door they never opened. Behind it are the specialist stations: a station that does nothing but matrix stews, run by cooks who tuned it for every oven in the building; a station of standard kitchen moves (sort, sum, scan, sieve) that any recipe can call; and a station for the big set dishes of the neural-network menu.

Each station has an order form. Fill it in correctly and the dish arrives, often faster than anything Tomás could make. Fill it in wrongly and the dish also arrives, looking perfectly fine, made from the wrong ingredients. The matrix station's form, for instance, reads every tray column by column, while Tomás's helpers have always laid trays out row by row.

This chapter is about using the specialist stations well: reading their order forms, checking what comes back, and deciding with measurements, not pride, when your own recipe is still worth cooking.

What you will be able to do

  • Call cuBLAS SGEMM correctly from row-major C++ data, using the column-major contract and the operand swap, and check every cublasStatus_t.
  • Use Thrust for whole-array algorithms and CUB at device and block level, including CUB's two-phase temporary-storage protocol.
  • Explain the determinism, precision-mode and error-reporting properties that each library documents, and what they mean for your tests.
  • Diagnose a wrong leading dimension from argument logs and a small known input.
  • Defend a library-versus-custom-kernel decision with a measurement table and the roofline (the CU401 practical exam).
Jargon box
BLAS
Simple words: a standard list of linear-algebra jobs (vector add, matrix times vector, matrix times matrix) with fixed names and argument orders. Analogy: the matrix station's printed order form, the same in every kitchen hall. Precise: Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms; level 1 (vector–vector), level 2 (matrix–vector) and level 3 (matrix–matrix) routines with a Fortran heritage, which is why matrices are stored column-major and described by a leading dimension. D6
cuBLAS
Simple words: NVIDIA's GPU library of BLAS routines. Analogy: the matrix station of the hall. Precise: a library whose functions take a cublasHandle_t created by cublasCreate, return a cublasStatus_t, and follow the BLAS column-major convention; in the installed headers cublasSgemm is a macro for cublasSgemm_v2. H2 D2
Column-major and leading dimension
Simple words: storing a matrix one column after another; the leading dimension is how far apart, in memory, two neighbouring columns start. Analogy: a tray read top to bottom, then the next column of the tray. Precise: element (i, j) of a column-major matrix X with leading dimension ldX is at X[i + j·ldX], with ldX ≥ number of rows; a sub-matrix of a bigger matrix keeps the big matrix's leading dimension. D6 (Listing 1, line 4.)
GEMM
Simple words: "general matrix multiply": C becomes α times A times B, plus β times the old C. Analogy: the matrix stew, with a dial for how much of yesterday's stew to keep. Precise: C = α·op(A)·op(B) + β·C with op(X) = X or XT, op(A) of size m × k, op(B) k × n, C m × n; SGEMM is the FP32 version. D6
Thrust
Simple words: a C++ library of whole-array algorithms (sort, transform, reduce, scan) that look like the standard library's. Analogy: ordering a standard kitchen move by name and handing over the trays. Precise: a header-only template library, part of the CUDA Core Compute Libraries (CCCL), whose algorithms run on a back end chosen by the iterators or an execution policy (thrust::host, thrust::device); thrust::device_vector owns GPU memory (RAII). D3 R2
CUB
Simple words: a library of GPU building blocks at three sizes: for a warp, for a block, and for the whole device. Analogy: the standard kitchen moves, sold both as a full service and as hand tools you use inside your own recipe. Precise: a header-only CUDA C++ template library (part of CCCL) with warp-level, block-level (cub::BlockReduce, cub::BlockScan, …) and device-level (cub::DeviceReduce, cub::DeviceScan, cub::DeviceRadixSort, …) primitives. D3 H1
Two-phase temporary storage
Simple words: call the routine once to ask how much scratch memory it needs, allocate it, call it again to do the work. Analogy: asking the station how many bowls to bring before placing the order. Precise: CUB device-level calls take d_temp_storage and temp_storage_bytes; when d_temp_storage is nullptr, "the required allocation size is written to temp_storage_bytes and no work is done". H1
cuDNN
Simple words: NVIDIA's library of neural-network building blocks. Analogy: the station for the big set dishes of the neural-network menu. Precise: a library of GPU routines for operations such as convolutions, normalisations and attention, distributed separately from the CUDA Toolkit; it was not installed in this build (R8), so this chapter shows no cuDNN code. D4 R8
Kernel (GPU)
Simple words, analogy, precise: as in F6-01: the recipe card every helper follows, a function launched on a grid of GPU threads. A library call usually launches one or more kernels you never see. Not the operating-system kernel of OS301. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

You have learned to cook every dish yourself. That was the point: now you know what good cooking looks like. But the hall has specialist stations, and their cooks have tuned their dishes for years, for every kind of oven. Most days, ordering from them is the smart choice.

Ordering has its own skill. Each station has a form, and the matrix station reads trays column by column. If your helpers lay trays out row by row, you must tell the station, or turn your order around so it reads correctly. The dish comes back looking normal either way, so you always taste a small, known dish first.

And sometimes you still cook yourself: when you need several steps in one go and the station only does one step, or when your dish is so unusual that no station makes it. You decide by measuring, not by guessing.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why libraries first

One analogy sentence: a specialist station's cooks have tuned each recipe for every oven, and you have one afternoon. The vendor libraries contain kernels tuned per GPU architecture, often several variants per routine, selected at run time by size, type and device. D2 D3 The CUB header itself states one consequence: a device reduction's floating-point result may differ between GPUs of different compute capability "because CUB can employ different tile-sizing for different architectures". H1 Throughout Track E the curriculum uses these libraries as the yardstick for your own kernels: E3–E7 accept a kernel when it reaches "X % of" CUB or cuBLAS throughput. D8 So you need them for two reasons: to build on, and to measure against.

cuBLAS and the column-major contract

The BLAS interface was defined for Fortran, which stores arrays column by column, and cuBLAS keeps that convention. D6 D2 The installed header declares the FP32 GEMM as

cublasStatus_t cublasSgemm_v2(cublasHandle_t handle, cublasOperation_t transa, cublasOperation_t transb,
                              int m, int n, int k, const float* alpha, const float* A, int lda,
                              const float* B, int ldb, const float* beta, float* C, int ldc);

(fragment from cublas_api.h, shown for reading; cublas_v2.h maps the name cublasSgemm to it). H2 The arguments say: op(A) is m × k, op(B) is k × n, C is m × n, each matrix described by a pointer and a leading dimension, and the scalars α and β are passed by pointer. Listing 1 implements exactly this contract on the CPU (sgemmColMajor, lines 10–24) so that you can test your understanding without a GPU.

Your C++ arrays are row-major. Figure 2 shows the way out: a row-major m × n array, read column-major with leading dimension n, is the n × m transpose. Therefore row-major C = A·B is the same memory as column-major CT = BT·AT, and you get it by calling GEMM with (n, m, k), B first with ld = n, A second with ld = k, C with ld = n. Listing 1's run shows both the mistake and the fix (R1): the "textbook" column-major call on row-major arrays returns a matrix in which 8 of 8 elements are wrong, with no error code anywhere; the swapped call matches the reference exactly.



Three more parts of the contract matter in practice. First, every call returns a cublasStatus_t; the header lists CUBLAS_STATUS_NOT_INITIALIZED = 1 among them and provides cublasGetStatusName and cublasGetStatusString for messages. H2 Listing 5 checks them all. Second, a handle carries state: the stream (cublasSetStream_v2) and the math mode (cublasSetMathMode). H2 Third, the math mode is a precision decision: the header's comment on CUBLAS_TF32_TENSOR_OP_MATH says it allows "accelerating single precision routines using TF32 tensor cores". H2 An "SGEMM" can therefore round its inputs to TF32 if you (or a framework) set that mode, and your tolerance from F6-25 must say which mode was measured.



Thrust: the standard library, on the GPU


Thrust offers algorithms in the style of the C++ standard library over iterator ranges. D3 Listing 2 runs SAXPY (transform), a sum of squares in one fused pass (transform_reduce), stable_sort_by_key, exclusive_scan and reduce with the thrust::host policy, so the build container can check every result (R2). The version macro prints Thrust 2.0.1, matching the installed package libthrust-dev 2.0.1-2 (R8). Listing 3 is the same work with thrust::device_vector, which allocates GPU memory in its constructor and frees it in its destructor: RAII for device memory (SP201).




Note transform_reduce: squaring and summing in one call means one pass over memory, the first, small example of the fusion idea of F6-38. Two separate calls (transform into a temporary, then reduce) would write and re-read a whole array. Library users fuse by choosing the right algorithm and by passing functors (Saxpy, Square), which Thrust inlines into its kernels; the functors are marked __host__ __device__ in Listing 3 so the same object can run on either side.


Thrust reports errors by throwing. In this build the first allocation failed with an exception whose type was printed as thrust::system::detail::bad_alloc and whose message begins std::bad_alloc: cudaErrorNoDevice (R4). The first version of Listing 3 caught only thrust::system_error, so the exception escaped and the program aborted (exit code 134, R4). The final listing also catches std::bad_alloc and exits cleanly with a message (R3). That is a real lesson from this build: check what a library actually throws, not what you expect it to throw.





CUB at two levels


CUB gives you the same primitives at device, block and warp level. D3 Two properties from its installed headers shape how you use it. H1


    

  • Device level, two phases. cub::DeviceReduce::Sum(d_temp_storage, temp_storage_bytes, in, out, n) with d_temp_storage == nullptr only writes the needed size; you allocate and call again (Listing 4, lines 49–53). The point is that CUB never allocates behind your back, so you can reuse one scratch buffer across calls and keep allocation out of timed loops.
    • 

  • Block level, inside your kernel. cub::BlockReduce<float, 256> needs a TempStorage object in shared memory, and its Sum result is defined only in thread 0: "The return value is undefined in threads other than thread0." Listing 4's kernel squares and sums in one pass and lets thread 0 write the block's partial.
    • 

  • Determinism. The DeviceReduce header promises "run-to-run" determinism for floating-point addition on the same GPU, but not across GPUs of different compute capability; the DeviceScan header says results "may vary from run to run" for such operators (it uses decoupled look-back, F6-16). H1 D7 If your test compares a GPU sum bit-for-bit with a stored value, it can fail on another GPU with nothing broken.
    •

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


One analogy sentence: a station is only faster if your order fits its menu; an order it must split into three separate dishes may lose to one recipe of your own. The engineering content of this chapter is a decision procedure.


The library-versus-custom decision


    

  1. Write the operation as library calls first. It is your correctness reference and your baseline time. If the result is already within your target, stop.
    2. 

  2. Place it on the roofline (F6-33). Compute arithmetic intensity from bytes and FLOPs. A compute-bound GEMM at large sizes is the library's home ground: tensor cores, tuned tiling, years of work (F6-26, F6-27). A chain of memory-bound calls is where a custom fused kernel can win, because the library must write and re-read intermediates between calls (F6-38).
    3. 

  3. Measure, do not assume. Same harness, same sizes, median of 20 runs after warm-up, both sides with the same precision mode (F6-06, curriculum 13.4). Include sizes that are not multiples of anything, tiny sizes and large sizes. D8
    4. 

  4. Count the whole cost. Temporary storage (CUB tells you the bytes), handle creation and its cost outside the timed region, extra launches, layout conversions (a transpose to fit column-major costs a full read and write), and your time to write, test and maintain a kernel.
    5. 

  5. Write the decision down with the numbers, the GPU, driver and library versions, and the conditions under which it should be revisited (new GPU, new library version, new shapes).
    6. 



The practical exam of CU401 asks for exactly this: "defend a library-versus-custom-kernel decision with measurements". A defensible memo has a table with your kernel, the library, both as a percentage of the roofline bound, and one sentence per row on why the gap is what it is.


Shapes, layouts and the cost of fitting the form


    

  • Sub-matrices are free if you pass the right leading dimension. A block of a bigger matrix is a pointer to its first element plus the big matrix's leading dimension. Copying the block into a compact array first is correct but costs a pass over memory. The forensic lab is the bug in the other direction.
    • 

  • Transposes are free through op: CUBLAS_OP_T reads a matrix transposed without moving it. Combined with Figure 2, any row-major/column-major combination can be expressed without copies.
    • 

  • Many small GEMMs are a separate problem: each launch has fixed overhead (F6-30), and BLAS libraries offer batched and strided-batched variants for that case. D2 Read the cuBLAS documentation's batched section before writing a loop of calls.
    • 

  • Epilogues. Bias plus activation after a GEMM is two extra memory-bound passes if done separately. cuBLASLt and CUTLASS can apply such epilogues inside the GEMM. D2 D5
    •

Libraries and streams


Library calls are asynchronous like kernel launches: they queue work on a stream and return (F6-28). For cuBLAS the stream is a property of the handle (cublasSetStream_v2); for CUB device calls it is an optional last argument; for Thrust it is chosen through the execution policy. H1 H2 D3 Two consequences: errors from inside a library's kernels can surface later, at the next synchronising call (F6-05); and timing a library call needs events on the same stream, exactly like your own kernels.


Not verified — check before relying on this
The way Thrust selects a stream through an execution policy (the thrust::cuda::par.on(stream) form) is written from memory. Check the Thrust documentation shipped with CCCL, or the installed headers under thrust/system/cuda/, before using it.



How the hardware actually does it


A library call is ordinary GPU work: host code in the library picks a kernel and a launch configuration from the arguments and the device, then launches it on the handle's or policy's stream. D1 What differs from your own kernels is the selection step: a GEMM routine may choose among many compiled kernels (different tile shapes, with or without tensor cores, split-K variants) by matrix size and device. D5 You can see which kernel ran, and with how many registers and how much shared memory, in Nsight Systems and Nsight Compute (F6-32, F6-33), exactly as for your own kernels; that is how you explain a library's speed instead of treating it as a black box. Block-level CUB, in contrast, is compiled into your kernel: its shared memory and registers appear in your own ptxas report (R6: 14 registers, 44 bytes of shared memory).

Worked example


Predict Listing 1's wrong result by hand, to see that it is not random. A is 2 × 3, B is 3 × 4, stored row-major: A = {1,2,3, 4,5,6}, B = {1,0,2,1, 0,1,1,2, 1,1,0,3}.


    

  1. The mistaken call passes (m, n, k) = (2, 4, 3), lda = 2, ldb = 3, ldc = 2. The library reads A's six numbers column-major with ld 2: columns (1, 2), (3, 4), (5, 6), so A′ = [[1, 3, 5], [2, 4, 6]].
    2. 

  2. It reads B with ld 3: columns (1, 0, 2), (1, 0, 1), (1, 2, 1), (1, 0, 3).
    3. 

  3. C′ = A′·B′, column by column: column 0 = 1·(1, 2) + 0·(3, 4) + 2·(5, 6) = (11, 14); column 1 = (1, 2) + (5, 6) = (6, 8); column 2 = (1, 2) + 2·(3, 4) + (5, 6) = (12, 16); column 3 = (1, 2) + 3·(5, 6) = (16, 20).
    4. 

  4. C′ is stored column-major with ld 2: memory 11, 14, 6, 8, 12, 16, 16, 20. Your code prints it row-major with 4 per row: (11, 14, 6, 8) and (12, 16, 16, 20). That is exactly R1's as_if_colmajor block.
    5. 

  5. The correct call passes (n, m, k) = (4, 2, 3), B with ld 4, A with ld 3, C with ld 4. The library sees BT (4 × 3) and AT (3 × 2) and computes CT (4 × 2) column-major with ld 4, which in memory is row-major C: (4, 5, 4, 14) and (10, 11, 13, 32). Check one element by hand: C[0][3] = 1·1 + 2·2 + 3·3 = 14. R1 reports 0 of 8 elements differ.
    6. 



The lesson for testing: use small integer matrices that are not square and not symmetric. With square or symmetric test data, several wrong calls give right answers.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A station is not a separate place. Library kernels run on the same SMs, in the same streams, competing for the same memory as yours; "ordering from the station" does not add any hardware. Concurrency with your kernels follows the stream rules of F6-28.
    • 

  • Block-level CUB is not ordering at all. It is a tool compiled into your own recipe: its shared memory and registers are counted against your kernel's occupancy (R6). The "station" picture fits only the device-level calls.
    • 

  • The order form does not reject wrong orders. A kitchen station would ask "column by column, really?"; cuBLAS accepts any leading dimension that is large enough and computes something (R1, forensic lab). Correctness comes from your tests, not from the library.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: CU301–CU303, where you wrote reductions, scans, sorts and GEMMs yourself and profiled them (F6-14–F6-34). What carries over: every measurement and correctness habit: CPU references, tolerances, median of 20 timed runs, checking every returned error, streams and events. D1 What changes: most dangerous first: a library's conventions are not yours (column-major, leading dimensions, two-phase calls, thread 0 only), and wrong arguments give wrong results without errors (R1); each library has its own error type and its own determinism promise (H1, H2); the code you call is a black box you profile rather than read. What comes next: F6-36 names the groups of threads that block-level CUB works with, and lets you write your own primitives at any group size, up to the whole grid.

Summary


    

  • Use the vendor libraries first: as building blocks, as correctness references and as the performance yardstick of Track E.
    • 

  • cuBLAS follows the BLAS column-major contract; row-major C = A·B is column-major CT = BTAT: swap the operands and m with n.
    • 

  • A sub-matrix is a pointer plus the parent matrix's leading dimension; wrong leading dimensions give wrong results without errors.
    • 

  • Thrust gives whole-array algorithms with RAII device vectors and reports errors by exceptions; catch what it really throws.
    • 

  • CUB device calls use a two-phase temporary-storage protocol; block primitives go inside your kernel and return results in thread 0.
    • 

  • Library results can differ between GPUs (tiling) and between math modes (TF32); tests use tolerances and record the mode.
    • 

  • Decide library versus custom with the roofline and measurements, and write the decision down with its conditions.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. The first row of the result (8, 9) is right; the second row is (10, 11) instead of (14, 15). With A[r][c] = 6r + c, 10 and 11 are A[1][4] and A[1][5], the elements just to the right of the block's first row, not A[2][2] and A[2][3]. The only argument that differs between the builds is ldb: 6 versus 2.


Hypotheses. A wrong pointer offset would also shift the first row (it is correct). A wrong k would change the number of terms, not which elements are read in row 2. A wrong leading dimension changes only where rows after the first start: consistent with the evidence.


Root cause. In the swapped call the block is read column-major with ldB: element (p, j) at block + p + j·ld. Block row r of the row-major matrix is "column" j = r. With ld = 6, row 1 starts at block + 6 = A + 8 + 6 = A + 14 → 14, 15. With ld = 2, it starts at A + 8 + 2 = A + 10 → 10, 11. A sub-matrix must keep the parent matrix's leading dimension (6, the parent's row length).


Why tests passed. For a whole matrix, the block width equals the row length, so "width" and "leading dimension" are the same number.


Fix and verification. ldb = 6 (the parent's row length); the "build good" lines of the evidence show (8, 9 / 14, 15).


Prevention. A test that multiplies interior sub-blocks of a non-square matrix by the identity and compares with the block; an assertion ld >= rows of the parent in the helper, and passing a small "matrix view" struct (pointer, rows, cols, ld) instead of loose integers. How the evidence was produced: real runs of forensic.cpp with the two ld values; no GPU involved.
