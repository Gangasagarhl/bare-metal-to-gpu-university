Story hook

In the great kitchen hall, Amara's row of helpers prepares sauce in three steps: taste every pot to find the strongest, then dilute every pot by the strongest one, then share the sauce out in proportion. For a while each step was its own order. After every step the helpers carried all the pots back to the big pantry at the far end of the hall, and for the next step they carried them out again. The cooking itself took a moment; the walking took the afternoon.

So Amara rewrote the recipe card: keep the pots at the station, do the three steps in a row, and walk to the pantry only with the finished sauce. That is a fused kernel. One problem remained: to dilute by the strongest pot, you must know the strongest pot before you start, which seemed to need one more walk along the row. Kenji, a helper with a good memory, showed a trick. He kept two numbers in his hands, "the strongest so far" and "my running tally, measured against the strongest so far". When a stronger pot appeared, he did not start again: he scaled his tally down once and carried on. That trick is the online normaliser.

A month later the sauce came out as grey sludge on the busiest day of the year. Somebody had "simplified" Kenji's card and dropped the scaling step. On quiet days nobody noticed. This chapter's forensic lab is that day.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why a chain of memory-bound kernels is limited by global-memory traffic, and count the bytes that fusion removes.
  • Derive the online normaliser for softmax and the merge rule for two partial states, and prove that the result equals the safe three-pass softmax.
  • Implement a fused row softmax and a fused layer norm with one warp per row, warp-shuffle merges and Welford statistics.
  • Measure numerical error against a double-precision reference, choose a tolerance and explain where FP32 overflow, underflow and cancellation come from.
  • Diagnose "NaN after fusion" from a per-chunk trace of the running maximum and normaliser.
Jargon box
Kernel fusion
Simple words: doing several steps of a calculation in one GPU program so the data between the steps never goes back to global memory. Analogy: keeping the pots at the station for all three recipe steps instead of walking to the pantry after each. Precise: combining operations that would be separate kernels (each reading its input from and writing its output to global memory) into one kernel whose intermediates stay in registers or shared memory; it removes the intermediate global-memory reads and writes and the extra launches. D6 (Not the operating-system kernel of OS301.)
Softmax
Simple words: turns a list of scores into shares that are all positive and add up to one; bigger scores get much bigger shares. Analogy: sharing the sauce in proportion to each pot's strength, after a very strong "boost" of the differences. Precise: yi = exi / Σj exj; it is unchanged when the same constant is subtracted from every xj, which is why implementations subtract the maximum. D2
Safe softmax
Simple words: softmax computed after subtracting the largest score, so no exponential can become too big. Analogy: measuring every pot against the strongest one. Precise: yi = exi−m / Σj exj−m with m = maxj xj; every exponent is ≤ 0, so every term is in (0, 1]; three passes over the data (max, sum, write). D2
Online normaliser
Simple words: finding the maximum and the sum in the same pass by fixing up the sum whenever the maximum grows. Analogy: Kenji's "strongest so far" and tally, scaled down once when a stronger pot appears. Precise: the state (m, d) is updated per element v by m′ = max(m, v), d′ = d·em−m′ + ev−m′; after the last element, d = Σ exj−m exactly as in the safe softmax, so softmax needs two passes instead of three. D2
State merge (associative combine)
Simple words: a rule for joining two partial answers into the answer for both parts. Analogy: two helpers who tasted different halves of the row compare notes. Precise: for softmax, (ma, da) ⊕ (mb, db) = (M, daema−M + dbemb−M) with M = max(ma, mb); because ⊕ is associative, a tree of shuffles can merge 32 lanes in five steps. D2
Layer norm
Simple words: shifting and scaling each row of numbers so that it has average zero and spread one. Analogy: adjusting every pot so the row as a whole is "evenly seasoned". Precise: yi = γi(xi − μ)/√(σ2 + ε) + βi, with μ and σ2 the mean and (population) variance of the row and γ, β learned per-feature parameters; this chapter's kernel computes the normalised part (γ = 1, β = 0). D4
Welford's algorithm
Simple words: a way to keep a running mean and spread in one pass without the big cancellation of "sum of squares minus square of sum". Analogy: adjusting your idea of the average a little with each new pot. Precise: per element: n ← n + 1, δ = x − mean, mean ← mean + δ/n, M2 ← M2 + δ(x − mean); variance = M2/n; two states merge with δ = meanb − meana, M2 = M2a + M2b + δ2nanb/n. D3
Catastrophic cancellation
Simple words: losing almost all correct digits when you subtract two nearly equal big numbers. Analogy: weighing a feather by weighing a truck with and without it. Precise: when a − b is computed from rounded a and b with a ≈ b, the absolute rounding errors of a and b remain while the result is small, so the relative error of the result can be large; E[x2] − E[x]2 in FP32 is the classic case. D5 (Taught in MA302.)

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Walking to the pantry takes much longer than stirring a pot. So if a recipe has three quick steps, do all three at your station before you walk. That is all fusion is: fewer trips.

The hard part is a step that needs to know something about the whole row first, like "which pot is strongest?". Kenji's trick is to keep a running answer and fix it up when he learns more. When he finds a stronger pot, he scales his old tally once, and carries on. At the end, his tally is exactly what he would have got by walking along the row twice.

And when two helpers have each tasted half the row, they can compare notes with the same fix-up rule. A whole row of 32 helpers can combine their notes in five rounds of whispering to a partner.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why memory-bound chains are worth fusing

One analogy sentence: every walk to the pantry costs the same whether you carry one pot or a full tray, and most deep-learning "glue" operations (softmax, normalisation, bias, activation) do only a few arithmetic operations per element they carry. Such operations have a low arithmetic intensity (operations per byte moved; F6-21) and sit on the memory roof of the roofline model (F6-33): their time is close to the bytes they move divided by the memory bandwidth you measured in F6-09. D6 D7 Removing bytes therefore removes time almost one to one, and fusing a chain of such operations removes all intermediate writes and their re-reads. Figure 1 counts the softmax case: three reads and two writes per element unfused (x read twice, the exponentials written and re-read, y written), two reads and one write fused. That is a predicted 5/3 ≈ 1.7× less traffic for long rows, before caches; you will measure what your GPU actually does.

From naive to safe softmax

The definition yi = exi/Σ exj fails in FP32 as soon as an input is large. The binary32 format of IEEE 754 has a largest finite value a little above 3.4 × 1038, and ex exceeds it just below x = 89; an overflowing operation returns infinity, and ∞/∞ or 0/0 is NaN. D5 Listing 1 shows it on this build's CPU: exp(88) prints 1.65164e+38 and exp(89) prints inf (R1). Rows whose values are only near 88 already lose accuracy because the sum overflows (case near_88: error 0.00491), rows above it give NaN (large, huge), and very negative rows underflow every term to zero and give 0/0 = NaN (negative). Subtracting the row maximum m first changes nothing mathematically (the factor e−m cancels) and makes every exponent ≤ 0, so the largest term is exactly 1 and the sum is between 1 and n. D2 The price is a third pass: max, then sum, then write.



The online normaliser


Milakov and Gimelshein's observation is that the maximum and the sum can be found in one pass if the sum is always kept relative to the current maximum. D2 Keep a state (m, d) that means "d = Σ exj−m over the elements seen so far, with m their maximum". Adding an element v:


m' = max(m, v)
d' = d * exp(m - m') + exp(v - m')


Why it is exact. By the invariant, d·em−m′ = Σseen exj−m·em−m′ = Σseen exj−m′, the old sum re-expressed against the new maximum; adding ev−m′ restores the invariant with v included. By induction, after the last element d is exactly the safe-softmax denominator (in exact arithmetic; in floating point both are rounded sums of the same kind of terms). The start state is (−∞, 0): the first update computes 0·e−∞ + e0 = 1. Every exponent is still ≤ 0, so nothing overflows. Listing 1's online column has the same error as safe on every case while reading the input twice instead of three times (R1).


Merging two states. If one lane has (ma, da) for its elements and another (mb, db) for different elements, the state of the union is (M, daema−M + dbemb−M) with M = max(ma, mb), by the same re-expression argument. The update rule is the special case where one side is a single element (mb = v, db = 1). The merge is commutative and associative (up to rounding), which is what a parallel reduction needs (F6-14). D2 One edge case matters: merging two empty states gives e−∞−(−∞) = eNaN, so Listing 4 returns early when M = −∞. F6-39 meets the same trap with masked attention scores.


Layer norm and Welford


Layer normalisation normalises each row (one token's feature vector, in a transformer) by its own mean and variance. D4 The tempting one-pass formula var = E[x2] − E[x]2 subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers when the mean is large compared with the spread, and FP32 keeps only about seven significant decimal digits (24-bit significand). D5 Listing 2 shows the damage on rows with a true variance of 10.0034: with an offset of 100 the one-pass result is 9.68; with 1,000 it is 7.31; with 10,000 it is 3760, which is not even close in size (R3). The two-pass method (mean first, then mean squared difference) is accurate but reads the row twice before the output pass. Welford's update keeps a running mean and M2 (the sum of squared differences from the current mean) in one pass and stays accurate: 10.0037 at offset 10,000. D3 Like (m, d), Welford states merge: the last column ("Welford x2") merges the states of the two halves with the pairwise formula and agrees to about four digits. That merge is what the warp shuffles of Listing 4 do.





Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


One analogy sentence: a recipe card that keeps more pots in each helper's hands lets fewer helpers fit in the hall, so fusion trades memory traffic against registers. The engineering questions are which operations to fuse, how to map rows to threads, and how to keep the numbers honest.


Mapping rows to the hardware


    

  • One warp per row (Listing 4) suits rows of a few hundred to a few thousand elements: each lane strides over the row, coalesced (F6-08), and the merge is five shuffles with no shared memory and no __syncthreads(). Lanes that own fewer elements (here 1,500 = 46 × 32 + 28 columns, so four lanes own one element less) are harmless because their state is merged like any other.
    • 

  • One block per row suits long rows: each warp merges its lanes, then warps merge through shared memory (or cub::BlockReduce with a custom operator, F6-35; or cg::reduce, F6-36).
    • 

  • Rows held in registers. If a row fits in the warp's registers (cols/32 values per lane), pass 2 can reuse the values loaded in pass 1, making the kernel one read and one write per element. Listing 4 re-reads instead, to stay correct for any row length; the cost is visible in the PTX (R5: ten global loads after unrolling, for two loops).
    • 

  • Several rows per warp suit very short rows (a warp of 32 lanes on a row of 64 elements does only two elements per lane); use tiled_partition with tiles of 8 or 16 (F6-36).
    • 



Precision, intrinsics and tolerances


Listing 4 uses __expf, which the installed header documents as returning "an approximation" of ex with the accuracy of single-precision intrinsics. H1 In the PTX for sm_80 it becomes ex2.approx.f32 (25 occurrences after unrolling, R5): a base-2 hardware approximation, with the multiplication by log2e done before it. D7 The division becomes div.rn.f32, a correctly rounded division. Whether __expf is accurate enough is a tolerance decision (F6-25): softmax outputs are probabilities, so a combination of absolute and relative tolerance per element, plus the check "each row sums to 1 within n·ε", is a sensible start; justify the numbers from your own error measurements, as Listing 1 does on the CPU with std::exp. Mixed precision (FP16 or BF16 inputs) changes the picture: load in the low precision, but keep m, d, mean and M2 in FP32. Online softmax in BF16 accumulation would lose the small terms that make up most of d. D5


What to fuse, and what not


    

  • Fuse chains of element-wise and row-wise operations that are each memory-bound: bias + activation, residual add + layer norm, scale + mask + softmax. Each fused-away intermediate saves one write and one read of a full tensor.
    • 

  • Fuse epilogues into GEMMs (bias, activation, scaling applied to the accumulator before it is stored): cuBLASLt and CUTLASS expose this; the decision versus a separate kernel is F6-35's library question. D8
    • 

  • Do not fuse when the fused kernel needs so many registers or so much shared memory that occupancy collapses and latency is no longer hidden (F6-20), when one of the operations needs a grid-wide result before the next can start (a global sum across rows needs a second launch or a cooperative grid barrier, F6-36), or when the unfused version is a library call you would have to beat (measure first).
    • 

  • Recompute instead of store. The second pass of Listing 4 recomputes ex−m instead of reading a stored value. Recomputation is cheap arithmetic traded for expensive traffic; F6-39 takes this to its conclusion with attention.
    •

How the hardware actually does it


A row softmax kernel does a handful of floating-point operations per element and is dominated by the loads and stores that the SM sends to the memory system (F1-59). The warp's 32 lanes load 32 consecutive floats per loop iteration, which the memory system serves as full cache-line transactions (F6-08). The exponentials go to the special-function units: the instruction ex2.approx.f32 in PTX is the special-function operation that the SASS of F6-39 shows as MUFU.EX2 (R5 here; the SASS is in F6-39's lab). The shuffles (shfl.sync.bfly.b32) move register values between lanes of a warp without touching shared memory. D1 D7 The register report shows the cost of the fused state: 25 registers per thread for the softmax kernel and 28 for layer norm on sm_80 (R6), far below anything that would limit occupancy; the fused attention kernel of F6-39 is a very different story.







Worked example


Online softmax of x = (1, 3, 2), by hand, then checked by a program.


    

  1. Start. (m, d) = (−∞, 0).
    2. 

  2. x = 1. m′ = 1; d′ = 0·e−∞ + e0 = 1. State (1, 1).
    3. 

  3. x = 3. m′ = 3 (the maximum grew); d′ = 1·e1−3 + e0 = e−2 + 1 = 0.135335 + 1 = 1.135335.
    4. 

  4. x = 2. m′ = 3; d′ = 1.135335·e0 + e−1 = 1.135335 + 0.367879 = 1.503214 (the program, with full precision, prints 1.503215).
    5. 

  5. Check against safe softmax. Σ exj−3 = e−2 + e0 + e−1: the same three terms, the same sum.
    6. 

  6. Write pass. y = (e−2, 1, e−1)/1.503215 = (0.090031, 0.665241, 0.244728); the three add up to 1.
    7. 

  7. Merge instead. Split as {1} and {3, 2}: states (1, 1) and (3, 1 + e−1) = (3, 1.367879). Merge: M = 3, d = 1·e−2 + 1.367879·e0 = 1.503215. Same answer, as associativity promises.
    8.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Helpers' hands are not unlimited; registers are counted. "Keep everything at the station" sounds free, but each kept value is a register; more registers per thread means fewer warps fit on an SM (F6-20). Fusion can make a kernel slower when it pushes occupancy too low or spills to local memory (F6-39's kernel shows a stack frame).
    • 

  • Kenji's tally is exact; the GPU's is rounded. In exact arithmetic the online normaliser equals the safe softmax; in FP32 the two orders of operations round differently and may differ in the last bits. The equality to test is "within a justified tolerance", not bit-identical.
    • 

  • The pantry is not equally far for every trip. Caches (L2) can keep an intermediate between two unfused kernels if it is small enough, so the real saving of fusion can be smaller than Figure 1's count. Measure.
    •

Forensic lab


Scenario. Priya's team shipped a "faster" fused softmax. Summary


    

  • Memory-bound chains spend their time on global-memory traffic; fusion removes intermediate writes and re-reads.
    • 

  • Naive softmax overflows above x ≈ 88 in FP32 and gives NaN; subtracting the row maximum fixes it.
    • 

  • The online normaliser (m, d) finds maximum and sum in one pass by rescaling d whenever m grows; it is exact in exact arithmetic.
    • 

  • States merge with an associative rule, so warps merge them with five butterfly shuffles.
    • 

  • Layer norm needs Welford (or two passes): E[x2] − E[x]2 cancels catastrophically in FP32.
    • 

  • Keep statistics in FP32 even with low-precision inputs; guard empty states.
    • 

  • Fusion costs registers; verify with the compiler report and measure against the unfused chain and the library.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. m stays 10.04 for all chunks while the chunk maximum grows 25.04, 40.04, …, 115.04. d grows by a factor of about e15 ≈ 3.3 × 106 per chunk (250.9 → 8.2 × 108 → 2.7 × 1015 …) and becomes inf at chunk 6. 512 outputs are NaN and the sum is NaN. In the correct run (R4) m follows the chunk maximum and d stays near 251.


Hypotheses. NaN already in the input would show as NaN in the chunk-max column (it does not). 0/0 needs d = 0 (d is never 0). Overflow in exp with a stale maximum explains the growth of d and the inf.


Root cause. With m frozen at 10.04, chunk 6 computes ex−10.04 with x up to 100.04, an exponent of 90, above the FP32 limit of about 88.7 (R1: e89 = inf). The flat run never has x − m above 0.04, so the missing rescale never matters: d simply accumulates the same sum as the correct version (2007.6 = 8 × 250.9), and the outputs sum to 1.


Why 512. With d = inf, outputs of chunks 0–5 are finite/inf = 0, and outputs of chunks 6–7 (2 × 256 = 512 values) have ex−m = inf, so inf/inf = NaN.


Fix. Delete the frozen branch: m′ = max(m, chunk max) and d = d·em−m′ + s. The verification run is fused_trace.out (R4): same input, 0 NaN, sum 1.


Prevention. Tests with rows whose maximum is in the last chunk, with growing rows, and with values above 88; a debug assertion that d is finite and that m equals the true maximum on sampled rows. How the evidence was produced: real runs of Listing 3 with the shipped mode frozen on a ramp and on a flat row; no GPU was involved.
