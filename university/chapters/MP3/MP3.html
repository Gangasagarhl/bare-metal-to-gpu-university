Story hook

Every great kitchen hall has a specialist station that has cooked one dish for years: the big platters of matrix stew. Its cooks are fast and its recipes are secret. Leila has spent a year learning the hall, and she wants to cook the same dish at her own station, from her own recipe cards, and find out how close she can get.

Before she lights a single burner, her two reviewers, Tomas and Wanjiru, sit down with her and write a scorecard: which platters will be compared (three sizes), how the timing is done (the same clock for both stations, twenty tastings after one warm-up, the middle result counts), what "the same dish" means (how much the taste may differ), and the score she aims for at each size. All three sign the card, and a short code is written under it, computed from its contents, so that nobody can change a line later without the code changing too.

Months later, the final tasting comes. The scorecard on the table has a different code from the one in the review notes. Somebody, somewhere, lowered two of the aims.

This handbook is about cooking the dish well. It is also about that scorecard.

What you will be able to do

  • Plan a GPU kernel library project: agree performance targets, references, tolerances and the measurement protocol with your reviewers at gate R1, before writing any kernel, and lock them with a fingerprint.
  • Build a correctness suite whose shape matrix, precisions and justified tolerances catch real bugs, and prove that it does by seeding faults that it must catch.
  • Implement an SGEMM that reaches the pre-agreed percentage of cuBLAS at three sizes, a mixed-precision GEMM on tensor cores, and a fused attention forward pass compared with the library implementation on your platform.
  • Measure with a protocol a reviewer can rerun: raw records of every timed run, labelled with GPU, driver and toolkit versions, medians of at least 20 runs after warm-up, the reference measured by the same harness.
  • Explain every remaining gap with profiler evidence and place each kernel on a roofline chart.
  • Diagnose a report whose numbers the evidence does not support, and write the incident up without blame.

Mega project

Simple words: the score you promise to reach, written down before you start. Analogy: the aim on Leila's signed scorecard. Precise: for one kernel, size and precision, the percentage X of the reference library's speed (reference median time ÷ your median time × 100) that the milestone must reach on your GPU; written in the plan before coding and never lowered afterwards without the owner's explicit approval and a log entry. Targets fingerprint
Simple words: a short code computed from the targets file; change one character and the code changes. Analogy: the code written under the scorecard. Precise: in this project, a 64-bit hash over every line of the targets file that defines the agreement (machine, references, targets, tolerances), printed by Listing 3 at R1 and by Listing 4 on every report; equal fingerprints mean the report used the targets that were signed. Shape matrix (test)
Simple words: the list of matrix sizes the tests use, each chosen to catch one kind of mistake. Analogy: tasting a tiny portion, an odd-sized one and a very long thin one, not only the usual platter. Precise: the set of (M, N, K) used by every correctness test: tiny sizes, sizes one below and one above a tile, tall-skinny and short-wide, K = 1, and tile multiples; the curriculum requires odd, tiny and large sizes and sizes that are not multiples of the tile. Seeded fault (mutant)
A deliberately broken copy of a correct kernel that the suite must fail: the mutation testing of F4-18 applied to GPU kernels. A suite that passes a seeded fault is too weak for that kind of bug. R2
Protocol check (benchmark report)
Simple words: a program that refuses to print a percentage the measurement rules do not allow. Analogy: the judge who will not score a tasting done with a different clock. Precise: in this project, Listing 4's rule set: a row is INVALID when the machine differs from the one agreed at R1, the case has no passing correctness check, either side was not timed with device events, no warm-up was recorded, or fewer than 20 timed runs exist. Best-of-N time
Simple words: the fastest of several runs. Analogy: quoting only your best lap. Precise: the minimum of N timed runs; it is more favourable than the median whenever runs vary, and the curriculum's protocol uses the median of at least 20 runs, so best-of-N may be shown next to it but never replace it in a verdict. Simple words: the evidence-based answer to "why is my kernel slower than the library?". Analogy: watching both stations work and noting exactly where Leila's cooks wait. Simple words: made-up numbers used only to test a program that reads real numbers. Analogy: plastic food used to rehearse the serving. Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F6 registry; the "specialist station" is the library proposal recorded in CU401's notes): the hall's specialist station = the vendor library (cuBLAS, the platform's attention); Leila's own station = your kernel library; the same platter = the same problem, size and number format; the signed scorecard = the targets file agreed at R1; the code under the scorecard = the targets fingerprint; twenty tastings after a warm-up, the middle one counting = the median of at least 20 timed runs; "how much the taste may differ" = the tolerance; watching where the cooks wait = profiler-backed gap analysis.

You are going to write your own versions of two famous GPU dishes, matrix multiplication and attention, and compare them with the versions NVIDIA's own libraries serve. The comparison is the point: "fast" means nothing until you say "compared with what, on which GPU, measured how". So the project starts with an agreement and ends with evidence. In between you cook, taste, measure and explain.

What you build

  • A library of your own kernels with one documented launcher per kernel: SGEMM (FP32), HGEMM (FP16 inputs, FP32 accumulation, on tensor cores) and a fused attention forward pass. Row-major storage throughout, as in CU302.
  • A correctness suite: a shape matrix, three precisions, an FP64 reference and one justified tolerance rule per precision; it runs on the CPU emulator of F6-21 without a GPU and with the same rule on the GPU; it must catch seeded faults.
  • A harness that labels every record with the GPU, driver and toolkit versions printed by the tools, times your kernels and the references in the same way, and writes every timed run as a raw record.
  • A report tool that turns raw records into the results table and refuses rows the protocol does not support.
  • A written report: every measurement labelled with GPU, driver and toolkit; the percentages against the R1 targets; a profiler-backed explanation of each gap; a roofline chart.

The starter lab of this handbook gives you a working skeleton of the first milestone: Listings 1–5, built and run in this build.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The measurement protocol, and why each rule exists

The curriculum's rules are short: correctness first; warm-up runs, then the median of at least 20 timed runs using device events; fixed clocks if your driver allows it, with the setting recorded; GB/s or TFLOP/s and the percentage of the reference; the reference is the vendor library on the same GPU, same data type, same sizes, measured by the same harness; datasheet peaks are context, not the target C1 §13.4. Each rule closes one way a number can mislead:

  • Correctness first. A kernel that skips work is fast. Listing 4 refuses a row without a passing check.
  • Warm-up. The first launch can include one-time costs (module loading, first-touch of memory, clock ramp-up); none of them belongs to the steady-state speed you are comparing D2. Listing 5 times a warm-up run and records it separately; Listing 4 never uses it.
  • Median of at least 20. The median is not moved by a few slow outliers; the mean is. The minimum (best-of-N) is moved by a few fast ones, which is why the report shows it only as a second column. MA202 and F12-13 develop the statistics.
  • Device events for both sides. A host wall clock around an asynchronous launch measures something else (F6-06). Listing 4 marks a row INVALID unless both sides were timed with events.
  • Same harness, same data, same machine. The percentage is a ratio of two times; the ratio means something only when everything except the kernel is the same. Listing 4 compares the measured GPU, driver and toolkit with the R1 values.
  • Clocks. GPUs change their clock with temperature and power. Unlocked clocks add spread and drift between runs taken minutes apart; the forensic data in this handbook imitate that with a bimodal set of times. Lock clocks only on a machine you are allowed to change (see the safety section), and record what you did either way.

Raw records are the deliverable, not the table. A table is one summary; the raw records let a reviewer recompute it, try a different statistic, or find the cluster of fast runs that the median hides. The record format of Listing 4 keeps one line per (kernel, implementation, size, precision) with the warm-up time and every timed run, after env lines that label the machine. It is the benchmark record of F12-13 reduced to what this project needs.

Not verified — check before relying on this

How to lock or query GPU clocks, and the names of the commands and options that do it, depend on the driver and its tools; none was available in this build (the run R10 shows nvidia-smi: not found). Take them from your driver's documentation, and record the command you used in the clocks line of the targets file and in the raw records.

Tolerances justified per MA302

Each element of C is a K-term dot product. In FP32 with any summation order, the computed value ĉ satisfies |ĉ − c| ≤ γK Σ|akbk| with γK = Ku/(1 − Ku) and u = 2−24 D7. Because the bound holds for every order, it holds for every tiling your library will ever use: the test does not need to know how the kernel sums. That is the property that makes it a good test rule and not just a good estimate.

For mixed precision, the inputs are first rounded to FP16 or BF16 (relative error at most uin each), the products of rounded inputs are exact in FP32 for these formats, and the sums are FP32. Measured against the FP64 product of the original FP32 inputs, the relative bound becomes (2uin + uin2)(1 + γK) + γK, the formula of F6-25 Listing 2, which that chapter checked against simulated dot products D7. Listing 1 implements exactly these two formulas; the suite's first lines print them (R1): for K = 4096, 2.44 × 10−4 for FP32 and 1.22 × 10−3 for FP16 inputs.

Three practical points. First, compare against FP64, not against another FP32 result: with an FP32 partner, both sides have error and the bound doubles (the factor 2 of the F6-21 harness when it compares with cuBLAS). Second, a near-1 ratio of error to bound is not a warning sign by itself: at K = 1 the bound is a single rounding and the suite reports 0.966 of it (R1), as the F6-21 run did. Third, a tolerance that is too loose hides bugs as surely as a missing shape: the seeded BF16-accumulator fault is caught only because the FP32-accumulation bound is tight at larger K (R1).

Targets: agreed first, never lowered quietly

The curriculum is explicit: "Targets are yours to set: each milestone says 'reaches X % of the reference'; write X in the plan before you start", and the agent "never lowers X after the fact without your explicit approval and a log entry" C1 §13.4; the MP3 card adds that the owner's approval is needed D1 §11.6. The curriculum also gives a starting point: "sensible first targets are often in the 50–80 % range for GEMM ... but your GPU decides" C1 §13.4. The reason for fixing targets early is the same as for fixing a gate's entry criteria at the previous gate (F12-29): a target chosen after the measurement is a description of the measurement, not a test of it.

A rule is easier to keep when breaking it is visible. Listing 3 prints a fingerprint of the agreement at R1; the reviewer writes it into the R1 gate record; Listing 4 prints the fingerprint of the targets it actually used on every report. A target can still change, openly: a gate, the owner's approval, a log entry, a new fingerprint recorded next to the old one with the reason. What cannot happen is a quiet change, which is the forensic lab of this handbook.

Choosing X. Base it on evidence you have before coding: the step table you reached in CU302 on the emulator (correctness only), any GPU measurements from CU302 labs, and the reference's own position on your roofline. A target that you will certainly meet teaches nothing; one far beyond the reference's own efficiency is a wish. Write the reasoning in the design note; the reviewers sign the reasoning as well as the number.

Gap analysis and the roofline chart

The roofline model bounds a kernel's attainable throughput by the smaller of the compute roof and arithmetic intensity × memory bandwidth D5; F6-33 built the model and placed kernels on it. For MP3 the chart has one point per kernel and size for yours and for the reference, the measured memory roof (your copy bandwidth from E2), and the compute roofs from your GPU's documentation, one for FP32 and one for the tensor-core path. Figure 3 shows the layout. Measure each point's intensity from the profiler's DRAM traffic, not from the algorithm's minimum, so that a kernel that re-reads data sits further left than the ideal.

The roofline says how far a kernel is from what the hardware allows; it does not say why. The why comes from the curriculum's list: memory throughput, issue stalls, occupancy, instruction mix C1 §13.4. For each kernel, compare yours with the reference on the same metrics in the same profiler (F6-33), read your register report and SASS (F6-34), and write the gap as a chain: "metric A differs by this much → because of this property of the code → shown by this SASS or report line → predicted effect of a change → measured effect". Volkov's point that lower occupancy can be faster when each thread has more independent work is often part of a GEMM gap explanation (F6-20) D9.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Profiler metric names, section names and command-line options are not given here: none of the NVIDIA profilers was available in this build. Use the names your profiler version shows and cite its documentation D4. Statements about how cuBLAS chooses among its kernels, or what its kernels do internally, may be made only from evidence you collect (profiler output, kernel names in a timeline) and must be phrased as observations on your GPU and version.

How the hardware actually does it

A GEMM is a sequence of multiply-adds whose operands must arrive from DRAM through the caches and shared memory into registers; your CU302 kernels raised the number of multiply-adds per byte moved at each level (tiling in shared memory, then in registers) and overlapped movement with compute (double buffering) D8. Tensor cores change the arithmetic part: one warp-wide matrix instruction does the work of many scalar multiply-adds, for specific shapes, layouts and number formats (F6-26, the hall's "tray machine") D2. Once arithmetic is that fast, operand delivery becomes the limit again, which is why the tensor-core roof in Figure 3 sits further right: a kernel needs higher arithmetic intensity to reach it.

Attention adds a softmax between two matrix products. The fused, tiled form never writes the N × N score matrix to DRAM, which is the whole point of the FlashAttention family of algorithms D6; your M4 kernel inherits that from F6-39, and its gap to the library will be about how well both products use the tensor cores and how the softmax work is hidden between them.

The vendor library is tuned for each GPU generation by people with access to its documentation and its designers. Reaching a fraction of it with your own code is a strong result, and the fraction depends on the GPU: the same source can land at different percentages on two GPUs (MP4 compares exactly this across vendors). That is why every number in your report carries its GPU, driver and toolkit, and why this handbook gives none: this build had no GPU (R8), and a number from another machine would not be evidence about yours.

Two machine effects matter for measurement. Clocks change with temperature and power limits, so two runs minutes apart can differ (lock or record, Layer 3). And the driver library on a machine may support a newer CUDA version than the toolkit you compiled with: Listing 5 prints both, and in this build the runtime reported 12000 and the driver call reported 13000 although no GPU was present (R8). The header documents the encoding (1000 × major + 10 × minor) and that a machine without a driver returns 0 H1; what driver library the container holds was not investigated, and the GPU count call failed as expected.

Worked example

Task. Leila prepares R1. Her reviewers ask three questions: are the targets complete and signed, are the tolerances justified, and does the arithmetic of the comparison make sense at 4096?

Step 1 — the targets file. She fills the template (Listing 3's input) from her device report and her plan. Her file is targets_example.txt; the machine lines read SYNTHETIC-TEST-DATA because this handbook has no GPU to describe, and in your file they hold the strings Listing 5 prints on your machine. Listing 3 accepts it: five targets, every required line present, two independent signatures, fingerprint bda53db755989690 (R4). Tomas writes the fingerprint into the R1 gate record (use the gate checker of F12-29 for the record itself).





Step 2 — tolerances. At K = 4096, γK = 4096 × 2−24 / (1 − 4096 × 2−24) = 2−12 / (1 − 2−12) ≈ 2.442 × 10−4. With FP16 inputs, uin = 2−11 ≈ 4.883 × 10−4, so 2uin + uin2 ≈ 9.768 × 10−4; times (1 + γK) plus γK gives ≈ 1.221 × 10−3. These are the values the suite prints for K = 4096 (2.44 × 10−4 and 1.22 × 10−3, R1). With inputs in [−1, 1], Σ|a·b| is at most K, so the absolute error of one FP32 element at K = 4096 is at most about 2.44 × 10−4 × 4096 ≈ 1.0.


Step 3 — the size of the comparison. One SGEMM at n = 4096 does 2n3 = 2 × 40963 = 137,438,953,472 floating-point operations. Its three matrices hold 3 × 40962 × 4 B = 201,326,592 B = 192 MiB. The minimum possible DRAM traffic is reading A and B and writing C once, so the best possible arithmetic intensity is 137,438,953,472 / 201,326,592 ≈ 682.7 FLOP/B (in general n/6 for FP32). Every real kernel moves more than this minimum; Figure 3 places it by its measured traffic.


Step 4 — reading a result. Later, Listing 4 reports (on the synthetic records) a median of 81.321 ms for Leila's kernel and 40.981 ms for the reference at 4096: 100 × 40.981 / 81.321 = 50.4 % of the reference, against a target of 60 %: NOT MET (R5). The best-of-20 column says 57.2 %, closer to the target, which is exactly why it may not be used for the verdict. The honest report says "50.4 %, target 60 %, not met; gap explained in section 5", and that report can still earn high marks for methodology and gap explanation.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The specialist station is not one recipe. The analogy suggests the library cooks one dish one way. A vendor library may choose among many kernels by size, type and GPU; your percentage can jump between two sizes because the reference changed method, not because yours changed. Treat each size as its own comparison and look at the reference in the profiler before explaining a jump (an observation on your GPU, never a claim about the library in general).
    • 

  • Tasting is not measuring. A taster's "close enough" is a feeling; a tolerance is a bound derived from the number formats, valid for any summation order. The analogy makes tolerance sound like a matter of taste; it is a calculation you can check (Layer 3).
    • 

  • The code under the scorecard proves nothing about who changed it. A fingerprint shows that two files differ, not who changed them or whether the change was approved; that lives in the gate record, the version history and the owner's approval.
    •

Risks and safety


Safety
Read the university's safety rules first. GPU work has no moving parts, but it has real risks to machines, data and money. Run benchmarks and profilers only on machines whose owner has designated them for it: the curriculum's runbook forbids GPU stress tests on machines the learner has not designated C1 §19.2. Changing clocks, power limits or profiler permissions changes the machine for everyone on it: do it only on a machine you own or with the machine owner's written permission, follow the owner's supervision rules (a learner under 18 does this only with the supervising adult), record the change, and restore the defaults afterwards. A kernel that hangs on the GPU that drives your display can freeze the screen (F6-36): test new kernels on the CPU emulator first and on a non-display GPU where you can. Watch temperatures with the vendor's tools and stop if they reach the limits the vendor documents. Cloud GPUs cost money by the hour: set a budget alert and shut instances down after each session.

Summary


    

  • MP3 builds a library of your own SGEMM, tensor-core HGEMM and fused attention kernels and compares them with the libraries of your platform.
    • 

  • Targets, references, tolerances and the protocol are agreed at R1, before coding, and locked by a fingerprint; they change only openly, with the owner's approval.
    • 

  • Correctness comes first: a shape matrix with a reason per shape, tolerances derived from MA302 that hold for any summation order, and seeded faults that the suite must catch.
    • 

  • Every number comes from raw records labelled with GPU, driver and toolkit; verdicts use the median of at least 20 event-timed runs after warm-up, with the reference measured by the same harness.
    • 

  • Every gap is explained with profiler evidence and every kernel appears on a roofline chart.
    • 

  • Gates R1, R2 and R4 are required; R0 and R3 are proposed; independent reviewers sign, the owner decides who.
