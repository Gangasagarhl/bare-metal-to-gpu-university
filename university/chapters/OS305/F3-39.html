Story hook

The school has one sports hall and three groups that need it. The first-aid team practises for a short time every 4 periods and must never be late. The basketball team needs a longer slot every 6 periods. The dance group needs the longest slot, every 12 periods. A rule is printed on the hall door: "a more urgent group may enter at any time; the group inside leaves at once and comes back when the hall is free". Everyone follows the rule, nobody negotiates, and the timetable that results can be worked out on paper before the term starts.

Ravi, the new PE teacher, thinks the rule is unfair: the dance group has the most to do, so surely it should be the most urgent. He changes the sign. That afternoon the first-aid team is late for eight of its twelve practices. In this chapter you build a small real-time kernel, the "rule on the door" and the machinery that enforces it, run three periodic tasks on the emulated microcontroller, predict their worst response times with a short formula, and compare the prediction with the measurement. Then you change the sign as Ravi did and watch the deadlines fall.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what an RTOS adds to bare metal: tasks with their own stacks, a scheduler, a tick, blocking and preemption.
  • Trace a context switch on Armv7-M: what the hardware saves, what the kernel saves, where the stack pointer is kept, and why PendSV has the lowest priority.
  • Assign priorities to periodic tasks by the rate-monotonic rule and compute utilisation and worst-case response times by response-time analysis (RTA).
  • Measure response times on the running system and explain the difference between the analysis and the measurement.
  • Diagnose a deadline miss caused by a priority assignment, from a timeline and a response-time table.
Jargon box
RTOS (real-time operating system)
Simple words: a small operating system whose main promise is "on time", not "fast on average". Analogy: the hall rule plus the person who enforces it. Precise: a kernel that provides tasks, a priority-based preemptive scheduler, timing services and synchronisation objects, designed so that the worst-case delay of each operation is bounded and can be analysed; on microcontrollers it is linked into one image with the application, usually without memory protection between tasks. D2 D3
Task (RTOS thread)
Simple words: one independent job of the firmware, with its own loop. Analogy: one group that needs the hall. Precise: a thread of execution with its own stack and saved registers, a priority and a state (ready, running, blocked or sleeping); RTOSes call it a task (FreeRTOS) or a thread (Zephyr). D3 D6
Task control block (TCB)
Simple words: the kernel's record card for one task. Analogy: the group's line on the hall booking sheet. Precise: the kernel data structure holding a task's saved stack pointer, priority, state and timing fields; in this course's kernel it is os::Task, whose first member is the saved stack pointer so that assembly code can reach it at offset 0. R1
Context switch
Simple words: putting one task aside and continuing another exactly where it stopped. Analogy: one group leaves the hall with a note of where they were in their practice; the next group picks up from its own note. Precise: saving the running task's registers (here partly by the exception hardware, partly by the kernel) on its stack, storing its stack pointer in its TCB, selecting the next task, and restoring that task's stack pointer and registers. D1 R1
PendSV
Simple words: an Armv7-M exception that software raises to say "switch tasks when nothing more urgent is running". Analogy: a note on the hall door: "change groups at the next break". Precise: the pendable service call, exception 14, made pending by setting ICSR bit 28; given the lowest priority, it runs only after every other handler has finished, which makes it the standard place for the context switch on Cortex-M. D1 D2
Fixed-priority preemptive scheduling
Simple words: the most urgent ready task always runs, and takes over at once when it becomes ready. Analogy: the rule on the hall door. Precise: a scheduling policy in which each task has a static priority, the scheduler always dispatches the highest-priority ready task, and a task becoming ready with a higher priority than the running one causes an immediate switch. D4
Rate-monotonic priority assignment
Simple words: the task that must run most often gets the highest priority. Analogy: the group that comes back most often goes first. Precise: for periodic tasks with deadlines equal to periods, assigning priorities in order of period (shorter period, higher priority); it is optimal among fixed-priority assignments for that task model. D4 D5
Response time and deadline
Simple words: how long a job takes from "it is due" to "it is done", and the latest it may be done. Analogy: from the bell that calls the group to the moment they finish; their deadline is the next bell. Precise: a job's response time is its completion time minus its release time; a job meets its deadline if its response time is at most its relative deadline (here equal to the period). The worst-case response time is the maximum over all jobs. D4
Response-time analysis (RTA)
Simple words: a formula that predicts each task's worst response time before you run anything. Analogy: working out on paper the latest any group could finish. Precise: for fixed-priority preemptive scheduling of independent periodic tasks, the worst-case response time Ri is the smallest solution of Ri = Ci + Σj more urgent ⌈Ri / Tj⌉ · Cj, found by iteration starting at Ri = Ci; C is the worst-case execution time, T the period. D4 R2
Utilisation
Simple words: the fraction of processor time the tasks need. Analogy: how much of the week the hall is booked. Precise: U = Σ Ci / Ti; U ≤ 1 is necessary for any schedule to work, and U ≤ n(21/n − 1) is a sufficient (not necessary) test for rate-monotonic scheduling of n tasks (the Liu and Layland bound). D4 D5

Related terms from earlier courses: thread and data race (SP203), interrupt latency (HW204).

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A microcontroller has one processor, but firmware often has several jobs: read a sensor often, steer a motor, save data now and then. An RTOS lets you write each job as its own little program, called a task, as if it had the processor to itself.

Underneath, the RTOS shares the processor by switching between tasks very quickly. Each task has a number that says how urgent it is. The rule is simple: the most urgent task that has work to do always runs. When a more urgent task wakes up, the running task is paused in the middle of what it is doing and continues later, exactly where it stopped.

"Real-time" does not mean "very fast". It means "on time, every time". With the rule and a little arithmetic, you can know before you start how late each job could possibly be.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From one loop to tasks

In F3-37, main was one loop that slept until an interrupt, then did whatever was due. That works until two jobs with different rhythms must both be on time: a long job in the loop delays every other job behind it, and splitting the long job into slices by hand turns the loop into a hand-written scheduler. An RTOS writes that scheduler once. Each job becomes a task with a plain loop ("do the work, then sleep until my next release"), and the kernel decides which task runs. D3 Bridge BR-05 summarises the step: tasks with priorities, a scheduler and kernel objects; deterministic timing; small memory; no memory isolation between tasks. C1

uRTOS: a kernel you can read in one sitting

This course writes its own teaching kernel, uRTOS, so that nothing is hidden. Listing 1 is its interface: create a task with a priority and a stack, start the scheduler with a tick period, ask the time, and sleep until a given tick. Listing 2 is the whole kernel in about 200 lines. Its parts:

  • Task table. Up to six Task records (TCBs), each with a saved stack pointer, a priority, a state (Ready or Sleeping), a wake-up tick and the time of its latest release.
  • Tick. SysTick interrupts every 25,000 processor clocks. Its handler counts ticks, wakes every sleeping task whose wake-up tick has come, and, if a woken task is more urgent than the running one, requests a switch.
  • Scheduler. os_selectNext picks the highest-priority Ready task and logs the switch with a timestamp. An idle task, priority 0, is always Ready, so there is always something to run.
  • Context switch. The PendSV handler saves and restores registers (Figure 2). It is requested from the tick handler (preemption) and from sleepUntil (the running task blocks).

Two of the three measurements match the prediction to the tenth of a tick. The third does not: the filter measured 8.8, RTA says 8.0. That gap is the most instructive number in this chapter (Layer 3).



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


Why 8.8 and not 8.0: analysis assumes zero overhead


RTA's last iteration for the filter is R = 2.8 + ⌈8.0/4⌉ · 0.8 + ⌈8.0/6⌉ · 1.8 = 2.8 + 2 · 0.8 + 2 · 1.8 = 8.0. The term ⌈8.0/4⌉ = 2 counts the sensor jobs released at 0 and 4; the sensor's release at exactly 8.0 is not counted, because the filter is assumed to finish at 8.0 exactly. On the emulated chip, the filter's job also pays for every context switch, every tick handler and the time-stamping code. Those few hundred processor clocks push its completion slightly past 8.0, the sensor job released at 8.0 now preempts it, and a whole sensor job (0.8 ticks) is added: ⌈8.0+/4⌉ = 3, R = 2.8 + 3 · 0.8 + 2 · 1.8 = 8.8, which is the measured value. R1 R2


The lesson generalises. Response time is not continuous in the execution time: when a job ends near a more urgent task's release, a tiny overrun costs a full preemption. Real analyses therefore (1) use worst-case execution times measured or bounded with margin, (2) add the kernel's own costs: the tick handler as a high-priority periodic "task", the context-switch time charged twice per preemption, and the longest time interrupts are disabled as a blocking term; and (3) check the margin to each deadline, not only "met / missed". D4 Here the margin was large (8.8 against 12), so the overhead changed the response time but not the verdict.



The context switch in detail


On exception entry the core pushes eight registers onto the current stack (Figure 2). Tasks run in Thread mode on the process stack pointer (PSP), while handlers run on the main stack pointer (MSP), so that hardware frame lands on the task's own stack. D1 PendSV (Listing 2, lines 154–173) then pushes r4–r11 onto the same stack, stores the result in currentTask->sp, calls os_selectNext, loads the new task's saved stack pointer, pops its r4–r11, sets PSP, and returns. The return pops the new task's hardware frame, including its PC: the new task continues exactly where it stopped. A new task has never stopped, so createTask (lines 75–99) builds a fake frame that looks as if it had: xPSR with the Thumb bit set, PC = the task function, LR = a function that reports a task returning, zeros for the rest. The very first task is started from SVC_Handler with EXC_RETURN 0xFFFFFFFD, "return to Thread mode, use PSP". D1 D2


Why PendSV and not a direct switch in the tick handler? Because the tick handler may itself have interrupted another handler; switching tasks there would return into the wrong context. PendSV at the lowest priority (line 196 sets 0xFF) is taken only when no other handler is active, so the switch always happens between task code, never inside a handler. SysTick at 0x80 is more urgent than PendSV and less urgent than the device interrupts left at 0. D1 D2



Time stamps you can trust


os::now() combines the tick count with SysTick's current-value register. Getting that right took three attempts in this build, and each failure is a classic. First, SysTick counts down and raises its interrupt when it reaches 0, so the part of the current tick already elapsed is reload + 1 − CVR, except that CVR = 0 means "exactly at the boundary". Second, if now() runs with interrupts disabled right after the counter wrapped, the tick handler has not yet counted the new tick; the pending bit PENDSTSET in ICSR shows that, and the code adds the missing tick. Third, a time base must come from one counter: an early version mixed a peripheral timer with SysTick and measured response times that drifted apart. R1 Each mistake produced plausible numbers that were wrong by exactly one tick; plausible-but-wrong is the normal failure mode of timing code.



What the emulator's timing means


With -icount shift=0, QEMU advances virtual time by one nanosecond per instruction, whatever the instruction, and its SysTick counts virtual time. D7 The calibration line shows the consequence: 20,000 loop iterations took 3,000 "processor clocks", far fewer than a real core would need. Every time in this chapter is therefore in "emulated ticks", repeatable and internally consistent, but not the duration on any real chip; caches, flash wait states, bus contention and interrupt latency are absent. The method (calibrate, measure response times from release to completion, compare with RTA) transfers to hardware; the numbers do not.



What uRTOS leaves out


uRTOS has no blocking objects other than sleep (they arrive in F3-40), no time slicing among equal priorities, no stack-overflow checks, no floating-point context (the Cortex-M3 has no FPU; on Cortex-M4F the switch must also handle the FPU registers), and no tickless idle. Production kernels such as Zephyr and FreeRTOS have all of these, plus years of testing. D6 uRTOS exists so that you can see every mechanism once; F3-40 maps each of its pieces to the corresponding Zephyr and FreeRTOS concepts.

How the hardware actually does it


A Cortex-M core has two stack pointers in hardware, MSP and PSP, and the CONTROL register selects which one Thread mode uses; Handler mode always uses MSP. Exception entry and return are performed by the core's microcode-like exception logic: it writes the eight-word frame to memory, fetches the vector, and on return recognises the EXC_RETURN pattern in the PC and reverses the process. D1 The NVIC arbitrates priorities in hardware, which is why PendSV's lowest priority is enough to guarantee "after all other handlers". SysTick is a 24-bit down-counter in the core's private peripheral area, clocked by the processor clock (or an implementation-defined reference clock); its interrupt is the RTOS tick. D1 D2 On a real chip a context switch costs a number of cycles set by the exception entry and exit sequences plus the kernel's own instructions; measure it with a cycle counter or a GPIO pin and an oscilloscope, never assume it.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A group leaving the hall takes a moment; a preempted task is frozen between two instructions. It does not get to finish its sentence, which is why shared data needs protection (F3-40).
    • 

  • The school's timetable is planned once; the scheduler decides at every tick and every block. Fixed priorities make the decisions predictable, but they are still made at run time. (Guide 8: "timetables are fixed in advance; schedulers decide continuously".)
    • 

  • The hall rule says nothing about cost; switching tasks costs time. The 0.8-tick difference for the filter came from that cost (Layer 3). R1

Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: HardFault at the first switch. Cause: wrong fake frame (Thumb bit missing in xPSR, PC with bit 0 set, stack not 8-byte aligned) or wrong EXC_RETURN. Check: stop in SVC_Handler and read the new task's stack in the debugger.
    • 

  • Symptom: random corruption in one task's variables. Cause: another task's stack overflowed into it. Fix: paint stacks and check the high-water marks (F3-36 lab step 4), or use the MPU.
    • 

  • Symptom: a periodic task drifts later every period. Cause: relative sleep ("sleep 4 ticks after finishing") instead of absolute releases. Fix: release += period; sleepUntil(release).
    • 

  • Symptom: a low-priority task never runs. Cause: a higher-priority task that never blocks (busy-waits). Fix: every task except idle must block or sleep.
    • 

  • Symptom: measured response times are off by exactly one tick. Cause: the time base (Layer 3). Check: compare now() a few clocks before and after a tick boundary.
    • 

  • Symptom: deadlines missed although U < 1. Cause: priorities not in rate-monotonic order (forensic lab), or blocking (F3-40).
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: bare metal (F3-36 to F3-38): one loop, interrupt handlers, and you owned every instruction. In SP203 you met threads on a PC, and in F3-03 a general-purpose scheduler. What carries over (bridge BR-05): interrupts, timers, drivers, memory maps, the debugger, and above all the habit of measuring timing. Threads and data races are the same ideas as in SP203. What changes: several stacks instead of one; a scheduler with fixed priorities instead of your loop; time as a first-class quantity with deadlines you can analyse; a kernel linked into your image, with no memory protection between tasks; and no fairness: a higher-priority task that never sleeps starves everyone below it. What comes next: F3-40 adds the kernel objects tasks use to cooperate (mutexes, queues), shows priority inversion happening, and maps uRTOS to Zephyr and FreeRTOS. Later in the bridge, Linux replaces the RTOS when you need processes, virtual memory and networking, at the cost of footprint and, without PREEMPT_RT, of real-time guarantees.

Summary


    

  • An RTOS turns the bare-metal loop into tasks with their own stacks, priorities and states, plus a tick, a scheduler and a context switch.
    • 

  • On Cortex-M, the hardware stacks eight registers, PendSV at the lowest priority saves the other eight and switches PSP, and SVC starts the first task.
    • 

  • Fixed-priority preemptive scheduling: the highest-priority ready task runs; rate-monotonic assignment gives shorter periods higher priority.
    • 

  • Utilisation 0.733 is below the three-task bound 0.780; RTA predicts 0.8, 2.6 and 8.0 ticks.
    • 

  • Measured: 0.8, 2.6 and 8.8 ticks, timeline identical to the ideal one; the 0.8 extra is a whole preemption caused by a small overhead at a release boundary.
    • 

  • Swapping the priorities (filter first) made the sensor miss 8 of 12 deadlines at the same utilisation.
    • 

  • Emulated time is repeatable but is not real-chip time; the method transfers, the numbers do not.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Gives: independent tasks with their own stacks, priorities and preemption; time services (sleep until a tick); a scheduler that you do not write per application; analysable worst-case timing. Takes away: some RAM (a stack per task) and processor time (ticks, switches), and the certainty that code runs only when your loop calls it.
    2. 

  2. Hardware: r0–r3, r12, LR, PC, xPSR. PendSV: r4–r11. The hardware saves exactly the registers a C/C++ function may clobber, so the interrupted code's view is preserved for any handler; the kernel adds the rest because it is about to run a different task, whose code would overwrite them.
    3. 

  3. The SysTick handler might have interrupted another handler (for example a UART handler); switching tasks there would return into a task instead of finishing that handler. PendSV at the lowest priority runs only when all other handlers are done, so the switch always happens between task code.
    4. 

  4. U = 0.2 + 0.3 + 0.2333 = 0.7333 ≤ 0.7798: schedulable under rate-monotonic priorities by the sufficient test.
    5. 

  5. Task 1: R = 1. Task 2: R = 2 + ⌈2/5⌉·1 = 3; 2 + ⌈3/5⌉·1 = 3: R = 3. Task 3: 6 → 6 + ⌈6/5⌉·1 + ⌈6/8⌉·2 = 6 + 2 + 2 = 10 → 6 + ⌈10/5⌉·1 + ⌈10/8⌉·2 = 6 + 2 + 4 = 12 → 6 + ⌈12/5⌉·1 + ⌈12/8⌉·2 = 6 + 3 + 4 = 13 → 6 + 3 + 4 = 13: R = 13 ≤ 20. All met. (U = 0.2 + 0.25 + 0.3 = 0.75.)
    6. 

  6. The filter's ideal completion fell exactly on the sensor's release at 8.0; the kernel's overhead made it finish a little later, so the sensor preempted it and its whole 0.8-tick job was added. The sensor is the most urgent task; nothing preempts it, so its own overhead adds only about 0.1 to its response time, not a whole job.
    7. 

  7. RTA: R = 3.6 → 3.6 + 0.8 + 1.8 = 6.2 → 3.6 + 2·0.8 + 2·1.8 = 8.8 → 3.6 + 3·0.8 + 2·1.8 = 9.6 → 3.6 + 3·0.8 + 2·1.8 = 9.6: R = 9.6 ≤ 12 (U = 0.8, above the bound, yet schedulable). The run shows your measured value; write it down and compare.
    8. 

  8. Each release is the previous completion plus 4, so periods stretch by the response time and the task drifts later; the task also runs less often than every 4 ticks. Listing 3 uses absolute releases (release += period).
    9. 



Forensic lab answer key


Observation. At tick 0 the filter runs for 2.8 ticks, then control for 1.8, then the sensor from 4.6 (the timeline's first A is in the third quarter of tick 4) to 5.4: its first job, released at 0 with deadline 4, is 1.4 ticks late. Sensor: 12 jobs, 8 misses, worst 5.4 ticks. Control 4.6 and filter 2.8 meet theirs.


Hypotheses. (a) Overload: no, U is unchanged at 0.733. (b) Kernel bug: no, the second section of rta.out predicts exactly 5.4 for the sensor with these priorities, and the ideal timeline matches the measured one. (c) Wrong order: yes.


Root cause. With the sensor least urgent, RTA gives R = 0.8 + ⌈R/6⌉·1.8 + ⌈R/12⌉·2.8: 0.8 → 0.8 + 1.8 + 2.8 = 5.4 → 0.8 + ⌈5.4/6⌉·1.8 + ⌈5.4/12⌉·2.8 = 5.4. R = 5.4 > 4: missed, matching the measurement. Priority must follow urgency of the deadline (here, the period), not the amount of work.


Reconciling the field report. The original firmware's real execution times may exceed the lab's C values (data-dependent work, interrupts, flash wait states), or "late" may be measured against a different deadline than the period (for example, end-to-end from sensor to output). Ask for measured execution-time distributions on the device and the definition of "late".


Fix and prevention. Restore rate-monotonic priorities (sensor 3, control 2, filter 1). Prevention: keep the RTA program in CI with the task table as its single source of truth, and fail the build if any predicted R exceeds its deadline minus a stated margin.
