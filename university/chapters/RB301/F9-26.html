Story hook

Analogy

Sami wants the teapot on the far side of the table. He reaches with his elbow down, close to his side. His sister Lea reaches for the same teapot with her elbow up and out, like a wing. Both hands end on the same handle; the shoulders and elbows are bent differently. Then Lea tries to reach the window latch behind the table. Her fingertips stop a few centimetres short, and no bending of shoulder or elbow will help. She has to stand up and step closer.

That is inverse kinematics: given where the hand must go, find the joint angles. Unlike forward kinematics (F9-25), the answer is not always unique (elbow up or elbow down), and sometimes there is no answer at all. A robot that does not handle both facts will either jump between postures in the middle of a motion or, as this chapter's forensic lab shows, freeze when asked to reach exactly as far as it can.

What you will be able to do

  • Derive the analytic inverse kinematics of a planar two-link arm with the law of cosines and atan2, including both elbow branches.
  • Determine whether a target is reachable from the workspace limits |L1 − L2| ≤ d ≤ L1 + L2, and handle the boundary safely in floating point.
  • Solve the course arm's inverse kinematics analytically (yaw, then a planar 2R) and numerically (damped least squares), and compare the two.
  • Verify every IK answer with a forward-kinematics round trip.
  • Diagnose an IK function that returns NaN or refuses targets exactly at full reach.
Jargon box
Inverse kinematics (arm)
Simple words: from where the tool must be to the joint angles that put it there. Analogy: deciding how to bend shoulder and elbow to touch the teapot. Precise: finding q with T(q) = Ttarget (or with the tool position equal to a target point); there may be zero, finitely many, or infinitely many solutions. B1
Workspace (reachable)
Simple words: all the places the tool can reach. Analogy: everything you can touch without stepping. Precise: the set of tool positions (or poses) T(q) over all allowed q; for a planar 2R arm without joint limits, the annulus |L1 − L2| ≤ d ≤ L1 + L2 around the shoulder. B1D1
IK branch (elbow-up / elbow-down)
Simple words: one of the different postures that reach the same target. Analogy: Sami's elbow down, Lea's elbow up. Precise: one of the discrete solution families of an IK problem; for the planar 2R arm, the sign of sin θ2. Which one is called "up" depends on the arm and the target; this chapter defines it in Layer 2. D1
atan2
Simple words: an angle finder that knows which quarter of the circle you are in. Analogy: a compass that tells north-east from south-west. Precise: atan2(y, x) returns the angle of the vector (x, y) in (−π, π], using the signs of both arguments; atan(y/x) loses the quadrant and fails at x = 0. S1D1
Numerical IK (Newton–Raphson, damped least squares)
Simple words: guess the angles, see how far off the tool is, correct the guess, repeat. Analogy: reaching in the dark: move, feel how far you are from the handle, adjust. Precise: iterate q ← q + Δq with J(q)Δq ≈ e (e = target − FK(q)); damped least squares solves (JTJ + λ2I)Δq = JTe, which stays well-behaved when J is nearly singular. B1R2
Redundant arm
Simple words: an arm with more joints than the task needs. Analogy: your arm can keep your hand on the teapot while your elbow swings up and down. Precise: an arm whose number of joints exceeds the dimension of the task (for example three planar joints for a 2D position); its IK solutions form a continuous family. B1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows one target reached by a planar 2R arm in its two postures, and the region the arm can reach at all. The triangle shoulder–elbow–target has sides L1, L2 and d; for a given d the triangle's shape is fixed, but it can be flipped about the line from shoulder to target. That flip is the second solution.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Forward kinematics is a walk: from the shoulder, follow the upper arm, then the forearm, and see where you land. Inverse kinematics asks the opposite: "I want to land on the teapot; how should I bend?"

For an arm with two parts, there is a neat trick. The shoulder, the elbow and the teapot make a triangle. You know all three sides: the upper arm, the forearm, and the straight distance from shoulder to teapot. A triangle with three known sides has one shape, so the elbow's bend is fixed. But the triangle can point its elbow corner up or down, so there are two ways to reach.

If the teapot is farther than both arm parts laid end to end, no triangle exists: out of reach. If the teapot is exactly at that distance, the triangle is squashed flat and the arm is fully stretched. That flat case looks harmless, but computers round their numbers, and a tiny rounding can make "exactly at full reach" look like "a hair beyond". The forensic lab is about that hair.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Planar 2R, step by step

Target (x, y), link lengths L1, L2, d2 = x2 + y2. From the forward kinematics of F9-25, d2 = L12 + L22 + 2L1L2 cos θ2 (expand the squares and use cos2 + sin2 = 1). So D1B1

c2 = (d² − L1² − L2²) / (2 L1 L2) s2 = ±√(1 − c2²) θ2 = atan2(s2, c2)

θ1 = atan2(y, x) − atan2(L2·s2, L1 + L2·c2)

The first atan2 is the direction to the target; the second is the angle at the shoulder between the target direction and link 1. The ± is the branch. In this course, "elbow-up" means s2 ≤ 0 (θ2 ≤ 0): for a target in front of the arm, subtracting a negative angle raises link 1 above the shoulder–target line, so the elbow is above it. D1

Reachability is |c2| ≤ 1, which is the same as |L1 − L2| ≤ d ≤ L1 + L2. Run R1 shows both branches for several targets, the outstretched case (0.55, 0) where both branches coincide, the folded case (0.05, 0) with θ2 = ±180°, and two targets out of reach; every answer passes the FK round trip to within 10−16 m. R1

Why atan2 and not acos

θ2 = acos(c2) gives only the positive branch and needs |c2| ≤ 1 exactly. Computing s2 explicitly and using atan2(s2, c2) chooses the branch by a sign and keeps the full quadrant information. For θ1, atan2(y, x) works in all four quadrants, including x = 0, where atan(y/x) divides by zero. S1D1

The course arm

The course arm of F9-25 decouples: joint 1 must point the arm's vertical plane at the target, q1 = atan2(y, x); then joints 2 and 3 solve a planar 2R problem in that plane with horizontal coordinate ρ = √(x2 + y2), vertical coordinate z − 0.10, and L1 = L2 = 0.30 m. A second family uses q1 + 180° with horizontal coordinate −ρ (reaching backwards over the top); it is usually excluded by the shoulder's joint limits. So the course arm has up to four position solutions, and joint limits decide which are allowed. D1B1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Numerical IK. Analytic solutions exist for many industrial arm designs (in particular six-joint arms whose last three axes meet in a point, a "spherical wrist", where position and orientation can be solved separately) but not for every arm. The general method is iterative. Linearise forward kinematics around the current guess: FK(q + Δq) ≈ FK(q) + J(q)Δq, where J is the matrix of partial derivatives (F9-27). Choose Δq so that J Δq ≈ e = target − FK(q), update, repeat. Pure Newton–Raphson solves J Δq = e; it converges very fast near a solution but takes huge steps where J is nearly singular. Damped least squares solves (JTJ + λ2I)Δq = JTe instead: λ trades a little accuracy per step for bounded steps. A step limit protects against the linear model being trusted too far from the current guess. B1R2

What run R2 shows. Starting from (0°, 17.2°, 17.2°) towards the target (0.25, 0.30, 0.35), the first steps are clipped at 0.2 rad, then the error falls from 4.7 × 10−5 to 1.5 × 10−7, 5.3 × 10−10, 1.9 × 10−12 and 6.6 × 10−15 m: a reduction by a roughly constant factor of a few hundred per step. That is linear convergence, the signature of the damping (pure Newton would square the error each step). The solver lands on (50.194°, −6.767°, 78.788°), which equals the analytic elbow-down solution to the printed digits; the elbow-up solution (50.194°, 72.021°, −78.788°) is equally valid. Which one an iterative solver finds depends on the starting guess. R2

Branch consistency. Along a trajectory, a robot must stay on one branch. An analytic solver should be told the branch; an iterative solver should start from the previous solution. A solver that picks "the nearest solution to zero" at every step can switch branches mid-motion, which commands a large, fast joint swing for a small tool motion. D1

Tolerance and the boundary. Floating-point arithmetic (MA302, F0-73) does not compute d2 − L12 − L22 exactly. For a target exactly at full reach, c2 comes out as 1 or as 1 + a few units in the last place, depending on the target's angle: checks.cpp prints 1.0000000000000004 and 1.0000000000000011 for some angles and exactly 1 for others. A robust solver accepts |c2| ≤ 1 + tol and then clamps c2 into [−1, 1] before any square root or acos (Listing 1, lines 28–30). The tolerance must be stated and tested, and it is a choice: 10−9 in c2 accepts targets about 10−9 × L1L2/d beyond the true reach, far below any real arm's accuracy. R3D1

Redundancy and orientation. A planar 3R arm reaching a 2D point has one spare degree of freedom: infinitely many solutions. Numerical IK picks one depending on the start; a secondary goal (stay near the middle of the joint ranges, avoid an obstacle) can be added in the null space of J (F9-27). Adding the tool orientation to the target removes the redundancy for a planar 3R arm and turns the problem into "solve the wrist position by 2R, then the last joint by subtraction of angles". B1

How the hardware actually does it

IK output is a set of joint targets that a joint controller (RB202 PID loops, RB304's ros2_control) must reach. Three physical facts bound the useful answers: joint limits (mechanical stops and cable routing), joint speed limits (a branch switch can demand a swing faster than the motor can do), and accuracy (encoder resolution, backlash and link flex, F9-25). The computing cost is small for analytic solvers and depends on iteration count for numerical ones; this chapter does not quote times, because none were measured on a robot computer. B1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. The link lengths and the absence of joint limits in the 2R examples belong to the university's simulated arm. A real arm's reachable workspace is smaller than the annulus because of joint limits and self-collision; take the limits from the manufacturer's documentation and your own measurements, recorded in the dossier.

Worked example

Planar 2R, L1 = 0.30 m, L2 = 0.25 m, target (0.40, 0.20).

  1. d2 = 0.16 + 0.04 = 0.20; L12 + L22 = 0.09 + 0.0625 = 0.1525; 2L1L2 = 0.15.
  2. c2 = (0.20 − 0.1525)/0.15 = 0.316667. Reachable, since |c2| ≤ 1.
  3. |s2| = √(1 − 0.3166672) = 0.948537; θ2 = ±atan2(0.948537, 0.316667) = ±71.539°.
  4. Direction to target: atan2(0.20, 0.40) = 26.565°. Shoulder correction for s2 > 0: atan2(0.25 × 0.948537, 0.30 + 0.25 × 0.316667) = 32.022°.
  5. Elbow-down: θ1 = 26.565° − 32.022° = −5.457°, θ2 = +71.539°. Elbow-up: θ1 = 26.565° + 32.022° = 58.587°, θ2 = −71.539°.
  6. Round trip (elbow-down): 0.30(cos(−5.457°), sin(−5.457°)) + 0.25(cos 66.082°, sin 66.082°) = (0.2986 + 0.1014, −0.0285 + 0.2285) = (0.40, 0.20).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Sami does not choose "elbow down" by a sign; the robot does. People pick a posture by comfort and habit and switch smoothly; a robot's branch is a bit in the code, and switching it mid-motion is a jump. D1
    • 

  • Lea knows when she is at full stretch; the computer may not. A body feels the end of its reach; floating-point arithmetic can put an exactly-reachable target a hair outside, with NaN as the result if nobody clamps (forensic lab, run R4). R4
    • 

  • People lean and step; a fixed-base arm cannot. The robot's workspace is a hard boundary unless the base moves too (a mobile manipulator, which combines F9-24 and this chapter). B1
    •

Goal


Build a tested IK module for the course arm: analytic with explicit branch and limits, numerical as a fallback, both verified by forward-kinematics round trips over the workspace, including its boundary.


Hardware and software needed


A computer with a C++ compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 with the flags of the build command. Optional: the course kit's arm for step 6 (untested on hardware).


Safety


Safety

Steps 1–5 are software only. Step 6 moves a real arm: first replay the planned joint targets in your simulator and check that no joint jumps; then run on the arm at the slowest speed, hands outside the reach, emergency stop in hand, learners below L3 with adult supervision. Expected observations


All round trips pass at the 10−15–10−16 level. Without clamping, a fraction of boundary targets yields NaN; with it, none do. The "smallest |q2|" rule switches branches where the line crosses θ2 = 0 and produces a jump.


Verification


The test program exits with code 0 only if: zero NaN results; every solution within limits and within 10−12 m of its target; every reachable target has at least one solution; every unreachable target (beyond the outer or inside the inner sphere) has none.

  • Round trip fails for targets behind the base → the q1 + 180° family uses +ρ instead of −ρ.

  • Numerical solver stalls → λ too large for the final accuracy; reduce λ near convergence or switch to Newton for the last steps.
