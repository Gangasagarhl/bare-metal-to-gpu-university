Story hook

Analogy

Noor sits at the kitchen table and reaches for her cup without thinking. Her eyes see the cup "a little ahead and down". Her hand does not care about that, because her hand starts at her shoulder, not at her eyes. Somewhere in her head, the picture from the eyes is turned into a reach from the shoulder. To do that, her brain uses something it always knows: where her head is on her neck, and where her neck is on her body. If she tilts her head, the cup jumps in the picture, yet her hand still goes to the same place.

A robot has the same problem with no instinct to help. Its camera reports the cup in the camera's own coordinates. Its arm needs the cup in the shoulder's coordinates. Its map needs the cup in the room's coordinates. Between them sit a camera bracket, a body and a floor, each a rotation plus a shift. If a single one of those links is typed backwards, the arm reaches with full confidence to a place where there is no cup. This chapter is about keeping all those links straight.

What you will be able to do

  • Name the frames of a mobile manipulator (world, base, camera, arm shoulder) and draw them as a frame tree with one parent per frame.
  • Compute a point's coordinates in any frame of the tree by chaining and inverting rigid transforms, checking each product with the matching-letters rule TAC = TABTBC.
  • Write and test a small C++ frame tree with lookups between any two frames, round-trip tests and separate handling of points and directions.
  • Explain why transforms carry time stamps and compute the error of using a stale transform on a turning robot.
  • Diagnose "the arm misses the cup" from a frame-tree dump and a pick log: one edge stored in the wrong direction.
  • State exactly which tf2 (ROS 2) facts this chapter could not verify, and where to check them.
Jargon box
Pose (3D)
Simple words: where something is and which way it faces, in space. Analogy: where your head is and where your face points. Precise: the position and orientation of a frame B relative to a frame A, written as the rigid transform TAB = [RAB tAB; 0 1] with a 3 × 3 rotation and a 3-vector; six independent numbers in all. D1B1
Rigid transform (SE(3))
Simple words: a turn plus a shift that never stretches or mirrors anything. Analogy: moving your whole hand without bending it. Precise: a map p ↦ Rp + t with R a rotation (RTR = I, det R = +1); the set of these maps with composition is the special Euclidean group SE(3). Products and inverses stay in the set. B1D1
Frame tree (transform tree)
Simple words: a family tree of all the frames on a robot, each one attached to exactly one parent. Analogy: eyes on head, head on neck, neck on body, body in the room. Precise: a rooted tree whose nodes are frames and whose edges store Tparent,child; the transform between any two frames is the product along the unique path between them, with edges walked towards the root inverted. MA201 introduced it (F0-54); here it becomes code. D1S1
Static and dynamic transform
Simple words: a link that never changes (a bolted bracket) versus one that changes all the time (the robot's place in the room, a joint). Analogy: your eye in your head never moves relative to the skull; your head on your neck turns. Precise: a static edge is valid for all time; a dynamic edge is a time series of stamped transforms, and a lookup must say for which time it wants the answer. S1R2
Extrinsic calibration
Simple words: measuring exactly where a sensor is bolted on and which way it points. Analogy: knowing exactly where your eyes are on your face. Precise: estimating the static transform between a sensor's frame and the robot's base frame, from measurements, as opposed to the sensor's internal (intrinsic) parameters. B1D1
tf2
Simple words: the ROS 2 library that keeps the robot's frame tree and answers "where is this frame relative to that one, at this time?". Analogy: the part of your brain that always knows where your head is on your body. Precise: the transform library of ROS 2; its exact API and conventions are not verified in this build (unverified box in Layer 3). S1

Figure 1 shows the robot used all through this chapter: a wheeled base standing in the room, a camera on a post looking down at the table, and an arm whose shoulder is mounted on the base. On the left the same robot is drawn as a frame tree. Each arrow points from child to parent and carries the transform that turns child coordinates into parent coordinates.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every part of a robot has its own "here" and its own "forward". For the camera, "forward" is the way the lens points. For the base, "forward" is the way the wheels roll. For the room, "forward" might be towards the window. A frame is one of these private points of view: an origin and three axes.

When the camera says "cup 0.8 m ahead of me", that sentence is only true in the camera's frame. To use it, the robot must translate the sentence into the shoulder's language: "cup 0.79 m ahead of me and 0.1 m below me". The dictionary between two neighbouring frames is a transform: a turn plus a shift. You learned to build it in MA201 (F0-54).

A robot keeps all its dictionaries in a family tree. Every frame has exactly one parent. To translate between any two frames, you walk up the tree from one and down to the other, using each dictionary on the way. The tree never needs a dictionary for every pair; one per family link is enough.

Some links never change, like the camera bracket. Some change all the time, like where the base stands in the room. For the changing ones, the robot must also remember when each value was true, because a picture taken a moment ago belongs to where the robot was a moment ago.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

One convention, used everywhere

This course keeps MA201's convention: TAB maps coordinates in frame B to coordinates in frame A, pA = TAB pB = RABpB + tAB. Read in the other direction, TAB is also "the pose of frame B, seen from frame A": its translation tAB is where B's origin is in A, and the columns of RAB are B's axes written in A's coordinates. "Modern Robotics" uses the same subscript order (Tsb for the body frame b seen from the fixed frame s). B1D1

Two rules follow by algebra. Chaining: TAC = TABTBC, inner letters matching. Inverting: TBA = TAB−1 = [RT, −RTt; 0 1]. A direction (an axis, a velocity) transforms with R alone, because a direction has no position to shift. D1

Building edges from simple turns

Each edge of Figure 1 is built from elementary rotations about x, y and z (F0-52) plus a translation. The camera edge is "shift (0.2, 0, 0.6), then pitch about the base's y axis by +30°". With x forward, y to the left and z up, a positive rotation about y tips the x axis downwards: Ry(30°)(1, 0, 0) = (cos 30°, 0, −sin 30°) = (0.8660, 0, −0.5). Run R1 prints the camera's x axis seen from the world as (0, 0.8660, −0.5): the same tilt, turned by the base's 90° heading. D1R1

Watch out

"Pitch down by 30°" is a sentence; Ry(+30°) is a matrix. Whether the sentence means +30° or −30° depends on which way y points. Check every hand-built rotation by transforming an axis and looking at the signs, as above, before trusting it.

Lookups in a tree

Store, for each non-root frame, its parent and Tparent,child. Then Troot,F is the product of the edges from the root down to F, and for any two frames D1

T_target,source = (T_root,target)⁻¹ · T_root,source

Listing 1 implements exactly this. Because each frame has one parent, there is exactly one path between two frames, so the answer cannot depend on which way you walk. A graph with a loop (two different chains from camera to base) could give two different answers; a tree rules that out by construction. D1S1

Time

The dynamic edge TWB changes as the robot drives. A camera reading taken at time tm must be converted with TWB(tm), not with whatever value is newest when the program gets round to it. Listing 3 shows the size of the error: a base turning at 0.5 rad/s, a pose 0.05 s too new, an object 2 m away, and the object lands 0.0500 m from its true place; interpolating between the stored samples removes the error (exactly, here, because the simulated turn rate is constant). R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The group behind the code. Rigid transforms form the group SE(3): closed under composition and inversion, with the identity as neutral element. The useful engineering consequence is that every product of edges is again a valid pose, so one data type covers edges, lookups and results. The danger is numerical: after many products R drifts away from orthonormality. A frame tree avoids long products by recomputing each lookup from the stored edges, rather than accumulating, so drift stays at the level of a few products; MA201 F0-54 and F0-55 show how to re-orthonormalise when you must accumulate. B1D1

Common ancestor versus root. Listing 1 goes through the root for every lookup. A production library walks only to the lowest common ancestor of the two frames. Two reasons: fewer multiplications, and accuracy. If the world origin is far away (map coordinates of hundreds of kilometres), going through the root adds and then subtracts large translations, and the rounding of doubles (MA302, F0-73) eats into a camera-to-shoulder answer that never needed the world at all. D1

Time and interpolation. A stamped edge is a sequence (tk, Tk). A lookup at time t between samples interpolates: translation linearly, rotation along the shortest arc between the two orientations (spherical linear interpolation of unit quaternions, F0-55). Listing 3 interpolates a single yaw angle linearly, which is the same thing for a rotation about one fixed axis. Asking for a time after the newest sample is extrapolation; a careful library refuses rather than guessing, and the caller either waits or uses the sample time explicitly. The lookup over several dynamic edges must use the same time for all of them, or a moving robot will be assembled from parts that were never in those places together. B1R2

Error propagation. A rotation error δθ in an edge moves a point at distance d from that edge's origin by about d·δθ (for small angles, a chord of length 2d·sin(δθ/2)). That is why the camera bracket's angle matters more than its position for far-away objects: 1° of bracket error at 2 m is about 0.035 m at the target, while a 1 mm position error stays 1 mm. Run R3 recomputes the stale-pose example with this chord formula: 2 × 2 × sin(0.0125) = 0.0500 m. D1R3

Not verified — check before relying on this

tf2 in ROS 2: what this build could not check. The course card asks you to use tf2. This build had no ROS 2 installation and could not open the ROS 2 documentation, so none of the following was verified; each item is the author's recollection and is listed so that you know exactly what to look up (ROS 2 documentation, the tf2 concept pages and tutorials, for the ROS 2 distribution recorded in your dossier):

  • that tf2 keeps a time-stamped buffer of transforms received as messages on two topics, one for dynamic and one for static ("latched") transforms, and that a broadcaster and a listener object exist for each side;
  • the argument order of the lookup call (target frame first, then source frame, then time) and whether the returned transform maps source coordinates into the target frame, as this chapter's lookup(target, source) does;
  • that a lookup at "time zero" means "the latest available", and the exact exceptions thrown for an unknown frame, a broken tree, or extrapolation into the future;
  • the message type of a stamped transform (header with time and parent frame id, child frame id, translation, rotation as a quaternion) and the field order (x, y, z, w) of that quaternion;
  • the standard frame names for mobile robots (map, odom, base_link and the rules between them) and the axis conventions (x forward, y left, z up for the body), which the ROS community publishes as numbered "REP" documents;
  • the command-line tools for printing the tree and echoing one transform.

The university's own FrameTree (Listing 1) implements the concepts so that you can test them; it is not tf2 and makes no claim about tf2's interface.

How the hardware actually does it

A frame tree is software, but every edge comes from hardware. Static edges come from the mechanical drawing of brackets and from extrinsic calibration (a known target seen from several poses, solved for the bracket transform). Dynamic edges come from sensors: wheel encoders and an IMU give the base's motion (RB201 F9-09, F9-10; this course's F9-24), joint encoders give each joint angle (F9-25), and a localisation filter gives the base in the map (RB302). Each sensor reading has a time stamp, taken by the driver from some clock, and the delay between the physical event and the stamp is a property of that sensor and driver that you measure, not assume. B1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. Every number in this chapter belongs to the university's simulated robot. Worked example

The robot base stands at (2, 1, 0) in the room, heading 90° (facing +y). The camera is 0.2 m ahead of and 0.6 m above the base origin, pitched 30° down. The shoulder is 0.1 m ahead and 0.3 m up, not turned. The camera sees the cup 0.8 m straight ahead along its x axis. Where is the cup for the arm, and on the map?

  1. Rotate into base axes. Ry(30°)(0.8, 0, 0) = (0.8 cos 30°, 0, −0.8 sin 30°) = (0.6928, 0, −0.4000).
  2. Shift by the bracket. pB = (0.6928 + 0.2, 0, −0.4 + 0.6) = (0.8928, 0, 0.2000). The cup is 0.2 m above the floor, on the table. Good.
  3. Into the shoulder frame. TSB = TBS−1 is a pure shift by −(0.1, 0, 0.3): pS = (0.7928, 0, −0.1000). The arm must reach 0.79 m forward and 0.1 m down. The letters: pS = TSBTBCpC.
  4. Onto the map. Heading 90° sends (x, y) to (−y, x): (0.8928, 0) becomes (0, 0.8928); add (2, 1, 0): pW = (2.0000, 1.8928, 0.2000).
  5. Direction check. The camera's x axis in the world is RWBRBC(1, 0, 0) = Rz(90°)(0.8660, 0, −0.5) = (0, 0.8660, −0.5): forward along +y and down, no shift added.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Your brain recalibrates itself; a frame tree does not. If Noor's glasses slip, she adapts within minutes. If a camera bracket is bent, the tree keeps the old numbers until a person re-measures them (extrinsic calibration). B1
    • 

  • Your body has no numbers and no sign convention. A frame tree has both, and a single minus sign (an edge stored in the wrong direction) produces a confident, wrong reach (forensic lab). R4
    • 

  • Your senses are roughly simultaneous; robot data is not. A camera picture, a wheel reading and a pose estimate each have their own time stamp and delay, and the tree must be asked "at what time?" explicitly (Listing 3). R2
    • 

  • The body is a chain of real joints; a frame tree can also hold frames that are not attached to anything physical (a "map" frame, a goal pose, a virtual frame at the tool tip). They are bookkeeping, and the analogy has no organ for them. D1

Summary


    

  • A frame is a point of view: an origin and three axes. A pose is a rigid transform TAB mapping B coordinates into A.
    • 

  • Chains multiply with matching inner letters; inverses are [RT, −RTt]; directions use R only.
    • 

  • A frame tree stores one edge per frame; any lookup is a product along the unique path.
    • 

  • Static edges come from brackets and calibration; dynamic edges come from sensors and carry time stamps.
    • 

  • Look up at the measurement's time; a stale pose costs about range × turn rate × delay.
    • 

  • An edge stored in the wrong direction gives a confident, position-dependent miss; dumps and ground-truth tests catch it.
    • 

  • tf2's interface and conventions must be checked in the ROS 2 documentation for your distribution.
