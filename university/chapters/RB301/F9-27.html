Story hook

Analogy

Mei is wiping a long whiteboard without moving her feet. Near her body, small turns of shoulder and elbow move the cloth easily in any direction. As the cloth gets farther away, her arm straightens, and something changes: to push the cloth even a little farther out, shoulder and elbow have to move a lot and fast, and at full stretch no amount of joint motion moves the cloth farther out at all. She steps sideways instead.

Later Mei pushes a heavy door shut. She straightens her arm and locks the elbow, and the door is easy: her straight arm carries the push without her elbow muscles working hard. The straight arm that was useless for moving outwards is excellent for pushing outwards. Both experiences come from one matrix, the Jacobian, which tells a robot how joint speeds become tool speeds and how tool forces become joint torques. Where it loses its inverse, the arm loses a direction of motion and gains a direction of free support.

What you will be able to do

  • Derive the Jacobian of a planar two-link arm by differentiating its forward kinematics, and check it against finite differences.
  • Compute tool velocities from joint velocities (ẋ = Jq̇) and joint velocities for a wanted tool velocity (q̇ = J−1ẋ).
  • Identify singular configurations from det J and explain what the arm can and cannot do there.
  • Map a tool force to joint torques with τ = JTF and explain the power argument behind it.
  • Implement resolved-rate control along a straight line, and diagnose and fix joint-speed blow-ups near a singularity with damped least squares.
Note

Maths you need here. A derivative is a rate of change (MA301 F0-64). If x depends on θ, then dx/dθ says how much x changes per unit of θ near the current θ; for small changes Δx ≈ (dx/dθ)Δθ. When x depends on several variables θ1, θ2, the partial derivative ∂x/∂θ1 is the same rate with θ2 held fixed. You need two rules: d(cos θ)/dθ = −sin θ and d(sin θ)/dθ = cos θ (with θ in radians), and the chain rule: if θ changes with time, dx/dt = (dx/dθ)(dθ/dt). The chapter's code checks every derivative numerically, so you can see these rules work. D1R1

Jargon box
Jacobian (manipulator)
Simple words: a table that says how fast the tool moves for each joint's speed. Analogy: how far your hand moves for a small twitch of the shoulder, and for a small twitch of the elbow. Precise: the matrix J(q) of partial derivatives of the forward-kinematics map, Jij = ∂xi/∂qj, so that ẋ = J(q)q̇; for full 3D poses, a 6 × n matrix mapping joint velocities to the tool's twist. B1D1
Singularity (kinematic)
Simple words: a pose where the arm cannot move its tool in some direction, however its joints move. Analogy: Mei's fully stretched arm cannot move the cloth farther out. Precise: a configuration where J loses rank (det J = 0 for a square J); there, some tool velocities need infinite joint velocities and some tool forces need no joint torque. B1
Manipulability
Simple words: a score for how freely the tool can move in all directions at this pose. Analogy: wiping near your body (easy) versus at full stretch (hard). Precise: w = √det(JJT), equal to |det J| for a square J; zero exactly at singularities. The velocity ellipsoid {Jq̇ : |q̇| = 1} shows the direction-by-direction picture. B1D1
Statics (τ = JTF)
Simple words: the joint torques that hold a push at the tool still. Analogy: how hard your shoulder and elbow work to hold a door. Precise: for a static arm with tool force F, the joint torques that balance it are τ = JTF (up to the sign convention for "applied" versus "resisted" force), from equal power τTq̇ = FTẋ. B1D1
Resolved-rate control
Simple words: moving the tool at a chosen speed by recomputing the joint speeds many times a second. Analogy: guiding your hand along a ruler while your joints sort themselves out. Precise: at every control period, set q̇ = J(q)−1(ẋdesired + K(xdesired − x)), and integrate q̇; the feedback term corrects drift. B1R3
Damped least squares (velocity)
Simple words: a softened inverse that never asks for crazy joint speeds. Analogy: near full stretch, Mei accepts that the cloth lags a little instead of whipping her arm. Precise: q̇ = JT(JJT + λ2I)−1ẋ; equal to J−1ẋ when λ = 0 and J is invertible, bounded for every J when λ > 0, at the cost of a tracking error near singularities. B1R2

The idea in pictures

Figure 1, left: the two columns of the Jacobian of a planar 2R arm drawn as velocity arrows at the tool. Turning the shoulder at 1 rad/s moves the tool perpendicular to the line from shoulder to tool; turning the elbow moves it perpendicular to the line from elbow to tool. Right: det J = L1L2 sin θ2 against the elbow angle. It is largest with the elbow at a right angle and zero when the arm is straight or folded.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Twitch only your shoulder: your hand moves along a circle around your shoulder. Twitch only your elbow: your hand moves along a smaller circle around your elbow. Any hand motion you make is a mix of these two twitches. The Jacobian is the robot's list of "what each twitch does to the hand", worked out fresh for the pose the arm is in now.

When the arm is bent, the two twitches push the hand in clearly different directions, so the robot can mix them to go any way it likes. When the arm is straight, both twitches push the hand sideways, in the same direction. Neither of them can push the hand farther out. The robot has lost one direction. That pose is called a singularity.

The same list works backwards for pushing. When the hand pushes on something, each joint has to resist part of the push. With a straight arm pushing outwards, the push goes straight through the bones and the joints hardly have to work. That is why you lock your elbow to push a door.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Differentiate the forward kinematics

From F9-25, x = L1c1 + L2c12 and y = L1s1 + L2s12, with c1 = cos θ1, s12 = sin(θ1 + θ2) and so on. Differentiating each with respect to θ1 and θ2: D1B1

J = [ −L1 s1 − L2 s12 −L2 s12 ] [ L1 c1 + L2 c12 L2 c12 ]

Column 1 is the tool velocity for θ̇1 = 1 rad/s with θ̇2 = 0; it equals the shoulder-to-tool vector (x, y) turned by 90° (−y, x), as rotation about the shoulder demands. Column 2 is the elbow-to-tool vector turned by 90°. Run R1 compares this J with central finite differences at 625 configurations; the largest difference is 1.0 × 10−10, the size expected from rounding in the differences with h = 10−6. R1

Velocities both ways and the determinant

ẋ = Jq̇ gives the tool velocity of given joint speeds. For a wanted tool velocity, solve Jq̇ = ẋ. A 2 × 2 system has a unique solution when det J ≠ 0, and D1

det J = (−L1 s1 − L2 s12)(L2 c12) − (−L2 s12)(L1 c1 + L2 c12) = L1 L2 (s12 c1 − s1 c12) = L1 L2 sin θ2

using sin(a − b) = sin a cos b − cos a sin b. The determinant depends only on the elbow angle and vanishes at θ2 = 0 (straight) and 180° (folded). Run R1 prints det J and L1L2 sin θ2 side by side and the joint speeds needed for a tool speed of 0.1 m/s along x: 13.50 and −29.71 deg/s at θ2 = 90°, then 109.57 and −241.04 deg/s at 10°, and 1,094.28 and −2,407.42 deg/s at 1°. Joint speeds grow like 1/sin θ2. R1

Forces: τ = JTF

If the arm holds still against a tool force, the joints must supply torques. Power is force times velocity: at the joints τTq̇, at the tool FTẋ = FTJq̇. If no energy is stored or lost in between, these are equal for every q̇, so τ = JTF. In run R1 the arm at (30°, 45°) holds a 10 N force pointing down (F = (0, −10) N, a load the tool supports): τ = (−3.2451, −0.6470) N m. Each torque is the force times the horizontal lever arm from that joint to the tool (0.3245 m and 0.0647 m), with the sign of a downward pull. At a singularity the same formula explains Mei's door: with θ2 = 0 along x, a force along the arm gives JTF = 0, so no torque is needed (run R2 checks it). D1B1R2

Watch out

Sign conventions differ between texts: some use F as the force the robot applies to the environment, others as the force the environment applies to the robot. The magnitudes are the same; the signs flip. State yours next to the formula, as Listing 2's output line does.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Jacobians of spatial arms. For an arm moving in 3D, the tool's velocity has six components: an angular velocity ω and a linear velocity. For a revolute joint i with unit axis zi through point pi (both in the base frame at the current configuration), the geometric Jacobian's column is (zi, zi × (ptool − pi)): turning about the axis turns the tool and moves the tool point perpendicular to its lever arm. "Modern Robotics" writes the same object in screw notation as the space Jacobian, whose i-th column is screw axis Si moved by the joints before it, and the body Jacobian in the tool frame; they are related by the adjoint map. Which point's velocity the linear part refers to (the tool point or the base origin) differs between the two forms and is a common source of disagreement between libraries. B1D1

Singular values. The singular value decomposition J = UΣVT (MA302 territory) gives the velocity ellipsoid's axes (columns of U) and lengths (σi). Manipulability w = σ1σ2⋯; the condition number σmax/σmin measures how unequal the directions are. Damped least squares replaces each 1/σi by σi/(σi2 + λ2), which is close to 1/σi for large σi and goes to zero instead of infinity as σi → 0; its largest possible value is 1/(2λ). That is why the forensic fix bounds joint speeds. B1D1

Redundancy and null space. For a redundant arm (more joints than task dimensions), J is wide, and q̇ = J+ẋ + (I − J+J)z for any z, where J+ is the pseudo-inverse: the second term moves the joints without moving the tool (the elbow swings while the hand stays). Secondary goals such as joint-limit avoidance are added through z. B1

Resolved-rate control in practice. Integrating q̇ = J−1ẋdesired alone drifts, because each step is a linearisation; adding K(xdesired − x) feeds the position error back. In run R3 a 0.29 m straight line in 3 s at 10 ms steps ends about 1.4 × 10−4 m from the wanted point with K = 2 s−1 (printed (0.4499, −0.0501) m against (0.4500, −0.0500) m). Joint speed and acceleration limits must be enforced after the inversion, and a controller must refuse or slow down before a singularity rather than inside it. R3B1

How the hardware actually does it

Joint velocities are limited by the motors and gearboxes; joint torques are limited by the motors' current limits and the gearbox ratings (RB201 F9-08). τ = JTF tells you, for a given pose, how large a tool force the arm can resist before some joint reaches its torque limit, and q̇ = J−1ẋ tells you how fast the tool can move before some joint reaches its speed limit; both bounds collapse near singularities in different directions. Many arms estimate joint torque from motor current; some have joint torque sensors or a force/torque sensor at the wrist. The datasheet values of your arm, not this chapter, define the limits. U1B1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. The 120 deg/s joint limit of the forensic lab and the 10 N load are values chosen for the university's simulated arm. A real arm's speed and torque limits, and whether its torque estimate comes from motor current or a sensor, come from the manufacturer's documentation and your own measurements, recorded in the dossier.

Worked example

L1 = 0.30 m, L2 = 0.25 m, pose (30°, 45°): s1 = 0.5, c1 = 0.8660, s12 = sin 75° = 0.96593, c12 = cos 75° = 0.25882.

  1. J11 = −0.30 × 0.5 − 0.25 × 0.96593 = −0.39148; J12 = −0.24148; J21 = 0.30 × 0.8660 + 0.25 × 0.25882 = 0.32451; J22 = 0.06470.
  2. det J = 0.30 × 0.25 × sin 45° = 0.05303 m2.
  3. Joint speeds (10, −20) deg/s = (0.17453, −0.34907) rad/s give ẋ = (−0.39148 × 0.17453 + 0.24148 × 0.34907, 0.32451 × 0.17453 − 0.06470 × 0.34907) = (0.01597, 0.03405) m/s.
  4. For ẋ = (0, 0.05) m/s: q̇ = J−1ẋ = (1/det)(−J12 × 0.05, J11 × 0.05) = (0.24148 × 0.05, −0.39148 × 0.05)/0.05303 = (0.22768, −0.36910) rad/s = (13.045, −21.147) deg/s.
  5. Tool force F = (5, 0) N: τ = JTF = (5J11, 5J12) = (−1.9574, −1.2074) N m.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Mei steps sideways; the arm cannot. A person leaves a singularity by moving the body. A fixed arm must plan around singular poses, or slow down and accept a tracking error (damped least squares). B1
    • 

  • Muscles get tired even when bones carry the load; robot joints do not. τ = JTF = 0 at a straight arm means zero motor torque, but the structure, bearings and brakes still carry the force; a body's "free" push still costs muscle effort to stay locked. D1
    • 

  • A twitch is not instantaneous. The Jacobian is a derivative: it predicts the effect of very small motions. For large motions the arm's geometry changes and J must be recomputed, which is why controllers do it every cycle. D1
    •

Summary


    

  • The Jacobian holds the partial derivatives of forward kinematics: ẋ = J(q)q̇.
    • 

  • For a planar 2R arm, each column is a joint-to-tool vector turned by 90°, and det J = L1L2 sin θ2.
    • 

  • At singularities (straight or folded arm) J loses rank: some tool velocities need unbounded joint speeds.
    • 

  • Forces map backwards: τ = JTF, from equal power at joints and tool.
    • 

  • Resolved-rate control recomputes q̇ = J−1(ẋd + K(xd − x)) each cycle.
    • 

  • Damped least squares bounds joint speeds near singularities at the cost of tracking error.
