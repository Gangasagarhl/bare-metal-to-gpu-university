Story hook

Analogy

Amara has used a manual wheelchair since she was small, and she steers it without thinking. Push both hand-rims equally and the chair rolls straight. Push the right rim a little harder and the chair curves to the left. Push one rim forward and pull the other back, and the chair spins on the spot. She can never slide sideways, however hard she pushes: the big wheels only roll forwards or backwards.

Most small robots move exactly like Amara's chair: two driven wheels on one axle, and a free caster to keep the body level. The robot's software must do in arithmetic what Amara does by feel. Going one way, it turns "go at this speed, turn at this rate" into two wheel speeds. Going the other way, it turns two measured wheel speeds into "this is how I moved", step after step, which is the robot's odometry. This chapter derives both directions, checks them against a simulator that knows the truth, and shows what happens when one number in the robot's configuration is wrong.

What you will be able to do

  • Derive the forward and inverse kinematics of a differential-drive robot from the rolling-without-slipping condition.
  • Compute the turning radius and the instantaneous centre of rotation for given wheel speeds.
  • Integrate odometry with an Euler step and with the exact arc, and measure their errors against simulator ground truth at several update rates.
  • Explain the no-sideways-slip (non-holonomic) constraint and what it means for planning.
  • Diagnose a wrong track-width parameter from a log in which odometry is perfect and the robot is not.
Jargon box
Track width
Simple words: how far apart the two driven wheels are. Analogy: the distance between the two big wheels of a wheelchair. Precise: the distance W between the contact points of the left and right driven wheels, measured along the axle; it sets how fast a speed difference turns the robot. Some texts use the half-width instead; check which one a formula uses. D1B1
Body twist (planar)
Simple words: how fast the robot goes forward and how fast it turns. Analogy: "roll forward briskly while curving left gently". Precise: the pair (v, ω): forward speed of the axle midpoint along the body x axis (m/s) and turn rate about the vertical axis (rad/s); a planar special case of the twist of "Modern Robotics". B1D1
Forward kinematics (wheeled)
Simple words: from wheel speeds to robot motion. Analogy: feeling how hard each hand pushed and knowing how the chair moved. Precise: v = r(ωR + ωL)/2, ω = r(ωR − ωL)/W for wheel radius r and wheel angular speeds ωL, ωR. D1B1
Inverse kinematics (wheeled)
Simple words: from wanted robot motion to wheel speeds. Analogy: deciding how hard each hand must push. Precise: ωR = (v + ωW/2)/r, ωL = (v − ωW/2)/r. D1
Instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR)
Simple words: the point the robot is circling around at this moment. Analogy: the spot on the floor the wheelchair curves around. Precise: for ω ≠ 0, the point on the axle line at signed distance R = v/ω from the axle midpoint; R = ∞ means straight, R = 0 means spinning on the spot. B1D1
Non-holonomic constraint
Simple words: a rule about how you may move, not about where you may be. Analogy: the wheelchair can reach any spot in the room, but never by sliding sideways. Precise: a constraint on velocities that cannot be integrated into a constraint on positions; here ẋ sin θ − ẏ cos θ = 0 (no velocity along the axle). B1
Odometry (dead reckoning from wheels)
Simple words: working out where you are by adding up how the wheels turned. Analogy: counting your pushes in the dark. Precise: integrating the body twist computed from wheel encoders into a pose estimate TWB(t); its error grows without bound because nothing corrects it (RB201 F9-09; RB302). B2U1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the robot from above. The two wheels sit on one axle, W apart. When the right wheel's rim moves faster than the left, both rims trace circles around the same centre on the axle line, the ICR. The farther a wheel is from the ICR, the faster its rim must move. That one picture contains both kinematics formulas.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Think of Amara's chair. If both wheels turn at the same speed, the chair goes straight, and its speed is the wheels' speed. If one wheel turns faster, the chair goes round a curve, and the bigger the difference, the tighter the curve. If the wheels turn at the same speed in opposite directions, the chair spins in place.

So two numbers in (left wheel, right wheel) give two numbers out (forward speed, turning speed). The forward speed is the average of the two wheels. The turning speed is their difference, divided by the distance between the wheels: wide-apart wheels need a bigger difference to turn as quickly.

To know where it is, the robot adds up these small moves, many times a second. That works well for a while. But every small mistake is added in too, and nothing takes it back out, so the robot slowly becomes less sure where it is. The robot cannot notice by itself if it is wrong. Only an outside check, like a tracker that watches from above, can tell the truth.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From rolling to the formulas

Rolling without slipping means the contact point of each wheel has zero velocity relative to the floor, so the wheel's centre moves forward at r times its angular speed: vL = rωL, vR = rωR. The body is rigid and turns at rate ω, so a point on the axle at lateral offset y (y pointing left) moves forward at v − ωy. The left wheel (y = +W/2) therefore moves at v − ωW/2 and the right wheel (y = −W/2) at v + ωW/2: in a left turn (ω > 0) the outer, right wheel is faster. So vL = v − ωW/2 and vR = v + ωW/2. Adding and subtracting gives the forward kinematics; solving for the wheels gives the inverse. D1B1

v = r (ωR + ωL) / 2 ω = r (ωR − ωL) / W ωR = (v + ωW/2) / r ωL = (v − ωW/2) / r

These are linear: a 2 × 2 matrix maps wheel speeds to (v, ω), and its inverse maps back. It is invertible as long as r ≠ 0 and W ≠ 0, so every (v, ω) can be produced by some wheel speeds; MA201's made-up example matrix (F0-49) now has its real entries. D1U1

From body velocity to pose

The pose (x, y, θ) changes as ẋ = v cos θ, ẏ = v sin θ, θ̇ = ω (the "unicycle model"). Odometry samples the wheels every Δt and assumes (v, ω) constant in between. Two ways to integrate one step: B2D1

  • Euler step: move in a straight line along the old heading, then turn: x += vΔt cos θ, y += vΔt sin θ, θ += ωΔt.
  • Exact arc: with constant (v, ω) the robot moves on a circle of radius R = v/ω: x += R(sin(θ + ωΔt) − sin θ), y −= R(cos(θ + ωΔt) − cos θ), θ += ωΔt, falling back to the straight line when ω is (nearly) zero.

The heading update is identical in both and, if the wheel angles are read exactly, exact: θ is a linear function of the wheel angles, θ = r(φR − φL)/W. Run R1 confirms it: the heading error stays at about 5 × 10−13 rad at every rate, while the position error depends on the method and the rate. D1R1

The ICR and the constraint

The wheel velocities are both perpendicular to the axle, so the robot's velocity has no component along the axle: ẋ sin θ − ẏ cos θ = 0. This is a rule about velocities only. The robot can still reach any (x, y, θ) (by driving forward, turning, backing up), but not along any path; parallel parking is the everyday example. Planners for wheeled robots (RB401) must respect this. B1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Discretisation error, measured. Run R1 drives a smooth wheel-speed profile for 10 s; a simulator integrates the true motion with 10 µs steps (ground truth), and odometry reads the wheel angles at 100, 50, 10 and 2 Hz. Halving the rate doubles the Euler error (1.41 × 10−3 m at 100 Hz, 2.82 × 10−3 m at 50 Hz): first order. The arc method's error quarters when the rate doubles (1.90 × 10−5 → 4.75 × 10−6 m): second order. It is not zero, because the true wheel speeds vary inside each interval and odometry only sees their average. R1U1

Both are small next to the real errors. In this simulation the only error source is the discretisation. On a real robot the dominant errors are systematic (a wrong wheel radius or track width, unequal wheels) and random (slip, uneven floor, encoder quantisation). A systematic track-width error rotates every turn by the factor Wconfig/Wtrue; a 1 % smaller left wheel turns a 10 m straight drive into a 0.33 rad heading error (run R2: 0.01 × 10 m / 0.30 m). Calibrate these parameters before worrying about the integration formula. R2B2

Wheel-speed limits. Each motor has a maximum speed (from its datasheet and your measurements, RB201). If the inverse kinematics asks for more, clipping each wheel separately changes the ratio ωR/ωL and therefore the curvature: the robot drives a different curve, not the same curve slower. Scaling both wheel speeds by the same factor keeps R = v/ω and is the usual choice when following a path. D1

Uncertainty grows. Odometry error accumulates; position uncertainty grows with distance and, through heading error, faster than linearly. Probabilistic motion models describe this growth with noise parameters fitted to the robot (B2); RB302 fuses odometry with an IMU and other sensors so that something can pull the estimate back. B2U1

How the hardware actually does it

The wheel angles come from encoders on the motor or wheel shaft (RB201 F9-08, F9-09): a count per fraction of a turn, read by a timer peripheral or an interrupt, and a gearbox ratio between motor and wheel. Odometry therefore sees angle increments quantised to one count, at the rate the software samples them; the formulas of this chapter use the increment divided by the sample time, as Listing 2 does with its exact simulated angles. The effective wheel radius under load and the effective track width (contact patches are not points) differ from the drawing, which is why they are calibrated. U1B2

Encoder counts per revolution, gear ratios, maximum wheel speeds and the effective radius come from the course kit's datasheets and your own calibration (lab step 6), recorded in the dossier.

Worked example

The simulated course robot has r = 0.05 m and W = 0.30 m. You want v = 0.4 m/s and ω = 0.5 rad/s.

  1. Inverse kinematics. ωW/2 = 0.5 × 0.15 = 0.075 m/s. ωR = (0.4 + 0.075)/0.05 = 9.5 rad/s; ωL = (0.4 − 0.075)/0.05 = 6.5 rad/s.
  2. Check with forward kinematics. v = 0.05 × (9.5 + 6.5)/2 = 0.4 m/s; ω = 0.05 × (9.5 − 6.5)/0.30 = 0.5 rad/s.
  3. Turning radius. R = v/ω = 0.8 m to the left of the axle midpoint.
  4. One 0.5 s step from (0, 0, 0). Euler: (0.4 × 0.5, 0, 0.25) = (0.2, 0, 0.25). Arc: 0.8 × sin 0.25 = 0.19792 and 0.8 × (1 − cos 0.25) = 0.02487, so (0.19792, 0.02487, 0.25). The Euler step overshoots forwards by 2 mm and misses the 25 mm of sideways drift that a curve produces.
  5. Spin on the spot at ω = 1 rad/s: ωR = +3, ωL = −3 rad/s; a quarter turn takes π/2 ≈ 1.5708 s.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Amara looks where she is going; odometry does not. She corrects her course by sight all the time. Odometry only counts wheel turns, so its error grows until another sensor corrects it (RB302). B2
    • 

  • Hands feel slip; encoders do not. If a wheel spins on a wet floor, she feels it. The encoder reports the spin as motion, and odometry moves the robot that never moved. B2
    • 

  • A person knows nothing of "W". She learns her chair by practice. The robot's formulas need the track width as a number, and a wrong number gives perfectly consistent, wrong turns (forensic lab, run R3). R3
    • 

  • The chair's front casters are not in the model. Neither are the robot's: the formulas assume the casters swivel freely and carry no force, which real casters only approximate when they flip direction. D1

Verification

Troubleshooting


    

  • Error does not shrink with N → you are converting ticks with integer division; use double arithmetic.
    • 

  • Arc odometry worse than Euler → the sign of the y update or the order of the θ update is wrong; test one constant-speed step against the worked example.
    • 

  • Spin-test W is nonsense → v was not zero during the spin, or the measured angle wrapped around 360°; record full turns.

Summary


    

  • v = r(ωR + ωL)/2 and ω = r(ωR − ωL)/W; the inverse gives wheel speeds for a wanted (v, ω).
    • 

  • The robot turns about an ICR on the axle line at R = v/ω.
    • 

  • The robot cannot move along its axle: a velocity constraint, not a position constraint.
    • 

  • Odometry integrates (v, ω): Euler is first order, the exact arc second order for varying speeds; the heading is exact if the angles are.
    • 

  • Real errors are dominated by calibration and slip, and odometry cannot detect them by itself.
    • 

  • A wrong track width gives consistent wrong turns with perfect-looking odometry; a spin test measures it.
    •
