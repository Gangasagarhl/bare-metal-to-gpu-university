Story hook

Analogy

Close your eyes and touch your nose. You can do it because your body knows the angle of your shoulder, your elbow and your wrist, and it knows how long your upper arm and forearm are. From those few facts it works out where your fingertip is, without looking. You never had to look at a table of numbers to learn it; you built it by moving.

A robot arm has the same facts: an encoder at every joint tells it the joint's angle, and its own drawing tells it the length of every link. Forward kinematics is the calculation that turns "shoulder 30°, elbow −60°" into "the tool is 0.52 m in front of me at table height". It is the simplest direction (one answer for every set of angles) and the foundation for everything else in this course: inverse kinematics (F9-26) searches for angles whose forward kinematics hits a target, Jacobians (F9-27) are its derivatives, and a robot model file (F9-29) is a written-down forward kinematics.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe a serial arm by its links and joints (revolute and prismatic) and distinguish joint space from task space.
  • Compute forward kinematics as a chain of fixed offsets and joint motions, by hand for a planar two-link arm and in code for a three-joint spatial arm.
  • Verify a forward-kinematics implementation against an independent closed-form formula and against the product-of-exponentials formula, over thousands of configurations.
  • Explain the product-of-exponentials and Denavit–Hartenberg descriptions, and why conventions must be stated.
  • Diagnose a joint axis defined in the wrong direction from a table of predicted and measured tool positions.
Jargon box
Link and joint
Simple words: the stiff pieces of an arm, and the hinges or slides between them. Analogy: upper arm and forearm; shoulder and elbow. Precise: a link is a rigid body; a joint constrains the relative motion of two links to one (or more) degrees of freedom. A serial arm alternates them from the base to the tool. B1
Revolute and prismatic joint
Simple words: a hinge that turns, and a slide that moves in a straight line. Analogy: your elbow; a drawer. Precise: a revolute joint allows rotation about a fixed axis (variable: an angle); a prismatic joint allows translation along a fixed axis (variable: a distance). B1
Joint space and task space
Simple words: describing the arm by its joint angles, or by where its tool is. Analogy: "shoulder up a bit, elbow bent" versus "hand on the table". Precise: joint space is the set of joint-variable vectors q; task space is the set of tool poses T(q) ∈ SE(3) (or tool positions only). Forward kinematics maps the first to the second. B1D1
Forward kinematics (arm)
Simple words: from joint angles to where the tool is. Analogy: touching your nose with your eyes closed. Precise: the map q ↦ Tbase,tool(q), here the product of each joint's fixed offset and its motion along the chain. B1D1
Home configuration
Simple words: the arm's pose when every joint reads zero. Analogy: standing with your arm stretched out sideways, the position you call "zero". Precise: the configuration q = 0 and its tool pose M = T(0); which physical pose is "zero" is a choice made by the designer and fixed by the encoders' zero (homing). B1
Screw axis
Simple words: a line in space plus a rule for how a joint moves along or around it. Analogy: the line through your elbow's hinge. Precise: S = (ω, v) ∈ ℝ6; for a revolute joint, ω is the unit axis direction and v = −ω × p for any point p on the axis, all expressed in the base frame at the home configuration. B1R2
Product of exponentials (PoE)
Simple words: a formula that gets the tool pose by turning each joint about its own line, starting from the home pose. Analogy: starting from "arm out sideways", turn the shoulder, then the elbow, and see where the hand ends up. Precise: T(q) = e[S1]q1 e[S2]q2 ⋯ e[Sn]qn M, where each factor is the rigid motion "rotate by qi about screw axis Si". B1R2
Denavit–Hartenberg (DH) parameters
Simple words: an older, compact recipe that describes each link with four numbers. Analogy: a standard form for writing down an arm. Precise: a convention that attaches a frame to each link by fixed rules so that each link transform is a product of two rotations and two translations with four parameters; several variants exist (often called classic and modified), and they are not interchangeable. B1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Hold your arm out. Now lift it at the shoulder, keeping the elbow straight: your hand moves on a big circle around your shoulder. Bend only the elbow: your hand moves on a smaller circle around your elbow. Every joint moves everything beyond it, but nothing before it.

So you can find the hand step by step. Start at the shoulder. Point the upper arm along the shoulder's angle and walk its length: now you are at the elbow. Turn by the elbow's angle, on top of the shoulder's angle, and walk the forearm's length: now you are at the hand. That walk is forward kinematics. For the robot, each "walk and turn" is one transform (F9-23), and the whole walk is their product.

There is always exactly one answer: the same angles always put the hand in the same place. The reverse question, "which angles put my hand on the cup?", can have two answers, or none. That harder question is the next chapter.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The planar two-link arm

Measure θ1 from the x axis and θ2 from the direction of link 1. The elbow is at L1(cos θ1, sin θ1); link 2 points in the direction θ1 + θ2, so the tip is D1B1

x = L1 cos θ1 + L2 cos(θ1 + θ2) y = L1 sin θ1 + L2 sin(θ1 + θ2) φ = θ1 + θ2

where φ is the tip's orientation. Each joint angle is measured relative to the previous link; that is why the angles add up along the chain.

Any serial arm: a chain of offsets and motions

Give each link a frame. Joint i sits at a fixed place in link i−1's frame (its offset) and rotates link i about an axis fixed in that place. The transform from link i−1 to link i is then "shift by the offset, then rotate by qi about the axis": Ti−1,i(qi) = Trans(offseti) · Rot(axisi, qi). The tool is a fixed point (or frame) in the last link. By F9-23's chaining rule, D1B1

T_base,tool(q) = T_01(q1) · T_12(q2) · … · T_(n−1)n(qn) · T_n,tool

For a prismatic joint, Rot(axis, q) is replaced by Trans(q · axis). Listing 1 implements the revolute case with Rodrigues' formula for the rotation about an arbitrary unit axis. B1

The course arm

Joint 1 turns the whole arm about the vertical z axis; joints 2 and 3 tip it up and down in the vertical plane the arm currently faces. Their axes are chosen as (0, −1, 0), so that a positive angle lifts the arm (Figure 1). Seen in that vertical plane, joints 2 and 3 are a planar 2R arm with L1 = 0.30 m and L2 = 0.25 + 0.05 = 0.30 m, mounted 0.10 m above the base. That gives an independent closed form to test against: reach = 0.30 cos q2 + 0.30 cos(q2 + q3), z = 0.10 + 0.30 sin q2 + 0.30 sin(q2 + q3), then x = reach · cos q1, y = reach · sin q1. Run R1 checks the chain against it at 9,261 configurations; the largest difference is 2.2 × 10−16 m, which is rounding. D1R1

Watch out

An implementation can be "verified" against a second implementation that shares its mistake. The closed form above was written from the geometry, not from the chain code, and the PoE check in Layer 3 starts from a different description of the arm (axes in the base frame instead of offsets per link). Agreement between methods with independent derivations is the evidence; agreement with a copy is not.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Product of exponentials. "Modern Robotics" describes an arm without attaching a frame to every link. You need only (1) the home configuration M = Tbase,tool(0), and (2) for each joint, its screw axis Si = (ωi, vi) in the base frame with all joints at zero: ωi the unit axis direction, vi = −ωi × pi for a point pi on the axis. Then T(q) = e[S1]q1 ⋯ e[Sn]qn M. For a revolute joint the factor e[S]q is the rigid motion "rotate by q about the line through p with direction ω": [R, (I − R)p], with R from Rodrigues' formula. B1D1

Why the order works: apply the last joint first, while all earlier joints are still at zero, so its axis is exactly where it was at home; then each earlier joint moves everything beyond it rigidly. For the course arm, the screw axes are S1 = ((0, 0, 1), 0), S2 = ((0, −1, 0), (0.10, 0, 0)) and S3 = ((0, −1, 0), (0.10, 0, −0.30)), and M is a pure translation to (0.6, 0, 0.1). Listing 3 implements this and compares the full transform (rotation and translation) with the chain of Listing 1 at 2,197 configurations: the largest difference is 1.7 × 10−16. R2

Denavit–Hartenberg. The older DH convention places a frame on each link by fixed rules (z along the joint axis, x along the common normal of consecutive axes) so that each link needs only four numbers (two angles, two lengths). It is compact and widespread in older documentation and in some vendor manuals. Its weaknesses are practical: the frames are often not where the drawing puts the parts, two variants (often called "classic" and "modified") place the frames differently, and parallel consecutive axes make the common normal ambiguous. If a datasheet gives DH parameters, find out which variant before you type a single number. B1

Cost. One chain evaluation for n joints is about 2n rigid-transform products; each product is 27 multiply-adds for R and 9 for t. That is tiny, which is why a controller can afford forward kinematics at every cycle and why sampling-based planners (RB401) can call it millions of times. D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The screw-axis notation and the e[S]θ formula above follow the author's recollection of "Modern Robotics"; the run R2 proves that the formula as implemented here agrees with the chain for this arm, but it does not prove that the book uses exactly these symbols, sign conventions or the same order of (ω, v). The Source Researcher must check the book's chapter on forward kinematics. Likewise, the exact DH variants and their names must be taken from the book's appendix or from the manual of the arm you use.

How the hardware actually does it

Forward kinematics needs the joint angles, and they come from encoders (RB201 F9-09). An incremental encoder counts changes, so after power-up the arm does not know its absolute angles until it is homed (driven to a reference switch or a hard stop) or unless it has absolute encoders. The encoder sits on the motor or on the joint; with a gearbox between them, backlash means the motor encoder can move a little without the link moving. Links bend under load, and the real offsets differ from the drawing by manufacturing tolerances. Kinematic calibration measures the tool at many poses (with a tracker or a probe) and fits corrected offsets and zero angles. Every one of these effects changes the numbers in courseArm(), never the structure of the code. B1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. The course arm in this chapter is a simulated arm with round-number dimensions. A real arm's link lengths, joint axis directions, zero positions, joint limits and encoder resolution come from its manufacturer's documentation and your own calibration, recorded in the dossier; nothing in this chapter was measured on a real arm.

Worked example

Part A, planar 2R. L1 = 0.30 m, L2 = 0.25 m, θ1 = 30°, θ2 = 45°.

  1. Elbow: 0.30 × (cos 30°, sin 30°) = (0.25981, 0.15000).
  2. Link 2 points at θ1 + θ2 = 75°: 0.25 × (cos 75°, sin 75°) = (0.06470, 0.24148).
  3. Tip: (0.32451, 0.39148), orientation 75°.

Part B, course arm at q = (90°, 30°, −60°).

  1. In the arm's vertical plane: reach = 0.30 cos 30° + 0.30 cos(−30°) = 0.5196 m; z = 0.10 + 0.30 sin 30° + 0.30 sin(−30°) = 0.10 m.
  2. Turn by q1 = 90°: x = 0.5196 cos 90° = 0, y = 0.5196 sin 90° = 0.5196.
  3. Tool at (0, 0.5196, 0.1000) in the base frame. The tool's x axis points along the forearm: (cos 90° cos(−30°), sin 90° cos(−30°), sin(−30°)) = (0, 0.8660, −0.5).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Your body's sense of joint position is approximate and self-correcting; the robot's is exact arithmetic on imperfect numbers. If a link length or a zero offset is wrong, every answer is wrong in the same consistent way, and nothing inside the robot notices (forensic lab). R4
    • 

  • Your joints are not ideal hinges. A shoulder rolls and slides; a revolute joint in the model turns about one fixed line. Robot joints are much closer to the model, but backlash and flexing are still outside it. B1
    • 

  • You learnt your arm; the robot is told it. Your brain built its model by moving and looking. The robot's model is typed in by a person, axis directions and all, which is where the sign errors come from. D1
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F9-23 (frame trees with static and dynamic edges) and F9-24 (a dynamic edge computed from wheel motion). What carries over: chaining with matching letters, rigid transforms, checking against ground truth. What changes: each dynamic edge is now one joint variable of a chain you design; the chain's structure (axes, offsets) is part of the robot's description; and the map from joint space to task space is non-linear, which will make its inverse hard. B1 What comes next: F9-26 inverts this map: given a target for the tool, find joint angles, and discover that there may be two answers or none.

Safety

Steps 1–5 are software only. Step 6 moves a real arm: arms move with force and can pinch fingers. Use the slowest speed setting, keep hands outside the arm's reach while it moves, keep the emergency stop in hand, and command one joint at a time in small steps; learners below L3 work only with adult supervision. Expected observations


All software comparisons agree to about 10−15 (rounding). A deliberately wrong offset or axis makes every comparison fail. On a real arm (if done), the differences are millimetres to centimetres and show patterns that point to specific offsets or zero errors.


Verification


The course practical-exam form: "compute and verify an arm's forward kinematics". You show (1) the hand computation of one pose, (2) the program's output for the same pose, and (3) a sweep against an independently derived reference with the worst difference printed and an exit code 0, and (4) a negative test: the same sweep with one planted error exits with code 1.


Troubleshooting


    

  • Agreement at q = 0 only → offsets and rotations composed in the wrong order inside a joint (rotate-then-shift instead of shift-then-rotate).
    • 

  • Agreement for q1 = 0 only → the yaw axis is not z, or the reference turns the reach vector the wrong way.
    • 

  • Rotation test fails, position test passes → the tool offset lies along the tool's x axis, so a wrong tool rotation does not move the tool point; test orientation separately.

Summary


    

  • A serial arm is links joined by revolute or prismatic joints; forward kinematics maps joint space to task space.
    • 

  • Planar 2R: x = L1cos θ1 + L2cos(θ1 + θ2), y likewise with sines.
    • 

  • Any serial arm: multiply "offset, then joint motion" for each joint, then the tool offset.
    • 

  • PoE describes the arm by its home pose and screw axes; it agrees with the chain to rounding (run R2).
    • 

  • DH parameters are compact but come in incompatible variants; always identify the variant.

    •
