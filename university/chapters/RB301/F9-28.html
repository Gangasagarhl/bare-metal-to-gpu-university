Story hook

Analogy

Tomás carries a full shopping bag home. Held close to his body, it is easy. Held out at arm's length, his shoulder burns within a minute, although the bag is no heavier. When he swings the bag to hang it on a hook, it is hard to start the swing and hard to stop it; and as the bag swings, it tugs his elbow open in a way it never did while he stood still.

A robot arm feels all three of these effects: gravity, which costs more torque the farther the load is from the joint; inertia, which resists starting and stopping; and the sideways tugs that appear only while the arm moves. F9-27 told you what torques hold a force still. This chapter writes down the torques needed to move an arm that has mass, and shows how to check a simulator of that motion before you trust it.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the equation of motion M(q)q̈ + c(q, q̇) + g(q) = τ and explain each term physically.
  • Compute gravity torques, the mass matrix and the Coriolis and centrifugal terms of a planar two-link arm with point masses.
  • Distinguish inverse dynamics (motion to torque) from forward dynamics (torque to motion) and use each for its job.
  • Simulate the arm with a numerical integrator and verify the simulation with three tests: round trip, gravity compensation and energy conservation.
  • Diagnose a simulator whose arm gains energy from nowhere and fix it by choosing a suitable integrator and step.
Note

Maths you need here. Three ideas from MA301 and MA302. (1) Torque and angular acceleration: for a mass m at distance r from a pivot, a torque τ produces angular acceleration θ̈ = τ/(mr2); mr2 is the moment of inertia, the rotational version of mass in F = ma. (2) Derivatives: velocity is the rate of change of position, acceleration the rate of change of velocity (F0-64). (3) Differential equations and integrators: if you know the acceleration for any state, you can step the state forward in time by small steps h; explicit Euler uses the rates at the start of the step, Runge–Kutta 4 (RK4) averages four trial rates per step and is much more accurate for the same h (F0-67, F0-76). The code checks each of these numerically. D1U1

Jargon box
Equation of motion (manipulator)
Simple words: the rule that links joint torques to how the arm speeds up. Analogy: how hard your shoulder must work to start, stop and hold a swinging bag. Precise: M(q)q̈ + c(q, q̇) + g(q) = τ for an open chain without friction, where τ is the vector of joint torques. B1D1
Mass matrix
Simple words: how hard each joint is to speed up, and how speeding up one joint pushes on the others. Analogy: a bag held far out is harder to swing than one held close. Precise: the symmetric, positive-definite matrix M(q) with kinetic energy ½q̇TM(q)q̇; it depends on the configuration. B1D1
Coriolis and centrifugal terms
Simple words: pushes between joints that appear only while the arm moves. Analogy: the swinging bag tugging your elbow open. Precise: the vector c(q, q̇), quadratic in the joint velocities: terms in q̇i2 are centrifugal, terms in q̇iq̇j (i ≠ j) are Coriolis. B1
Gravity torque
Simple words: the torque each joint needs to hold the arm still against its own weight. Analogy: the burning shoulder holding the bag at arm's length. Precise: g(q) = ∂P/∂q, the gradient of the potential energy P(q) with respect to the joint angles. B1D1
Inverse and forward dynamics
Simple words: inverse: "what torques give this motion?"; forward: "what motion do these torques give?". Analogy: planning how hard to pull, versus feeling where the bag goes when you pull. Precise: inverse dynamics evaluates τ = M q̈ + c + g; forward dynamics solves M q̈ = τ − c − g for q̈ and is integrated in time to simulate. B1
Energy check (simulation)
Simple words: a test that a simulated arm with no motors and no friction keeps its total energy. Analogy: a playground swing never goes higher than where it was let go. Precise: with τ = 0 and no dissipation, E = ½q̇TMq̇ + P(q) is constant along exact solutions; the change of E in a simulation measures integration error. D1R1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

An arm with nothing in it but air would be easy to move. A real arm has weight, and weight does two things. First, it pulls down. Holding the arm still costs effort, and more effort the farther out the weight is, like the bag at arm's length. Second, weight makes the arm lazy: it does not want to start moving, and once moving it does not want to stop. Pushing a full shopping trolley shows the same thing.

When two parts are joined, they push each other around. Swing your upper arm and your forearm flops outwards. A robot has to plan for that flop, or its hand wobbles.

Robot builders write all of this as one rule: motor push = laziness × speeding up + flop pushes + weight. With the rule, a computer can work out the push each motor needs. It can also work out how the arm would move for a given push, which is a simulator. A good simulator obeys one more rule. A swing that nobody pushes never goes higher than where it started. If your simulated swing climbs higher and higher, the simulator is wrong, not the swing.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The model

Two links of lengths L1 = 0.30 m and L2 = 0.25 m move in a vertical plane; θ1 is measured from the horizontal and θ2 relative to link 1 (as in F9-25). A point mass m1 = 1.0 kg sits at the elbow and m2 = 0.5 kg at the tool; the links themselves are massless, there is no friction, and gravity g = 9.81 m/s2 acts along −y. These values belong to the university's simulated arm, not to a product. D1

Energy gives the equation

The positions are p1 = L1(c1, s1) and p2 = p1 + L2(c12, s12). Differentiating (F9-27) gives their speeds; the kinetic energy ½m1|ṗ1|2 + ½m2|ṗ2|2 works out to ½q̇TM(q)q̇ with D1B1

M11 = (m1 + m2) L1² + m2 L2² + 2 m2 L1 L2 cos θ2 M12 = M21 = m2 L2² + m2 L1 L2 cos θ2 M22 = m2 L2²

The potential energy is P = (m1 + m2) g L1s1 + m2 g L2s12. The Lagrange equations (B1; outside this chapter's derivation) turn these two energies into

M(q) q̈ + c(q, q̇) + g(q) = τ g(q) = ( (m1 + m2) g L1 c1 + m2 g L2 c12 , m2 g L2 c12 ) c(q, q̇) = ( −h (2 θ̇1 θ̇2 + θ̇2²) , h θ̇1² ), h = m2 L1 L2 sin θ2

Each term can be read physically. g is weight times horizontal lever arm, as in Tomás's shoulder. M changes with θ2: an outstretched arm (θ2 = 0) has M11 = 0.24125 kg m2, a folded one (180°) only 0.09125 kg m2 (run R1), so, leaving aside the coupling term, the shoulder accelerates a folded arm about 2.6 times faster for the same torque. The off-diagonal M12 means that accelerating one joint pushes on the other. c is zero when nothing moves and when the arm is straight (sin θ2 = 0). D1R1

Inverse and forward dynamics

Inverse dynamics evaluates the left side: given q, q̇ and the wanted q̈, it returns τ. A controller uses it to compute feed-forward torques for a planned trajectory. Forward dynamics solves the 2 × 2 system M q̈ = τ − c − g for q̈; M is always invertible, because kinetic energy is positive for any motion. A simulator integrates q̈ to get q̇ and q. The two must agree. Run R1 computes τ for 1,000 states and accelerations and feeds it back through forward dynamics; the accelerations come back within 4.8 × 10−14 rad/s2. B1R1

Simulating and checking

To simulate, step the state (q, q̇) forward with an integrator. With no torque and no friction, total energy must stay constant, which gives a check that does not depend on knowing the answer. With RK4 and h = 1 ms, run R1 swings the arm from the horizontal for 5 s; the largest energy change is 8.7 × 10−10 J. Gravity compensation is the second check. Applying τ = g(q) at (30°, 45°) (4.1404 and 0.3174 N m) holds the arm; after 2 s it has moved 0 rad in the printed precision. R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Structure that always holds. For any open chain, M(q) is symmetric positive definite, and with a suitable choice of the matrix C(q, q̇) in c = C(q, q̇)q̇, the matrix Ṁ − 2C is skew-symmetric. The second property expresses energy conservation, and many proofs of controller stability rest on it. B1

Efficient algorithms. Writing out M and c symbolically grows quickly with the number of joints. Recursive Newton–Euler inverse dynamics computes τ in time proportional to the number of joints: a forward pass propagates velocities and accelerations from base to tool, and a backward pass propagates forces and torques from tool to base. Forward dynamics then either builds M column by column from inverse-dynamics calls or uses an articulated-body algorithm, also linear in the number of joints. "Modern Robotics" presents the Newton–Euler recursion in its screw notation. B1

Real links have inertia tensors. A real link has a mass, a centre of mass and a 3 × 3 rotational inertia about that centre, not a point mass. These ten numbers per link (with the link frame) are what a robot description's inertial data hold (F9-29). They are hard to measure; CAD estimates miss cables and screws, and identification experiments fit them from measured torques. B1D1

Missing terms. Real joints add friction (viscous, Coulomb, and the stiction that holds a joint until a threshold), gearbox losses and backlash, and the motor rotor's own inertia multiplied by the square of the gear ratio, which often dominates M for highly geared arms. RB201 F9-08 covers motors; RB202 the controllers that cope with what the model omits. U1B1

Integrators. Explicit Euler adds energy to oscillating systems at every step; semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler, which updates velocity first and then position with the new velocity, keeps energy bounded instead of growing; RK4 has a much smaller error per step but is not exactly energy-preserving either. Run R2 measures all three on the forensic swing (Answers). Contacts, joint limits and stiff gearing need smaller steps or implicit methods. R2U1

How the hardware actually does it

A joint motor produces torque roughly in proportion to its current, through its torque constant and gear ratio (RB201). A joint driver in torque or current mode lets a controller command τ directly; many hobby servos accept only position commands and hide their torque. Gravity compensation on a real arm needs the real masses and centres of mass; a common check is to command the gravity torque, release the arm by hand, and see whether it drifts. Friction can hide model errors in this test, since a slightly wrong model is held in place by stiction. U1B1

Worked example

The simulated arm (L1 = 0.30, L2 = 0.25 m, m1 = 1.0, m2 = 0.5 kg, g = 9.81 m/s2), held outstretched and horizontal, q = (0, 0).

  1. Gravity torques. g1 = 1.5 × 9.81 × 0.30 + 0.5 × 9.81 × 0.25 = 4.4145 + 1.22625 = 5.64075 N m; g2 = 1.22625 N m.
  2. Mass matrix. M11 = 1.5 × 0.09 + 0.5 × 0.0625 + 2 × 0.5 × 0.075 = 0.24125; M12 = 0.03125 + 0.0375 = 0.06875; M22 = 0.03125 kg m2; det M = 0.0028125.
  3. Released from rest. τ = 0, q̇ = 0 so c = 0: q̈ = −M−1g. q̈1 = (−0.03125 × 5.64075 + 0.06875 × 1.22625)/0.0028125 = −32.70 rad/s2; q̈2 = (0.06875 × 5.64075 − 0.24125 × 1.22625)/0.0028125 = +32.70 rad/s2. Because q̈1 + q̈2 = 0, the forearm's absolute angle does not accelerate at first: the shoulder turns downwards and the forearm drops parallel to itself, staying horizontal for the first instant.
  4. Inverse dynamics. To accelerate the shoulder at 2 rad/s2 from rest with q̈2 = 0: τ = M(2, 0) + g = (0.4825 + 5.64075, 0.1375 + 1.22625) = (6.12325, 1.36375) N m. The elbow needs extra torque, 0.1375 N m, to keep its angle while the shoulder speeds up: the coupling term M12.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Muscles tire; motors heat. Tomás's shoulder burns while holding still because muscles spend energy even when nothing moves. A motor holding a gravity torque does no mechanical work either, but its current heats its windings; a brake or a non-back-drivable gearbox can hold the load with no current at all. D1U1
    • 

  • A body has springs; this model has none. Tendons and muscles are elastic and store energy in a swing; the chapter's model is rigid and frictionless. D1
    • 

  • A real swing slows down. A playground swing loses energy to friction and air; "never goes higher" is the honest rule, "stays at the same height for ever" holds only in the frictionless model. D1
    •

Lab


Goal


Build and verify a dynamics simulator for the course arm in the vertical plane, then use inverse dynamics to compute feed-forward torques for a planned motion and check them in the simulator.


Hardware and software needed


A computer with a C++ compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 with the flags of the build command. Optional: the course kit's arm for step 6 (untested on hardware).


Safety


Safety

Steps 1–5 are software only. Step 6 commands torques on a real arm: a wrong sign in a gravity model makes the arm accelerate instead of hold. Start with the torque limit at a small fraction of the rated value, support the arm by hand at first, keep hands clear of pinch points, emergency stop in reach; learners below L3 with adult supervision.

Expected observations


The tests pass at the same orders of magnitude as run R1. Open-loop feed-forward follows the plan closely in simulation (the model is exact there) and drifts slowly; with feedback it holds. On real hardware (untested), expect friction and model errors to show up as drift and offsets.


Verification


Your program exits with code 0 when the round trip is below 10−9 rad/s2, the compensated arm moves less than a threshold you state, and the energy change of a 5 s free swing with your integrator and step is below a threshold you state and justify.

  • Energy test fails only when gravity is on → g(q) inconsistent with P(q); check that g is the derivative of P.

  • Energy test fails only when moving with gravity off → c or M wrong.
    • 

  • Feed-forward overshoots everywhere → your planned accelerations are in degrees per second squared.

Summary


    

  • M(q)q̈ + c(q, q̇) + g(q) = τ: inertia, motion coupling and weight balance the joint torques.
    • 

  • g is weight times horizontal lever arm; M is symmetric, positive definite and depends on the pose; c is zero at rest.
    • 

  • Inverse dynamics gives torques for a motion; forward dynamics gives motion for torques and drives simulators.
    • 

  • Verify a dynamics model with a round trip, a gravity-compensation hold and an energy test.
    • 

  • Explicit Euler adds energy to swinging systems; RK4 or semi-implicit Euler with a suitable step does much better.
    • 

  • Real arms add friction, rotor inertia and unknown masses: the model is a starting point for control, not the truth.
