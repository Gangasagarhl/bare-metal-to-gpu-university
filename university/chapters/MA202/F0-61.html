Story hook

Analogy

Samira is reading on the sofa when the family dog starts barking at the front door. Is someone there? Her little brother Yusuf jumps up: "The dog always barks when someone comes, so someone must be at the door!"

Samira is not so sure. Visitors are rare on a weekday afternoon. And the dog barks at many things: the neighbour's cat, a delivery van next door, leaves blowing past. She thinks: "The dog barks at almost every visitor, yes. But most of the barking I hear is not about visitors." A moment later the doorbell rings. Now she is nearly certain, and goes to open.

Samira did three things without writing a formula. She started from how likely a visitor was before any clue (the prior). She weighed how well each clue fits "visitor" compared with "no visitor" (the likelihood). And she combined them into a new, updated belief (the posterior). That is Bayes' rule. Robots use it every time a sensor reports something, and it is the first step on the road to the Kalman filter of RB302.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute conditional probabilities and explain why P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different numbers.
  • Apply the law of total probability and Bayes' rule, with probabilities and with natural frequencies (counts).
  • Explain the base-rate effect: why a good detector of a rare fault produces mostly false alarms.
  • Update a belief step by step with a sequence of noisy readings (a two-state Bayes filter), in C++.
  • Diagnose a claim such as "the detector is 98 % accurate, so 98 % of its alarms are real" from a day's inspection log.
Jargon box
Conditional probability
Simple words: the chance of something, given that you already know something else happened. Analogy: the chance that a visitor is at the door, given that the dog is barking. Precise: P(A | B) = P(A and B)/P(B), defined when P(B) > 0; read "probability of A given B". In general P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A). D1
Law of total probability
Simple words: add up all the separate ways something can happen. Analogy: the dog barks either because of a visitor or because of something else; the total barking is both added. Precise: if H and not-H split all possibilities, P(E) = P(E | H) P(H) + P(E | not H) P(not H); more generally the sum over any partition. D1
Bayes' rule
Simple words: the recipe for turning "how likely is this clue if my idea is true" into "how likely is my idea, now that I have seen this clue". Analogy: Samira's reasoning about the barking dog. Precise: P(H | E) = P(E | H) P(H) / P(E), with P(E) from the law of total probability. D1
Prior, likelihood, posterior
Simple words: what you believed before the clue; how well the clue fits each possibility; what you believe after. Analogy: "visitors are rare"; "the dog barks at 9 out of 10 visitors but also often at nothing"; "now I think a visitor is fairly likely". Precise: the prior is P(H); the likelihood is P(E | H) viewed as a function of the hypothesis H; the posterior is P(H | E). D1D2
Base rate
Simple words: how common something is before you test for it. Analogy: how many afternoons out of ten bring a visitor at all. Precise: the prior probability of a condition in the population being tested; ignoring it when interpreting a test result is the base-rate fallacy. D1
Sensitivity and false-alarm rate
Simple words: how often a detector catches a real problem; how often it complains when there is none. Analogy: how often the dog barks at a real visitor; how often it barks at nothing. Precise: sensitivity (true-positive rate) = P(alarm | condition); false-alarm rate (false-positive rate) = P(alarm | no condition); 1 − false-alarm rate is called specificity. D1
Odds and likelihood ratio
Simple words: odds compare "yes" to "no" (1 to 9); the likelihood ratio says how many times better the clue fits "yes" than "no". Analogy: barking is 4.5 times more common with a visitor than without. Precise: odds(H) = P(H)/P(not H); likelihood ratio = P(E | H)/P(E | not H); Bayes' rule in odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. D1
Bayes filter
Simple words: applying Bayes' rule again and again, each new reading updating the last belief. Analogy: Samira's belief after the bark becomes her prior when the bell rings. Precise: a recursive estimator that keeps a probability distribution (belief) over states and, for each measurement z, sets belief(x) ← η · P(z | x) · belief(x), where η normalises; with motion, a prediction step comes before each update (RB302, F9-31). D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

"The dog barks when a visitor comes" and "a visitor has come when the dog barks" sound alike. They are not the same. The first is about visitors: almost all of them get barked at. The second is about barks: and most barks are about cats and vans, because cats and vans pass far more often than visitors do.

So to judge a clue, you need two things. First: how common is the thing you are wondering about, before any clue? Visitors are rare. Second: how much more often does the clue happen when the thing is true than when it is false? The dog barks at most visitors, but also at plenty of other things. A clue that happens a lot either way tells you little.

The easiest way to get the answer right is to imagine many afternoons and count. Out of 100 ten-minute stretches, a visitor comes in 10. The dog barks in 9 of those 10. In the other 90 stretches, the dog barks at something else 18 times. So there are 27 barks, and only 9 of them are about visitors: one in three.

New clues keep updating the belief. After the bark, Samira thought "one in three". Then the doorbell rang, which almost never happens without a visitor. Her belief jumped to nearly certain. Each clue starts from the belief the last clue left behind. That chain of updates is what a robot's software does with every sensor reading.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A and B)/P(B): restrict attention to the outcomes where B happened, and ask what share of them also has A. With two dice (F0-56), P(total = 8 | red die = 6) = 1/6 (only blue = 2), while P(red die = 6 | total = 8) = 1/5 (five boxes total 8, one of them has red = 6). Rearranged, P(A and B) = P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A): the multiplication rule of F0-56's tree. A and B are independent exactly when P(A | B) = P(A). D1W1

Bayes' rule and total probability

Setting the two forms of P(H and E) equal gives Bayes' rule:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E), with P(E) = P(E | H) P(H) + P(E | not H) P(not H).

For the dog: H = "visitor", P(H) = 0.1 per ten-minute stretch, P(bark | H) = 0.9, P(bark | not H) = 0.2 (exercise values). P(bark) = 0.9 × 0.1 + 0.2 × 0.9 = 0.27, and P(H | bark) = 0.09/0.27 = 1/3 (run R4). D1R4

For the detector of Figure 1: P(alarm) = 0.95 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.059, P(faulty | alarm) = 0.0095/0.059 ≈ 0.161. And the other direction of evidence: P(faulty | no alarm) ≈ 0.00053, so a quiet detector is very reassuring even though an alarm is not very alarming (run R1). D1R1

Odds form

Dividing Bayes' rule for H by Bayes' rule for not-H cancels P(E): posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. For the dog: prior odds 0.1 : 0.9 = 1 : 9; likelihood ratio 0.9/0.2 = 4.5; posterior odds 4.5 : 9 = 1 : 2, which is probability 1/3. The odds form makes the two ingredients visible separately: the base rate (prior odds) and the strength of the clue (likelihood ratio). A clue with ratio 1 changes nothing, however often it occurs. D1R4

The base-rate effect

When the condition is rare, even a good detector produces mostly false alarms, because false alarms are a small fraction of a very large group. The same detector gives P(faulty | alarm) ≈ 0.16 when 1 % of parts are faulty and ≈ 0.68 when 10 % are (run R4). The detector did not change; the population did. This is why an intrusion-detection system with a low false-alarm rate can still flood its operators with false alarms, and why F0-59's 3σ threshold rings on healthy fridges: rare events times many trials. D1R4

Updating again and again

The posterior after one clue is the prior for the next, provided the clues are conditionally independent given the hypothesis (the doorbell does not ring because the dog barked). After the bark, P(visitor) = 1/3; the doorbell rings with probability 0.7 for a visitor and 0.01 otherwise; Bayes' rule gives P(visitor | bark, bell) ≈ 0.972. A second, independent alarm on the same part raises the detector's 0.161 to about 0.785 (run R4). This repeated update, with a normalisation at each step, is the Bayes filter of Figure 2. D1D2R4

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Belief over many states. With more than two hypotheses (a robot that could be in any of 100 cells of a corridor), the belief is a whole distribution. The update is the same: multiply each state's prior by the likelihood of the measurement in that state, then divide by the sum so that the belief adds up to 1. The normalising constant η = 1/P(z) is the total probability of the measurement. D2

Prediction and update. A moving robot alternates two steps. Prediction spreads the belief according to the motion model (the robot moved about one cell, give or take), which increases uncertainty. Update sharpens it with the measurement (Bayes' rule), which decreases uncertainty. RB302 builds the general Bayes filter in F9-31; when the belief is Gaussian and the models are linear, the same two steps become the Kalman filter's formulas for a mean and a variance (F0-59's closure under addition is what keeps the belief Gaussian). D2U1

Overconfidence is the classic failure. If the sensor model claims readings are more reliable than they are, each update moves the belief too far, and a few wrong readings make the filter certain of the wrong answer. RB302's forensic lab ("the overconfident filter") is this mistake with a Kalman filter. The cure is to measure the sensor's error rates or noise (F0-62) instead of guessing them. D2U1

Numerics. Multiplying many likelihoods smaller than 1 underflows towards 0 in floating point. Filters therefore normalise after every update (as Listing 2 does), or add logarithms of likelihoods instead of multiplying them. D3W1

How the hardware actually does it

A sensor never says "the door is open". It produces a number (a distance, a light level), and software turns that number into a decision or a likelihood. The likelihoods P(reading | state) come from characterising the sensor: placing it in each known state many times and counting how often each reading occurs, which is exactly the measurement work of F0-62. The values 0.6 and 0.2 in Listing 2 are exercise numbers; for a real sensor they must be measured, and they depend on the surface, the light, the distance and the sensor's own datasheet limits. D2W1

On the computer, a two-state update is a handful of multiplications and one division; a grid of thousands of states is the same work repeated per cell, which is independent from cell to cell and therefore parallelises well (the reason some localisation methods run on GPUs). The probabilities are ordinary double values; the danger is not speed but underflow and lost normalisation (Layer 3). D2D3

Worked example

Samira's afternoon, with exercise numbers: in a given ten-minute stretch a visitor comes with probability 0.1. The dog barks with probability 0.9 if a visitor comes and 0.2 if not. The doorbell rings with probability 0.7 if a visitor comes and 0.01 if not.

  1. Before any clue: P(visitor) = 0.1; odds 1 : 9.
  2. Total probability of barking: P(bark) = 0.9 × 0.1 + 0.2 × 0.9 = 0.27.
  3. After the bark: P(visitor | bark) = 0.09/0.27 = 1/3. Odds check: 1 : 9 × 4.5 = 1 : 2.
  4. Natural frequencies: in 100 stretches: 10 with a visitor (9 barked), 90 without (18 barked). 9 of 27 barks are visitors.
  5. After the bell: prior 1/3, likelihood ratio 0.7/0.01 = 70. Posterior odds 1 : 2 × 70 = 35 : 1, so P ≈ 0.972.
  6. The bell alone (from the original prior 0.1) would give ≈ 0.886: the bark added real information, but much less than the bell, because its likelihood ratio is 4.5 against 70.
  7. Yusuf's mistake: he used P(bark | visitor) = 0.9 as if it were P(visitor | bark). That swap ("the transposed conditional") is the most common error with Bayes' rule.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Samira's numbers are feelings; a filter's numbers must be measured. At home, "visitors are rare" is a rough sense. In a robot, the prior and the likelihoods are model parameters, and wrong values make the filter confidently wrong (Layer 3). D2
    • 

  • Clues at home are rarely independent. The dog barks because the bell rang, sometimes. Multiplying likelihoods as if the clues were independent double-counts shared evidence. Sensors that share a cause (F0-60) have the same problem. D1
    • 

  • The world changes between clues. The visitor may leave while Samira walks to the door. A robot's Bayes filter handles this with the prediction step; the simple repeated update of Listing 2 assumes nothing changes between readings. D2
    •

Lab


Goal


Measure a simple "detector" at home, estimate its sensitivity and false-alarm rate from counts, and predict with Bayes' rule how many of its alarms are real for two different base rates.


Hardware and software needed


A detector you can test by observation, for example: "the kettle's switch clicks off ⇒ the water boiled" or a friend guessing which hand hides a coin from your face. A notebook for counts. A computer with a C++ compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0.


Safety


If you use the kettle, an adult handles the hot water. Otherwise none beyond normal computer use.


Steps


    

  1. Run at least 40 trials with the condition true and false in known proportions (for the coin game: you decide in advance, by a die roll, which hand has the coin).
    2. 

  2. Fill a 2 × 2 table: condition true/false × detector says yes/no.
    3. 

  3. Estimate sensitivity = (true and yes)/(true) and false-alarm rate = (false and yes)/(false).
    4. 

  4. Change Listing 1's three inputs to your estimates and two base rates: 50 % and 5 %.
    5. 

  5. Compare the printed P(condition | alarm) for both base rates and write one sentence on why they differ.
    6. 

  6. Extend Listing 2 so that it reads its sequence of readings from standard input, and feed it your detector's answers on one fixed condition.
    7. 



Expected observations


With a 50 % base rate, P(condition | alarm) is close to what the detector's accuracy suggests; at 5 % it falls sharply, as in run R1 (1 % base rate, 0.161) and run R4 (10 % base rate, 0.679). Estimates from 40 trials are rough: if one cell of your table has 0 or 1 counts, say so.


Verification


The counts version and the formula version of Listing 1 agree; your hand calculation for one base rate matches the program; the updated Listing 2 reproduces run R2 when fed the same six readings.


Troubleshooting


    

  • Posterior exactly 0 or 1 → a sensitivity or false-alarm estimate of exactly 0 or 1 from too few trials → add trials, or report the estimate as "below 1 in 40".
    • 

  • Counts and formula differ in the third digit → rounding of the counts to whole parts → use more parts (100,000) in the counts version.
    • 

  • Belief goes the wrong way for a "closed" reading → likeOpen and likeClosed not complemented for "closed" → test with one reading by hand (Check yourself 5).

Summary


    

  • P(A | B) = P(A and B)/P(B); in general P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A).
    • 

  • Law of total probability: P(E) = P(E | H)P(H) + P(E | not H)P(not H).
    • 

  • Bayes' rule: P(H | E) = P(E | H)P(H)/P(E). Odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio.
    • 

  • A good detector of a rare condition produces mostly false alarms: always include the base rate.
    • 

  • Counting with natural frequencies (out of 10,000) is the safest way to get Bayes problems right.
    • 

  • A Bayes filter repeats the update: the posterior after one reading is the prior for the next; normalise every time.
    • 

  • Sensor models (likelihoods) must be measured; overconfident models make filters wrong with certainty.
    •
