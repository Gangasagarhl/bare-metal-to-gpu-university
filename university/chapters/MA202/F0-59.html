Story hook

Analogy

Nadia's family has a digital thermometer on the kitchen wall. The room is quiet, the heating steady, yet the last digit never stops moving: 20.1, 19.8, 20.4, 19.9, 20.0. Nadia's brother Tariq writes down a reading every few seconds for an afternoon and makes a histogram, like the ones in F0-57.

The bars form a smooth hill. It is highest in the middle, falls off evenly on both sides, and has thin tails that never quite reach zero. Tariq tries again with the readings of his own step counter's daily totals over a month, then with the weights of a bag of apples, one apple at a time. Again and again, the same hill appears.

That shape is the Gaussian, also called the normal distribution or the bell curve. It is the default model for measurement noise, the noise model inside every Kalman filter in RB302, and the shape behind statements like "within three sigma". It also has limits: some things that look like hills are not Gaussian, and an alarm built on the wrong assumption can ring all day, as the forensic lab of this chapter shows.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the Gaussian density with mean μ and standard deviation σ, and explain what each part of the formula does.
  • Convert a value to a z-score and use the 68 %, 95 % and 99.7 % rules and the Gaussian CDF to compute probabilities.
  • Explain why sums of many small independent effects tend to be Gaussian (the central limit theorem), and recognise data that are not Gaussian.
  • Generate Gaussian random numbers from uniform ones and check them against the density in C++.
  • Diagnose a "3σ" alarm threshold computed with the variance instead of the standard deviation.
Jargon box
Gaussian (normal) distribution
Simple words: the smooth, symmetric hill shape that many measurements make when you draw their histogram. Analogy: the thermometer readings piling up around the true room temperature, fewer and fewer further out. Precise: the continuous distribution N(μ, σ2) with density f(x) = exp(−(x − μ)2/(2σ2)) / (σ√(2π)); its mean is μ and its standard deviation σ. D1
Standard Gaussian
Simple words: the Gaussian with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, the "unit" bell. Analogy: the thermometer's wobble measured in "wobble units" instead of degrees. Precise: Z ~ N(0, 1); its CDF is written Φ(z) and equals (1 + erf(z/√2))/2. D1D2
z-score
Simple words: how many standard deviations a value is above (positive) or below (negative) the mean. Analogy: "this reading is two wobbles too high". Precise: z = (x − μ)/σ; if X ~ N(μ, σ2), then Z = (X − μ)/σ ~ N(0, 1). D1
Error function (erf)
Simple words: a ready-made function that gives Gaussian probabilities, because the bell's area has no simple formula. Analogy: a measuring cup already marked for the bell shape. Precise: erf(x) = (2/√π) × the area under exp(−t2) from 0 to x; P(|Z| < k) = erf(k/√2). C++ provides std::erf and std::erfc = 1 − erf in <cmath>. D1D2
Central limit theorem
Simple words: add up many small, independent random nudges, and the total has a bell shape, whatever the shape of the nudges. Analogy: the thermometer's wobble is the sum of many tiny electrical and air-current effects. Precise: for independent, identically distributed Xi with mean μ and finite variance σ2, the distribution of (X1 + … + Xn − nμ)/(σ√n) approaches N(0, 1) as n grows. D1
Tail (of a distribution)
Simple words: the far ends of the hill, where values are rare. Analogy: the afternoon reading that is unusually high. Precise: the probability P(X > t) (upper tail) or P(X < −t) (lower tail) for large t. A distribution is heavy-tailed when its tails fall much more slowly than a Gaussian's with the same σ. D1
Box–Muller transform
Simple words: a recipe that turns two uniform random numbers into Gaussian ones. Analogy: two fair spinners combined so that their result lands in a bell shape. Precise: for independent U1, U2 uniform on (0, 1), Z = √(−2 ln U1) cos(2π U2) is standard Gaussian (and the same with sin gives a second, independent one). D1R1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Many measurements wobble around a centre. Small wobbles are common; big wobbles are rare; up-wobbles and down-wobbles are equally likely. When you draw a histogram of such readings, you get a hill with a smooth top and thin sides. Mathematicians have one exact formula for that hill: the Gaussian.

A Gaussian needs only two numbers. The mean says where the top of the hill is. The standard deviation says how wide the hill is. Nadia's thermometer has mean 20 °C and standard deviation 0.5 °C in this chapter's example. Once you know those two numbers, you know the whole hill.

The hill comes with a handy rule. About two readings in three lie within one standard deviation of the mean: between 19.5 and 20.5 °C. About 95 in 100 lie within two: between 19 and 21 °C. Nearly all, 997 in 1,000, lie within three: between 18.5 and 21.5 °C. A reading of 22 °C would be four standard deviations away. That almost never happens by wobble alone, so something real has changed.

Why does this shape turn up so often? Because a wobble is usually the total of many tiny pushes: a draught, a flicker in the electronics, a warm hand nearby. Add up many small random pushes and the total always forms this hill. But not everything is made that way. How long a program takes to run is not: it can be a bit faster than usual, or a lot slower, but never a lot faster. Its hill is lopsided, and F0-63 treats it differently.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The formula, piece by piece

f(x) = exp(−(x − μ)2/(2σ2)) / (σ√(2π)).

  • (x − μ)2: the squared distance from the mean, the same quantity the variance averages (F0-58). Squaring makes the curve symmetric.
  • Dividing by 2σ2: measures that distance in units of σ. In z-score form the exponent is −z2/2.
  • exp(−…): equals 1 at the mean and falls off very fast: at z = 1 it is about 0.61, at z = 3 about 0.011.
  • 1/(σ√(2π)): the constant that makes the total area exactly 1. A smaller σ makes a taller, narrower hill. For σ = 0.5 °C the peak is about 0.80 per °C (run R4); for the standard Gaussian, about 0.40.

The mean of N(μ, σ2) is μ and its standard deviation is σ, so the two parameters are exactly the two numbers of F0-58. D1R4

Standardising and the CDF

If X ~ N(μ, σ2), then Z = (X − μ)/σ ~ N(0, 1). This means one table, or one function, answers every Gaussian question: P(X ≤ x) = Φ((x − μ)/σ), with Φ(z) = (1 + erf(z/√2))/2. Examples for the thermometer N(20, 0.52), all computed by run R4: D1D2R4

  • P(T < 19) = Φ((19 − 20)/0.5) = Φ(−2) ≈ 0.0228.
  • P(19.5 < T < 21) = Φ(2) − Φ(−1) ≈ 0.977 − 0.159 = 0.819.
  • Within k σ: P(|Z| < 1) ≈ 0.683, P(|Z| < 2) ≈ 0.954, P(|Z| < 3) ≈ 0.9973. The interval ±1.96σ holds 0.950.
  • One tail: P(Z > 3) ≈ 0.00135, about 1 in 741.

Sums and averages of Gaussians

The Gaussian is closed under adding: if X ~ N(μ1, σ12) and Y ~ N(μ2, σ22) are independent, X + Y ~ N(μ1 + μ2, σ12 + σ22). The means add and the variances add (F0-58); being Gaussian is extra. Example: N(1, 0.32) plus N(2, 0.42) is N(3, 0.52). The mean of 4 independent thermometer readings is N(20, 0.252): still Gaussian, half as wide. This closure is why a Kalman filter (RB302) can carry a whole belief as only a mean and a variance. D1D3R4

The central limit theorem in practice

The central limit theorem says that a standardised sum of many independent, identically distributed terms with finite variance approaches N(0, 1), whatever the shape of the terms. Run R2 adds 12 uniform values (each flat, nothing like a bell) and subtracts 6: the result has mean ≈ 0 and sd ≈ 1 by F0-58's rules, and 67.7 % of 200,000 sums lie within one sd, against 68.3 % for a Gaussian, and 95.6 % within two, against 95.4 %. A single uniform value has 57.7 % within one sd: not bell-shaped at all. D1R2

The theorem explains why measurement noise is often close to Gaussian: it is usually the total of many small independent disturbances. It does not promise that everything is Gaussian. Values bounded on one side (times, counts of rare events), mixtures of two situations, and values driven by a few big causes are often not. D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Tails decide the risk. Run R2 also draws an exponential variable (one standard model for waiting times) with mean 1 and sd 1 (measured: 1.001 and 1.000). Within two sd it holds 95.0 %, almost like a Gaussian, so the middle looks fine. But 1.87 % of its values lie above +3 sd, against 0.135 % for a Gaussian: fourteen times more extreme values. Meanwhile the sum of 12 uniforms has lighter tails than a Gaussian (0.094 % above +3 sd), because it can never go beyond ±6. Two variables can agree in mean and sd and disagree by a factor of ten in the tail. Alarm thresholds, timeouts and "worst case" estimates live in the tail, so check the tail with data, not with the bell. D1R2

Many readings make rare events common. A one-sided 3σ threshold is exceeded by Gaussian noise with probability 0.00135. A sensor read 10,000 times a day therefore crosses it about 13.5 times a day with nothing wrong (run R4). Thresholds must be chosen for the number of tests made, not for one reading, or combined with a rule such as "several readings in a row". R4

Generating Gaussians. Listing 1 uses the Box–Muller transform: from two independent uniforms U1, U2 on (0, 1), Z = √(−2 ln U1) cos(2πU2). The radius √(−2 ln U1) and the uniform angle 2πU2 together give a point whose x coordinate is standard Gaussian. The uniform01 of F0-57 never returns 0, so the logarithm is always finite. The C++ library also offers std::normal_distribution; this course writes its own transform so that the printed outputs depend only on the fully specified std::mt19937 engine and on <cmath>, whereas the algorithm inside std::normal_distribution is left to each library. D1D2R1

Several variables at once. A robot's position (x, y) with uncertainty is described by a two-dimensional Gaussian: a mean vector and a covariance matrix (F0-60, using MA201's matrices). Its "bands" are ellipses instead of intervals. RB302 draws them as the uncertainty ellipses of the Kalman filter's predict–update cycle. D3U1

How the hardware actually does it

Electronic measurement chains add many small, independent disturbances: thermal motion of charges in resistors, fluctuations in amplifiers, interference. Their sum is the main reason Gaussian noise is the standard first model for a sensor's reading-to-reading wobble. HW302 (F1-72) names the physical sources; F0-62 measures the result. Not every source fits: the digital rounding of an analog-to-digital converter is closer to uniform than Gaussian, and occasional glitches (a bad cable, a missed sample) produce heavy tails. D4D1

Inside the computer, std::exp, std::log, std::cos and std::erf are library functions that compute floating-point approximations. Their last digits can differ between libraries and processors; that is one reason the outputs of this chapter are recorded with the toolchain that produced them. D2

Not verified — check before relying on this

The accuracy guarantees of std::erf, std::exp and std::log (how many units in the last place they may be off) were not checked in this build; the C++ standard largely defers to the C standard's mathematical library, and implementations document their own accuracy. Check ISO/IEC 14882's <cmath> clause and your C library's documentation before relying on the last digits of R1, R2 or R4.

Worked example

Nadia's kitchen thermometer, with the heating steady, gives readings modelled as N(20 °C, (0.5 °C)2).

  1. z-score of a reading of 21.2 °C. z = (21.2 − 20)/0.5 = 2.4.
  2. How unusual is a reading this high? P(T > 21.2) = 1 − Φ(2.4) ≈ 0.0082, about 1 reading in 120. With a reading every few seconds, that happens several times an hour without any change in the room.
  3. Band holding 99.7 % of readings: μ ± 3σ = 18.5 to 21.5 °C.
  4. Too cold: P(T < 19) = Φ(−2) ≈ 0.023.
  5. Averaging 4 readings gives N(20, 0.252). The same 21.2 °C is now z = 1.2/0.25 = 4.8 away for the average: an average that high means the room really changed.
  6. Alarm design. Rule "alarm when one reading exceeds μ + 3σ = 21.5 °C": false-alarm probability 0.00135 per reading. Rule "alarm when the average of 4 readings exceeds 20 + 3 × 0.25 = 20.75 °C": the same false-alarm probability per average, but it detects a real rise of 1 °C much more reliably, because the noise of the average is smaller.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The thermometer's wobble is not exactly Gaussian. Its display shows one decimal, so readings come in steps of 0.1 °C: the true distribution is discrete. The Gaussian is a model that fits well in the middle and must be checked in the tails. D1
    • 

  • "Many tiny nudges" is not a guarantee. The central limit theorem needs many independent contributions with finite variance, none of them dominant. A single big cause (the oven door opening) produces a separate bump or a long tail, not a wider bell. D1
    • 

  • Real readings are bounded. The Gaussian gives a tiny but non-zero probability to −300 °C. Real sensors have limits and saturate (F0-57), so the model is wrong far out, even when it is excellent near the middle.
    •

Summary


    

  • N(μ, σ2) has density exp(−(x − μ)2/(2σ2))/(σ√(2π)); μ sets the centre and σ the width.
    • 

  • z = (x − μ)/σ turns every Gaussian question into a standard-Gaussian one: P(X ≤ x) = Φ(z) = (1 + erf(z/√2))/2.
    • 

  • About 68.3 %, 95.4 % and 99.7 % of values lie within 1, 2 and 3 σ; one tail beyond 3σ holds about 0.135 %.
    • 

  • Independent Gaussians add to a Gaussian; means and variances add.
    • 

  • The central limit theorem explains why sums of many small independent effects look Gaussian; it does not make everything Gaussian.
    • 

  • Tails matter most and fail first: skewed data such as run times can have ten times more extreme values than a Gaussian with the same σ.
    • 

  • A threshold must use σ, not σ2, and must account for how many readings are tested.
    •
