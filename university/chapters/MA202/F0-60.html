Story hook

Analogy

Hana keeps a small notebook by the kitchen window. Each afternoon at three o'clock she writes two numbers: how many hours the sun has shone that day, and the kitchen temperature. After a week she notices a pattern. Sunny days come with a warm kitchen; grey days with a cooler one. The two numbers seem to rise and fall together.

Her cousin Jonas has a different problem. He hung two thermometers side by side and averages them, because his teacher said that averaging two independent readings makes the noise smaller. But his average wobbles almost as much as each thermometer alone. The two thermometers rise and fall together too, and that spoils his plan.

How strongly two quantities move together is measured by the covariance and the correlation. They tell a robot how its x and y position errors are linked, tell a drone whether two sensors share a fault, and tell an engineer whether averaging will help. This chapter teaches how to compute them, how to read them, and the three ways they most often mislead.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute the covariance and the correlation coefficient of paired data, by hand and in C++.
  • Read a scatter plot and a correlation value, and explain what r = 0.96, r = −0.7 and r = 0 do and do not mean.
  • Apply Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y) to sums, differences and averages of correlated readings.
  • Write a 2 × 2 covariance matrix and say what each entry means.
  • Distinguish uncorrelated from independent, and correlation from causation.
  • Diagnose an average of two sensors that does not reduce the noise, by estimating their correlation from the evidence.
Jargon box
Paired data and scatter plot
Simple words: two numbers measured together each time, drawn as one dot per pair. Analogy: each line of Hana's notebook: sunshine and temperature on the same afternoon. Precise: observations (xi, yi), i = 1 … n, of two random variables X and Y; the scatter plot puts a point at each (xi, yi). D1
Covariance
Simple words: a number that is positive when two quantities tend to be above their averages at the same time, negative when one tends to be above while the other is below. Analogy: sunshine and kitchen warmth going up together. Precise: Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)] = E[XY] − μXμY; from data, sxy = Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ)/(n − 1). Its unit is the product of the two units. Cov(X, X) = Var(X). D1
Correlation coefficient
Simple words: the covariance rescaled to lie between −1 and +1, so it has no units. Analogy: a score for "how neatly do the notebook's dots line up?". Precise: ρ = Cov(X, Y)/(σXσY), estimated from data by r = sxy/(sxsy) (Pearson's correlation coefficient). |r| = 1 exactly when the points lie on a straight line; r measures linear association only. D1
Uncorrelated versus independent
Simple words: uncorrelated means "no straight-line tendency"; independent means "knowing one tells you nothing at all about the other". Analogy: the height of a ball thrown up and down is fully determined by time, yet over a symmetric flight it is uncorrelated with the time measured from the top. Precise: independence implies Cov = 0; Cov = 0 does not imply independence (example: Y = X2 with X symmetric about 0). D1R2
Covariance matrix
Simple words: a table holding all the variances and covariances of several quantities at once. Analogy: one page of Hana's notebook that summarises how every column wobbles and how every pair of columns moves together. Precise: for a random vector (X1, …, Xk), the k × k matrix Σ with entries Σij = Cov(Xi, Xj); the diagonal holds the variances, and Σ is symmetric (MA201 F0-51, transpose). D1D2
Common cause (confounder)
Simple words: a third thing that pushes two quantities at once, so they move together although neither pushes the other. Analogy: the one power adapter that makes both of Jonas's thermometers wobble together. Precise: a variable Z that influences both X and Y; it produces correlation between X and Y without any causal link between them. D1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1, left: Hana's six afternoons as a scatter plot. The dashed lines cross at the two means. Every point in the top-right or bottom-left region has deviations of the same sign, so the product (x − x̄)(y − ȳ) is positive; points in the other two regions give negative products. All six points sit in the positive regions or close to the lines, so the covariance is positive and the correlation is close to 1. Right: points on the curve y = x2. Y depends completely on X, yet the positive products on the right cancel the negative ones on the left, and the correlation is zero.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Look at one line of Hana's notebook. Was the sunshine above or below its usual amount that day? Was the temperature above or below its usual value? If both were above, or both below, the two numbers agreed. If one was up and the other down, they disagreed.

Covariance counts agreement. For each day, multiply "how far sunshine was from its average" by "how far temperature was from its average". Up times up gives a positive number. Down times down also gives a positive number, because minus times minus is plus. Up times down gives a negative number. Add the products and divide by n − 1: a big positive total means the two usually agree.

The covariance has awkward units, here "hours times degrees". To get a clean score, divide by the two standard deviations. The result is the correlation, always between −1 and +1. Plus 1 means the dots lie exactly on a rising line. Minus 1 means a falling line. Zero means no straight-line pattern at all.

Two warnings. First, zero correlation does not mean "no connection": the dots can follow a curve, as on the right of Figure 1. Second, a strong correlation does not tell you which one causes the other, or whether a third thing causes both. Jonas's two thermometers do not push each other; one power adapter pushes both.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Definitions

Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)]. From n pairs: sxy = Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ)/(n − 1), with the same n − 1 as the sample variance (F0-58). The correlation coefficient is r = sxy/(sxsy), where the (n − 1)s cancel: r = Sxy/√(SxxSyy) with the raw sums Sxy = Σ dx·dy, Sxx = Σ dx2, Syy = Σ dy2. D1

Properties you will use: Cov(X, Y) = Cov(Y, X); Cov(X, X) = Var(X); Cov(aX + b, cY + d) = ac Cov(X, Y), so changing units scales the covariance but leaves |r| unchanged; −1 ≤ r ≤ 1, with |r| = 1 only for an exact straight line. D1

Reading r

Run R2 computes r for four simulated relationships with 10,000 points each. y = 2x plus small noise: r ≈ 0.970. y = −x plus noise as large as x: r ≈ −0.700 (theory −1/√2 ≈ −0.707). An independent value: r ≈ −0.014, which is zero up to random variation. y = x2: r ≈ −0.001, although y is a function of x. The size of |r| measures how tightly the points hug a line, not how steep the line is. D1R2

Variances of sums and differences

F0-58 said variances add for independent variables. The general rule is

Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y), and Var(X − Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) − 2 Cov(X, Y).

Example: Var(X) = 4, Var(Y) = 9, Cov = 3 (so r = 0.5): Var(X + Y) = 19 and Var(X − Y) = 7 (run R4). Positive correlation makes sums wobble more and differences wobble less. D1R4

The rule has a direct consequence for averaging two sensors with the same σ and correlation ρ: Var((A + B)/2) = (σ2 + σ2 + 2ρσ2)/4 = σ2(1 + ρ)/2. The noise of the average is σ√((1 + ρ)/2): 0.71σ for independent sensors, 0.87σ for ρ = 0.5, 0.97σ for ρ = 0.88, and no improvement at all for ρ = 1 (run R4). Averaging only removes the part of the noise the sensors do not share. D1R4

The covariance matrix

For two variables, the covariance matrix is

Σ = [[Var(X), Cov(X, Y)], [Cov(X, Y), Var(Y)]].

For Hana's data, Σ = [[8.4, 5.2], [5.2, 3.47]] in units of hours2, hour·°C and °C2 (run R1). A robot's position estimate (x, y) carries such a matrix: large diagonal entries mean large uncertainty along an axis, and the off-diagonal entry says whether errors in x and y lean together, which tilts the uncertainty ellipse. With MA201's matrix algebra, the variance of any combination a·X + b·Y is the number (a, b) Σ (a, b)T, which reproduces the sum rule above for a = b = 1. D1D2R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The best straight line. The line that minimises the sum of squared vertical distances to the points (least squares) has slope b = sxy/sx2 and passes through (x̄, ȳ). For Hana's data b = 5.2/8.4 ≈ 0.62 °C per hour of sunshine and the intercept is about 17.6 °C (run R4). The correlation is the slope you get after converting both variables to z-scores. RB302's Kalman gain is built from the same ingredient: a covariance divided by a variance. D1D2R4

Correlation is not causation. A correlation can come from X causing Y, Y causing X, a common cause Z, a coincidence in a small sample, or the way the data were selected. Hana's sunshine plausibly warms the kitchen, but the data alone cannot prove it: on sunny days the family might also bake more. Establishing cause needs a controlled change (switch one thing, hold the others fixed) or a model of the mechanism. D1

Small samples correlate by accident. With six points, a sizeable r can arise between unrelated variables purely by chance; with 10,000 points, run R2's independent pair gave |r| ≈ 0.014. Report n with every r. D1R2

Correlated failures. F0-56's formula 1 − (1 − p)n assumed independent machines. Machines sharing a power feed, a network switch, a software version or a batch of disks have positively correlated failures: they fail together more often than independence predicts, which is exactly the case redundancy is meant to survive. Distributed-systems courses (F5) design for this by placing replicas so that their failures are as uncorrelated as possible. D1U1

How the hardware actually does it

Sensor noise is correlated whenever two measurements share a source of disturbance: a common power supply with ripple, a common reference voltage inside a converter, the same vibrating mounting plate, or the same temperature drift. A three-axis sensor whose axes share one converter and one supply often shows correlated noise between axes; F0-62 measures the covariance matrix of a simulated three-axis accelerometer at rest. Independent noise averages away; shared noise does not, and must be removed at its source or modelled. D3D2

In software, a covariance is a few multiply-add loops. For many variables, the covariance matrix of k columns is computed as a matrix product of the centred data with its own transpose divided by n − 1: the same operation GPUs accelerate as GEMM (MA201 F0-50, CU302). The F0-58 warning applies: centre the data first; the one-pass form E[XY] − μXμY suffers the same cancellation as the variance. D2W1

Worked example

Hana's notebook (x = hours of sunshine, y = kitchen temperature at 3 pm, °C; exercise data): (2, 19), (4, 20), (5, 21), (7, 21), (8, 23), (10, 24).

  1. Means. x̄ = 36/6 = 6; ȳ = 128/6 ≈ 21.333.
  2. Deviations. dx = −4, −2, −1, 1, 2, 4. dy = −2.333, −1.333, −0.333, −0.333, 1.667, 2.667.
  3. Products. 9.333, 2.667, 0.333, −0.333, 3.333, 10.667; Sxy = 26. Only one product is negative, and it is small.
  4. Covariance. sxy = 26/5 = 5.2 hour·°C.
  5. Spreads. Sxx = 42, so sx = √(42/5) ≈ 2.898 h; Syy ≈ 17.333, so sy ≈ 1.862 °C.
  6. Correlation. r = 26/√(42 × 17.333) ≈ 0.964.
  7. Units check. In °F (multiply y by 1.8, add 32) the covariance becomes 1.8 × 5.2 = 9.36 hour·°F; r stays 0.964.
  8. Best line. slope 5.2/8.4 ≈ 0.62 °C per hour; through (6, 21.33). Six afternoons are a tiny sample: report "r = 0.96, n = 6".

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • "Rising together" hides the shape. Hana's picture suggests a straight line. Covariance only measures the straight-line part; a relationship that rises and then falls (the kitchen gets warm, then the blind is closed) can give a small covariance despite a strong link. D1
    • 

  • Sunshine warming the kitchen is a cause; covariance does not know that. The analogy invites a causal reading. The number would be the same if the cause ran the other way or if a third thing drove both. D1
    • 

  • The notebook has few lines. Six afternoons give a rough estimate; the true correlation of "sunshine" and "temperature" in that kitchen could be quite different. A robot's covariance matrix is estimated from thousands of readings, or derived from a model, not from a handful.
    •

Summary


    

  • Covariance averages the products of paired deviations: positive when two quantities move together, negative when they move oppositely.
    • 

  • Correlation r = covariance/(sd × sd) is unit-free, between −1 and +1, and measures straight-line association only.
    • 

  • Independent implies uncorrelated; uncorrelated does not imply independent (y = x2).
    • 

  • Var(X ± Y) = Var X + Var Y ± 2 Cov(X, Y): correlated noise does not average away; the average of two equal sensors has sd σ√((1 + ρ)/2).
    • 

  • The covariance matrix holds variances on the diagonal and covariances off it; robots and drones carry one for every estimate.
    • 

  • Correlation is not causation; look for common causes, and report n.
    •
