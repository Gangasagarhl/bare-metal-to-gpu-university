Story hook

Analogy

It is games evening at Amina's home. Her little brother Leo has a rule: whoever rolls a total of 7 with two dice wins a biscuit. Leo is sure that 7 is no more special than 12. "Every number is equally likely," he says. Their grandmother smiles and says nothing.

Amina takes a sheet of paper and draws a square of six by six boxes. Across the top she writes the red die's faces, down the side the blue die's faces, and in every box the total. Six boxes say 7. Only one box says 12. "There are thirty-six ways the dice can land," she says, "and six of them make 7. Only one makes 12." Leo counts twice, then quietly changes his favourite number.

Counting the ways something can happen, and comparing that with all the ways anything can happen, is the oldest idea in probability. Engineers use it to ask how likely it is that at least one of a hundred servers fails today, that a sensor reading is a glitch, or that a benchmark result was luck. This first chapter of MA202 teaches the counting.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe a random experiment by its sample space and its events, and compute probabilities of equally likely outcomes by counting.
  • Apply the complement rule, the addition rule (with and without overlap) and the multiplication rule for independent and for dependent steps.
  • Count arrangements and choices with factorials, ordered arrangements and "n choose k", by hand and in C++.
  • Compute "at least one" probabilities through the complement, for example the chance that at least one of many machines fails.
  • Simulate a random experiment with a seeded pseudo-random generator and compare frequencies with exact probabilities.
  • Diagnose a probability calculation that adds chances of overlapping events and gets an answer above 1.
Jargon box
Random experiment
Simple words: anything you can repeat whose result you cannot predict exactly, such as rolling a die. Analogy: Leo's roll on games evening. Precise: a procedure with a well-defined set of possible results, exactly one of which occurs each time it is performed. D1
Outcome and sample space
Simple words: an outcome is one possible result; the sample space is the list of all of them. Analogy: one box of Amina's six-by-six square; the whole square. Precise: the sample space S is the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment; an outcome is an element of S. For two dice, S has 36 outcomes (red value, blue value). D1R1
Event
Simple words: a group of outcomes you care about, such as "the total is 7". Analogy: the boxes Amina shaded on her paper. Precise: a subset A of the sample space; A "happens" when the outcome lies in A. D1
Probability
Simple words: a number from 0 (never) to 1 (always) that says how likely an event is. Analogy: the share of the drawer's socks that are red, if you grab one without looking. Precise: a function P that gives every event A a number P(A) with P(A) ≥ 0, P(S) = 1, and P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) when A and B cannot happen together. When all outcomes are equally likely, P(A) = (outcomes in A) / (outcomes in S). D1
Complement
Simple words: "not A": everything that is not in the event. Analogy: all the unshaded boxes. Precise: the event Ac = S minus A; P(Ac) = 1 − P(A). D1
Disjoint events
Simple words: events that can never happen at the same time. Analogy: "total 7" and "a double" (two equal faces): no box is both. Precise: events A and B with no outcome in common (A and B = ∅). Also called mutually exclusive. D1
Independent events
Simple words: knowing that one happened does not change the chance of the other. Analogy: the red die does not know what the blue die did. Precise: A and B are independent when P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). D1
Factorial
Simple words: the number of ways to put n different things in a row. Analogy: the ways to line up four different cups on a shelf. Precise: n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 2 × 1, with 0! = 1. D1
Binomial coefficient ("n choose k")
Simple words: the number of ways to pick k things out of n when the order does not matter. Analogy: the ways to pick three friends out of eight to wash the dishes. Precise: C(n, k) = n! / (k! (n − k)!), also written as n over k in brackets. D1
Pseudo-random number generator (PRNG)
Simple words: a recipe that produces numbers that look random but are completely fixed by a starting number, the seed. Analogy: a very long shuffled list of dice rolls written in a notebook; the seed says on which page to start reading. Precise: an algorithm that produces a deterministic sequence from a seed; std::mt19937 is the Mersenne Twister engine of the C++ standard library (KID102 F0-38 used one for the guessing game). D2D3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is Amina's square. Every box is one outcome of rolling two dice, and the number in it is the total. Because the dice are fair and do not influence each other, the 36 boxes are equally likely. To find the probability of an event, count its boxes and divide by 36.

Figure 2 shows a second way to draw chance: a tree. Each branch is one step of the experiment with its probability written on it. You multiply along a path and add the paths that belong to your event. The tree is the worked example of this chapter: two socks taken from a drawer, one after the other, without putting the first one back.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Some things you cannot predict. You do not know which face a die will show, or which sock you will pull out of a dark drawer. But you can still say something useful: how likely each result is.

Start by listing every possible result. For one die the list is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. For two dice it is Amina's square of 36 boxes. If every result on the list is equally likely, the chance of something is a fraction: the number of results you want, divided by the number of all results. A total of 7 has 6 boxes out of 36, so its chance is 6/36, which is 1/6.

Chances live between 0 and 1. A chance of 0 means "never": no box shows 13. A chance of 1 means "always": every box shows a total of at least 2. A chance of 1/2 means "half the time, in the long run".

Three rules do most of the work. First, the chance that something does not happen is 1 minus the chance that it does. Second, if two things can never happen together, the chance of "one or the other" is their two chances added. Third, if two things do not affect each other, the chance of "both" is their two chances multiplied.

The words "in the long run" matter. If Leo rolls the dice 36 times, he will probably not see exactly 6 sevens. If he rolls them thousands of times, the share of sevens gets closer and closer to 1/6. Listing 2 does exactly that with a computer that rolls simulated dice.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The model: sample space, events, probability

A probability model has three parts: the sample space S of all outcomes, the events (subsets of S), and a probability P that gives each event a number. The rules P(A) ≥ 0, P(S) = 1, and P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) for disjoint A and B are the whole foundation; everything else in this chapter follows from them. D1

When the outcomes are finitely many and equally likely, the model becomes counting: P(A) = |A| / |S|, where |A| is the number of outcomes in A. This is the "naive definition" of probability. It is correct only when the equal-likelihood assumption is justified, for example by symmetry of a fair die. For the totals 2 to 12 of two dice it would be wrong to treat the eleven totals as equally likely: they are events made of different numbers of boxes. The equally likely outcomes are the 36 ordered pairs. D1R1

The rules you will use every day

  • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Example: P(total ≤ 9) = 1 − P(total ≥ 10) = 1 − 6/36 = 30/36 ≈ 0.833 (run R1).
  • Addition, disjoint: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) if A and B share no outcome. "Total 7" and "a double" share none, because a double always has an even total: P = 6/36 + 6/36 = 12/36.
  • Addition, general: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). "Red die shows 6" and "blue die shows 6" overlap in the box (6, 6), which would be counted twice: P = 6/36 + 6/36 − 1/36 = 11/36 ≈ 0.306.
  • Multiplication, independent: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Two fair coins: P(two heads) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4.
  • Multiplication, step by step: P(A and then B) = P(A) × P(B given A). The second factor is the probability of B after A has happened. In Figure 2 the second sock is drawn from a drawer that now has one sock fewer, so the second branch probabilities change. F0-61 studies "given" (conditional probability) in depth.

All the numbers above are recomputed by run R5. D1R5

Counting without listing

Listing 36 boxes is fine; listing every way to shuffle a deck of cards is not. The multiplication principle counts for you: if a choice is made in steps, with a ways for the first step and b ways for the second whatever the first was, there are a × b ways in total. Four-digit door codes with digits 0–9 and repeats allowed: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000. D1R3

Three counts follow from it:

  • Arrangements of all n items: n! ways. Four different cups on a shelf: 4! = 24.
  • Ordered selections of k from n (first, second, third place from eight runners): n × (n − 1) × … × (n − k + 1) = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336.
  • Unordered selections of k from n: each unordered team of 3 appears 3! = 6 times among the ordered selections, so C(8, 3) = 336 / 6 = 56. In general C(n, k) = n! / (k! (n − k)!).

Run R3 prints these counts. The test question for which formula to use is always: does the order matter? D1R3

"At least one": go through the complement

Questions of the form "what is the chance that at least one of these things happens?" are hard to count directly, because "at least one" covers many cases (exactly one, exactly two, …). The complement has only one case: "none of them happens". If the n events are independent and each has probability p, P(none) = (1 − p)n, so

P(at least one) = 1 − (1 − p)n.

For one die rolled 4 times, P(at least one six) = 1 − (5/6)4 ≈ 0.518. For an exercise where each of n machines independently fails on a given day with probability 0.01, run R3 prints about 0.096 for 10 machines, 0.634 for 100 and 0.993 for 500. The 0.01 is an invented exercise number, not a property of any real machine. The shape of the answer is the lesson: rare events per machine become near-certain events per cluster. Distributed systems (F5, F8) are designed around this. D1R3U1

Probability and frequency

If you repeat an experiment many times independently, the fraction of times an event happens (its relative frequency) tends to get close to its probability. Run R2 rolled two simulated dice 360,000 times and counted sevens: the frequency was 0.222 after 36 rolls, 0.175 after 360, and 0.1667 after 360,000, against the exact 1/6 ≈ 0.1667. This is the law of large numbers, which F0-58 states precisely with the mean. The important practical point: a frequency from a few trials can be far from the probability. Thirty-six rolls gave 8 sevens instead of 6. D1R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Axioms instead of counting. The equally-likely model breaks down as soon as outcomes are not symmetric (a loaded die, a sensor reading) or there are infinitely many outcomes (a time measured in seconds). The general definition keeps only the axioms: non-negativity, P(S) = 1, and additivity over disjoint events (for countably many events at once). Every rule of Layer 2, including the general addition rule and the complement rule, is derived from these axioms, not assumed separately. F0-57 extends the model to numerical outcomes, where probabilities are given by distributions instead of counts. D1

Independence is a property of the model, not of the words. Two events are independent if and only if P(A and B) = P(A) P(B); you check it, you do not assume it from the story. "Disjoint" and "independent" are different and almost opposite: two disjoint events with non-zero probabilities are never independent, because knowing one happened tells you the other did not. For the cluster formula, independence is the critical assumption: machines that share a power supply, a network switch or a software bug do not fail independently, and 1 − (1 − p)n then misjudges the risk. F0-60 measures such dependence with covariance. D1

Big counts overflow. Factorials grow fast. Listing 3 computes C(n, k) step by step, multiplying and then dividing at each step, so that every intermediate value is itself a binomial coefficient and stays a whole number; computing n! first would overflow a 64-bit integer for much smaller n. For large n, engineers work with logarithms of counts or with floating-point approximations, and say so. W1D3

How the hardware actually does it

A computer that follows exact steps cannot roll a die. Simulations therefore use a pseudo-random number generator: a deterministic algorithm that turns a seed into a long sequence of numbers with good statistical properties. Listing 2 uses std::mt19937, the 32-bit Mersenne Twister engine from the C++ standard library's <random> header. The engine's algorithm is specified by the C++ standard, so the same seed gives the same sequence with every conforming library; that is why runs R2 and R4 are repeatable. D2D3R2

Turning a 32-bit number into a die face needs care. There are 232 = 4,294,967,296 possible values, which is not a multiple of 6, so "value modulo 6" would make faces 1 to 4 a tiny bit more likely than 5 and 6. Listing 2 throws away the four largest values and draws again (rejection sampling), so the remaining 4,294,967,292 = 6 × 715,827,882 values split exactly evenly among the faces. W1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Many processors and operating systems also offer sources of unpredictable numbers gathered from physical noise, used for cryptographic keys rather than simulations, and the C++ library exposes such a source through std::random_device where one is available. Which source a given library and machine use, and whether std::random_device is truly non-deterministic on a given platform, was not checked in this build. Check the C++ standard's description of random_device (ISO/IEC 14882, the random number generation clause) and your library's and operating system's documentation. The listings of this course use fixed seeds on purpose, so that their output can be checked.

Worked example

Priya's drawer holds 3 red socks and 2 blue socks. In the dark she takes one sock, keeps it, and takes a second. What is the probability that the two socks match?

Method 1: tree (Figure 2). First sock red: 3/5. Given that, 2 red remain among 4 socks, so the second is red with probability 2/4. Path probability: 3/5 × 2/4 = 0.3. First sock blue: 2/5; then 1 blue remains among 4, so 1/4. Path: 2/5 × 1/4 = 0.1. The two matching paths are disjoint, so P(match) = 0.3 + 0.1 = 0.4.

Method 2: counting. Ignore the order in which the two socks were taken. The number of unordered pairs of 5 socks is C(5, 2) = 10, all equally likely. Matching pairs: C(3, 2) = 3 red pairs plus C(2, 2) = 1 blue pair = 4. P(match) = 4/10 = 0.4. Two independent methods agree: a good habit for any probability answer.

Complement: P(no match) = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6. Check with the tree: the two mixed paths are 0.3 + 0.3 = 0.6.

A trap. If you treated the two draws as independent (3/5 × 3/5 for red, red), you would get 0.36 + 0.16 = 0.52. That would be right only if Priya put the first sock back before drawing again. The step-by-step multiplication rule needs the probability after the first step.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Real dice and socks are not perfectly fair. A worn die, or socks that cling together, break the equal-likelihood assumption on which counting rests. The mathematics stays correct; the model stops matching the object. Engineers test the assumption with data (F0-57's histograms) before trusting the counts. D1
    • 

  • Simulated dice are not random at all. The computer's dice follow a fixed recipe from the seed. They imitate the statistics of fair dice well enough for simulations, but anyone who knows the seed can predict every roll, so they must never be used for secrets. D2
    • 

  • The share of red socks is a probability only for one blind grab. Once a sock is taken out, the drawer changes; the next grab has a different probability (Figure 2). A probability belongs to a precisely described experiment, not to the drawer itself.
    • 

  • Most engineering outcomes are not countable boxes. A temperature or a run time can take any value in a range. There the "count the boxes" picture is replaced by distributions and areas (F0-57, F0-59). D1
    •

Summary


    

  • A probability model has a sample space of outcomes, events (sets of outcomes), and probabilities between 0 and 1 with P(S) = 1.
    • 

  • With equally likely outcomes, probability is counting: P(A) = |A| / |S|. Be sure the counted things really are equally likely.
    • 

  • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Addition: add for disjoint events, subtract the overlap otherwise.
    • 

  • Multiplication: multiply for independent events; for steps, multiply by the probability given the earlier steps.
    • 

  • Counting: n! arrangements; n(n − 1)…(n − k + 1) ordered selections; C(n, k) unordered selections.
    • 

  • "At least one" = 1 − P(none) = 1 − (1 − p)n for independent events; rare per item becomes likely per cluster.
    • 

  • Frequencies approach probabilities only over many trials; simulations use seeded pseudo-random generators so they can be repeated.
    •
