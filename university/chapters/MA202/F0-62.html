Story hook

Analogy

Grandpa Tomás bakes bread every Sunday and swears by his kitchen scale. His granddaughter Aiko is not convinced. She puts a sealed 500 g bag of rice on it: 512 g. She lifts it and puts it back: 509 g. Again: 511, 510, 513. "Your scale is wrong in two different ways," she says.

"First, it always shows too much, about 10 grams too much. That is the same every time, so we can fix it: subtract 10 from every reading. Second, it flickers by a gram or two from one weighing to the next. That one we cannot fix by subtracting, but if we weigh several times and average, the flicker shrinks." Grandpa writes "−10 g" on a sticky note on the scale and keeps baking.

Every sensor in a robot or a drone is Grandpa's scale. An accelerometer at rest reads a little off and flickers; a gyroscope reports a slow turn while lying still. Engineers separate the two errors, the constant bias and the random noise, measure each, remove what can be removed and describe the rest with a standard deviation. That description is what a Kalman filter needs. This chapter does it for a simulated IMU lying on a table.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the measurement model z = x + b + n (plus quantisation and limits) and explain each term.
  • Distinguish accuracy from precision, and bias from noise, in data and in pictures.
  • Measure a sensor's bias and noise standard deviation from readings taken at rest, with the standard error of the bias estimate.
  • Predict what averaging does to noise (1/√N), what it does not do to bias, and the noise added by a quantiser (Δ/√12).
  • Record an IMU at rest (simulated in this build) and report its noise statistics, as in the course lab.
  • Diagnose a gyroscope heading that drifts because its bias was calibrated while the robot was moving.
Jargon box
Measurement model
Simple words: a formula that says how a reading is made from the true value plus errors. Analogy: "what the scale shows = what the rice weighs + 10 g + a flicker". Precise: z = x + b + n, where x is the true value, b the bias, n zero-mean random noise (often modelled as Gaussian with standard deviation σ); real sensors add quantisation and range limits. D1D2
Bias
Simple words: a sensor's reading when the true value is zero; an error that is the same (or changes only slowly) every time. Analogy: a kitchen scale that shows 10 g with nothing on it. Precise: a constant or slowly varying offset added to a sensor's output; it is estimated (calibrated) and subtracted. Averaging does not reduce it. (Same entry as HW302 F1-65.) D2D1
Sensor noise
Simple words: small random wobbles in a sensor's readings. Analogy: the scale's last digit flickering between weighings. Precise: the part of a measurement that differs from the true value in ways that change from reading to reading; real sensors always have some. Described by its standard deviation (and, for time series, by how it depends on time). D1D3
Accuracy and precision
Simple words: accuracy is being right on average; precision is giving the same answer every time. Analogy: a scale that is always 10 g too high is precise but not accurate. Precise: accuracy concerns the systematic error (bias, the difference between the mean reading and the true value); precision concerns the random error (spread, the standard deviation of repeated readings). D3
Calibration
Simple words: measuring something whose true value you already know, to find out how wrong the sensor is. Analogy: weighing the sealed 500 g bag of rice. Precise: determining the relation between a sensor's readings and known reference values (here: the bias, as the mean reading minus the reference), so that later readings can be corrected. D3D2
Resolution and quantisation noise
Simple words: the smallest step the sensor can show, and the rounding error that the steps add. Analogy: a scale that shows whole grams only. Precise: a quantiser maps a value to the nearest multiple of its step Δ; when the input varies over many steps, the rounding error is close to uniform on [−Δ/2, Δ/2] and has standard deviation Δ/√12. D3R3
IMU, accelerometer, gyroscope
Simple words: an IMU is a small sensor package that feels tilting, pushing and turning; the accelerometer feels pushes (and gravity), the gyroscope feels turning speed. Analogy: the balance sense of your body (the registered F9 analogy). Precise: an inertial measurement unit contains accelerometers, which measure specific force along their axes, and gyroscopes, which measure angular rate, usually three perpendicular axes each; see HW302 F1-65. At rest and level, an ideal accelerometer's vertical axis reads +1 g (upwards). D2
Drift (of an integrated gyro)
Simple words: the angle error that keeps growing when you add up turning rates that include a bias. Analogy: walking with your eyes closed and turning a tiny bit too far at every step. Precise: the growth of error in an angle obtained by integrating rate measurements; a constant bias b produces an error b·t that grows linearly with time, and noise adds a random walk. (Same entry as HW302 F1-65.) D2R4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

No sensor tells the exact truth. Its readings are wrong in two ways at once. One part of the error stays the same every time: the scale that always adds 10 grams. The other part changes every time: the last digit that flickers. The first is called bias, the second noise.

You find the bias by measuring something you already know. Put the 500 g bag on the scale many times and average: if the average is 510.6 g, the bias is about 10.6 g. From then on, subtract it. A robot does the same with its turning sensor: while it stands perfectly still, the true turning speed is zero, so whatever the sensor reports on average is its bias.

You describe the noise with its standard deviation, the "typical wobble" from F0-58. You cannot subtract noise, because you never know which way it will go next time. But averaging helps: average four readings and the wobble halves; average a hundred and it shrinks ten times. Averaging does nothing to the bias, though. Ten thousand readings from a scale that adds 10 grams still average 10 grams too high.

Two more things change readings. Sensors show values in small steps, like a scale that shows whole grams only; the rounding adds a little extra noise. And every sensor has a largest value it can show; beyond that it gets stuck at the top, as Kofi's light sensor did in F0-57.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The model and its parameters

With the model z = x + b + n, readings taken while x is known (at rest: rate 0, level: specific force +1 g on the vertical axis) give b and σ directly: the bias estimate is b̂ = z̄ − x, the noise estimate is the sample standard deviation s of the readings. The bias estimate has its own uncertainty, the standard error s/√N (F0-58). For Aiko's eight weighings (512, 509, 511, 510, 513, 509, 511, 510 g; exercise data) z̄ = 510.625 g, so b̂ = 10.625 g, s ≈ 1.41 g and the standard error of b̂ ≈ 0.50 g (run R5). D1D3R5

An IMU at rest

The course lab records an IMU lying still and level. In this build no IMU exists, so Listing 1 is the university's simulator: it writes 1,000 samples (10 s at 100 samples per second) of a three-axis accelerometer in m/s2 and a three-axis gyroscope in deg/s, with biases, Gaussian noise, a vibration component shared by the two horizontal accelerometer axes, and quantisation steps of 0.005 m/s2 and 0.06 deg/s. All of these are simulator settings chosen for teaching, not values of any real part. Listing 2 analyses the file exactly as it would analyse a real recording. R1R2

Run R2's results: the gyroscope biases are estimated as 0.302, −0.117 and 0.050 deg/s, each with a standard error of about 0.003 deg/s, against simulator settings 0.30, −0.12 and 0.05; the noise standard deviations are about 0.10 deg/s, matching the setting. The accelerometer's vertical axis averages 9.890 m/s2; with the simulator's 9.81 m/s2 for 1 g, its bias is 0.080 m/s2. The horizontal axes' sds (0.026 and 0.025 m/s2) are larger than their own noise setting of 0.02, because the shared vibration adds to both: the predicted total is √(0.022 + 0.0152 + 0.0052/12) ≈ 0.025 m/s2 (run R5). R2R5

Correlated noise between axes

Run R2 also measures r(ax, ay) ≈ 0.42 and r(ax, az) ≈ 0.06. The theory value for the shared vibration is 0.0152/(0.022 + 0.0152) = 0.36; estimates of r from 1,000 samples scatter by a few hundredths, so 0.42 is consistent with 0.36, and 0.06 is consistent with 0 (the scatter for independent axes is about 1/√1000 ≈ 0.03). As F0-60 showed, correlated noise does not average away between axes; a filter needs the full covariance matrix, not only the per-axis variances. R2R5D1

What averaging does and does not do

Run R3 averages the gyro x column in blocks of k samples. The sd of the block means falls from 0.099 (k = 1) to 0.052 (k = 4), 0.026 (k = 16) and 0.010 (k = 64), close to 0.099/√k, while the mean of the block means stays at 0.30 deg/s: the noise shrinks, the bias does not. The k = 64 value rests on only 15 blocks and is correspondingly rough. R3D1

Quantisation

A quantiser with step Δ rounds to the nearest multiple of Δ. When the input moves over many steps (because of noise or because the signal changes), the rounding error is spread evenly between −Δ/2 and +Δ/2, and its standard deviation is Δ/√12: run R3 measures 0.0174 for Δ = 0.06 against 0.0173 from the formula. Two consequences: the quantiser's noise adds to the sensor's own noise as a variance (F0-58), and when the sensor's noise is much smaller than Δ, rounding stops being random. Run R3's part C shows the extreme case: a constant true value of 0.02 with no noise is read as 0 every time, and averaging 1,000 readings still gives 0. A little noise (called dither) is what lets averaging recover values finer than one step. D3R3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Bias is not truly constant. Real biases change with temperature, with time since power-on and between power-ons; the noise may not be independent from one sample to the next. Then averaging a long record does not keep improving the estimate: beyond some averaging time, the slow bias changes dominate. Gyroscope and accelerometer datasheets describe these effects with several numbers beyond a single σ. D2D4

Not verified — check before relying on this

Inertial-sensor datasheets often specify noise as a noise density (noise per square root of bandwidth) and slow bias changes as bias instability, and the Allan variance (variance of differences of successive block averages, plotted against the block length) is a common method to separate noise types in a long recording at rest. The exact definitions, units and the standard documents that define them were not checked in this build. Check the datasheet of the IMU named in your dossier and the IEEE standards for inertial-sensor test procedures (title, number and clause to be recorded by the Source Researcher) before using these terms or converting between them.

From noise to filter parameters. RB302's Kalman filter needs a measurement-noise variance R (here σ2 per axis, or the covariance matrix across axes) and a process-noise model. Measured values from a rest recording are the honest starting point; choosing R too small makes the filter overconfident (F0-61 Layer 3; RB302's forensic lab). D1U1

Integration turns small biases into large errors. A heading computed by integrating the gyro rate grows an error b·t from an uncorrected bias b: 0.30 deg/s becomes 18° after one minute (run R5). Even a well-calibrated bias has a standard error; with σ = 0.1 deg/s and 1,000 rest samples the residual is about 0.003 deg/s, about 0.19° per minute. Noise integrates too, but as a random walk whose spread grows like √t rather than t. This is why gyro-only headings drift and why DN301 fuses gyros with other sensors. D2R5

How the hardware actually does it

A MEMS (micro-electro-mechanical systems) accelerometer or gyroscope converts tiny movements of a structure inside the chip into an electrical signal; an analog-to-digital converter turns that into integer codes, and a scale factor from the datasheet converts codes to m/s2 or deg/s. The bias comes from manufacturing imperfections and temperature; the noise from the electronics and the mechanics; the step Δ from the converter's code width times the scale factor; the limits from the selected full-scale range. HW302 (F1-65) describes the sensing principles. D2D4

Every numerical property of a real IMU (range, scale factor, noise, bias limits, sample rates) comes only from the datasheet of the named part or from your own measurement, never from a "typical" value (AH-21). The simulator in this chapter uses invented values on purpose, so that nobody mistakes them for a product's specification. U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The sign convention of a real accelerometer (whether the axis pointing up reads +1 g or −1 g at rest), its axis directions, its scale factor and how it reports values (registers, units, byte order) differ between parts and were not checked for any part in this build. Read them from the datasheet of the IMU named in your dossier before writing the expected values of Listing 2 (line 29) for a real recording. The simulator's 9.81 m/s2 for 1 g is a rounded setting; the value of g at your location is a physics question for your dossier.

Worked example

Aiko calibrates Grandpa's scale with the sealed 500 g bag (assumed exact for the exercise). Readings: 512, 509, 511, 510, 513, 509, 511, 510 g.

  1. Mean: 4,085/8 = 510.625 g.
  2. Bias: b̂ = 510.625 − 500 = 10.625 g.
  3. Noise: deviations −1.375 … +2.375; sample sd s ≈ 1.41 g (precision of one weighing).
  4. Uncertainty of the bias: s/√8 ≈ 0.50 g. Report: bias 10.6 ± 0.5 g (n = 8).
  5. Correcting a reading: the scale shows 734 g for a bowl of flour; corrected 734 − 10.625 ≈ 723.4 g, still ±1.4 g from noise for one weighing.
  6. Averaging to reach 0.25 g: N = (1.41/0.25)2 ≈ 32 weighings. Averaging improves precision; it would never have found the 10.6 g bias without the known bag.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Grandpa's bias is fixed; a sensor's drifts. The sticky note "−10 g" works for a kitchen scale for a while. An IMU's bias changes with temperature and between power-ons, so robots recalibrate at every start-up, and good estimators keep estimating the bias while running (DN301). D2
    • 

  • The 500 g bag is a perfect reference only in the story. Real references have their own uncertainty, and an IMU's "reference" (truly at rest, truly level) is an assumption you must make true: a table that vibrates or tilts becomes part of the bias. D3
    • 

  • Weighings are independent; sensor samples may not be. Aiko lifts the bag between weighings. Samples 0.01 s apart can share slow disturbances, so 1/√N overstates how much averaging helps (Layer 3). D4
    •

Goal

Simulator variant: a computer with a C++ compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 with the flags shown in the build commands. Method hints


At rest, the gyro's sd should be its noise sd; a window with an sd forty times larger was not at rest. The heading grows by (true rate − subtracted bias) × time: compute the slope from the table and compare.

Summary


    

  • Every reading is z = x + b + n, then rounded to a step and clipped at limits.
    • 

  • Bias (accuracy) is removed by calibration against a known reference; noise (precision) is described by its sd and reduced by averaging as 1/√N.
    • 

  • The bias estimate has a standard error s/√N; report it.
    • 

  • Quantisation adds noise with sd Δ/√12 when the input spans many steps; values finer than the step need noise (dither) to be recovered by averaging.
    • 

  • Noise can be correlated between axes; a filter needs the covariance matrix.
    • 

  • Integrated biases grow without limit: an uncorrected gyro bias b gives a heading error b·t.
    • 

  • Real sensor numbers come only from the named part's datasheet or from your measurement; the real-IMU lab is untested on hardware in this build.

  4. The calibration procedure. The model z = x + b + n assumes x = 0 during calibration; while being carried, x was a varying turn rate with a non-zero average, so the "bias" absorbed part of the motion. Noise is small and averages out; the bias estimate measured the wrong thing.
    5. 

  5. Only accept a calibration when a rest check passes: for example, the window's sd must be below a threshold (a few times the known noise sd), otherwise wait and retry. Test: feed a recorded moving window (like this one) and assert the firmware refuses it; feed a rest window and assert it accepts and estimates the bias within a tolerance.

How the evidence was produced: gyro_bug.cpp (seed 162) simulates the first 3 s as a true rate of 6 sin(πt) deg/s (being carried and turned), plus bias 0.30 deg/s and noise sd 0.10 deg/s; then 60 s at rest with the same bias and noise; it estimates the bias from the first 3 s and integrates (R4). The 3 s window covers one and a half periods of the motion, so its average is not zero (6 × 2/(3π) ≈ 1.27 deg/s). Wrong answers and why: "the gyro noise is too high" — the rest sd is 0.1 deg/s and noise alone would not produce a straight-line drift; "the gyro is broken" — on the floor its readings are steady around 0.30 deg/s, a normal bias.
