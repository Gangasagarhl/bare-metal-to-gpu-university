Story hook

Analogy

Nadia wants to prove that her new route to school is faster. On Monday she walks the old route: 17 minutes. On Tuesday she tries the new one: 12 minutes. "Five minutes faster!" she tells the family at dinner.

Her brother Samir has been writing down his own walking times on the old route for a month. "On Monday you waited at the level crossing for a train," he says. "Look at my list. Most days the old route takes 12 or 13 minutes. Twice it took 20, once when the crossing was closed and once when I forgot my bag and went back." He sorts his list and points at the middle: 12.5 minutes. "That is a normal morning. Walk your new route for two weeks, and we compare the middle of your list with the middle of mine."

Two weeks later the middles are 12.4 and 12.5 minutes. The new route is about the same. Nadia's "five minutes" came from comparing one unlucky morning with one ordinary one. Computer benchmarks fall into exactly this trap, and this chapter gives you Samir's tools: many runs, the sorted list, the middle, the spread, and the honest decision about whether two lists differ.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute the median, the nearest-rank percentiles, the quartiles and the interquartile range (IQR) of a list of timings by hand and in code.
  • Explain why benchmark timings are skewed and why the median and the IQR describe them better than the mean and the standard deviation.
  • Flag outliers with Tukey's fences and decide what to do with them (report, investigate, never silently delete).
  • Follow the measurement protocol of the curriculum (warm-up, then the median of at least 20 runs) and report a timing with its spread and conditions.
  • Refute a performance claim based on one run, using the raw timings (forensic lab).
  • Build and test the course's benchmark-statistics helper benchstats.hpp, used by every later performance lab.
Jargon box
Median and percentile
Simple words: the median is the middle value after sorting; the 90th percentile is the value that 90 % of results are at or below. Analogy: Samir's sorted list of walking times, and his finger on the middle. Precise: for n sorted values, the median is the value at position (n+1)/2 for odd n (the mean of the two middle values for even n); the nearest-rank p-th percentile is the value at rank ⌈p/100 × n⌉. (The same definitions as SP302 F2-51.) D1W1
Quartiles and interquartile range (IQR)
Simple words: the quartiles cut the sorted list into four equal parts; the IQR is the width of the middle half. Analogy: "on a normal half of the mornings, the walk takes between 12.1 and 12.9 minutes". Precise: in this course, q1 is the nearest-rank 25th percentile and q3 the nearest-rank 75th percentile; IQR = q3 − q1. Other tools use other quartile rules (unverified box). D1D2
Outlier and Tukey fences
Simple words: an outlier is a value far from the others; the fences are a rule for "far". Analogy: the morning the level crossing was closed. Precise: a value below q1 − 1.5·IQR or above q3 + 1.5·IQR is flagged as an outlier; the factor 1.5 is a convention, not a law. A flag means "investigate", not "delete". D2
Skewed distribution
Simple words: a distribution with a long tail on one side. Analogy: a walk can be a few minutes slower than usual for many reasons, but it cannot be much faster than the fastest possible pace. Precise: a distribution that is not symmetric about its centre; timings are right-skewed: bounded below by the time the work needs, with a tail of slow runs caused by interference. In a right-skewed sample the mean lies above the median. D1D3
Robust statistic
Simple words: a summary number that a few strange values cannot move much. Analogy: one closed crossing changes Samir's average but not his middle value. Precise: a statistic whose value changes little when a small fraction of the data is replaced by arbitrary values; the median and the IQR are robust, the mean and the standard deviation are not. D1D2
Warm-up run
Simple words: a run you do first and do not count. Analogy: the first morning of a new route, when you still look for the way. Precise: an untimed execution of the workload before measurement, so that one-time costs (first touch of memory pages, filling caches, loading code, the processor changing its clock speed) do not land in the timed runs. (Same entry as SP302 F2-51.) D3
Run-to-run variation
Simple words: the same program, run again on the same machine, takes a different time. Analogy: the same walk takes a different time every morning. Precise: the spread of repeated measurements of an unchanged workload, caused by everything the program does not control: other processes, interrupts, frequency changes, cache and memory state, timer resolution. (Same entry as SP302 F2-51; this chapter measures it with the IQR.) D3
Interleaving (A/B comparison)
Simple words: taking turns: A, B, A, B, instead of all A first and then all B. Analogy: Nadia and Samir walking their routes on the same mornings, so a rainy week slows both. Precise: alternating the measurements of the versions being compared, so that slow changes of the machine (other load, temperature, frequency) affect both versions alike instead of biasing one. D3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When you time something more than once, you get a different number every time. A computer program is no different: the same program, on the same computer, takes a little longer or a little shorter on every run, because the computer is busy with other things too.

So one time tells you very little. Time it many times, say 30. Then sort the times from fastest to slowest and look at the one in the middle: that is the median, "a normal run". Look at how wide the middle half of the list is: that is the spread. A narrow middle half means the times agree with each other.

Sometimes one run is much slower than all the others: the computer did something else at that moment, or the program did some one-time setup. That run is an outlier. Do not hide it; write down that it happened. But do not let it decide your answer either. The median ignores it; the average does not.

And to compare two programs, time both many times, take turns between them, and compare their medians. If their middle halves overlap, you cannot say that one is faster.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why timings are not Gaussian

F0-59's Gaussian is symmetric. Timings are not: a run cannot be faster than the work allows, but it can be slower by any amount when the operating system schedules another process, a page fault happens, a cache is cold or the processor changes its clock speed. The distribution has a hard edge on the left and a long tail on the right. In such data the mean is pulled towards the tail, and the standard deviation is dominated by the few slow runs. That is why the curriculum's protocol and SP302 report the median, and why this course adds the IQR as the measure of spread. D1D3C1

The order statistics

Sort the n values. The value at position k is the k-th order statistic. With the definitions of the jargon box:

  • median: position (n+1)/2 for odd n; the mean of positions n/2 and n/2+1 for even n;
  • nearest-rank p-th percentile: position ⌈p/100 × n⌉ (at least 1);
  • q1, q3: the 25th and 75th percentiles; IQR = q3 − q1;
  • fences: q1 − 1.5·IQR and q3 + 1.5·IQR.

For the worked example's ten timings, q1 is at rank ⌈2.5⌉ = 3 and q3 at rank ⌈7.5⌉ = 8. A consequence of nearest rank that matters in practice: with 30 runs, the 99th percentile is at rank ⌈29.7⌉ = 30, the maximum. A "p99 latency" needs at least 100 values before it differs from the worst run you saw (run R5). W1R5

Robust versus non-robust

In the worked example, two slow runs (25.3 and 30.6 ms) move the mean from 12.01 ms (the other eight) to 15.2 ms, above every normal run, and give a standard deviation of 6.84 ms for data whose middle half is 0.5 ms wide. The median (12.05 ms) and the IQR (0.5 ms) do not notice. Replace 30.6 by 3,000 and the median and the IQR stay exactly the same; the mean becomes meaningless. R1D1

The measurement protocol

The curriculum's protocol (section 13.4) is: warm-up runs, then the median of at least 20 timed runs; fixed clocks if the driver allows it, with the setting recorded; report the result and its percentage of the reference. This course's lab uses 3 warm-ups and 30 timed runs and adds the IQR. A complete report line names the median, the IQR, n, the warm-ups, the machine, the compiler and its flags, and the date. The build container of this university cannot fix its clock frequency; its logs say so. C1R2

Comparing two versions

To claim "B is faster than A", measure both with the protocol, interleaved (A, B, A, B, …), and compare their medians. The project helper uses a cautious rule fit for this level: if the two middle halves [q1, q3] overlap, do not claim a difference from these runs. That rule is a first filter, not a statistical test (Layer 3). D3W1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Uncertainty of a median. The median of 30 runs is itself a random quantity; another 30 runs give another median. Its spread can be estimated without assuming a distribution, for example by resampling the measured runs many times with replacement and taking the median of each resample (the bootstrap), or from order statistics with a binomial argument (each run is below the true median with probability one half, F0-57). Both are beyond this chapter's lab and are suggested as a project extension. D1

Runs are not independent. F0-58's √n rules assume independent samples. Consecutive runs share the machine's state: a background job, the frequency, the temperature. Slow drifts make consecutive runs similar and make the spread inside one session smaller than the spread between sessions. Interleaving protects comparisons from drifts; repeating the whole measurement on another day shows how stable the median itself is. SP302 (F2-51) measured the effect of a busy machine. D3

Which statistic for which question. The minimum estimates "how fast can it go when nothing interferes" and is useful for comparing code changes on a noisy machine, but it hides interference that users will see. The median answers "what does a normal run take". High percentiles (p90, p99) answer "how bad is it for the unlucky requests", which matters for latency; they need many runs. The mean is right when totals matter (total energy, total time of a batch of independent jobs), because totals add up. State which question you are answering. D3D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Statistics tools and spreadsheets compute quartiles and percentiles with several different rules (some interpolate between ranks), so their q1, q3 and p90 can differ from this course's nearest-rank values for small n. Which rule a given tool uses was not checked in this build. The origin of the 1.5·IQR fences in Tukey's work (D2) and the exact wording there were not opened either. Before comparing numbers with another tool, read its documentation for the rule it uses, or compute both with benchstats.hpp.

How the hardware actually does it

A timing is the difference of two clock readings. std::chrono::steady_clock is a clock that never goes backwards, which is the property a duration needs; its tick length and the cost of reading it depend on the system and are measured, not assumed (SP302 Listing clockinfo.cpp in F2-51). The time between the two readings includes everything the processor did meanwhile: the work, but also interrupts, other threads scheduled on the same core, page faults and cache misses. D4D3

The timing steps of this chapter are built with -O2 and without the sanitizers, unlike every other listing in MA202: sanitizers add checks to every memory access and would make the program measure the checks. The optimiser, in turn, may delete work whose result is never used; benchstats::keep (Listing 1, lines 116–121) is the same barrier SP302 uses, and F2-51 shows what happens without it. R2

Not verified — check before relying on this

The build machine is a shared cloud virtual machine (its CPU model string is recorded in each timing log). Its clock frequency, the other loads on the physical host and the virtual machine's scheduling were not observable in this build. In the forensic capture, run 1 of A includes the construction of the 4,000,000 readings (by the program's design, Listing 4 lines 13–23); how that time divides between filling the values, first touch of fresh memory pages and other effects was not measured. Every time in this chapter is a measurement of one session on that machine (AH-23), not a property of any processor.

Worked example

Ten timings of a program in milliseconds, in the order measured (exercise data): 12.1, 11.8, 12.0, 25.3, 11.9, 12.2, 12.0, 11.7, 12.4, 30.6.

  1. Sort: 11.7, 11.8, 11.9, 12.0, 12.0, 12.1, 12.2, 12.4, 25.3, 30.6.
  2. Median (n = 10, even): mean of the 5th and 6th values = (12.0 + 12.1)/2 = 12.05 ms.
  3. Quartiles: q1 at rank ⌈0.25 × 10⌉ = 3 → 11.9 ms; q3 at rank ⌈7.5⌉ = 8 → 12.4 ms; IQR = 0.5 ms, about 4.1 % of the median.
  4. Fences: 11.9 − 0.75 = 11.15 ms and 12.4 + 0.75 = 13.15 ms. Flagged: 25.3 and 30.6 ms.
  5. p90: rank 9 → 25.3 ms. With 10 runs, p90 already lands on an outlier.
  6. Mean and sd: sum 152.0 → mean 15.2 ms; sd ≈ 6.84 ms. Without the two flagged runs the mean would be 12.01 ms.
  7. Report: "median 12.05 ms, IQR 0.5 ms (n = 10; 2 of 10 runs above the high fence, at 25.3 and 30.6 ms)". The outliers stay in the report; a reader may care about them.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Walks are a few per day; runs are thousands per second. Samir needed a month for 30 walks; a program can be timed 30 times in a second. The cheapness tempts people to collect millions of runs and report a mean with many digits, which looks precise and hides the drift between sessions (Layer 3). D3
    • 

  • A walk cannot be optimised away. A compiler can delete work whose result is unused, and the timing then measures nothing: a "faster" result that no route-walker ever meets. R2
    • 

  • The weather affects both routes alike only if they are walked on the same days. The analogy hides how strongly a computer's state changes within seconds; interleaving at the scale of single runs has no counterpart in the walk to school. D3
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F0-58 (mean, sd), F0-59 (the Gaussian and its tails), F0-62 (measurements have bias and noise). What carries over: repeat, summarise, report the spread with the centre, and keep the raw data. What changes: timing noise is skewed and full of outliers, so the robust statistics (median, IQR, percentiles) replace the mean and sd as the main report, and the measuring program itself (warm-up, optimiser, sanitizers) becomes part of the measurement. Lab


Goal


Time a program 30 times and report the median and the spread, following the curriculum's measurement protocol. This is the course lab of MA202.


Hardware and software needed


Any computer with a C++20 compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 on a Linux x86_64 cloud build container. The files benchstats.hpp and timing30.cc.


Safety
No physical hardware is involved. If you run long benchmarks on a laptop, keep it on a hard surface so it can cool, and stop if it becomes uncomfortably hot to touch.


Steps


    

  1. Build timing30.cc with -O2 and without sanitizers (build command above). Write the command, the compiler version line and the machine into your notebook.
    2. 

  2. Close other heavy programs. Run it. Copy the raw list and the report line.
    3. 

  3. Run it twice more, a few minutes apart. Compare the three medians with their IQRs: do they agree within about one IQR?
    4. 

  4. Start a heavy job on the same machine (for example a second copy of the program in a loop) and run once more. What changes: the median, the IQR, the outlier count?
    5. 

  5. Write the final report line: median, IQR, n, warm-ups, machine, compiler and flags, date, and the conditions of step 4.
    6. 



Expected observations


On this build's machine (run R2), the 30 runs formed a cluster with a few slower runs on one side, and the IQR was a small percentage of the median: between about 4 % and 17 % in this build's repeated runs of the lab, which also shows that the spread itself varies between sessions. The exact values change from run to run, so read them from the output above rather than from this text. On your machine, expect a different median; the shape (a cluster with a tail on the slow side) is what to compare.


Verification


The report's n is 30; the median lies inside [q1, q3]; the mean lies above the median when there is a slow tail. Recompute the median by hand from the printed raw list: sort it and take the mean of the 15th and 16th values.


Troubleshooting


    

  • All times near 0 → the sort was optimised away → check that keep is called on a value of the sorted copy.
    • 

  • Times much larger than a classmate's → sanitizers or -O0 in the build → rebuild with the command from step 1.
    • 

  • Medians of repeated sessions disagree by much more than the IQR → a slow drift or other load → interleave and repeat at a quiet time; record the conditions.
    • 



Extension


Time the sort for 50,000, 100,000, 200,000 and 400,000 elements and report median and IQR for each. Does the median grow roughly in proportion to the size? (The sort's growth rate is an algorithms topic; here, only report what you measured.)

Summary


    

  • Timings are right-skewed: a hard lower edge and a tail of slow runs.
    • 

  • Median (middle value; mean of the two middle values for even n) and nearest-rank percentiles (rank ⌈p/100 × n⌉) describe them robustly; the IQR = q3 − q1 measures the spread.
    • 

  • Tukey's fences q1 − 1.5·IQR and q3 + 1.5·IQR flag outliers for investigation; never delete them silently.
    • 

  • Protocol: warm-ups, at least 20 timed runs (30 in this course), median with IQR, all conditions recorded, raw runs kept.
    • 

  • Compare versions with interleaved runs and medians; overlapping middle halves mean no claim.
    • 

  • One run per version proves nothing: in the forensic capture, "6.6× faster" was a one-time setup cost in run 1; the medians differ by 0.2 %.
