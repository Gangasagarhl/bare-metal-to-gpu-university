Story hook

Analogy

Ravi makes tea for the family every morning, and he has started timing the kettle. Ten mornings, ten numbers: 182 seconds, 176, 190, 185 and so on. His aunt asks the obvious question: "So how long does the kettle take?" Ravi could read out all ten numbers, but nobody wants that.

He tries one number: about 183 seconds. That is the centre. But his aunt asks a second question: "Can I count on 183, or is it sometimes much longer?" One number cannot answer that. Ravi needs a second one: how far a typical morning is from the centre. A few seconds, it turns out. Two numbers, the centre and the typical distance from it, tell the family almost everything they need about the kettle.

Those two numbers are the mean and the standard deviation. They are the most used summary in all of engineering: sensor noise is a standard deviation, a Kalman filter tracks variances, and a benchmark report without a spread is not a benchmark report. This chapter shows how to compute them correctly, including a way to compute them that looks right and gives a negative answer.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute the expected value of a discrete random variable and the mean of a data set, by hand and in C++.
  • Compute the variance and standard deviation, explain their units, and choose between dividing by n and by n − 1.
  • Apply the rules for shifting and scaling, for sums of independent variables, and for the mean of n readings (spread falls as 1/√n).
  • Explain the law of large numbers and use the standard error of a mean.
  • Diagnose a variance computed with the one-pass "mean of squares minus square of mean" formula that is wrong or negative, and fix it.
Jargon box
Expected value (mean of a distribution)
Simple words: the long-run average value of a random variable. Analogy: the balance point of the scoreboard bars of F0-57 if they were weights on a ruler. Precise: for a discrete X, E[X] = Σ x · P(X = x) over all possible x; written μ (mu). For a fair die, E[X] = (1 + 2 + … + 6)/6 = 3.5, a value the die itself never shows. D1R4
Sample mean
Simple words: the ordinary average of measured numbers. Analogy: Ravi's ten kettle times added up and divided by ten. Precise: x̄ = (x1 + … + xn)/n; an estimate of E[X] computed from data. D1
Deviation
Simple words: how far one value is from the mean, with a sign. Analogy: "this morning was 7 seconds slower than usual". Precise: xi − x̄; the deviations of a data set always add up to 0. D1
Variance
Simple words: the average of the squared distances from the mean: a measure of spread. Analogy: how wobbly the kettle is, measured in "seconds squared". Precise: Var(X) = E[(X − μ)2] = E[X2] − μ2, written σ2. From data: s2 = Σ(xi − x̄)2/(n − 1) (sample variance) or the same sum divided by n. D1
Standard deviation
Simple words: the typical distance of a value from the mean, in the same units as the data. Analogy: "the kettle is usually within a few seconds of its average". Precise: σ = √Var(X); from data, s = √s2. Abbreviated sd or std dev. D1
Standard error of the mean
Simple words: how much the average itself would wobble if you repeated the whole set of measurements. Analogy: Ravi's ten-morning average changes a little from one fortnight to the next, much less than single mornings do. Precise: for n independent readings each with standard deviation σ, the mean has standard deviation σ/√n; estimated by s/√n. D1R2
Law of large numbers
Simple words: the average of many independent repeats gets closer and closer to the expected value. Analogy: after a year of mornings, Ravi's average kettle time hardly changes when one more morning is added. Precise: for independent, identically distributed Xi with mean μ, the sample mean x̄n converges to μ as n grows. D1
Catastrophic cancellation
Simple words: subtracting two nearly equal big numbers that each carry rounding error, so that only the error is left. Analogy: weighing a feather by weighing a truck with and without it. Precise: loss of significant digits when computing a − b with a ≈ b in floating-point arithmetic; the relative error of the difference can be huge even if a and b are accurate to many digits. MA302 treats it fully. D2R3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 plots Ravi's ten kettle times as dots, one per row, on a common time axis. The vertical line is the mean, 182.8 s. Each dashed line is a deviation: how far that morning was from the mean. The shaded band reaches one standard deviation either side of the mean. Most dots lie inside the band; a few lie outside.

Figure 2 shows why averaging works. For each n, a computer rolled n dice, took their mean, and repeated that 4,000 times. The dot is the average of those 4,000 means; the bar reaches one standard deviation of the means either side. Every time n grows four times, the bar becomes half as long.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

To find the centre of some numbers, add them and divide by how many there are. That is the mean, the everyday "average". Ravi's ten kettle times add up to 1,828 seconds, so the mean is 182.8 seconds. Imagine each time as a small weight on a long ruler. The mean is the point where the ruler balances.

Now the wobble. For each morning, subtract the mean: that is the deviation. Some are negative (faster mornings), some positive (slower ones). You cannot average them as they are, because they always cancel to zero. So you square them first: squares are never negative. The average of the squares is the variance.

The variance has a strange unit: seconds squared. To get back to seconds, take the square root. That is the standard deviation, about 4.6 seconds for Ravi's kettle. It says: a typical morning is a few seconds away from 182.8. A morning 30 seconds away would be very unusual.

The last idea is the most useful. One morning can be far from the mean. The average of many mornings cannot wobble as much, because fast and slow mornings cancel. With four times as many mornings, the average wobbles half as much. Engineers use this every day: to measure something more precisely, measure it many times and average.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Expected value

For a discrete random variable, E[X] = Σ x · p(x): each value weighted by its probability. A fair die gives E[X] = 3.5. Bernoulli(p) gives E[X] = 0 · (1 − p) + 1 · p = p. Binomial(n, p) gives np: 5 heads in 10 fair tosses, on average. For a continuous variable the sum becomes the area under x · f(x), which you will compute with integrals in MA301; for symmetric densities such as the uniform on [a, b], the mean is the centre, (a + b)/2. D1R4

Expectation is linear: E[aX + b] = a E[X] + b, and E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] for any X and Y, independent or not. Two dice therefore have an expected total of 3.5 + 3.5 = 7, which matches the peak of Figure 1 in F0-56. D1

Variance and standard deviation

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)2]. Expanding the square gives the equivalent form Var(X) = E[X2] − μ2, which is handy on paper. For a die: E[X2] = 91/6 ≈ 15.17, so Var(X) = 15.17 − 12.25 ≈ 2.917 and σ ≈ 1.708 (run R4). Bernoulli(p) has variance p(1 − p); Binomial(n, p) has np(1 − p). D1R4

The standard deviation has the units of the data; the variance has those units squared. Report σ to people ("4.6 s"); calculate with σ2 when combining sources of spread, because variances add and standard deviations do not (next subsection). D1

Shifting, scaling and adding

  • Shift: Var(X + b) = Var(X). Adding 10 s to every kettle time moves the mean by 10 s and leaves the spread alone.
  • Scale: Var(aX) = a2 Var(X), so σaX = |a| σX. A thermometer reading 20 °C with sd 0.5 °C, converted with F = 1.8 C + 32, reads 68 °F with sd 0.9 °F (run R4).
  • Sum of independent variables: Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y). Two dice: variance 2 × 2.917 = 5.833, sd 2.415, not 2 × 1.708. Without independence there is an extra covariance term (F0-60).
  • Mean of n independent readings with the same σ: Var(x̄) = σ2/n, so sd(x̄) = σ/√n. Twenty-five readings with sd 0.4 give a mean with sd 0.08; to get from 0.4 to 0.1 you need (0.4/0.1)2 = 16 readings.

Run R2 checks the last rule by simulation: the measured spread of the means (1.706, 0.876, 0.424, 0.213, 0.107) follows σ/√n (1.708, 0.854, 0.427, 0.213, 0.107) closely. D1R2R4

From data: n or n − 1?

With data, you do not know μ; you use x̄ instead. Deviations from x̄ are on average a little smaller than deviations from the true μ, because x̄ is computed from the same data and sits in its middle. Dividing the sum of squared deviations by n − 1 instead of n corrects for this, so that s2 = Σ(xi − x̄)2/(n − 1) has expected value σ2. This is called Bessel's correction. For Ravi's ten times, the two choices give 19.36 and 21.51 s2 (sd 4.40 and 4.64 s). D1R1

The rule used in this course: divide by n − 1 when you estimate the spread of the process that produced the data (the kettle in general), and by n when the data are the whole population you care about (these exact ten mornings). Always say which one you used; for large n the difference is small. D1

The law of large numbers and the standard error

As n grows, x̄ gets closer to μ (law of large numbers), and σ/√n says how close: the standard error. It is the honest "±" for a measured mean. Ravi's mean of 182.8 s from ten mornings with s = 4.64 s has a standard error of about 4.64/√10 ≈ 1.5 s. Saying "the kettle takes 182.8 s" is false precision; "182.8 ± 1.5 s (mean ± standard error, n = 10)" is a measurement. D1R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

How far can a value be from the mean? Chebyshev's inequality holds for every distribution with a finite variance: P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k2. At most 25 % of values lie two or more standard deviations away, and at most about 11 % lie three or more away. These bounds are loose; for the Gaussian (F0-59) the true fractions are much smaller. Use Chebyshev when you know nothing about the shape, which is often the honest situation with run times. D1R4

The mean is not robust. One wild value moves the mean and inflates the standard deviation a lot. Add a single 260 s morning (someone forgot to switch the kettle on) to Ravi's ten times: the mean jumps from 182.8 to 189.8 s and the sample sd from 4.6 to 23.7 s (run R4). Benchmarks are full of such values, which is why F0-63 reports medians and percentiles instead. R4

How to compute a variance on a computer. The textbook form E[X2] − μ2 invites a one-pass program: sum x and x2, then subtract. When the mean is large compared with the spread, the two terms are huge and nearly equal, and their difference is dominated by rounding error (catastrophic cancellation). Run R3 shows this on 1,000 readings between 99,998 and 100,002, true variance 2: in float the formula gives −24,576; in double it gives 2 for these values but 0 when the same wobble sits around 1,000,000,000. The two-pass method, which first computes x̄ and then sums (xi − x̄)2, gives 2. Use two passes when the data fit in memory, or a one-pass running update that keeps the mean and the sum of squared deviations as it goes. D2R3

Not verified — check before relying on this

The one-pass running update (for each new x: n ← n + 1; δ = x − mean; mean ← mean + δ/n; M2 ← M2 + δ · (x − mean); variance = M2/(n − 1)) is widely named after B. P. Welford, and textbooks on numerical computing discuss its accuracy. The attribution and the accuracy statement were not checked in this build. Check them in the MA302 numerical-methods textbook chosen by the Dean or in Donald E. Knuth, "The Art of Computer Programming", volume 2 (Seminumerical Algorithms), before relying on them. The update formula itself is tested in this chapter's mini-project against the two-pass result.

How the hardware actually does it

A float stores about 24 significant binary digits and a double about 53. Near 100,000 the gap between neighbouring float values is 1/128 of a unit, and near 10,000,000,000 (100,000 squared) it is 1,024. Each square in the forensic program's float version is already rounded to that grid of 1,024, and the running sum of 1,000 squares grows to about 1013, where neighbouring float values are about a million apart. After dividing by n, the mean of squares is uncertain by thousands; subtracting the square of the mean leaves only that error, here −24,576. The arithmetic unit did exactly what IEEE 754 binary floating-point arithmetic prescribes; the formula asked it for more digits than the format has. D2D3R3

Note

The grid spacings above follow from the formats: a float has a 24-bit significand, so between 216 = 65,536 and 217 its spacing is 216−23 = 2−7 = 1/128, between 233 and 234 (1010 lies there) it is 233−23 = 210 = 1,024, and between 243 and 244 (1013 lies there) it is 220 = 1,048,576. The format sizes are stated in IEEE 754 (title only in this build); MA302 (F0-73) treats the formats in depth. D3

A GPU or a multi-threaded CPU computes means and variances by splitting the data, summing parts in parallel and combining the partial results (a reduction). The order of additions then changes from run to run or from machine to machine, so results can differ in the last digits; CU302 and MA302 turn this into test tolerances. Parallel variance needs a way to merge partial (n, mean, M2) triples, which exists and is part of the same family as the running update above. D2U1

Worked example

Ravi's ten kettle times (seconds): 182, 176, 190, 185, 179, 188, 181, 184, 177, 186.

  1. Mean. Sum = 1,828; x̄ = 1,828/10 = 182.8 s.
  2. Deviations. −0.8, −6.8, +7.2, +2.2, −3.8, +5.2, −1.8, +1.2, −5.8, +3.2. Check: they add up to 0.
  3. Squared deviations. 0.64, 46.24, 51.84, 4.84, 14.44, 27.04, 3.24, 1.44, 33.64, 10.24; sum = 193.6 s2.
  4. Variance. Sample: 193.6/9 ≈ 21.51 s2. Population: 193.6/10 = 19.36 s2.
  5. Standard deviation. s = √21.51 ≈ 4.64 s (or 4.40 s dividing by n).
  6. Standard error of the mean. 4.64/√10 ≈ 1.47 s. Report: 182.8 ± 1.5 s (mean ± standard error, n = 10, sd 4.6 s).
  7. Prediction. If Ravi averages 40 mornings instead of 10, the standard error halves to about 0.73 s; the standard deviation of single mornings stays about 4.6 s. More data makes the mean more certain; it does not make the kettle steadier.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The balance point can be where no weight is. On the ruler, the mean is the balance point, but nothing needs to sit there: a die's mean is 3.5, a face it never shows. The mean is a property of the whole set, not a typical member. D1
    • 

  • "Typical distance" is not the average distance. The standard deviation squares the distances before averaging and then takes the root, so large distances count more than in a plain average of |deviations|. For Ravi's data the average absolute deviation is 3.8 s and the sd 4.4 s (dividing by n). W1
    • 

  • Averaging only helps against independent wobble. Ravi's kettle is slower in winter, when the tap water is colder. Averaging more winter mornings does not remove that; it is a shift of the mean, not noise around it. F0-62 calls this bias and F0-60 treats wobbles that move together. D1
    •

Lab


Goal


Measure a repeatable household task at least 15 times, report its mean with standard deviation and standard error, and verify σ/√n with your own data by averaging groups.


Hardware and software needed


A stopwatch (a phone or a kitchen timer) and a repeatable task: the kettle, walking from the door to the window, or tapping a pencil 20 times. A computer with a C++ compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 with the flags shown in the build command. No data are given here on purpose: your numbers are yours.


Safety


If you time a kettle, let an adult handle the boiling water; you only start and stop the watch. Otherwise none beyond normal computer use.

  1. Time the task at least 16 times. Write the times in a file mine.in, one per line.
    2.

    3. 

  3. Split your times into 4 groups of 4 (in the order measured) and compute the mean of each group by hand or with the program.
    4. 

  4. Compute the standard deviation of the four group means. Compare it with s/√4 = s/2.
    5.

Expected observations


The deviations add up to 0 (up to rounding). The spread of the group means is smaller than the spread of single times, near s/2, but with only four groups the agreement is rough; run R2 needed 4,000 repeats to match σ/√n to two digits. If your first few times are systematically different (you were still learning to start the watch), that is a shift, not noise: note it.

Method hints


A variance can never be negative, and a nonzero wobble cannot have variance 0: those are checks you can apply to any output. Summary


    

  • The mean (expected value) is the balance point: E[X] = Σ x p(x); from data, x̄ = Σxi/n.
    • 

  • The variance is the mean squared deviation; the standard deviation is its square root, in the data's units.
    • 

  • Shifting does not change spread; scaling by a multiplies the sd by |a|; variances of independent variables add.
    • 

  • The mean of n independent readings has sd σ/√n (standard error): four times the data, half the uncertainty.
    • 

  • Divide by n − 1 to estimate a process's variance from a sample, and say which divisor you used.
    • 

  • The mean and sd are sensitive to outliers; F0-63 adds robust summaries.
    • 

  • Never compute a variance as "mean of squares minus square of mean" in limited precision; use two passes or a running update.
    •
