Story hook

The take-away stand gets a new lamp over the order window. The electrician leaves two things: a wiring plan of the stand, with every switch, socket and cable drawn as small symbols and labels, and the lamp's rating plate: "230 V, 60 W, max 0.5 A". Amara wants to add a second lamp on the same switch. The wiring plan tells her which cable goes where; the rating plates tell her whether the switch can carry both lamps. Without the plan she would be guessing which wire is live. Without the plates she would be guessing whether the switch melts.

A firmware engineer has the same two documents: the board's schematic, which says that "the green LED" is wired to pin PA5 through a 330 Ω resistor, and the chip's datasheet, which says how much current that pin may give. In this chapter you read both for the university's teaching board, compute what your firmware must do, and find four mistakes in a "cost-down" revision before anyone builds it. You then write the course project: the board-support note that gathers everything HW303 taught about one board.

What you will be able to do

  • Read a schematic: symbols, reference designators, values, nets, net labels, power symbols and "do not populate" marks.
  • Find in a datasheet the numbers a firmware engineer needs: pin current limits, input and output levels, pull resistor values, supply and decoupling rules, and distinguish absolute maximum ratings from operating conditions.
  • Work out from a schematic whether each LED and button is active-high or active-low, which GPIO settings it needs, and whether its current is within limits.
  • Check a netlist automatically for basic rule violations, and explain each one.
  • Write a board-support note that a classmate can use to bring up the board, with every fact's status (verified, measured, untested) stated.
Jargon box
Schematic
Simple words: a drawing of which parts of a circuit are connected to which, using symbols. Analogy: the stand's wiring plan. Precise: a diagram of a circuit's components (as standard symbols with reference designators and values) and their electrical connections (wires, net labels, power symbols); it shows connectivity, not physical layout. D1 D2
Netlist
Simple words: the schematic written as a list of "this pin connects to that wire". Analogy: the electrician's cable list. Precise: a text description of a circuit's components and the nets each terminal is connected to, exported by schematic-capture tools and used for layout and checking. D2 R1
Net and net label
Simple words: one electrical connection, and the name written on it. Analogy: one cable, with its tag. Precise: a net is a set of terminals that are electrically the same node; a net label gives it a name, and two wires with the same label are connected even if no line is drawn between them. D2
Reference designator
Simple words: the short name of each part on a board, like R1 or U1. Analogy: the numbered tag on each appliance. Precise: a unique identifier made of a letter prefix for the part type (R resistor, C capacitor, D diode/LED, U integrated circuit, SW switch, J connector) and a number, linking schematic, bill of materials and the printed board. D1
Absolute maximum ratings
Simple words: limits that may damage the chip if exceeded, even briefly. Analogy: "never above 250 °C" on the oven: not a cooking temperature. Precise: the datasheet table of stress limits (supply voltage, pin voltages, pin and total currents, temperatures) beyond which permanent damage may occur; operation is guaranteed only within the separate, narrower operating conditions. D1 D3
Electrical characteristics (min/typ/max)
Simple words: the tables saying what the chip guarantees under normal conditions. Analogy: the rating plate's normal values. Precise: datasheet tables of parameters (output voltage at a given current, input thresholds, leakage, pull-up resistance, supply current) with minimum, typical and maximum values under stated conditions; designs must use min/max, because typical values are not guaranteed. D1 D3
Decoupling capacitor
Simple words: a small capacitor next to a chip's power pin that smooths its supply. Analogy: a small water tank next to the tap for sudden demand. Precise: a capacitor (often 100 nF ceramic per supply pin, plus bulk capacitance) placed close to an IC's supply and ground pins to supply fast current transients and reduce supply noise; datasheets specify the arrangement. D1 D3
DNP (do not populate)
Simple words: a part that is drawn in the schematic but not soldered on the board. Analogy: a socket drawn on the plan but never installed. Precise: a marking on a schematic or bill of materials for footprints left empty in a given build variant; firmware must treat the part as absent. D2
Active-high and active-low
Simple words: whether "on" means the pin is high or low. Analogy: a switch that is "on" when up, versus one that is "on" when down. Precise: a signal is active-high if its asserted state is the high voltage level and active-low if it is the low level; an LED from pin to ground is active-high, an LED from supply to pin is active-low, a button to ground with a pull-up reads low when pressed. D1 R1
Board-support note
Simple words: the short document that tells a programmer everything about one board. Analogy: the stand's handbook for a new cook. Precise: in this course, a document collecting a board's chip and document revisions, memory map and linker settings, clock configuration, pin assignments with active levels, start-up and build commands, debug connection, bring-up evidence and known issues, each with its verification status. C1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A schematic is a map of a circuit. Every part has a symbol, like a little picture, and a name tag, like R1 for the first resistor. Lines between them are wires. If two wires have the same name written on them, they are connected, even if no line joins them on the page.

A datasheet is the instruction leaflet of one chip. Among many pages, it has a few tables that say "never more than this" and "this is how strong each pin is". If you connect a light directly to a pin and it takes too much current, the pin can be damaged. The schematic tells you what is connected; the datasheet tells you whether that is safe, and how the program must switch it.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Reading a schematic for firmware

Analogy first: wiring plan and rating plates. Precisely: as a firmware engineer, you read a schematic to answer a fixed list of questions for every part your code touches: which MCU pin, through which parts, to which supply or ground; which value each part has; whether it is fitted in this build variant. D2 C1 For U-BOARD-1 rev A, following the nets of Figure 1:

  • D1: PA5 → R1 (330 Ω) → D1 anode; D1 cathode → GND. The pin must be high to light it: active-high, and the pin sources the current.
  • D2: +3V3 → R3 (470 Ω) → D2 anode; D2 cathode → PB0. The pin must be low to light it: active-low, the pin sinks the current. Writing 1 to PB0 turns D2 off.
  • SW1: PC13 → SW1 → GND, and R2 (10 kΩ) from PC13 to +3V3. Not pressed: R2 pulls PC13 high; pressed: the switch pulls it low. Active-low, no internal pull needed.
  • Decoupling: C1 and C2, 100 nF each, from +3V3 to GND: one per VDD pin. R1

Reading a datasheet for firmware


The datasheet sections a firmware engineer opens most often are: the pinout and alternate-function tables (which peripheral can use which pin; see F1-76), the absolute maximum ratings, the operating conditions, the I/O port characteristics (output voltage at a given current, input thresholds, internal pull-up and pull-down resistance ranges, maximum current per pin and per group of pins), the supply-current tables (for battery budgets), and the power-supply scheme with its decoupling recommendation. D1 D3 Three reading rules:


    

  1. Absolute maximum is not a design value (Figure 2). A design that runs a pin at its absolute maximum current is wrong, even if it works on the bench today. D1
    2. 

  2. Design with min and max, never typical. An internal pull-up given as "typ 40 kΩ" may have a guaranteed range much wider than that; a button debouncing calculation must use the range. D3
    3. 

  3. Read the conditions column. "VOH ≥ VDD − 0.4 V at IOH = 8 mA" holds at that current, not at 20 mA. D3
    4. 



The teaching chip U-MCU-1 has one datasheet rule in this course: an I/O pin may source or sink at most 8 mA (the limit line of the netlist), plus a decoupling rule of one capacitor per VDD pin. Both are definitions for teaching, not values of a real part. R1



The LED current, step by step


With an ideal pin (high = 3.3 V, low = 0 V) and an LED dropping a fixed forward voltage VF = 2.0 V, the resistor carries the rest: I = (3.3 − 2.0) V / R. For R1 = 330 Ω: 1.3 / 330 = 3.9 mA; for R3 = 470 Ω: 2.8 mA, both below 8 mA. R1 (Ohm's law is F1-02; LEDs as diodes are F1-08.) Real pins have an output resistance, so their output voltage falls as the current rises, and real LEDs' forward voltage depends on colour and current; both make the real current somewhat lower than this estimate, which is why the model's numbers are an upper-bound style check, not a measurement. D3 D4



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


Pin limits are not the only limits


Besides a per-pin limit, datasheets usually give a limit for the total current of all I/O pins, or of a group of pins sharing a supply pin, and for the total current into VDD and out of VSS. D1 Eight LEDs at 6 mA each may all be within the per-pin limit and still exceed the total. A board-support note should list the worst case: all outputs on at once. Inputs have limits too: a voltage above the supply on an input forces current through its protection diodes; some pins are specified as tolerant of a higher voltage (often marked "FT" or similar in the pinout table) and others are not. D1 D3



Variants, revisions and the firmware


Boards change. A "cost-down" revision may remove parts (marked DNP), change values or swap pins, and the firmware must follow. Good practice is that the board revision is readable by firmware (a few resistors to GPIOs forming an ID, or an EEPROM), and that the board-support note records which revision each fact was checked on. D2 D4 A netlist checker like Listing 1, run on every revision's export, turns some schematic review items into tests; real projects use the electrical-rule check of their schematic tool for the general rules, and simple scripts for project-specific ones. D2



Floating inputs


An input pin that nothing drives is not "0": its voltage drifts with leakage currents and nearby signals, and a CMOS input sitting near its threshold can draw extra supply current and read random values. D3 Every input therefore needs a defined level: an external pull resistor, the chip's internal pull (configured in the GPIO registers, F1-76), or an always-present driver. Unused pins are typically configured as analog or as outputs, according to the vendor's recommendation in the reference manual. D1

How the hardware actually does it


A GPIO output stage is two transistors (F1-76, Figure 1); each has an on-resistance, so the pin behaves like an ideal switch in series with a few tens of ohms, and the output voltage moves away from the rail as current rises. D3 Too much current heats that transistor and the thin metal tracks that feed it, which is why limits exist per pin and per supply pin. D3 An LED is a diode whose current rises steeply once its forward voltage is reached, so it must never be connected to a pin without a series resistor or another current limit: the resistor, not the LED, sets the current. D4 Decoupling capacitors exist because a CMOS chip draws its current in short spikes on every clock edge; the supply wiring's inductance cannot deliver those spikes quickly, so a capacitor right at the pin does. D3 Removing them may not stop a chip working at the bench, but may cause rare resets and corrupted reads that are very hard to trace in firmware.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A building's wiring plan often matches the walls; a schematic does not match the board's layout. Parts next to each other in the schematic may be far apart on the board; for layout questions (where is the LED physically?) you need the board drawing, not the schematic. D2
    • 

  • A rating plate gives one number; a datasheet gives conditions. The same pin may be allowed 8 mA at one supply voltage and less at another, or a total budget across pins. D1
    • 

  • Cables in a building carry power to devices; a board's "wires" are also signals whose meaning (active-high or active-low) is only in the schematic. The same pin value means "on" for D1 and "off" for D2.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: an LED is on when the code says "off". Cause: active-low LED treated as active-high. Fix: read the schematic; wrap the polarity in one function (ledOn()) named in the board-support note.
    • 

  • Symptom: a button press is detected randomly. Cause: floating input (pull resistor DNP or not configured). Fix: fit the resistor or enable the internal pull.
    • 

  • Symptom: a pin "works" at 30 mA on the bench. Cause: design based on absolute maximum or not checked at all. Fix: design to operating conditions with margin; the chip may degrade or fail later.
    • 

  • Symptom: rare resets or glitches with no software cause. Cause: missing or misplaced decoupling. Fix: check the schematic and the board against the datasheet's supply scheme.
    • 

  • Symptom: firmware written for one board revision misbehaves on another. Cause: parts changed or DNP. Fix: record the revision in the board-support note and check the netlist of each revision.
    •

Summary


    

  • A schematic shows connectivity: symbols, reference designators, values, nets and labels, DNP marks; a netlist is its text form.

  • For each part your code touches: which pin, through what, to which supply, fitted or not.

Forensic lab answer key


Changes from rev A. R1 changed from 330 Ω to 33 Ω; R2 marked DNP; C2 marked DNP.


Root cause. Four problems, as reported. (1, 2) D1's current is (3.3 − 2.0) / 33 = 39.4 mA (ideal-pin estimate): above the 8 mA pin limit and above the LED's 20 mA limit. In reality the pin's output resistance limits the current somewhat, but the pin and LED are overstressed; this explains "D1 went dim after a day" (a damaged LED or pin). The value was almost certainly a typo or a parts-list merge (330 → 33). (3) With R2 not fitted and no firmware pull, PC13 floats when the button is open, so it reads random values: "the button presses itself". (4) With C2 removed, only one 100 nF capacitor serves two VDD pins, against the teaching datasheet's rule; it explains no complaint yet, but invites rare resets.
