Story hook

At the take-away stand every appliance has its own wall switch, and they are all off at night. In the morning Amara flips the switches she needs: the hot plate, the order window's light. The juicer stays off; nobody ordered juice yet. Ravi once spent ten minutes pressing the juicer's buttons before he noticed its wall switch was down. The juicer had not complained. It had simply ignored him.

The stand also has a rhythm. Amara taps a slow beat at opening time, while she is alone. When the second cook arrives they switch to a fast beat, and everyone's work speeds up: the hot plate's timer, the rhythm at the order window, even how fast tickets are called. One day a helper kept counting the old slow beat while calling out ticket numbers. The customers heard numbers too fast to follow.

Peripherals in a microcontroller have both: a power switch for their clock, and a speed set by a shared rhythm. This chapter shows how a peripheral is built and controlled, how the clock tree feeds it, and what goes wrong when the beat changes and one helper does not notice.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe a peripheral as a register block: control, status and data registers, reset values and access types.
  • Trace a clock from its oscillator through PLL, system clock switch and bus prescalers to a peripheral, and compute every frequency on the way.
  • Explain clock gating and why a peripheral with its clock off "ignores" writes.
  • Compute a UART divisor from the real peripheral clock and the error of the resulting baud rate.
  • Recognise the limits of an emulator: show with QEMU's own log which parts of a chip it does not model.
  • Diagnose garbled serial output caused by a divisor computed for the wrong clock.
Jargon box
Register block
Simple words: the set of control knobs and display windows of one peripheral, each at its own address. Analogy: an appliance's control panel. Precise: a contiguous range of memory-mapped registers belonging to one peripheral instance, at a base address plus fixed offsets, each register with documented fields, reset value and access type. D8 §2.1.3 D10 §4.3.1 Table 4-7
Access type
Simple words: what you are allowed to do with a register bit: read it, write it, or clear it by writing a 1. Analogy: a dial you can turn, a lamp you can only look at, a "reset" button. Precise: the documented behaviour of a field on read and write, such as read-write, read-only, write-only, or write-1-to-clear; manuals use short codes for these, such as RW, RO, WO and WC (write-clear: writing 1 clears the bit, writing 0 does nothing) in the RP2350 datasheet (F1-78). D8 App. A
Clock tree
Simple words: the network that takes one or two basic ticking signals and makes all the speeds the chip's parts need. Analogy: the stand's rhythm, passed on and slowed down for each helper. Precise: the oscillators, phase-locked loops, multiplexers, dividers (prescalers) and gates that generate and distribute the clock signals of a chip, from the clock sources to each bus and peripheral. D8 §8.1.2, §8.1.3
Oscillator (internal RC, crystal)
Simple words: the part that produces the basic tick. Analogy: the first beat Amara taps. Precise: a clock source; an on-chip oscillator (an RC or ring oscillator) needs no external parts but its frequency varies with process, voltage and temperature, while a crystal oscillator uses an external crystal for a precise, stable frequency. D8 §8.1.2.2, §8.1.2.3
PLL (phase-locked loop)
Simple words: a circuit that makes a faster clock from a slower one. Analogy: the second cook joins and the beat doubles. Precise: a feedback circuit that locks an internal voltage-controlled oscillator to a multiple of its reference input; on microcontrollers it multiplies an oscillator's frequency to produce the high system clock. D8 §8.1.2.6, §8.6.1
Prescaler
Simple words: a divider that slows a clock down by a fixed number. Analogy: "move at every second beat". Precise: a frequency divider in the clock path, set by a register field, producing fout = fin / N. D8 §8.1.3.3
Clock gating (clock enable)
Simple words: switching a peripheral's clock on or off with one bit. Analogy: the appliance's wall switch. Precise: a gate in a peripheral's clock path controlled by an enable bit in the clock controller; with the clock off, the peripheral's logic does not change state (it keeps its configuration), which saves power in the clock network; what its registers do meanwhile is chip-specific. D8 §8.1.3.5
Baud rate and divisor
Simple words: how many symbols per second a serial line sends, and the number that sets it. Analogy: how fast ticket numbers are called. Precise: the symbol rate of a UART; the peripheral derives it from its input clock through a programmable divisor, so the divisor must be computed from the peripheral clock actually present. D10 §4.3.1 D8 §12.1.3.2.1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every helper machine on the chip has a little control panel. Some knobs you turn (control), some lamps you only look at (status), and one tray where you put things in or take them out (data). You find each panel by its house number.

Every machine also needs a heartbeat to work. The chip makes one fast heartbeat and hands slower copies to the different groups of machines. And every machine has its own switch for that heartbeat. If the switch is off, the machine does nothing, even if you turn its knobs.

When you change the heartbeat speed, every machine's speed changes too. A machine that sends letters down a wire must be told the new speed, or the letters arrive as nonsense.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Peripherals are register blocks

Analogy first: an appliance's control panel. Precisely: each peripheral instance occupies a block of addresses (its base address, from the memory map) and offers registers at fixed offsets. The vendor's manual documents every register with its offset, reset value and fields, and every field with its bit positions and access type; Arm's CMSDK manual, for example, gives each UART register's offset, type, width and reset value in one table. D10 §4.3.1 Table 4-7 D8 §2.1.3 The kinds repeat across chips: control registers enable the block and choose modes; status registers report events (a byte received, a timer overflowed) and are set by hardware; data registers move the payload; configuration registers hold divisors, thresholds, compare values; the CMSDK UART has one of each kind (CTRL, STATE, DATA, BAUDDIV). D10 §4.3.1 Table 4-7 Several instances of the same peripheral (USART1, USART2, …) share one register layout at different base addresses, which is why the drivers in F1-73 and F1-74 described the layout once as a struct and placed it at a base address. CMSIS device headers do exactly this: one register-layout typedef per peripheral; ST's header for the STM32F100xB uses one USART_TypeDef for every USART, each at its own base address. D5 "Device Header File <Device.h>" D9 USART_TypeDef, USART1_BASE, USART2_BASE

Status bits deserve care: some are cleared by writing 1 to them (the CMSDK UART's overrun bits, fields marked WC in the RP2350 datasheet), some by a read (the SysTick COUNTFLAG of F1-77 is cleared when software reads its register), reading a FIFO data register removes the value it returns (the RP2350's RF and RWF field types), and other manuals use other rules. The access type tells you which. D10 §4.3.1 Table 4-7 D8 App. A, §12.1.8 Table 1083 D11 B3.3.3 F1-78 trains reading these codes; its forensic lab is a "write 0 to a write-1-to-clear flag" bug.

From oscillator to peripheral

Analogy: one rhythm, passed on and slowed down. Precisely: a microcontroller has one or more clock sources, typically an on-chip oscillator that runs as soon as the chip is powered and the option of an external crystal oscillator for accuracy; the RP2350 boots from its ring oscillator and leaves the crystal oscillator off until software enables it. D8 §8.1.2.2, §8.1.2.3 A PLL multiplies a source up to a higher frequency. A multiplexer chooses which signal becomes the system clock. Prescalers derive the bus clocks from it, and enables switch each destination's clock on or off. D8 §8.1.3 Switching to a faster clock is therefore a deliberate sequence in start-up code. On the RP2350 it is: enable the crystal oscillator, wait for its XOSC_STABLE flag, start the PLL, wait for its LOCK bit, then switch the system clock over through a glitchless multiplexer. D8 §8.1.2.3, §8.1.2.6, §8.1.3.2 Chips with on-chip flash may also need more flash wait states before the faster clock is selected (unverified for a named part in this pass; see the box in "How the hardware actually does it").

Every peripheral speed depends on its input clock


A UART does not know what a baud rate is; it divides its input clock by the divisor you wrote. The CMSDK manual's own example: with a 12 MHz peripheral clock and 9,600 baud, BAUDDIV must be 12,000,000 / 9,600 = 1,250. D10 §4.3.1 In the teaching chip's simple model, divisor = round(fperipheral / baud) and the real baud = fperipheral / divisor. From 8 MHz at 115,200 baud: 8,000,000 / 115,200 = 69.44 → 69; 8,000,000 / 69 = 115,942 baud, +0.64 %. From 36 MHz: 312.5 → 313 (rounded half up), 115,016 baud, −0.16 %. R1 A receiver samples each bit at times computed from its own clock, so the two ends tolerate only a small mismatch (box below); Listing 1 uses 2 % as its warning threshold, an exercise value. R1

Note
Real UARTs usually oversample (for example 16 samples per bit) and many have fractional divisors, so the formula in a real reference manual is more detailed than the teaching model's: the RP2350's UART uses divisor = UARTCLK / (16 × baud), split into a 16-bit integer part and a 6-bit fractional part. The idea is the same: the divisor is a function of the real peripheral clock. D8 §12.1.3.1, §12.1.3.2.1



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


What "clock off" really means


Clock gating exists to save power: the RP2350 datasheet says its clock enables help reduce power in the clock distribution networks for unused components, which keep their configuration while unclocked. D8 §8.1.3.5 Chips differ in the default: on the RP2350 every clock enable resets to "on", and peripherals are instead held in reset at power-up until software releases them; ST's STM32F1 header has a clock-enable bit per peripheral in the RCC enable registers, and software sets the ones it needs (their reset values are in ST's manual; box in "How the hardware actually does it"). D8 §8.1.3.5, §7.5.1 D9 RCC_APB2ENR_IOPCEN, RCC_APB2ENR_USART1EN The consequence for drivers is the most common microcontroller bring-up bug, also listed as a trap in the bridge chapter BR-07: "a device that reads zeros because its clock is off". C1 What exactly a gated peripheral does on a register access is chip-specific: writes may be ignored, reads may return zero, or the access may fault. The teaching model (Listing 1) chooses "reads 0, writes ignored" and says so in its output. R1


Two more details belong in every driver's first lines, both from the reference manual: the reset-and-clock controller often also has a reset bit per peripheral (ST's header defines RCC_APB2RSTR next to RCC_APB2ENR; the RP2350 has a separate reset controller), and the pins of a peripheral must be routed to it by the GPIO or pin-multiplexing block (the RP2350's "function select"). D9 RCC_TypeDef D8 §7.5, §9.4 A UART that is clocked and configured but whose TX pin is still a plain input sends nothing to the outside.

Order matters: the clock-change problem


Changing the system clock changes every bus clock derived from it. Any peripheral configured before the change keeps its divisors, which were computed for the old frequency (the divisor formulas above depend on the input clock). D10 §4.3.1 D8 §12.1.3.2.1 Two disciplines prevent this: configure clocks first and peripherals after (and never change clocks at run time without reconfiguring), or compute divisors from a single function that reports the current peripheral clock, the way CMSIS keeps the global variable SystemCoreClock, which "must always reflect the actual system clock speed" and is refreshed by SystemCoreClockUpdate() after every clock change. D5 "System and Clock Configuration" The forensic lab is the bug this prevents.



How the hardware actually does it


An on-chip oscillator's frequency varies with process, voltage and temperature: the RP2350's ring oscillator runs at a nominal 11 MHz during boot but is guaranteed only to lie between 4.6 MHz and 19.6 MHz. A crystal oscillator takes its frequency from an external crystal and gives a precise, stable reference; the RP2350 accepts 1–50 MHz crystals, and the Pico 2 design uses 12 MHz. D8 §8.1.2.2, §8.1.2.3 A PLL contains a voltage-controlled oscillator (750–1,600 MHz on the RP2350) whose output is divided by a feedback divider and compared with the reference in a phase-frequency detector; the loop steers the oscillator until the divided output matches the reference, so the output is the reference times the feedback ratio, divided down by post-dividers. The LOCK bit tells software when this has settled. D8 §8.6.1, §8.1.2.6 A prescaler is a counter that produces one output cycle per N input cycles, a direct use of the counters of F1-17. Switching or gating a clock must never produce a truncated pulse (a glitch), which can corrupt the logic it drives; the RP2350's dividers synchronise their enable to the end of a clock cycle, and its system and reference clocks have a glitchless multiplexer. D8 §8.1.3.2, §8.1.3.3

Worked example


Task. The teaching chip has an 8 MHz crystal. You need SYSCLK = 64 MHz, UART2 (on APB1) at 9,600 baud with less than 1 % error, and TIM2 counting at exactly 1 MHz. Choose the PLL source and multiplier, the APB1 divider, the UART2 divisor and the TIM2 prescaler.


    

  1. PLL. From the crystal: 8 MHz × M = 64 MHz → M = 8. (From IRC/2 = 4 MHz you would need M = 16, also allowed by the model; the crystal is more accurate.) PLL input 8 MHz is inside the 1–25 MHz window.
    2. 

  2. APB1. APB1 ≤ 36 MHz, so divide 64 MHz by 2: APB1 = 32 MHz. (A divider of 1 gives 64 MHz, too fast.)
    3. 

  3. UART2 divisor. 32,000,000 / 9,600 = 3,333.33 → 3,333. Real baud = 32,000,000 / 3,333 = 9,600.96; error = +0.01 %.
    4. 

  4. TIM2 clock. APB1 divider is 2, not 1, so by the teaching chip's rule TIM2 runs at 2 × 32 = 64 MHz.
    5. 

  5. TIM2 prescaler. To count at 1 MHz: 64 MHz / (PSC + 1) = 1 MHz → PSC = 63 (the "+1" convention of the teaching timer, F1-76). Forgetting step 4 and using 32 MHz would give PSC = 31 and a timer running twice too fast.
    6. 



Check the clock part by adding a configuration to clocktree.in (lab step 2); the model prints the same frequencies and divisor. The rule in step 4 is the teaching chip's; a real chip documents its own rule in its clock-tree chapter. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A juicer with its switch off is visibly dark; a gated peripheral looks exactly like a working one. In the emulator's placeholder blocks the access completed and reads returned 0; what a real chip returns for a gated peripheral is chip-specific. There is no lamp; you must check the enable bit. R2 R2
    • 

  • Helpers can adapt to a new rhythm; peripherals cannot. When the beat changes, a UART keeps dividing by its old divisor without any notion of "too fast". Every derived speed must be recomputed by software. R1
    • 

  • The analogy suggests one rhythm for everyone. Real chips have several independent clock domains (a low-speed oscillator for a real-time clock, separate clocks for USB or ADCs), and signals crossing between domains need synchronisation (F1-18): the RP2350 has a 32 kHz low-power oscillator for its always-on timer and a separate 48 MHz PLL for USB and the ADC, and its UART synchronises signals between its bus clock and its UART clock. D8 §8.1.2.1, §8.1.2.6, §12.1.2.10

Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a peripheral "does not react"; its registers read 0. Cause: its clock enable bit is off. How to find it: read back what you wrote (Listing 2's method); read the RCC enable register in the debugger. Fix: enable the clock first, then configure.
    • 

  • Symptom: serial output is garbage at the right baud setting. Cause: divisor computed for a different clock (clock changed later, wrong APB divider, crystal not fitted so the chip fell back to the internal oscillator). How to find it: compute the real baud from the real peripheral clock (Listing 1); measure the bit width with a logic analyser on hardware. Fix: configure clocks before peripherals; compute divisors from the current clock.
    • 

  • Symptom: the chip hangs during clock set-up. Cause: waiting forever for an oscillator or PLL "ready" flag (crystal missing or not oscillating). How to find it: halt in the debugger and look at where PC is. Fix: add a timeout and a fallback to the internal oscillator.
    • 

  • Symptom: works in the emulator, not on the board. Cause: the emulator does not model clocks or pin routing (Listing 2's log). Fix: treat emulator success as "the logic is right", never as "the driver is right".
    •

Forensic lab


Scenario. Lena's board prints a clean banner when she runs her first test at reset clock speed. After she adds clock_init() to run the chip from the crystal and PLL, the terminal at 115,200 baud shows only random characters. "The UART code did not change, so it must be the cable," she says.


Evidence pack. Her clock and UART settings, entered into the clock-tree model in the order her start-up code applies them (forensic.in), and the model's report (R3). Her start-up order, from her commit: uart_init(115200) first (it computes the divisor from a constant 8000000), then clock_init().






Questions.


    

  1. Observation: what is UART1's real input clock after clock_init(), and what divisor is in the register?
    2. 

  2. Hypotheses: list three possible causes of garbage at the terminal (for example cable, wrong baud on the terminal, wrong divisor) and say which evidence supports or excludes each.
    3. 

  3. Root cause: compute the real baud rate by hand and compare it with the model's number.
    4. 

  4. Fix: give two fixes, one that changes the order and one that changes how the divisor is computed. Compute the correct divisor.
    5. 

  5. Prevention: propose an assertion or test that would catch this before the board is even connected.
    6. 



Method hints. Separate "what the code assumed" from "what the hardware has". Follow UART1's clock path through Figure 1 for her configuration. A terminal shows garbage when the real baud is far from its setting; by how much is it off here?

Summary


    

  • A peripheral is a register block: control, status, data and configuration registers with documented fields, reset values and access types.
    • 

  • A clock tree runs from oscillators through a PLL, a system-clock switch and bus prescalers to gated peripheral clocks.
    • 

  • Peripherals start with their clock off; a gated peripheral silently ignores you or reads zeros.
    • 

  • Every peripheral speed (baud rate, timer tick) derives from its real input clock; divisors must be computed from it.
    • 

  • Changing clocks after configuring peripherals invalidates their divisors (forensic lab: +504 % baud error).
    • 

  • QEMU's STM32F100 model logs RCC and GPIO accesses as unimplemented and returns 0: emulator success does not prove a driver right.
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Control (an enable bit, a mode field), status (a "data received" or "overflow" flag), data (the byte to send), configuration (a baud divisor, a compare value). Reset value and access type belong to every one of them.
    2. 

  2. AHB 48 MHz; APB2 48 MHz → UART1 48 MHz; APB1 24 MHz → UART2 24 MHz; TIM2 = 2 × 24 = 48 MHz (divider not 1). Forgetting the timer rule gives 24 MHz, the common wrong answer.
    3. 

  3. To save power: unclocked logic does not toggle; firmware enables only what it uses. A second reason: peripherals start in a known, inactive state.
    4. 

  4. Good answers say: the UART only divides its input clock by the stored number; that number was right for the old clock; after the change the same number gives a different baud rate; the receiver samples at the wrong times and decodes wrong bits.
    5. 

  5. 24,000,000 / 115,200 = 208.33 → 208; 24,000,000 / 208 = 115,384.6 baud; error +0.16 %, below 2 %.
    6. 

  6. (a) The peripheral's clock is not enabled (writes ignored, reads 0). (b) The register or field is write-only or reads back differently by design (for example a "write 1 to trigger" bit). (c) Wrong base address: you wrote to reserved space. Distinguish by reading the RCC enable register and a register with a known non-zero reset value; check the access type in the manual.
    7. 

  7. "PROBLEM: the crystal is selected but none is fitted (xtal 0)", and the PLL input of 0 Hz outside its 1–25 MHz window. A real chip would wait forever for the crystal's ready flag unless the code has a timeout.
    8. 



Forensic lab answer key


Root cause. The divisor was computed for 8 MHz (69, from 8,000,000 / 115,200 = 69.4) before clock_init() switched to 12 MHz × 4 = 48 MHz; UART1 sits on APB2 /1, so its real clock is 48 MHz. Real baud = 48,000,000 / 69 = 695,652, which is +503.86 % (about six times too fast). The terminal at 115,200 baud cannot decode that.


Evidence. Model output lines "uart1 clock (apb2) 48000000 Hz" and "divisor 69 (computed for 8000000 Hz), real baud 695652, error +503.86 %"; the start-up order in the scenario.


Fix. Call clock_init() before uart_init(), and compute the divisor from the current peripheral clock: 48,000,000 / 115,200 = 416.67 → 417, real baud 115,108, error −0.08 %. Better: both.


How the evidence was made. The Lab Engineer entered the scenario's clock settings into Listing 1 with assume uart1 8000000, which models a divisor computed for the reset clock, and ran it (R3).


Wrong answers and why. "The cable": the banner was clean before the clock change with the same cable. "The terminal's baud": it was right before, and nothing changed it. "APB2 too fast": 48 MHz is within the teaching chip's 72 MHz limit; the model says ok. Prevention: an assertion in uart_init() that the system clock is in its final state (or a design where the divisor function reads the current clock), and a host-side unit test that runs the clock and UART set-up on a model like Listing 1 and checks the error is below 2 %.
