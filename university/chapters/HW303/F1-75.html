Story hook

At the take-away stand every appliance has its own wall switch, and they are all off at night. In the morning Amara flips the switches she needs: the hot plate, the order window's light. The juicer stays off; nobody ordered juice yet. Ravi once spent ten minutes pressing the juicer's buttons before he noticed its wall switch was down. The juicer had not complained. It had simply ignored him.

The stand also has a rhythm. Amara taps a slow beat at opening time, while she is alone. When the second cook arrives they switch to a fast beat, and everyone's work speeds up: the hot plate's timer, the rhythm at the order window, even how fast tickets are called. One day a helper kept counting the old slow beat while calling out ticket numbers. The customers heard numbers too fast to follow.

Peripherals in a microcontroller have both: a power switch for their clock, and a speed set by a shared rhythm. This chapter shows how a peripheral is built and controlled, how the clock tree feeds it, and what goes wrong when the beat changes and one helper does not notice.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe a peripheral as a register block: control, status and data registers, reset values and access types.
  • Trace a clock from its oscillator through PLL, system clock switch and bus prescalers to a peripheral, and compute every frequency on the way.
  • Explain clock gating and why a peripheral with its clock off "ignores" writes.
  • Compute a UART divisor from the real peripheral clock and the error of the resulting baud rate.
  • Recognise the limits of an emulator: show with QEMU's own log which parts of a chip it does not model.
  • Diagnose garbled serial output caused by a divisor computed for the wrong clock.
Jargon box
Register block
Simple words: the set of control knobs and display windows of one peripheral, each at its own address. Analogy: an appliance's control panel. Precise: a contiguous range of memory-mapped registers belonging to one peripheral instance, at a base address plus fixed offsets, each register with documented fields, reset value and access type. D1 D3
Access type
Simple words: what you are allowed to do with a register bit: read it, write it, or clear it by writing a 1. Analogy: a dial you can turn, a lamp you can only look at, a "reset" button. Precise: the documented behaviour of a field on read and write, such as read-write, read-only, write-only, or write-1-to-clear; reference manuals use short codes for these (F1-78). D1
Clock tree
Simple words: the network that takes one or two basic ticking signals and makes all the speeds the chip's parts need. Analogy: the stand's rhythm, passed on and slowed down for each helper. Precise: the oscillators, phase-locked loops, multiplexers, dividers (prescalers) and gates that generate and distribute the clock signals of a chip, from the clock sources to each bus and peripheral. D1 D2
Oscillator (internal RC, crystal)
Simple words: the part that produces the basic tick. Analogy: the first beat Amara taps. Precise: a clock source; an on-chip RC oscillator needs no external parts but is less accurate, while a crystal oscillator uses an external quartz crystal for an accurate frequency. D2 D4
PLL (phase-locked loop)
Simple words: a circuit that makes a faster clock from a slower one. Analogy: the second cook joins and the beat doubles. Precise: a feedback circuit that locks an internal oscillator to a multiple of its reference input; on microcontrollers it multiplies an oscillator's frequency to produce the high system clock. D2 D4
Prescaler
Simple words: a divider that slows a clock down by a fixed number. Analogy: "move at every second beat". Precise: a frequency divider in the clock path, set by a register field, producing fout = fin / N. D1
Clock gating (clock enable)
Simple words: switching a peripheral's clock on or off with one bit. Analogy: the appliance's wall switch. Precise: a gate in a peripheral's clock path controlled by an enable bit in the clock controller; with the clock off, the peripheral's logic does not change state, which saves power, and on many chips its registers cannot be written. D1 D2
Baud rate and divisor
Simple words: how many symbols per second a serial line sends, and the number that sets it. Analogy: how fast ticket numbers are called. Precise: the symbol rate of a UART; the peripheral derives it from its input clock through a programmable divisor, so the divisor must be computed from the peripheral clock actually present. D3 D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every helper machine on the chip has a little control panel. Some knobs you turn (control), some lamps you only look at (status), and one tray where you put things in or take them out (data). You find each panel by its house number.

Every machine also needs a heartbeat to work. The chip makes one fast heartbeat and hands slower copies to the different groups of machines. And every machine has its own switch for that heartbeat. If the switch is off, the machine does nothing, even if you turn its knobs.

When you change the heartbeat speed, every machine's speed changes too. A machine that sends letters down a wire must be told the new speed, or the letters arrive as nonsense.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Peripherals are register blocks

Analogy first: an appliance's control panel. Precisely: each peripheral instance occupies a block of addresses (its base address, from the memory map) and offers registers at fixed offsets. A reference manual documents every register with its offset, reset value and fields, and every field with its bit positions and access type. D1 D3 The kinds repeat across chips: control registers enable the block and choose modes; status registers report events (a byte received, a timer overflowed) and are set by hardware; data registers move the payload; configuration registers hold divisors, thresholds, compare values. D1 Several instances of the same peripheral (USART1, USART2, …) share one register layout at different base addresses, which is why the drivers in F1-73 and F1-74 described the layout once as a struct and placed it at a base address. D5

Status bits deserve care: some are cleared by reading a data register, some by writing 1 to them, some by writing 0; and a few registers have side effects on read. The access type tells you which. D1 F1-78 trains reading these codes; its forensic lab is a "write 0 to a write-1-to-clear flag" bug.

From oscillator to peripheral

Analogy: one rhythm, passed on and slowed down. Precisely: a microcontroller has one or more clock sources: an internal RC oscillator that runs as soon as the chip is powered, and usually the option of an external crystal oscillator for accuracy. D2 D4 A PLL multiplies a source up to a higher frequency. A multiplexer chooses which signal becomes the system clock. Prescalers derive the bus clocks from it, and gates switch each peripheral's clock on or off. D1 After reset, chips typically run from the internal oscillator with the PLL off; switching to a faster clock is a deliberate sequence in start-up code: start the source, wait until it is stable, configure and start the PLL, wait for lock, adjust flash wait states if needed, then switch the system clock. D1 D2

On a real chip such a configuration is outside the datasheet's operating conditions, and nothing guarantees correct operation. D4



Every peripheral speed depends on its input clock


A UART does not know what a baud rate is; it divides its input clock by the divisor you wrote. D3 In the teaching chip's simple model, divisor = round(fperipheral / baud) and the real baud = fperipheral / divisor. From 8 MHz at 115,200 baud: 8,000,000 / 115,200 = 69.44 → 69; 8,000,000 / 69 = 115,942 baud, +0.64 %. From 36 MHz: 312.5 → 313 (rounded half up), 115,016 baud, −0.16 %. R1 A receiver samples each bit at times computed from its own clock, so the two ends tolerate only a small mismatch, a few percent at most for a frame of about ten bits; Listing 1 uses 2 % as its warning threshold. D3 R1


Note
Real UARTs usually oversample (for example 16 samples per bit) and many have fractional divisors, so the formula in a real reference manual is more detailed than the teaching model's. The idea is the same: the divisor is a function of the real peripheral clock. D1 D3



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


What "clock off" really means


Clock gating exists to save power: a CMOS circuit that does not toggle consumes much less dynamic power, so peripherals start with their clocks off and firmware enables only what it uses. D2 D1 The consequence for drivers is the most common microcontroller bring-up bug, also listed as a trap in the bridge chapter BR-07: "a device that reads zeros because its clock is off". C1 What exactly a gated peripheral does on a register access is chip-specific: writes may be ignored, reads may return zero, or the access may fault. The teaching model (Listing 1) chooses "reads 0, writes ignored" and says so in its output. R1


Two more details belong in every driver's first lines, both from the reference manual: the reset-and-clock controller often also has a reset bit per peripheral (to put it back into its reset state), and the pins of a peripheral must be routed to it by the GPIO or pin-multiplexing block, which has its own clock. D1 A UART that is clocked and configured but whose TX pin is still a plain input sends nothing to the outside.

Meanwhile the USART worked without any clock enable at all. R2 On this model, a driver with a missing clock enable passes, and a driver with a correct GPIO setup cannot be tested. Order matters: the clock-change problem


Changing the system clock changes every bus clock derived from it. Any peripheral configured before the change keeps its divisors, which were computed for the old frequency. D1 Two disciplines prevent this: configure clocks first and peripherals after (and never change clocks at run time without reconfiguring), or compute divisors from a single function that reports the current peripheral clock, the way vendor libraries keep a global variable with the system frequency that must be updated on every change. D5 The forensic lab is the bug this prevents.



How the hardware actually does it


An RC oscillator is a resistor-capacitor timing network with an amplifier; its frequency drifts with temperature and supply voltage, which is why the vendor trims it at the factory and documents its accuracy. A crystal oscillator uses the mechanical resonance of a quartz crystal and is far more stable. D2 D7 A PLL contains a voltage-controlled oscillator whose output is divided and compared in phase with the reference; the comparison steers the oscillator until the divided output matches the reference, so the output is the reference times the divider ratio. The "lock" flag in the clock controller tells software when this has settled. D7 A prescaler is a counter that toggles its output every N/2 input cycles, a direct use of the counters of F1-17. A clock gate is a logic gate combined with a latch so that the clock is never cut in the middle of a pulse (a glitch-free gate). D2


Not verified — check before relying on this
No number in this chapter describes a real chip's oscillators, PLL ranges or bus limits: all of them are U-MCU-1 teaching values. For your lab microcontroller, take them from its datasheet ("electrical characteristics", "clock sources") and its reference manual ("reset and clock control"), and record them in the board-support note of F1-79.



Worked example


Task. The teaching chip has an 8 MHz crystal. You need SYSCLK = 64 MHz, UART2 (on APB1) at 9,600 baud with less than 1 % error, and TIM2 counting at exactly 1 MHz. Choose the PLL source and multiplier, the APB1 divider, the UART2 divisor and the TIM2 prescaler.


    

  1. PLL. From the crystal: 8 MHz × M = 64 MHz → M = 8. (From IRC/2 = 4 MHz you would need M = 16, also allowed by the model; the crystal is more accurate.) PLL input 8 MHz is inside the 1–25 MHz window.
    2. 

  2. APB1. APB1 ≤ 36 MHz, so divide 64 MHz by 2: APB1 = 32 MHz. (A divider of 1 gives 64 MHz, too fast.)
    3. 

  3. UART2 divisor. 32,000,000 / 9,600 = 3,333.33 → 3,333. Real baud = 32,000,000 / 3,333 = 9,600.96; error = +0.01 %.
    4. 

  4. TIM2 clock. APB1 divider is 2, not 1, so by the teaching chip's rule TIM2 runs at 2 × 32 = 64 MHz.
    5. 

  5. TIM2 prescaler. To count at 1 MHz: 64 MHz / (PSC + 1) = 1 MHz → PSC = 63 (the "+1" convention of the teaching timer, F1-76). Forgetting step 4 and using 32 MHz would give PSC = 31 and a timer running twice too fast.
    6. 



Check the clock part by adding a configuration to clocktree.in (lab step 2); the model prints the same frequencies and divisor. The rule in step 4 is the teaching chip's; a real chip documents its own rule in its clock-tree chapter. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A juicer with its switch off is visibly dark; a gated peripheral looks exactly like a working one. Its address still decodes, the bus access completes, and depending on the chip you read zeros or stale values. There is no lamp; you must check the enable bit. D1 R2
    • 

  • Helpers can adapt to a new rhythm; peripherals cannot. When the beat changes, a UART keeps dividing by its old divisor without any notion of "too fast". Every derived speed must be recomputed by software. R1
    • 

  • The analogy suggests one rhythm for everyone. Real chips have several independent clock domains (a low-speed oscillator for a real-time clock, separate clocks for USB or ADCs), and signals crossing between domains need synchronisation (F1-18). D2
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a peripheral "does not react"; its registers read 0. Cause: its clock enable bit is off. How to find it: read back what you wrote (Listing 2's method); read the RCC enable register in the debugger. Fix: enable the clock first, then configure.
    • 

  • Symptom: serial output is garbage at the right baud setting. Cause: divisor computed for a different clock (clock changed later, wrong APB divider, crystal not fitted so the chip fell back to the internal oscillator). How to find it: compute the real baud from the real peripheral clock (Listing 1); measure the bit width with a logic analyser on hardware. Fix: configure clocks before peripherals; compute divisors from the current clock.
    • 

  • Symptom: the chip hangs during clock set-up. Cause: waiting forever for an oscillator or PLL "ready" flag (crystal missing or not oscillating). How to find it: halt in the debugger and look at where PC is. Fix: add a timeout and a fallback to the internal oscillator.
    • 

  • Symptom: works in the emulator, not on the board. Cause: the emulator does not model clocks or pin routing (Listing 2's log). Fix: treat emulator success as "the logic is right", never as "the driver is right".
    •

Summary


    

  • A peripheral is a register block: control, status, data and configuration registers with documented fields, reset values and access types.
    • 

  • A clock tree runs from oscillators through a PLL, a system-clock switch and bus prescalers to gated peripheral clocks.
    • 

  • Peripherals start with their clock off; a gated peripheral silently ignores you or reads zeros.
    • 

  • Every peripheral speed (baud rate, timer tick) derives from its real input clock; divisors must be computed from it.
    • 

  • Changing clocks after configuring peripherals invalidates their divisors (forensic lab: +504 % baud error).
    • 

  • QEMU's STM32F100 model logs RCC and GPIO accesses as unimplemented and returns 0: emulator success does not prove a driver right.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Control (an enable bit, a mode field), status (a "data received" or "overflow" flag), data (the byte to send), configuration (a baud divisor, a compare value). Reset value and access type belong to every one of them.
    2. 

  2. AHB 48 MHz; APB2 48 MHz → UART1 48 MHz; APB1 24 MHz → UART2 24 MHz; TIM2 = 2 × 24 = 48 MHz (divider not 1). Forgetting the timer rule gives 24 MHz, the common wrong answer.
    3.

Forensic lab answer key


Root cause. The divisor was computed for 8 MHz (69, from 8,000,000 / 115,200 = 69.4) before clock_init() switched to 12 MHz × 4 = 48 MHz; UART1 sits on APB2 /1, so its real clock is 48 MHz. Real baud = 48,000,000 / 69 = 695,652, which is +503.86 % (about six times too fast). The terminal at 115,200 baud cannot decode that.


Evidence. Model output lines "uart1 clock (apb2) 48000000 Hz" and "divisor 69 (computed for 8000000 Hz), real baud 695652, error +503.86 %"; the start-up order in the scenario.


Fix. Call clock_init() before uart_init(), and compute the divisor from the current peripheral clock: 48,000,000 / 115,200 = 416.67 → 417, real baud 115,108, error −0.08 %. Better: both.


How the evidence was made. The Lab Engineer entered the scenario's clock settings into Listing 1 with assume uart1 8000000, which models a divisor computed for the reset clock, and ran it (R3).


Wrong answers and why. "The cable": the banner was clean before the clock change with the same cable. "The terminal's baud": it was right before, and nothing changed it. "APB2 too fast": 48 MHz is within the teaching chip's 72 MHz limit; the model says ok. Prevention: an assertion in uart_init() that the system clock is in its final state (or a design where the divisor function reads the current clock), and a host-side unit test that runs the clock and UART set-up on a model like Listing 1 and checks the error is below 2 %.
