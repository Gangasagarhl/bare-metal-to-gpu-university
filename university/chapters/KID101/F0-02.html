Story hook

Analogy

Lena and her grandfather live in two flats that face each other across a small yard. At night, they send signals with their bedroom lights. One light on means "Good night". Light off means "I am still awake".

One evening Lena wants to say more. "How can I tell you what I had for dinner with one light?" she asks. Grandfather smiles. "Use more lights," he says. "Two lights give four patterns. Three lights give eight."

So they tape a code to their windows. Off-off means soup. Off-on means rice. On-off means pasta. On-on means pizza. Now two simple lights can say four different things.

Computers talk in exactly this kind of light-switch alphabet. Every number, letter and picture inside a computer is made of on-and-off switches. This chapter shows you how.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain a bit using a light switch: on or off, one or zero, nothing in between.
  • Count in binary from 0 to 15 with four switches, and back again.
  • Use place values (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128) to turn a row of switches into a number.
  • Turn a number up to 255 into a row of eight switches.
  • Find a mistake caused by reading switches from the wrong end.
Jargon box
Bit
Simple words: the smallest piece of information: on or off, 1 or 0. Analogy: one light switch. Precise: a binary digit, which has exactly one of two values, written 0 and 1. D1 ch. 11
Binary
Simple words: a way of writing numbers using only the two digits 0 and 1. Analogy: a row of switches where each switch is worth double the one to its right. Precise: the base-2 number system: each position has a place value that is a power of two (1, 2, 4, 8 and so on). D4 §2.1
Place value
Simple words: how much a digit is worth because of where it sits. Analogy: the price tag stuck under each switch. Precise: the number a position stands for; in our usual decimal numbers the places are 1, 10, 100; in binary they are 1, 2, 4, 8 and so on. D4 §2.1
Byte
Simple words: a group of eight bits. Analogy: a row of eight light switches. Precise: a unit of eight bits; it can hold 256 different patterns, so one byte can stand for a whole number from 0 to 255. D3 §2.1, §2.1.1 D1 ch. 11

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows a row of eight switches, a byte. Under each switch is its place value. Each switch is worth double the one to its right. You add up the values of the switches that are on.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A light switch has two positions: on and off. There is no "a little bit on". That makes switches very easy to read. You never have to guess.

Computers store information in tiny parts that act like switches. Each one is either on or off. We write on as 1 and off as 0. One switch is called a bit. D1 ch. 11

One bit can only say two things, like yes or no. That is not much. So computers use many bits side by side, like Lena's two lights. Two bits give four patterns. Three bits give eight. Every new switch doubles the number of patterns.

Counting with switches

Now the clever part. Give each switch a price tag. The switch on the right is worth 1. The next one is worth 2. Then 4, then 8. Each tag is double the one to its right. This tag is the switch's place value.

To read a row, add up the tags of the switches that are on. Off switches count as nothing. With switches worth 8, 4, 2 and 1, the pattern on-off-on-on means 8 + 2 + 1. That is 11. Writing numbers this way is called binary. D4 §2.1

You already use place values every day. In the number 352, the 3 is worth three hundred, because of where it sits. Binary works the same way. It only uses doubling instead of "times ten".

A byte: eight switches

A row of eight bits has its own name: a byte. Its place values are 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2 and 1, as Figure 1 shows. When all eight are off, the byte means 0. When all eight are on, it means 128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1. Add them up, and you get 255. D3 §2.1.1 R1

So one byte can stand for any whole number from 0 to 255. That is 256 different patterns, because zero counts too. Bigger numbers need more bytes. You will see in F0-03 that a byte can also stand for a letter.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. Why does every new switch double the patterns? Each old pattern can now come in two versions: with the new switch off, and with it on. So one switch gives 2 patterns, two give 2 × 2 = 4, three give 2 × 2 × 2 = 8, and eight give 2 multiplied by itself eight times, which is 256. Mathematicians write this as 2 to the power 8. D1 ch. 11 You will practise powers of two in MA101 and binary conversion in MA102.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

At this level, Layer 3 waits for later courses. MA102 covers bytes and words, hexadecimal and negative numbers in binary. HW102 shows how circuits store and add bits. Keep one idea: a pattern of bits has no meaning by itself; a program decides what it means. D1 ch. 11 D5 §1.1

How the hardware actually does it

A computer does not have tiny light switches with plastic handles. It has electronic parts that can be in one of two states, such as a higher or lower voltage. The computer treats one state as 1 and the other as 0. Very many of these two-state parts, millions on a single chip, together store all of a computer's numbers, letters and pictures. D3 ch. 2 intro

Not verified — check before relying on this

"Millions on a single chip" is the sense of scale given by the textbook in D3; it is not a measured number for any real computer. The exact count of bits in a real computer depends on its memory and storage size. Check the datasheet of a named memory part (or the computer's own system information) before quoting a number.

Add the "yes" values: 32 + 8 = 40, 40 + 4 = 44, 44 + 1 = 45. So 00101101 is 45. The real run of Listing 2 printed the same total. R1

From a number to switches

Now go the other way with 13 and four switches (8, 4, 2, 1). Start at the biggest switch.

  1. Is 13 at least 8? Yes. Switch 8 on. 13 − 8 = 5 left.
  2. Is 5 at least 4? Yes. Switch 4 on. 5 − 4 = 1 left.
  3. Is 1 at least 2? No. Switch 2 off.
  4. Is 1 at least 1? Yes. Switch 1 on. 1 − 1 = 0 left. Done.

So 13 is on-on-off-on, written 1101. Check: 8 + 4 + 1 = 13. Listing 1 uses exactly this method, and its row for 13 says "ON ON off ON". R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A real switch can be broken or half-pressed. A bit, when the computer reads it, is always exactly 0 or 1. There is no "half on". D3 ch. 2 intro
    • 

  • Switches do not know what they mean. In Lena's code, on-on meant pizza. In binary, on-on means 3. The same switches can mean a number, a letter or a colour. The program decides the meaning, not the switches. D1 ch. 11 D5 §1.1
    • 

  • You can see a light switch. You cannot see a bit. It is far too small, and it is an electrical state, not a moving part.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: Kofi got 96, 48 and 40. Ama meant 6, 12 and 20.


Root cause: Ama wrote each byte backwards, with the 1 switch on the left. 6 is 00000110; written backwards it is 01100000, which the program reads as 64 + 32 = 96. 12 is 00001100; backwards, 00110000 = 48. 20 is 00010100; backwards, 00101000 = 40.


Proof that the program is fine: the same program read 00000101 as 5 and 00101101 as 45 in the Listing 2 run, and those are correct.


Fix: the rows should be 00000110, 00001100 and 00010100.


How the fault was made: the Lab Engineer reversed the three correct rows by hand and fed them to an unchanged copy of Listing 2.


Wrong answers and why: "The program adds wrongly": ruled out above. "One switch was missing": each row has eight switches and the program did not print "Skipped". Prevention: agree on "smallest place value on the right" and send one test number first, such as 1.
