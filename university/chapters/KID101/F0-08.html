Story hook

Analogy

Hana's family has a recipe for banana bread. This time it comes out of the oven flat and salty. Everyone is puzzled. Her uncle says, "The oven is broken!" Her cousin says, "The bananas were bad!"

Hana does not guess. She gets out the recipe card and reads it, line by line. Then she checks the salt jar and the sugar jar. The labels have been swapped! The recipe was right. The ingredients were not what the labels said.

Hana did not shout the first idea that came to mind. She collected clues. She tested each idea. She found the real cause, and she fixed it. That is exactly what programmers do when a program goes wrong. They become detectives.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a bug is: the program does what was written, not what was meant.
  • Use the detective method: symptom, guesses, test, cause, fix, check, prevent.
  • Spot an off-by-one bug in a counting loop.
  • Trace a robot's route step by step and find the first step where it goes wrong.
  • Write a short bug report that someone else can act on.
Jargon box
Bug
Simple words: a mistake that makes a program do the wrong thing. Analogy: a mistake in the recipe. Precise: a fault in a program (or in the data, the hardware or another program it depends on) that makes it behave differently from what was intended. D1
Debugging
Simple words: finding and fixing bugs. Analogy: Hana checking the recipe and the jars. Precise: the process of locating the cause of a wrong behaviour, correcting it, and checking that the correction works. D1
Symptom
Simple words: what you notice is wrong. Analogy: "the bread is flat and salty". Precise: an observable sign of a fault, such as wrong output, a crash or a program that never ends; it is not the cause itself. D1
Root cause
Simple words: the real reason behind the symptom. Analogy: the swapped jar labels. Precise: the underlying fault that, once fixed, makes the symptom go away and stay away. D1
Test
Simple words: a small try that checks if something works. Analogy: tasting a pinch from each jar. Precise: running a program (or part of it) with chosen inputs and comparing what it does with what it should do. D1
Off-by-one error
Simple words: a mistake where something happens one time too many or one time too few. Analogy: stirring 19 times when the recipe says 20. Precise: a bug in which a count, a limit or a position is wrong by exactly one, often from choosing "less than" instead of "less than or equal". D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A bug is a mistake that makes a program do the wrong thing. Remember F0-01: the computer does exactly what is written. So when a program goes wrong, the computer is usually doing what we wrote. We wrote something different from what we meant. D1

Bugs are normal. Everyone who writes programs makes them, including the people who wrote this course. What matters is how calmly and cleverly you find them. Finding and fixing bugs is called debugging.

Clues first, guesses second

The thing you notice is the symptom. "The bread is flat." "The program prints 9 numbers, not 10." A symptom is a clue, not the answer. The real reason is the root cause. Hana's symptom was salty bread; the root cause was swapped labels. D1

A good detective makes at least two guesses before deciding. Then they test one guess at a time. A test is a small try with a known right answer. If the result matches the guess, good. If not, cross that guess off and try the next.

The bug with a famous name

One bug is so common that it has its own name: the off-by-one error. Something happens one time too many or one time too few. You want to print 1 to 10, and you get 1 to 9. You fence a garden with 10 metres of fence and posts every metre, and you buy 10 posts instead of 11. D2

Off-by-one errors hide at the edges: the first item, the last item, the limit. That is why good testers always test the edges.

Fix, check, prevent

When you find the cause, fix it. Then run the same test again. Did the symptom go away? If not, back to guessing. If yes, do one last thing: keep that test, so the bug can never sneak back without you noticing.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

At this level, the depth waits for F0-36 (reading compiler errors) and F0-37 (the debugger, which pauses a program so you can look inside). Later faculties use the same detective method on crash dumps, slow boots and GPU programs. Keep one rule: never fix what you have not first explained with evidence.

How the hardware actually does it

Most bugs live in the program: the computer carried out wrong steps correctly. But not all. Some faults come from the hardware, from wrong data, or from another program. A detective keeps all of these on the list of guesses, and lets the evidence decide. D1

Worked example

The poster that stopped at 9

Ali wants a poster with the numbers 1 to 10. His program (Listing 1) prints only 1 to 9. Let us use the detective method.

  1. Symptom: the output is "1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9". The 10 is missing. Nothing else is wrong. R1
  2. Guesses: (a) the loop starts at the wrong number; (b) the loop stops too early; (c) the printing drops the last number.
  3. Test guess (a): the output starts with 1. So the start is right. Cross off (a).
  4. Test guess (b): read the loop's condition: n < 10. Try it by hand at the edge. When n is 9: is 9 less than 10? Yes, print 9. When n is 10: is 10 less than 10? No, stop. So 10 is never printed. Guess (b) explains the symptom exactly.
  5. Root cause: the condition says "less than 10" but Ali meant "up to and including 10". An off-by-one error.
  6. Fix: change it to n <= 10 ("less than or equal to 10").
  7. Check: the fixed program prints 1 to 10. R1
  8. Prevent: a test that counts the numbers printed: there must be exactly 10.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Not every bug is in the recipe. Hana's bug was in the jars, not the recipe card. Programs too can fail because of wrong input data, another program, or the machine itself. D1
    • 

  • A cook tastes as she goes. A computer does not notice that something tastes wrong. It finishes the wrong steps and reports success, as Listing 1 did with exit code 0. R1
    • 

  • Recipe mistakes are usually visible on the card. Some program bugs show up only for certain inputs, like a robot route that works on one map but not another. You may need many tests to see them.

Lab


Be the robot, debug the route


Goal: write a route for a paper robot, test it, and debug it with the detective method.


You need: squared paper, a pencil, a small object as the robot (a coin or an eraser with an arrow drawn on it), a friend.


Safety
None beyond normal paper and pencil work. If you play it on the floor with a person as the robot, walk slowly and keep the area clear.


    

  1. Draw a grid of 6 by 5 squares. Number the columns 0 to 5 from left to right and the rows 0 to 4 from top to bottom.
    2. 

  2. The robot starts at column 0, row 0, facing east (right). It knows three commands: FORWARD (one square), LEFT and RIGHT (turn a quarter, staying on the same square).
    3. 

  3. Mark a goal square, for example column 3, row 2. Write a route of commands to reach it.
    4. 

  4. Your friend moves the robot, one command at a time, writing column, row and facing after each one.
    5. 

  5. If the robot ends in the wrong place, use the detective method: find the first step where the written position differs from your plan.
    6. 

  6. Fix that step and test again.
    7. 



What you should see: most route bugs are a turn the wrong way or a FORWARD too many. The step-by-step table shows exactly where.


Check you succeeded: a friend follows your final route and lands on the goal the first time.


If something goes wrong: confused about left and right → turn the paper so the robot faces up, then decide. Robot off the grid → a FORWARD too many → count squares. Different friends get different results → a command was unclear → write each command on its own line.


Extension: add a wall square. Change the route to go around it.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: the robot ended at column 4, row 1. It should be at column 3, row 2.


Planned versus actual: steps 1 to 5 match the plan (column 3, row 0, then turn to face south, then column 3, row 1). At step 6, the plan says FORWARD to column 3, row 2; the evidence shows "LEFT → column 3, row 1, facing east". This is the first difference.


Root cause: step 6 is LEFT instead of FORWARD. After it, the robot faces east, so step 7's FORWARD takes it to column 4, row 1.


Fix: FORWARD, FORWARD, FORWARD, RIGHT, FORWARD, FORWARD. Check: run it (on paper or in the simulator) and confirm "end: column 3, row 2".


How the fault was made: the Lab Engineer replaced the second southward FORWARD with LEFT and added a FORWARD after it, so the robot ends near the goal but not on it.


Wrong answers and why: "Step 7 is the bug": FORWARD did exactly one square in the direction the robot faced; it only went the wrong way because of step 6. "The RIGHT turn was wrong": after step 4 the robot faces south, as planned. Prevention: write the expected position next to every command before running.
