Story hook

Analogy

Maya wants to make a cheese sandwich. Her little brother Teo has never made one. So Maya writes the steps on a card: "Put butter and cheese on the bread."

Teo reads the card very carefully. He puts the butter dish on top of the bread bag. Then he puts the cheese on top of the butter dish. He did exactly what the card said! But it is not a sandwich.

Maya laughs and tries again. This time she writes small steps, one at a time. Open the bag. Take out one slice. Put it on the plate. Teo follows every step, and out comes a real sandwich.

A computer is like Teo on his very first day, every day. It does exactly what the steps say. It never guesses what you meant. This chapter is about writing steps so clear that even a computer gets them right.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain that a computer follows exact steps, one after another, and never guesses.
  • Write an algorithm in words that another person can follow without asking questions.
  • Test your algorithm by having someone follow it exactly, and fix the steps that fail.
  • Find a missing or wrong step by comparing the steps with the result.
  • Say where the "computer is a cook with a recipe" idea stops being true.
Jargon box
Computer
Simple words: a machine that follows a list of exact steps to work with information. Analogy: a cook who follows a recipe word for word. Precise: a machine that carries out a stored list of instructions, one at a time, on data such as numbers and letters. D1 §1.4 D2 §5.1.1
Instruction
Simple words: one single step that tells the machine to do one thing. Analogy: one line of a recipe, like "crack the egg". Precise: one command that a computer can carry out; a program is made of many of them. D2 §4.1
Algorithm
Simple words: a list of clear steps that solves a problem, every time. Analogy: a recipe that always makes the same dish if you follow it exactly. Precise: a method for solving a problem that is suited to be carried out by a computer: a finite list of exact steps that, followed in order, solves the problem. It can be written in words, drawn, or written as a program. D4 ch. 1
Program
Simple words: an algorithm written in a language a computer can follow. Analogy: the recipe, translated into the cook's own language. Precise: a list of instructions written in a programming language, such as C++, that is turned into instructions the computer can carry out. D1 §1.2 D3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Think about the last time you followed a recipe. Each line told you one thing to do. You did the lines in order. If you skipped a line, the cake came out flat.

A computer works the same way. It gets a list of steps. Each step is called an instruction. The computer does the first instruction, then the next one, then the next. It never skips one, and it never adds one of its own. D1 §1.4.1 D2 §4.1.3

A list of clear steps that solves a problem is called an algorithm. A recipe for a sandwich is an algorithm. So are the steps for tying a shoe or brushing your teeth. You already know hundreds of algorithms. You never wrote them down.

Here is the hard part. People are clever at filling gaps. If a recipe says "add the egg", you know to crack it first. A computer does not know that. It will try to add the whole egg, shell and all. So the steps for a computer must be complete, exact and in the right order.

What makes a good step?

A good step does one thing. It uses words with one meaning. It says what to use and where to put it. "Put one slice of bread on the plate" is a good step. "Make it nice" is not, because nobody knows what "nice" means.

When you write an algorithm in a computer's language, it becomes a program. You will write real programs in KID102. In this course, you write algorithms in words first. That is how many programmers start, too: on paper.

How do you test an algorithm?

You give it to a friend. Your friend plays the computer. They must do exactly what the words say, and nothing else. They may not ask you questions. Where they get stuck, your algorithm has a gap. You fix that step and try again. That is the "be the robot" lab of this chapter.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. A computer keeps its program in its memory, as a list of instructions. A part called the processor fetches one instruction, carries it out, and then moves on to the next one. This repeats very many times every second. D2 §5.1.4 D1 §1.4.1 Some instructions say "jump to another step", which is how a program can repeat steps or choose between them. D2 §4.1.3 You will meet repeating and choosing in F0-07, and the parts inside the computer in F0-05.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

At this level, the deeper story is kept for later courses. In HW102 you will see how real instructions are coded as patterns of bits and carried out by circuits. The one idea to keep from today is that the machine does what is written, not what is meant. D2 §4.1

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a computer, the instructions are stored as patterns of tiny on-and-off signals. You will learn this "light-switch alphabet" in F0-02. A part called the processor reads one instruction at a time and does what it says, very fast. It does not understand the goal of the program at all. It only follows the steps. D1 §1.4.1 D2 §5.1.4

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A cook has common sense; a computer has none. A cook who reads "add the egg" cracks it first. A computer never fills a gap. It does only what the instructions say. D1 §1.4.1
    • 

  • A cook can stop and ask. A cook can say "I don't understand step 3". A computer cannot ask what you meant. A wrong program still runs, and gives a wrong result.
    • 

  • A recipe is read once. A real computer can jump back and repeat steps, or skip steps when a test says so. Recipes rarely work like that. You will see this in F0-07. D2 §4.1.3
    •

What changes: a computer never fills gaps, never asks, and never guesses, so every step must be written down exactly. Lab


Be the robot


Goal: write an algorithm in words that a friend can follow exactly, without asking you anything.


You need: paper, a pencil, a friend or family member, two cups and some dry beans or buttons. No computer is needed.


Safety
No sharp knives, hot water or stove in this lab. Use dry beans or buttons, not food you will eat. If young children are nearby, keep small items away from them.


    

  1. Put one cup with ten beans on the left. Put one empty cup on the right.
    2. 

  2. Write steps that move exactly five beans into the right cup. Number every step.
    3. 

  3. Give the paper to your friend. Your friend is now the robot. The robot does only what the words say. The robot may not ask questions. You may not talk.
    4. 

  4. Watch. Make a mark next to any step where the robot got stuck or did something odd.
    5. 

  5. Fix the marked steps. Try again. Repeat until the robot gets it right the first time.
    6. 

  6. Swap roles. Now you are the robot for your friend's steps.
    7. 



What you should see: the first try almost always fails somewhere. That is normal. Programmers fix their steps many times too.


Check you succeeded: a new person, who has not seen the lab, follows your steps once and ends with exactly five beans in the right cup.


If something goes wrong:


    

  • The robot picks up all the beans at once → your step did not say "one bean" → write "pick up one bean".
    • 

  • The robot keeps going after five → no step says when to stop → add "stop when you have moved five beans".
    • 

  • The robot laughs and guesses → remind them to act like a machine: only the words count.
    • 



Extension: change your steps so they work for any number of beans your friend chooses.

Forensic lab answer key


Root cause: the step "OPEN THE BUTTER TUB" is missing in Sami's steps. Without it, "DIP THE KNIFE IN THE BUTTER" cannot work, so the knife stays clean, and "SPREAD THE KNIFE ON THE FIRST SLICE" spreads nothing.


Evidence: Nadia's list has "OPEN THE BUTTER TUB" as step 4; Sami's list goes straight from the bread to "DIP THE KNIFE IN THE BUTTER". Sami's plate says "first slice has butter: no" and "butter tub left open: no". Nadia's says "yes" for both. The tub was never opened.


Fix: add "OPEN THE BUTTER TUB" before "DIP THE KNIFE IN THE BUTTER". Run the steps again and check that the plate says "first slice has butter: yes".


How the fault was made: the Lab Engineer deleted one line from Nadia's steps to make Sami's steps. The cook's program is the same for both runs.


Wrong answers and why: "The knife is broken" is wrong: the knife step worked in Nadia's run with the same program. "The spread step is wrong" is wrong: the spread step is the same in both lists; it had nothing on the knife. Prevention: before each step, ask "what must be true for this to work?", and test with a literal helper.


Extra find: Nadia's sandwich is right, but she left the butter tub open. A careful detective can suggest one more step: "CLOSE THE BUTTER TUB".
