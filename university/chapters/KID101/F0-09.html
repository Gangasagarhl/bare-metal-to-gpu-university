Story hook

Analogy

Ines is on holiday at her aunt's house. She wants to tell her friend Tomás a long story about a lost kitten. It does not fit on one postcard. So she writes it on five postcards and posts them on the same day.

The postcards travel through different post offices. Card 3 reaches Tomás first. Then card 1. Card 2 never arrives at all. Tomás reads them in the order they come: the story makes no sense!

The next time, Ines is smarter. She writes "card 1 of 5", "card 2 of 5" and so on in the corner. Now Tomás can put them in order, and he can see that card 2 is missing. He sends Ines a short note: "Please send card 2 again."

Computers talk to each other in almost exactly this way. This chapter shows you how.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain that computers send messages as small numbered pieces called packets, each with an address.
  • Describe what can go wrong on the way: pieces lost, out of order, or arriving twice.
  • Explain how numbering pieces and asking again lets the receiver rebuild the message.
  • Play the postcard game and rebuild a message that arrived scrambled.
  • Find why a received message came out scrambled, from the evidence.
Jargon box
Network
Simple words: computers joined together so they can send each other messages. Analogy: the postal service, with post offices and roads between them. Precise: a set of computers and connecting devices, linked by cables or radio, that pass data from one computer to another. D4 §2.4 D7 §1.8
Packet
Simple words: one small piece of a message that travels on its own. Analogy: one postcard of Ines's story. Precise: a unit of data sent across a network, made of a header (with information such as the addresses) and a piece of the message. D4 §1.1, §3.1
Address
Simple words: the number or name that says which computer a packet is for, and which sent it. Analogy: the address written on a postcard, and the sender's name. Precise: an identifier of a computer (or a part of one) on a network, carried in each packet so it can be delivered, and so the reply can find its way back. D4 §3.1
Protocol
Simple words: agreed rules for how computers talk. Analogy: Ines and Tomás agreeing to write "card n of 5" and to ask for missing cards. Precise: a set of rules that sender and receiver both follow: what a message looks like, in what order things are sent, and what to do when something goes wrong. D5 §3.1, §3.8
Router
Simple words: a box that passes packets along towards where they are going. Analogy: a post office that sorts postcards and sends each one onwards. Precise: a network device that receives packets and forwards each one towards its destination address. D4 §2.4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Say you send a photo to a friend's phone. Your computer does not send it in one big lump. It cuts the message into many small pieces called packets. Each packet travels on its own, like one postcard of Ines's story. D4 §1.1 D5 §3.1

Addresses

Every packet carries an address: which computer it is going to, and which computer sent it. That way it can be delivered, and the answer can find its way back. D4 §3.1

The journey

Computers joined together make a network. Packets travel along cables, or through the air by radio, as with Wi-Fi. On the way they pass through boxes called routers. A router is like a post office. It looks at the address and sends the packet onwards, closer to where it is going. D4 §2.4

What can go wrong

The journey is not perfect. A packet can get lost. Packets can take different ways and arrive in a different order. Sometimes the same packet even arrives twice. The network does not promise to deliver every piece, in order, once. D1 §12.1.1, §12.2.2 D4 §1.4 D2 §2.3

How the message still arrives whole

So computers follow agreed rules, called a protocol. A common idea is the one Ines used. Number every piece. The receiver sorts the pieces by number. If a number is missing, the receiver asks for that piece again. If a number arrives twice, the receiver keeps only one. D1 §12.2.2 D6 §1.5

With these rules, a message can get through even when the network loses and mixes up pieces. Both sides must follow the same rules, or they cannot understand each other. That is like the codes of F0-03: an agreement on both ends.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. The internet uses many protocols stacked on top of each other. One of them, called IP (the Internet Protocol), carries the addresses and gets each packet to the right computer, but does not promise that it arrives. Another, called TCP (the Transmission Control Protocol), works on top of it: it numbers the data, puts it back in order, and resends what was lost, so programs see one tidy stream of data. D4 §1.4 D5 §2.2 D1 §12.2.1 You will study these in DS201.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

At this level, the depth waits for the F5 faculty, which studies networks and computers that work together from far apart, where lost and late messages are an everyday problem. Keep this idea: a receiver can only fix what it can detect, so the sender must include enough information, such as numbers and totals, for the receiver to notice trouble. D5 §2.2

How the hardware actually does it

Each computer has a part for networking: a network card or chip, for a cable or for radio. It turns the bits of a packet into signals on the wire or in the air, and back again on the other side. Routers are computers built for passing packets along. All the work of numbering, sorting and asking again is done by programs, following the protocol. D7 §1.8 D4 §2.4

Not verified — check before relying on this

How big real packets are, and how often real networks lose them, are not given here: they depend on the kind of network and on the protocols in use. Check "TCP/IP Illustrated" (D1, the full book; only its Chapter 12 was opened in this pass) or the protocol's own standard (D4, D5) before quoting a number.

Worked example

Ines's message is: "MEET ME AT THE BIG PARK GATE AT FOUR!" That is 37 characters, counting spaces and the "!". Each postcard holds 8 characters.

  1. How many cards? 8 × 4 = 32 is not enough; 8 × 5 = 40 is enough. So 5 cards. The last one is not full.
  2. Cut the message: card 1 "MEET ME ", card 2 "AT THE B", card 3 "IG PARK ", card 4 "GATE AT ", card 5 "FOUR!". Each card is marked "card n of 5". R1
  3. The network: cards arrive in the order 3, 1, 5, 4. Card 2 is lost.
  4. Sort by number: 1, 3, 4, 5. The receiver knows there should be 5 cards, because every card says "of 5". So it checks 1 to 5 and sees that 2 is missing.
  5. Ask again: the receiver asks for card 2. It arrives.
  6. Rebuild: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 joined together: "MEET ME AT THE BIG PARK GATE AT FOUR!" The real run of Listing 1 printed exactly this. R1

Notice how the words were cut in the middle: "B" is on card 2 and "IG" on card 3. The network does not care about words. Only the numbers bring them back together.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Postcards rarely arrive twice; packets can. Networks can deliver the same packet more than once, so receivers must be ready to throw away copies. D1 §12.1.1 D5 §3.8
    • 

  • Post offices keep postcards safe; routers may drop packets. When a router is too busy, it can drop packets. IP sends no receipt back, so the receiver or sender must notice by itself. D5 §3.8 D4 §1.4
    • 

  • Postcards take days; packets are much faster. But "fast" is not "instant", and a reply may come back late. A computer waiting for an answer cannot tell if a packet is lost or only slow. D2 §1.3, §2.3 D5 §3.8.1
    •

Lab


The postcard network game


Goal: send a message through a "network" of people that loses and mixes up cards, and rebuild it using a protocol.


You need: eight paper slips per round, pencils, at least three people: a sender, a network, a receiver.


Safety
None beyond normal paper and pencil work. If you pass cards between rooms, walk.


    

  1. Round 1, no protocol. The sender writes a short message, cuts it into five or six slips, with no numbers. The network shuffles the slips, hides one, and hands the rest to the receiver. Can the receiver read the message?
    2. 

  2. Round 2, with a protocol. Agree the rules first: every slip says "n of total"; the receiver sorts by number and asks for missing slips by number.
    3. 

  3. The sender writes a new message on numbered slips. The network shuffles, hides one, and also copies one slip twice.
    4. 

  4. The receiver sorts, throws away the copy, asks for the missing number, and rebuilds the message.
    5. 

  5. Swap roles and play again.
    6. 



What you should see: round 1 usually fails; round 2 succeeds even with lost and copied slips.


Check you succeeded: in round 2, the receiver reads the exact message, and can explain which slip was missing and which was a copy.


If something goes wrong: the receiver cannot tell a slip is missing → no total on the slips → add "of total". A word appears twice → a copy was kept → keep one slip per number. The message is cut oddly → that is fine; numbers fix the order, not the words.


Extension: what if the "please send again" note gets lost too? Invent a rule that handles it.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: the receiver printed "IG PARK MEET ME FOUR!GATE AT ". The words are in the wrong order, and "AT THE B" is missing completely.


Root cause: the cards carry no numbers and no total (first line: "no numbers on them"). So the receiver can only glue them in arrival order (3, 1, 5, 4), which scrambles the message, and it has no way to notice that a card is missing.


Evidence: the four "arrived" lines are in the order of cards 3, 1, 5, 4 from the worked example, and the output is exactly those four texts joined. Listing 1, with numbers and totals, sorted the same cards and asked for card 2.


Fix: add the protocol rules: write "card n of total" on every card; sort by number; check every number from 1 to the total; ask for missing ones; keep one card per number. Listing 1 does all of these; check by running it with the same network.


How the fault was made: the Lab Engineer wrote a receiver with no numbering at all, using the same pretend network as Listing 1.


Wrong answers and why: "The network is broken": networks may lose and reorder pieces; the same network worked with Listing 1's protocol. "The sender cut the words badly": cutting words in the middle is fine when the cards are numbered. Prevention: test every receiver with a network that loses, reorders and duplicates on purpose.
