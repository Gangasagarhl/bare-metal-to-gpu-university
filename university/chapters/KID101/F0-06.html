Story hook

Analogy

Kwame is reading on the sofa. Ding-dong! His ears hear the doorbell. His brain thinks: "That must be Grandma." He calls out with his voice: "Coming!" Then his legs carry him to the door.

Something came in (the sound of the bell). Something happened inside (Kwame thought about it). Something went out (his voice, his legs). In, inside, out.

A computer does the same three things all day long. It takes things in through its "senses", works on them inside, and sends results out through its "voice". This chapter is about the in and the out.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the input → processing → output pattern with an everyday example.
  • Sort devices into input devices and output devices, and name some that do both.
  • Explain what a sensor does: it measures something and turns it into a number.
  • Trace a night-light program: light level in, decision inside, lamp on or off out.
  • Find whether a fault is in the input, the processing or the output.
Jargon box
Input
Simple words: information that goes into the computer. Analogy: what your ears and eyes take in. Precise: data that a program receives from outside itself, for example from a keyboard, a mouse, a microphone, a sensor or a file. D1
Output
Simple words: information or action that comes out of the computer. Analogy: your voice and your hands. Precise: data or signals that a program sends out, for example to a screen, a speaker, a printer, a lamp, a motor or a file. D1
Processing
Simple words: the work the computer does between the input and the output. Analogy: your thinking: "the bell rang, so it is Grandma". Precise: the steps of the program that turn input data into output data. D2
Sensor
Simple words: a part that measures something about the world, like light or heat, and turns it into a number. Analogy: one of your senses. Precise: a device that responds to a physical quantity (light, temperature, sound, movement) and produces a signal that the computer can read as a value. D3
Device
Simple words: a piece of equipment connected to the computer. Analogy: a tool or helper in the house. Precise: a hardware unit that the computer communicates with, such as a keyboard, screen or sensor. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A computer on its own is like a person in a dark, silent room. It can think very fast. But it cannot know anything about the world, and nobody can know what it thought. It needs ways in and ways out.

Ways in: input

Anything that goes into the computer is input. When you press a key, the keyboard sends a number for that key. When you click, the mouse sends where it is and which button. A microphone sends sound, as a list of numbers like the samples in F0-03. A camera sends the colours of many pixels. D1

Each of these is a device: a piece of equipment joined to the computer. Input devices are like the computer's senses.

Ways out: output

Anything that comes out of the computer is output. The screen shows letters and pictures. The speaker plays sounds. A printer puts ink on paper. A computer can also switch on a lamp or turn a motor. Output devices are like the computer's voice and hands. D1

Some devices do both. A touch screen shows pictures (output) and feels your finger (input). A headset has speakers and a microphone.

In between: processing

Between input and output is processing. That is the program, running in the kitchen of F0-05. It reads the input, follows its steps, and makes the output. Without the program, the input would go nowhere. D2

Sensors: senses that measure

Some input devices measure the world. They are called sensors. A light sensor measures how bright it is. A temperature sensor measures how warm it is. Each one turns what it measures into a number the computer can read. D3

Think of a night light. A light sensor says how bright the room is, as a number. The program decides: "Is it dark?" If yes, the output turns the lamp on. In, inside, out, again and again.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. A sensor does not send "dark" or "bright". It sends a number, and the program decides what the number means. The limit between dark and bright is a choice written in the program. In Listing 1 that choice is 30, on our own made-up scale from 0 to 100. A real light sensor has its own range and units, given in its datasheet. D3 Also, a program's input does not always come from a device. In Listing 1, the "sensor readings" come from a text file, so that everyone gets the same run.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

At this level, the depth waits for later courses. In HW101 you will see how a sensor makes an electrical signal, and in the F1 and F4 faculties how the CPU talks to devices through the bus. Keep this: devices send and receive numbers; programs give them meaning. D1

How the hardware actually does it

Every device is connected to the computer, by wires or by radio. Input devices turn something from the world, a key press or light, into electrical signals that become numbers in memory. Output devices do the reverse: the computer sends numbers, and the device turns them into light, sound or movement. The CPU reaches the devices through the bus, the corridors of F0-05. D1 D3

Worked example

Trace the night light by hand. The limit is 30. The rule is: if the light level is less than 30, lamp ON; otherwise, lamp off.

Look closely at 30. It is exactly on the limit. "Less than 30" means 29 or below, so 30 gives "off". Edge cases like this are where many mistakes hide. The real run of Listing 1 gives the same outputs. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Your senses come with meaning; a sensor does not. When you see darkness, you know it is dark. A light sensor gives only a number. The program decides what counts as dark. D3
    • 

  • You can tell when your eyes are tired. A broken or dirty sensor still sends numbers, and the program may trust wrong ones. Real sensors can be noisy or off by some amount; you will measure that in later courses.
    • 

  • Your voice and hands are part of you. A computer's outputs are separate devices. If the speaker is unplugged, the program still "says" its sound, and nobody hears it.

Summary


    

  • Computers work in three parts: input, processing, output.
    • 

  • Input devices are the computer's senses: keyboard, mouse, microphone, camera, sensors.
    • 

  • Output devices are its voice and hands: screen, speaker, printer, lamp, motor.
    • 

  • Some devices do both, like a touch screen.
    • 

  • A sensor turns something it measures into a number.
    • 

  • The program decides what the numbers mean, and limits are choices written in the program.
    • 

  • To find a fault, check input, processing and output one at a time.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: both runs show the same six inputs (80, 55, 31, 30, 12, 0). Leo's outputs are ON for 80, 55 and 31, and off for 30, 12 and 0. Listing 1 gives the opposite for every reading except 30.


Root cause: processing. Line 9 of Leo's program uses "greater than" (>) instead of "less than" (<), so the decision is flipped.


Why not input or output: the input lines are identical in both runs, so the sensor readings are not the cause. The output prints both "lamp ON" and "lamp off" when asked, so the output works.


Why 30 is the same: 30 is neither less than 30 nor greater than 30, so both programs choose the "else" branch: lamp off.


Fix and check: change line 9 to if (light < darkLimit) {; run again with the same input and compare with the worked-example table.


How the fault was made: the Lab Engineer copied Listing 1 and changed only the comparison on line 9.


Wrong answers and why: "The sensor is upside down": the inputs are the same as in the good run. "The lamp is broken": the program printed both outputs. Prevention: test with a dark reading, a bright reading and the limit itself before using a new program.
