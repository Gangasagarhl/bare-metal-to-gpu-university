Story hook

Analogy

It is a rainy afternoon, and the power is out. Amira and her brother Dev play a game in the hallway: walk to the kitchen door in the dark. Amira holds her hands out in front of her.

She takes a small step. Her fingers touch nothing. Another step. Still nothing. Next step, her fingertips touch the wall. She stops. She turns to the right and feels again. Clear. She steps on.

Dev notices something. "You do the same thing every time," he says. "Feel. Decide. Move. Then feel again." Amira laughs. "That's how you walk when you can't see."

A robot works in exactly that loop: feel, decide, move, again and again. A robot is a computer that can feel the world with sensors and move in it with motors. This chapter shows how.

Jargon box
Robot
Simple words: a machine with a computer inside that senses the world and moves in it by itself. Analogy: a body with senses, a brain and muscles. Precise: a machine that uses sensors, a computer running a program, and actuators to act in the physical world, following that program rather than direct moment-by-moment human control. D1
Drone
Simple words: a flying robot, often with spinning propellers. Analogy: a body that must keep its balance in the air all the time. Precise: an unmanned aircraft; a multirotor drone uses a computer (the flight controller) that reads its sensors and changes the speed of its motors to stay balanced and move. D3
Actuator
Simple words: a part that makes something move or happen. Analogy: a muscle. Precise: a device that turns a command from the computer into a physical action, such as a motor turning a wheel. D1
Motor
Simple words: a part that spins when it gets electricity. Analogy: the muscle that turns a wheel or a propeller. Precise: an electric machine that turns electrical energy into turning motion; the most common actuator in small robots and drones. D2
Control loop
Simple words: the robot's repeating cycle: sense, think, act, and again. Analogy: Amira's "feel, decide, move" in the dark hallway. Precise: a loop in which a program reads sensors, decides on commands from what it read, sends the commands to the actuators, and repeats, so that each action is based on fresh measurements. D1 D4
Simulator
Simple words: a program that pretends to be the real world, so you can test a robot safely. Analogy: practising a dance in your room before the show. Precise: software that models a robot and its surroundings well enough to run the robot's program and observe what would happen, without real hardware. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

In this course you have met every part a robot needs. A computer that follows exact steps (F0-01). Inputs and outputs (F0-06). Decisions and loops (F0-07). A robot puts them all together in a machine that moves. D1

Senses, brain, muscles

A robot's senses are its sensors. A bump switch feels when it touches something. A distance sensor measures how far away a wall is. A camera sees colours. Each sensor gives the computer numbers, as you learned in F0-06. D1

A robot's brain is its computer, running a program. A robot's muscles are its actuators. The most common one is the motor, which spins when it gets electricity. Motors turn wheels, move arms, or spin propellers. D1 D2

The loop that never stops while the robot runs

A robot cannot plan everything at the start, because the world can change. So it runs a loop. Sense: read the sensors. Think: decide what to do. Act: send commands to the motors. Then the robot has moved, so the old sensor numbers are out of date. So it senses again. This repeating cycle is called a control loop. D1 D4

That is exactly Amira in the dark hallway: feel, decide, move, feel again.

Drones

A drone is a flying robot. A common kind has several propellers, each spun by its own motor. Its sensors feel tilting and turning. Its computer changes the speed of each motor, over and over. That keeps it level and moves it where it should go. If the loop stopped, the drone would not stay balanced. D3

Practise safely first

Real robots and drones can hurt people. Spinning propellers can cut, and motors can pinch fingers. So engineers test new programs in a simulator first. A simulator is a program that pretends to be the robot and its world. In this course, you only use paper and our own tiny simulator.

Safety

In KID101 you do not build or fly a real robot or drone. In the whole F0 faculty there are no propellers, no LiPo batteries and no mains electricity. Real robot kits come later (KID103, RB101), always with adult supervision, battery kits designed for children, and the simulator first. Flying a real drone outdoors is controlled by rules in most countries. Always check your national aviation authority's current rules with an adult first. See the safety rules.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. Real sensors are not perfect. Their numbers wobble a little (noise), and some drift slowly away from the truth over time. A real robot's program must expect this: it often combines several sensors and trusts each one as much as it deserves. D4 Real wheels also slip, so "I turned the wheel ten times" does not always mean "I moved exactly that far". Our simulator is perfect on purpose, so you can see the loop clearly first.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

At this level, the depth waits for the robotics faculty (F9) and the drone faculty (F10). There you will meet control loops that steer back to a line, sensors that drift, and flight software that keeps a drone level. Keep the key idea from today: act only on fresh information, and sense again after every action. D1

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a robot is a small computer, with a CPU and memory like the kitchen of F0-05. Sensors are wired to its inputs; motors are wired, through a motor driver, to its outputs. A motor needs much more electric power than the computer can give directly, so a motor driver takes the computer's small command signal and controls the bigger flow of power from the battery to the motor. D2

Not verified — check before relying on this

How many times per second a real robot or drone runs its control loop, and the voltages and currents of real motors and batteries, depend on the exact product. None are given here. Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Your senses come with understanding; sensors give only numbers. Amira knows "that's a wall". A sensor gives a number, and the program decides what it means. D1
    • 

  • Real senses and real sensors both make mistakes, but differently. Sensors are noisy and can drift in particular, measurable ways; our simulator's sensor is perfect, which no real sensor is. D4
    • 

  • Muscles feel tired; motors do not warn you. A motor keeps pushing against a wall if the program tells it to, which can damage the robot or hurt fingers. That is why safety rules and the simulator come first.
    • 

  • A robot runs its loop at a steady pace, on a timer. Amira stepped when she felt like it. A robot's computer repeats its loop on a schedule, and sees only what its sensors measure.
    •

Be the robot in the dark


Goal: act out the sense–think–act loop with a blindfolded "robot" and a written rule, then trace the same rule on paper.


You need: a clear room or hallway, a soft scarf as a blindfold, a grown-up, a friend, paper and a pencil.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: the first "BUMP!" line comes right before the tick 4 line: during tick 4, the robot at column 4, row 1, facing east, drove into the wall at column 5. Every tick from 4 to 12 says "path clear, forward" but the position stays column 4, row 1; the run ends "goal not reached, bumps: 9".


Root cause: in Kai's program the sensor is read once, on line 6, before the loop. The value "no wall" from the start is reused every tick, even after the robot reaches the wall. The robot acts on old information.


Evidence: "path clear" at tick 4 is false: the worked example and Listing 1's run both show a wall ahead at that spot. In Listing 1, the sensor line is inside the loop (line 7); in Kai's program it is outside (line 6).


Fix and check: move const bool wall = robot.sensorWallAhead(); inside the loop, as the first line of each tick. Run again: the output should match Listing 1's run, with "goal reached, bumps: 0".


How the fault was made: the Lab Engineer copied Listing 1 and moved the sensor line out of the loop.


Wrong answers and why: "The sensor is broken": the same sensor function works in Listing 1. "The motor is too weak": the simulator's motor stops at walls on purpose and reports BUMP; the robot should never have driven forward there. Prevention: print the fresh sensor value every tick, and test that the robot turns at the first wall.
