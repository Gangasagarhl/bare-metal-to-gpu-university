Story hook

Analogy

It is laundry day. Omar tips a basket of clean socks onto the bed. There are blue ones, striped ones, and one lonely green one. His job is to pair them all.

His sister Aiko watches. Omar grabs a sock, then digs through the whole pile for its partner. Then he grabs another and digs again. "That takes forever," Aiko says. "Try my way. Pick up a sock. If its partner is already on the bed, join them. If not, lay it out on its own."

Both ways work. Both end with neat pairs. But Aiko's way needs far less digging. Two good algorithms can solve the same problem, and one can be much faster. That is what this chapter is about.

Jargon box
Sorting
Simple words: putting things in order, like smallest to biggest. Analogy: lining up cards in your hand from low to high. Precise: rearranging a list so that each item is no bigger than the next one (or no smaller, for the other direction). D2
Comparison
Simple words: looking at two things and deciding which is bigger. Analogy: holding two cards side by side. Precise: a test between two values whose answer is yes or no, such as "is the left one bigger than the right one?". Counting comparisons is one way to measure how much work a sorting method does. D2
Swap
Simple words: two things change places. Analogy: two children in a line trading spots. Precise: exchanging the values in two positions of a list. D2
Sorting network
Simple words: a fixed plan of "compare these two, swap if needed" stations that always sorts. Analogy: a chalk path on the floor where people meet at boxes, compare, and step left or right. Precise: a fixed sequence of compare-and-swap operations on fixed positions, chosen so that any starting order comes out sorted. R1

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Sorting means putting things in order. You sort when you line up from shortest to tallest, or put books from A to Z. Computers sort all the time: names in a contact list, songs by title, scores from best to worst.

Here is the surprise. A computer cannot look at a whole pile and "see" the order. It can only do one small thing at a time. It looks at two items and asks: "Is this one bigger than that one?" That is a comparison. Then it may swap them. Sorting a list is many comparisons and swaps, in a clever order. D2

Method 1: find the smallest

Lay five cards in a row, face up. Look along the row and find the smallest card. Swap it with the first card. Now the first card is done. Do the same for the rest of the row, starting at the second card. Keep going until one card is left. That card is the biggest, and it is already in its place.

This is an algorithm. It is exact. It always ends. It works for any five cards, and for fifty too. You will act it out in the worked example.

Method 2: the sorting network

A sorting network is a fixed plan. Draw four lines on the floor, as in Figure 1. Four friends each hold a card and stand on a line. At each station, two friends meet and compare cards. The smaller card steps to the upper line. The bigger card steps to the lower line. R1

The plan never changes, whatever cards people hold. That is why it is a great game: nobody has to think, only compare. And it always works. Our computer tried every possible starting order of four cards, all 24 of them, and every one came out sorted. R1

Which method is better?

A good way to compare algorithms is to count their work. For sorting, we count comparisons. Fewer comparisons for the same job means less work. In the worked example you will count both methods for yourself.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. Count the "find the smallest" method for five cards. The first look compares the first card with 4 others. The next look compares 3 pairs, then 2, then 1. That is 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 comparisons, every time, whatever the cards are. With ten cards it is 9 + 8 + … + 1 = 45. The work grows much faster than the number of cards. The sorting network has another advantage: its stations 1 and 2 use different spots, so two pairs of friends can compare at the same moment. Doing work at the same time like this is called working in parallel, and it is the big idea behind GPUs much later in this university.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Computer-science books give these methods names: "find the smallest" is known as selection sort, and the floor game is a sorting network. Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

At this level, the deeper story waits. You will measure the speed of real sorting code in later courses, and in the GPU faculty you will see work split across many helpers at once. Keep one habit from today: to compare algorithms, count their steps on the same input. R1

How the hardware actually does it

Inside the computer, a comparison is one tiny step done by the processor: it checks whether one number is bigger than another and remembers the yes-or-no answer. A swap moves two numbers between places in memory. A sorting program is a long list of these small steps. The processor never sees "the whole list" at once. Where the analogy breaks


    

  • You can see the whole pile of socks at once. A computer cannot. It only compares two items at a time, so "look for the partner" is not a step it can do in one go. D1
    • 

  • Friends on the floor may take shortcuts. Someone might say "I can see 2 is smallest, I'll go first". A computer follows the plan exactly, every time, even if the cards were already sorted.
    • 

  • Socks have no single "order". Pairing socks is matching, not sorting by size. Computers sort by a rule you choose, like "smaller number first". Without a rule, there is no sorting.

Lab


The sorting-network game on the floor


Goal: play the network of Figure 1 with four people and show that it always sorts.


You need: chalk or tape, a floor or a playground, four players and one caller, cards with numbers (any four different numbers).


Safety
Walk, do not run. Play on a dry, flat floor with space around the lines. Use tape or chalk that an adult says may be used on that floor.


    

  1. Draw four long lines next to each other. Mark five stations as in Figure 1: short marks joining spots 0–1 and 2–3 near the start, then 0–2, then 1–3, then 1–2.
    2. 

  2. Each player takes a card and stands at the start of a line.
    3. 

  3. The caller says "station 1". The two players on spots 0 and 1 meet, compare, and the smaller goes to spot 0.
    4. 

  4. Continue through stations 2 to 5, always in order.
    5. 

  5. At the end, check: are the cards in order from spot 0 to spot 3?
    6. 

  6. Shuffle the cards and play again. Try "backwards" (biggest on spot 0) too.
    7. 



What you should see: every game ends sorted. Stations 1 and 2 can happen at the same time, because they use different players.


Check you succeeded: five different games all end sorted, and you can explain the station rule to someone new.


If something goes wrong: not sorted at the end → a station was skipped → replay with the caller ticking each one. Big card on top → rule flipped → "smaller goes up". Players argue → write the rule on a card at each station.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: only 8 of 24 orders came out sorted; 16 failed. The trace for 9 4 7 2 has only four stations and ends 2 7 4 9, which is not sorted.


Root cause: station 5 (compare spots 1 and 2) is missing. After the first four stations, the smallest card is always on spot 0 and the biggest on spot 3, but the two middle cards can still be in the wrong order. For 1 3 2 4, nothing swaps at all, and 3 and 2 stay wrong in the middle.


Fix and proof: add the station for spots 1 and 2 at the end. Listing 1 has exactly that one extra station and its run shows 24 of 24 sorted.


How the fault was made: the Lab Engineer copied Listing 1 and deleted the last pair, {1, 2}, from line 9.


Wrong answers and why: "The swap rule is wrong": the first four stations swap correctly in the trace. "Station 3 is in the wrong place": with it in place, 2 still reaches spot 0 in the trace. Prevention: never trust one game; test every order, or as many as you can.
