Story hook

Touch a hot pan and your hand pulls back before you feel the pain. The signal does not travel up to the brain and back: a short circuit in the spinal cord, close to the muscle, reacts first, and tells the brain afterwards. The brain still decides what to do next; it is not part of the fastest loop.

Robots are built the same way. A small microcontroller next to the motors and sensors runs the fastest, most time-critical loops with an RTOS, and reports to the big Linux computer, which runs ROS 2 and makes the plans. micro-ROS is a way of making that microcontroller a member of the ROS 2 system: its tasks publish and subscribe to topics as if they were ROS 2 nodes, through a small agent on the Linux side. This chapter builds the shape of such a microcontroller node for an IMU, on the course's own RTOS, runs it on an emulated chip, and decodes its data on the host. Then it shows what happens when the node's buffer and publishing rate are chosen without arithmetic: data disappears, quietly, unless you count it.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why a robot puts its fastest loops on a microcontroller and how micro-ROS connects it to ROS 2 through an agent.
  • Structure a microcontroller node with RTOS tasks: an on-time sampling task, a publishing task, and a lock-free ring between them, all in static memory.
  • Frame messages on a serial link with a length, a sequence number and a checksum, and decode them on the host.
  • Budget ring size, publishing rate and link bandwidth from the sample rate, and detect losses from sequence numbers.
  • Decide which parts of a design to check against the micro-ROS documentation before building on a real board.
Jargon box
micro-ROS
Simple words: a version of ROS 2 small enough for a microcontroller. Analogy: the reflex circuit that also reports to the brain. Precise: a framework that runs a reduced ROS 2 client library on a microcontroller with an RTOS, with static memory and a fixed number of entities, and connects to a ROS 2 system through an agent on a larger computer, over a serial, UDP or similar transport (names and details: unverified box in Layer 2). D1
micro-ROS agent
Simple words: a program on the big computer that speaks for the microcontroller in the ROS 2 world. Analogy: an interpreter at the border. Precise: a process on the host that receives the microcontroller's compact protocol messages over the transport and creates and operates the corresponding DDS entities (publishers, subscribers) on the client's behalf, so that other ROS 2 nodes see ordinary topics. Listing 2 plays this role for this chapter's own frame format. D1
Sequence number
Simple words: a counter put into every message, so that the receiver can see if one is missing. Analogy: page numbers in a book: a missing page is visible at once. Precise: a monotonically increasing number assigned by the producer to each sample when it is produced (not when it is sent), so that losses anywhere between production and reception show as gaps at the receiver. D2
Frame (serial link)
Simple words: one packaged message on a stream of bytes, with a start marker, a length and a check value. Analogy: an envelope with the number of pages written on it and a seal. Precise: a delimited unit on a byte stream: a start byte, a length, a payload and a checksum (here CRC-16 with polynomial 0x1021 and initial value 0xFFFF over length and payload), so that the receiver can find message boundaries and reject corrupted messages. D3
Static allocation
Simple words: deciding all memory when the program is built, not while it runs. Analogy: a toolbox with a fixed slot for each tool. Precise: every buffer, stack and object has a size fixed at build time and lives in the image's data or zero-initialised (.bss) sections; nothing is allocated at run time, so memory use is known from the linker output (OS305). R5

Terms from earlier chapters: RTOS, task (RTOS thread), fixed-priority preemptive scheduling, ring buffer (SPSC), semihosting (OS305); lock-free ring buffer (F9-49); hardware interface (F9-50).

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot has a big brain (a computer running ROS 2) and small helpers (little chips next to the motors and sensors). The small helpers are fast and never get distracted, so they do the jobs that must happen exactly on time, like reading the tilt sensor many times a second.

The helper writes each reading on a numbered card and puts the card in a small box. Every now and then it sends all the cards in the box down a wire to the big brain. The big brain checks the numbers on the cards: if card 12 comes right after card 4, it knows cards 5 to 11 got lost. If the box is too small for the number of cards between two sendings, cards get lost, and the numbers are how you find out.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why a microcontroller, and why micro-ROS

F9-47 to F9-50 made Linux behave well enough for a 1 kHz loop with care, and the build container still missed the jitter requirement. A microcontroller with an RTOS has a small, analysable timing: a few tasks, fixed priorities, interrupt latency in the order of microseconds, no virtual memory, no page faults (OS305, F3-39). So the fastest, hardest loops (motor current, IMU sampling, emergency stop) go there, and Linux receives their results. D1 micro-ROS lets the microcontroller's tasks take part in ROS 2 directly: the board publishes and subscribes to topics through a client library, and an agent on the host bridges to DDS. D1

The node, on uRTOS

Listing 1 is the firmware: the course's teaching RTOS uRTOS (copied unchanged from F3-39) with two tasks and a static ring:

  • The IMU task (priority 3) reads the simulated IMU every 2 ticks, gives each sample a sequence number, and pushes it into the ring. If the ring is full, it counts a drop and goes on; it never waits.
  • The publisher task (priority 2) wakes every 10 ticks, pops every sample in the ring, and sends each as a frame (Figure 2) over the UART as hexadecimal text.
  • The ring has 16 slots in static memory and uses the same release/acquire protocol as F9-49's (here with the compiler's atomic built-ins, because the freestanding build has no C++ library).

The host side


Listing 2 reads the firmware's output, finds frames, checks length and CRC, decodes the sample, and checks sequence numbers and the tick spacing. It plays the role a micro-ROS agent plays (receive, check, hand on), without creating a ROS 2 topic. Result: R2




61 frames, none bad, no gaps, every received sample exactly 2 ticks after the previous one. The gyroscope's z value is the simulated triangle wave (−1,000, −900, −800 …), the accelerometer shows 1,000 mg on z: a level, slowly turning board.



The arithmetic that keeps data


Three numbers must agree. With samples every s ticks and publishing every P ticks, the ring receives P/s samples between two publisher runs, plus whatever accumulates while the publisher is late. A ring of N slots of this design holds N − 1. Here: 10 / 2 = 5 per period, 15 usable slots: margin for a publisher up to 20 ticks late. Third, the link: each frame is 49 characters as text (46 hexadecimal digits, "F ", newline); on a real UART at 115,200 baud with 10 bits per character, that is about 4.3 ms per frame, so at most about 235 samples per second. Hexadecimal text doubles the size; a binary frame (23 bytes) would allow about 500. The micro-ROS protocol has its own overhead per message; check it for your configuration.



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


Priorities on the microcontroller


The sampling task has the higher priority because its timing matters (sample spacing is part of the data's meaning: an IMU integrated with irregular spacing drifts); the publisher's timing only needs to keep the ring from filling. This is rate-monotonic in spirit (F9-46): the shorter period gets the higher priority. The UART output in Listing 1 waits while the transmit buffer is full (busy-waiting in board::putChar); that is acceptable only because the publisher has the lower priority, so the IMU task preempts it. On a real board, the UART would be driven by an interrupt or DMA, and the publisher would block on a semaphore instead of spinning (F3-40).



Time across two computers


The tick in each frame is the microcontroller's own clock. The host has a different clock. ROS 2 messages carry timestamps; to give an IMU message a host timestamp, the agent can stamp it on arrival (adding the link's variable delay) or the two clocks can be related by a synchronisation protocol (micro-ROS offers a time synchronisation with the agent; unverified). For the course robot, record both: the microcontroller tick for spacing, the host time for fusion with other sensors (RB401 uses them).



Memory and size


The image is small: R5




2,816 bytes of code; 5,380 bytes of zero-initialised data, of which 2 × 256 words = 2,048 bytes are the two task stacks and 16 × 20 = 320 bytes the ring (a sample is 20 bytes with padding; F3-39's kernel and logs take most of the rest). All of it is known at link time. A micro-ROS client is larger (its middleware buffers, the entity tables); its documentation gives typical figures per board, which this build could not check.



Losing data on purpose


Sensor streams are usually sent best-effort: a lost IMU sample is less harmful than a stalled stream waiting for a retransmission, and the next sample comes in 2 ticks. Commands to the board (a target velocity, an emergency stop) are different: they need to arrive or the board must notice they did not (a command timeout that stops the motors). Whatever the transport promises, sequence numbers and timeouts in your own data make losses visible end to end, including the losses inside the board that no transport can see: the forensic lab.



How the hardware actually does it


On the emulated board, the UART is the CMSDK APB UART model at address 0x40004000 (OS305's board support); writing a byte to its data register sends it, and QEMU forwards it to its standard output, which the lab saves. On a real microcontroller, the UART shifts each byte out bit by bit at the configured baud rate, with a start and a stop bit; a USB-to-serial adapter or a USB device class on the microcontroller itself (HW205) carries it to the host. The IMU on a real board sits on an I2C or SPI bus (HW204); the sampling task would start a bus transfer (ideally with DMA), and the sample would be ready a few hundred microseconds later, a delay the sampling task must not wait for while holding up other work. Ticks in this build are virtual: QEMU's -icount shift=0 advances virtual time one nanosecond per instruction (F3-39's unverified box), so the 25,000-clock tick is not the timing of any real chip. Worked example


Task. A design review proposes: IMU at 1 kHz, publishing at 50 Hz to save processor time, ring of 16 slots, binary frames of 23 bytes on a 115,200-baud UART. Check it.


    

  1. Samples per publishing period: 1,000 / 50 = 20. Usable slots: 15. Fails: at least 5 samples lost every period, 250 per second, even with a perfectly punctual publisher.
    2. 

  2. Fix A: ring of 32 (31 usable; 11 slots of margin, 11 ms of publisher lateness). Fix B: publish at 100 Hz (10 per period; 5 slots of margin).
    3. 

  3. Link: 1,000 frames/s × 23 bytes × 10 bits = 230,000 bit/s, twice the link's 115,200. Fails regardless of the ring. Options: a faster link (a higher baud rate the hardware supports, or USB), fewer samples (average on the board and send 250 Hz), or smaller frames (several samples per frame sharing one header and checksum).
    4. 

  4. Lesson: three budgets (ring, publisher rate, link), each checked with a line of arithmetic before any code.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A reflex acts without the brain; this chapter's node only reports. A real board that controls motors must also act on its own when the host is silent: a command timeout that stops the motors is the reflex that matters.
    • 

  • Nerves do not lose signals in a queue; a microcontroller node can, if the arithmetic of Layer 2 is wrong.
    • 

  • The spinal cord and the brain share one body clock; the microcontroller and the host have separate clocks that must be related explicitly.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: periodic gaps in the received sequence. Cause: the ring is too small for the publishing period, or the publisher is starved. Check: the board's drop counter and maximum fill. Fix: Layer 2's arithmetic.
    • 

  • Symptom: gaps without drops on the board. Cause: loss on the link (corrupted frames rejected by CRC, or a host that reads too slowly). Check: the host's bad-frame counter and its input buffer.
    • 

  • Symptom: irregular sample spacing. Cause: the sampling task waits (on the bus, on the UART, on a lock) or has a lower priority than slow work. Fix: sampling never waits; highest priority among the data tasks.
    • 

  • Symptom: the board resets after a while. Cause: a task stack too small (the frame building uses stack). Check: stack painting and high-water marks (F3-39's techniques).
    • 

  • Symptom: micro-ROS client cannot create a publisher. Cause (from memory, unverified): the build-time limit on entities is too small. Check: the micro-ROS configuration documentation.

Transition box

Where you came from: F3-39 built uRTOS with fixed-priority preemptive scheduling; F9-49 separated a real-time path from slow work with a lock-free ring; F9-50 put the control loop behind hardware interfaces. What carries over: priorities by urgency, never waiting on the real-time path, static memory, a counted drop instead of a block, and arithmetic before code. What changes: the system now spans two computers with separate clocks joined by a link of limited bandwidth, and data loss can happen in three places (the board's ring, the link, the host). What comes next: this course ends here. RB401 uses timestamped sensor data for mapping and localisation, and RB403 (F9-66, F9-67) decides for the course robot which loops run on the microcontroller and which on Linux, and builds the operating system image that meets the jitter requirement for real.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Produced 61, published 19, dropped 42; the ring had 4 slots and a maximum fill of 3. The host saw 19 frames, 0 bad, 5 gaps, 35 samples missing; every gap shown is 7 samples (seq 3 → 11, 13 → 21, 23 → 31), and the spacing between received samples reaches 16 ticks. R4


Hypotheses. (a) Link corruption would show as bad frames: 0. (b) The board's drop counter is 42: confirmed. (c) The IMU task produced 61 samples, as in the normal run: it kept its period.


Root cause. Publishing every 20 ticks with sampling every 2 ticks gives 10 samples per period; a 4-slot ring holds 3. 7 of every 10 samples are dropped: exactly the gap size. The difference between 42 dropped and 35 missing is the 7 samples dropped after the last received one (seq 54–60), which leave no gap because nothing follows them.


Why the estimate fails. The host integrates gyro rate × (number of samples × 2 ticks): with 7 of 10 samples missing, it integrates 30 % of the true rotation, and the angle estimate falls behind in steps every publishing period.


Fix. Restore a ring with more usable slots than samples per period plus margin (16 with a 10-tick publisher, as in the normal run; or 32 with 20 ticks). On the host, integrate with the tick differences carried in each frame, not with the sample count, and treat a gap as a fault for the estimator. Prevention. A build-time check (a static_assert that RING_SIZE - 1 exceeds PUB_PERIOD / kImuPeriod with margin), the health counters of the mini-project, and an alarm on any sequence gap.


How the evidence was made. imu_node.cc unchanged, built with -DRING_SIZE=4 -DPUB_PERIOD=20 (step 4 of run.sh), run in QEMU and decoded by Listing 2.
