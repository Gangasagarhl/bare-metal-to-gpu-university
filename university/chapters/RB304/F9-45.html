Story hook

Amara plays the drum in the school band. The band does not need her to play fast. It needs her to play on the beat: every beat, at the same spacing, for the whole song. One beat that comes a little too late is not "almost right". The dancers step on the wrong foot, the singer stumbles, and the song has to restart. A drummer who plays every beat perfectly but one is worse than a drummer who plays a little slower and never misses.

Her friend Tomás rides his bicycle beside the band on the way home. He corrects his balance many times every second without thinking: feel the lean, steer a little, feel again. If his corrections came at uneven moments, sometimes quickly, sometimes after a long pause, he would wobble even if each correction were exactly right. A robot's control loop is both of these at once. It must correct like Tomás, and it must keep time like Amara. This chapter gives the words engineers use for "on the beat" and "how uneven the beats are", and you measure both on a real computer.

What you will be able to do

  • Define release time, start latency, response time, relative and absolute deadline, slack, period error and jitter, and compute each from a trace.
  • Distinguish hard, firm and soft deadlines by the consequence of a miss, and "fast" from "real-time".
  • Explain why a periodic loop must sleep until absolute release times, and measure the drift of the relative-sleep mistake.
  • Measure the lateness and period of a 1 kHz loop on Linux and report them as a distribution (median, p99, maximum), not as one number.
  • Relate delay and jitter in a control loop to lost phase margin, with a worked calculation.
Jargon box
Real-time system
Simple words: a computer system whose answer is only correct if it also arrives on time. Analogy: a drummer who must hit every beat; late is wrong, even if the note is right. Precise: a system whose correctness depends on the logical result and on the time at which it is produced; timing requirements are stated as deadlines on tasks or on end-to-end chains. "Real-time" is not a speed: a slow system can be real-time if it is always on time. D1 D2
Job, release time
Simple words: one run of a repeating task, and the moment it becomes due. Analogy: one beat of the song, and the moment it should sound. Precise: a periodic task with period T releases a job at rk = φ + k·T (φ is the phase, the first release); the job may start at or after its release, never before. D1
Start latency (release jitter at the start)
Simple words: how late a job actually began after it became due. Analogy: how long after the beat Amara's stick touched the drum. Precise: sk − rk, where sk is the start time. On an operating system it includes the timer's own lateness, the wake-up and the time until the scheduler lets the task run; F9-48 calls the first part wake-up latency. D1
Response time
Simple words: how long from "due" to "done". Analogy: from the beat to the end of the drum's sound. Precise: fk − rk, the finishing time minus the release time; the worst-case response time is its maximum over all jobs (glossary entry from F3-39). D1
Relative and absolute deadline
Simple words: how much time a job is allowed, and the clock time by which it must be done. Analogy: "within this beat", and "before the next beat at 3:00:01.250". Precise: the relative deadline D is a property of the task; the absolute deadline of job k is dk = rk + D. D = T is called an implicit deadline, D < T a constrained deadline. D1 D2
Slack
Simple words: the time left over between finishing and the deadline. Analogy: the quiet moment after the drum sound and before the next beat. Precise: D − (fk − rk); negative slack means the deadline was missed. D1
Jitter
Simple words: how uneven the timing is from one job to the next. Analogy: beats that are not evenly spaced. Precise: the variation of a timing quantity over the jobs of a task. It must always be said which quantity: start (release) jitter = max − min of start latency; response jitter = max − min of response time; period jitter = the spread of start-to-start intervals around T. Some texts use the absolute spread, others the largest deviation from the ideal; reports must state the definition. D1 D2
Hard, firm and soft deadline
Simple words: how bad it is to be late. Analogy: a missed beat that stops the show, one that is skipped, or one that makes the song a little worse. Precise: a deadline is hard if a miss can cause a failure with serious consequences, firm if a late result is useless but a miss is not catastrophic (the result is dropped), soft if a late result still has some, decreasing, value. The classification belongs to the requirement, not to the code. D1 D2
Drift (of a periodic loop)
Simple words: a loop that slowly falls behind its intended schedule. Analogy: a drummer who waits one beat-length after each hit instead of following the conductor: the band slowly gets ahead of her. Precise: the growing difference between the k-th actual release and φ + k·T, caused by computing each release from the previous wake-up (relative sleep) instead of from the fixed schedule (absolute sleep). R2

Related terms from earlier courses: response time and deadline and utilisation (OS305), median and percentile (MA202), tail latency (DS201), interrupt latency (HW204), control loop (RB101).

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Some computer jobs only need to be right. A calculator must give the right answer; nobody minds if it takes a moment. Other jobs must be right and on time. A robot that keeps its balance must look at its sensors and correct its motors again and again at a steady beat. If one correction comes late, the robot may already have tipped.

Engineers call these jobs real-time. Real-time does not mean "very fast". It means "always on the beat". A drummer who plays slowly but steadily is better for the band than one who is quick but sometimes misses.

Two things are measured. First: did each job finish before its deadline? Second: how uneven were the beats? The unevenness is called jitter. A good robot program keeps both small, and you prove it by measuring many beats, not one.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The periodic task model

Analogy first: a beat every T, a stroke that starts a little after each beat and must end before a limit. A periodic task τ with period T, worst-case execution time C and relative deadline D releases job k at rk = φ + k·T. The job starts at sk ≥ rk, finishes at fk, and must satisfy fk ≤ dk = rk + D. Everything else follows from these four times: start latency sk − rk, response time fk − rk, slack D − (fk − rk). D1 D2 You used this model in F3-39, with D = T, for tasks on a microcontroller.

Listing 1 computes every quantity from a trace of ten jobs. The trace is constructed for this lesson (it is not a measurement): a 1,000 µs task whose deadline is 800 µs, with two disturbed jobs. Its output, from the real run in this build: R1



Read it slowly. Job 3 started 240 µs late but still had 239 µs of slack; job 6 started 390 µs late, also ran longer (480 µs of execution instead of about 300), and missed its deadline by 70 µs. Notice the start-to-start column: job 3's lateness made the interval before it 1,229 µs and the one after it 774 µs. A late job stretches one interval and shrinks the next, because the releases stay on the fixed grid. That is the point of absolute releases, and the reason period jitter is roughly twice the start jitter in the worst case.



Which jitter?


"The loop has 50 µs of jitter" is not a measurement until it says of what. The three common choices give different numbers on the same trace (Listing 1): start jitter 379 µs, response jitter 561 µs, worst period error 377 µs. R1 Control engineers usually care about two of them: the variation of the sampling instants (start latency, if the sensor is read at the start of the job) and the variation of the input–output delay (from the read to the write of the actuator command). D4 A requirement should name the quantity, the statistic (maximum, or a percentile with the number of samples) and the conditions of the test.



Hard, firm, soft


The class of a deadline comes from the consequence of a miss, which only the system's requirements can say. D1 For a robot: the current loop of a motor driver or the joint-torque loop of an arm that a person stands next to is treated as hard; a camera frame that arrives too late for the planner is firm (drop it, use the next); the frame rate of a monitoring display is soft. Two consequences for the design follow. First, hard deadlines are verified by analysis plus measurement with margin (F9-46, F9-48); a test that "passed for an hour" is evidence, not proof. Second, hard work should be kept small and isolated, so that most of the robot's software (planning, logging, user interfaces) can live with soft guarantees.



Fast is not real-time


A faster processor lowers C, which helps. It does not bound the delays that come from outside the job: a timer that fires late, another program holding the processor, a page of memory that must be fetched, an interrupt handler that runs in between. Those delays decide the worst case, and they are properties of the operating system and its configuration, the subject of F9-47. D3 The measurement in the lab makes this concrete: the work in each cycle took 200 µs, yet some cycles started milliseconds late.



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


Absolute releases: the one rule every periodic loop needs


Listing 2 runs a 1 kHz loop on Linux in two ways. In the absolute version the loop keeps a variable release, adds T to it every cycle and sleeps until that clock time with clock_nanosleep(CLOCK_MONOTONIC, TIMER_ABSTIME, ...). In the relative version it sleeps "1 ms from now" after doing its work. D5 The measured result on the build container: R2






The absolute loop made 3,000 releases in 2,999.058 ms against an ideal 2,999.000 ms: 0.058 ms of difference after three seconds, which is the lateness of the last wake-up, not an accumulated error. The relative loop took 3,948 ms for the same 3,000 cycles: each cycle lasted 1 ms of sleep plus 200 µs of work plus the wake-up lateness, so its real rate was 759.6 Hz. A controller tuned for 1 kHz that runs at 760 Hz has the wrong gains for its derivative and integral terms (both contain T) and, worse, its rate changes with the load. R2 D4


Look also at the minimum period of the absolute loop: 233.7 µs. After a wake-up 3.4 ms late, the next releases were already in the past, so the loop ran several cycles back to back to catch up. That is correct for a loop whose jobs each represent one sample time, but it is a design decision: some controllers must instead skip missed releases and record an overrun. Decide it explicitly (F9-50's controller manager counts overruns).



Distributions, not averages


The absolute loop's lateness had a median of 99.0 µs, a 99th percentile of 1,074.8 µs and a maximum of 3,434.3 µs in this run. R2 An average would hide exactly what matters. For deadlines the maximum is the statistic of interest, and the measured maximum is only a lower bound on the true worst case: a longer run can only make it larger. That is why F9-48 treats test duration, load and the histogram's tail with care, and why F9-46 adds analysis. D3 D6


Why was even the median lateness about 0.1 ms on an idle machine? Several things add up between the release and the moment the loop reads the clock: the kernel's high-resolution timer must fire, the sleeping thread must be woken and scheduled, and Linux by default lets an ordinary thread's timer fire a little late so that nearby timers can be grouped (the "timer slack"). D5 The build container is also a virtual machine whose virtual processors share real ones with other work. Which of these dominated here was not measured; F9-48 shows how a real-time policy changes the picture.

What delay and jitter do to a controller


A controller designed in discrete time (MA301, F9-21) assumes samples exactly T apart. A constant delay τ between sampling and actuation is a known effect: it adds a phase lag of ω·τ radians at angular frequency ω without changing the gain, so it eats into the phase margin at the crossover frequency. D4 A constant delay can be included in the design; a varying one (jitter) cannot be compensated exactly and must be bounded. Two practical rules follow from this: (1) read the sensors at the start of the job and write the actuators as soon as possible after computing, or at a fixed point (for example at the start of the next job, which makes the delay exactly T and therefore constant); (2) keep the work of the control job small compared to T so that the response-time variation is small. D4 F9-50's controller manager follows rule 1 in the order read → update → write.


Note
Jitter can also be added on purpose to be removed: some systems time-stamp each sample with a hardware clock and let the estimator use the true sample time. That moves the problem from scheduling to estimation (RB302). It does not help the actuation side.



End-to-end deadlines


A robot's real requirement is usually end to end: "an obstacle seen by the LiDAR must change the wheel commands within 100 ms". That chain crosses several tasks, a network (DDS in RB303), queues and executors. Its worst-case latency is at least the sum of each stage's worst response time plus, for stages that sample periodically without synchronisation, up to one period of each sampling stage (the data may arrive right after a sample). D1 Writing the chain down stage by stage is the first step of every real-time design review, and the form the course project uses.



How the hardware actually does it


Time on a computer comes from counters. A processor and its platform have one or more free-running counters (on x86 the time-stamp counter, on Arm the generic timer) and timer devices that raise an interrupt when a programmed time is reached. Linux builds its clocks (CLOCK_MONOTONIC) from a chosen clock source and its high-resolution timers from a programmable timer event device. D3 The build container reports its clock source itself: the lab of F9-47 reads /sys/devices/system/clocksource/clocksource0/current_clocksource and recorded tsc (its config.out). A sleeping loop therefore wakes through a chain: timer interrupt → interrupt handler → the kernel marks the thread runnable → the scheduler chooses it → it returns from clock_nanosleep and reads the clock. Each link has a cost and can be delayed by whatever the processor is doing with interrupts or preemption disabled. On a virtual machine there are two such chains, the host's and the guest's. Interrupt latency itself was introduced in HW204; F9-47 follows the chain into the kernel.



Worked example


Task. A balancing robot's control job runs at 1 kHz (T = 1,000 µs). Measured worst execution times: read IMU and encoders 120 µs, compute 250 µs, write motor commands 80 µs. The controller was designed with a delay of at most one sample period between the sample and the output, and the loop's crossover frequency is 20 Hz. On the target, the measured worst start latency is 180 µs. (1) Choose the relative deadline. (2) Is the job schedulable with that start latency? (3) How much phase margin does the worst-case input–output delay cost at crossover, compared with zero delay?


    

  1. Deadline. The output must be written before the next sample: D = T = 1,000 µs at most. To keep the input–output delay small and nearly constant, choose a tighter deadline, for example D = 600 µs.
    2. 

  2. Response time. Worst case = start latency + C = 180 + (120 + 250 + 80) = 630 µs. With D = 600 µs this misses by 30 µs; with D = 1,000 µs it meets with 370 µs of slack. Either relax D to 700 µs (slack 70 µs) or reduce the start latency (F9-47, F9-48). Note the margin: 70 µs on a measured maximum is thin.
    3. 

  3. Delay. The sample is taken at the start of the job (after the start latency); the output is written at the end. The input–output delay is at most 120 + 250 + 80 = 450 µs measured from the sample; measured from the ideal release it is 630 µs. At ω = 2π·20 = 125.66 rad/s, a delay of 450 µs costs 125.66 × 0.000450 = 0.0565 rad = 3.24°; 630 µs costs 0.0792 rad = 4.54°. A full-period delay (1,000 µs) would cost 7.20°.
    4. 

  4. Conclusion. Writing at a fixed point (the start of the next job) makes the delay a constant 1,000 µs (7.2°, designable). Writing as soon as possible gives 3.2°–4.5°, smaller but varying by 1.3°. Which is better depends on the controller's phase margin; both must appear in the design note.
    5. 



The arithmetic of steps 2–3 is checked by the reader; it uses only the formulas of Layer 3.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A drummer hears the band and adjusts; a periodic loop keeps an abstract schedule. The loop does not know the plant's rhythm; its beat is a clock count, and a drift of the clock itself (or a different clock in another computer) is invisible to it. Multi-computer robots need clock synchronisation for that.
    • 

  • One late beat is audible; one late control job may not be. A single miss often has no visible effect, and that hides the problem until two misses in a row, at the worst moment, make the robot fall. Requirements therefore count misses over long runs and state the allowed pattern (for example "no two consecutive misses"), which no drummer analogy expresses.
    • 

  • The drummer's beats all cost the same effort; computer jobs do not. Execution time varies with data, caches and interrupts, so "on time" must be shown for the worst case, not the typical one.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the loop runs slower than its nominal rate, and slower still under load. Cause: relative sleep (sleep_for, usleep, a sleep with a constant duration after the work). Find it: print first-to-last time divided by the cycle count (Listing 2's "effective rate"). Fix: absolute releases with TIMER_ABSTIME (or std::this_thread::sleep_until on a steady clock).
    • 

  • Symptom: "jitter 5 µs" in one report and "jitter 300 µs" in another for the same system. Cause: different definitions (standard deviation versus max − min; start versus period). Fix: name the quantity and the statistic; report a histogram with the maximum and the number of samples.
    • 

  • Symptom: the timing looks perfect in the development build and poor in the field, or the reverse. Cause: measuring a sanitizer or debug build, or measuring on an idle machine. Fix: measure the optimised build under the load the robot really has (F9-48).
    • 

  • Symptom: times jump backwards or by seconds. Cause: using the wall-clock time (CLOCK_REALTIME, system_clock), which can be set or adjusted. Fix: CLOCK_MONOTONIC or steady_clock (SP202 discussed steady clocks).
    • 

  • Symptom: several control cycles run back to back after a stall. Cause: catch-up of absolute releases. Fix: decide: catch up (each job is one sample time) or skip and count an overrun; document the choice.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F3-39 scheduled periodic tasks on a microcontroller with deadlines equal to periods and timing in emulated ticks; RB303 built ROS 2 systems where callbacks run "when the executor gets to them". What carries over: the task model (period, execution time, release, response time) and the habit of measuring from releases on a fixed grid; absolute sleepUntil in uRTOS is the same rule as TIMER_ABSTIME here. R2 What changes: on Linux your loop is one thread among hundreds, the clock and the wake-up go through a general-purpose kernel (and here a virtual machine), and the worst case is set by things outside your code, so timing becomes a distribution you must measure; deadlines are no longer always equal to periods, and jitter needs a stated definition. What comes next: F9-46 turns the task model into schedulability tests (rate-monotonic and EDF), so you can predict worst cases before you measure them.

Summary


    

  • Real-time means correct and on time; timing requirements are deadlines, and "fast" is a different property.
    • 

  • A periodic job has a release r, start s, finish f and deadline r + D; start latency, response time and slack are differences of these.
    • 

  • Jitter is a variation and must name its quantity (start, response, period) and statistic.
    • 

  • Hard, firm and soft come from the consequence of a miss, i.e. from the requirements.
    • 

  • Periodic loops sleep until absolute release times; the relative-sleep loop measured 759.6 Hz instead of 1 kHz.
    • 

  • Measured on the build container: median lateness about 0.1 ms, maximum several milliseconds; the maximum is a lower bound of the worst case.
    • 

  • Delay costs phase (ω·τ); constant delay can be designed for, jitter must be bounded.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Effective rate 759.6 Hz; 3,000 cycles took 3,948.151 ms instead of 2,999 ms (949 ms of drift). Median period 1,299.0 µs = 1,000 µs sleep + 200 µs work + about 99 µs of wake-up lateness (compare the absolute run's median lateness of 99.0 µs). The maximum period, 5,224.5 µs, shows that one cycle was stretched by several milliseconds. R2


Hypotheses. (a) The camera node takes processor time so the control job runs longer or wakes later; (b) the loop's rate itself depends on load because the sleep is relative. Evidence that distinguishes them: the source shows a relative sleep, and the median period equals sleep + work + lateness exactly, which (b) predicts. (a) may also be true, but under absolute releases it would add jitter, not lower the rate.


Root cause. Relative sleep adds the work time and every wake-up delay to each period. Under load the work and the lateness grow, so the rate falls further. The PID was tuned for T = 1 ms: its integral term accumulates less per real second and its derivative term divides by the wrong T, and the delay inside the loop is larger, so the response overshoots differently from the simulator.


Fix. release += kPeriodNs; clock_nanosleep(CLOCK_MONOTONIC, TIMER_ABSTIME, &r, nullptr); with r computed from release (Listing 2's absolute branch). Proof: the absolute run's effective rate of 1,000.0 Hz and zero drift (loop_abs.out), repeated under the camera load.


Prevention. A test that runs the loop for N cycles and asserts |elapsed − N·T| < one period; and a code-review rule: periodic loops use absolute releases.


How the evidence was made. The printout is the real output of Listing 2's relative mode on the build container (loop_rel.log); nothing was edited. The robot and camera node are the scenario's story.
