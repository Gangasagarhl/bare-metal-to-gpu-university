Story hook

Amara has cooked for years in the first kitchen building, where helpers stand in rows of 32 and move together, one step at a time. Today she carries the same recipe cards across the street to the second building. Everything looks familiar: tables, pantry, rows of helpers. Then she counts a row. There are 64 helpers in it.

One of her cards says: "pass your bowl to the helper sixteen places along, add, then eight places along, then four, two, one; the first helper now holds the row's total". In the old building that was exactly right. In the new one, the first helper ends up with the total of only the first 32 bowls. The other half of the row's work sits in front of helper 32, and nobody collects it. No error is shouted, nothing spills. The answer is half the size it should be.

This chapter is about that second building: how an AMD GPU groups its work-items, why the group is 64 wide on the data-centre (CDNA) parts and 32 or 64 wide on the graphics (RDNA) parts, and how to write code that never needs to know the number by heart.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a compute unit (CU) and a wavefront are, and how they correspond to NVIDIA's SM and warp.
  • Compute how a workgroup of any size is cut into wavefronts, and how many lanes stay idle.
  • Show from real compiler output that warpSize is a compile-time constant of the target on AMD (64 for gfx90a and gfx942, 32 for gfx1030 and gfx1100 in this build's toolchain).
  • Find and fix a reduction that hard-codes 32 lanes, and predict its wrong answer before running it.
  • Read the instructions that compute a lane's position (v_mbcnt_lo_u32_b32, v_mbcnt_hi_u32_b32) in AMD assembly.
Jargon box
Compute unit (CU)
Simple words: AMD's name for one building block of the GPU that runs groups of work-items. Analogy: one section of the second kitchen building, with its own rows, table and supervisors. Precise: the replicated processor unit of AMD GCN/CDNA GPUs that runs the wavefronts of the workgroups assigned to it, with vector and scalar execution resources, a register file and the LDS; HIP reports the number of them as multiProcessorCount. It plays the role of NVIDIA's SM. D2 H1
Wavefront (wave)
Simple words: the group of AMD work-items that always do the same step together. Analogy: a row of helpers who move in step: 64 in a row on CDNA. Precise: the set of work-items executed together with one instruction stream and one 64-bit or 32-bit execution mask; AMD's counterpart of NVIDIA's warp of 32 threads. NVIDIA calls its group a warp; AMD calls its group a wavefront (a vendor term). D2 D10
Wave64 and wave32
Simple words: a wavefront of 64 lanes or of 32 lanes. Analogy: a long row or a short row. Precise: the wavefront size a kernel is compiled for; the compiler records it as .wavefront_size in the kernel metadata (64 for gfx90a and gfx942, 32 by default for gfx1030 and gfx1100 in this build: R2). RDNA can also run wave64 (F7-09). D4 R2
Lane
Simple words: one position in a wavefront. Analogy: one helper's place in the row. Precise: the index of a work-item inside its wavefront, 0 to width − 1; HIP returns it from __lane_id(), which the HIP 5.7 header builds from the mbcnt builtins. H1
warpSize (HIP)
Simple words: the number of lanes in a wave, as a name your code can use. Analogy: the number painted at the start of every row. Precise: in HIP 5.7's device code, static constexpr int warpSize = __AMDGCN_WAVEFRONT_SIZE;, a constant fixed when the kernel is compiled for a target; on the host, the same number comes from hipDeviceProp_t::warpSize. H1 D1
SIMD unit (in an AMD CU)
Simple words: the part of a CU that actually performs the vector instructions of the wavefronts assigned to it. Analogy: one supervisor's counter inside a section: the rows assigned to it take turns there. Precise: a vector execution unit with its own wavefront slots and its own part of the vector register file; a CU contains several, and occupancy on AMD is counted in wavefronts per SIMD (F7-10). The count per CU is a product fact to take from the architecture documents. D2 D6

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows one workgroup of 256 work-items arriving in each building. In the first, it is cut into eight warps of 32; in the second, into four wavefronts of 64. Inside the AMD compute unit, the wavefronts are spread over SIMD units, each with its own slots, while the workgroup's LDS lives in the CU and is shared by all its waves.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A GPU does not move its helpers one by one. It moves them in rows, and every helper in a row does the same step at the same moment. In NVIDIA's building a row has 32 helpers. In AMD's data-centre building a row has 64. In AMD's graphics building, rows can be 32 or 64.

Most of the time you do not notice. Your recipe says "add your two numbers", and every helper adds. You only notice when the recipe talks about the row itself: "pass to the helper sixteen places along", "the first one in the row writes the answer", "count how many in the row said yes". Then the length of the row matters. A good recipe never writes the row length as a number. It asks the building: "how long are your rows?"

Layer 2 — the student explanation

How a workgroup is cut into waves

A HIP launch creates a grid of workgroups (blocks), exactly as in CUDA; HP301 showed that the launch syntax and the built-in index variables carry over. D1 Each workgroup runs on one compute unit, and the hardware cuts it into wavefronts: work-items 0 to 63 form wave 0, 64 to 127 wave 1, and so on, in the order of their flattened index. D2 The arithmetic is the same as for warps, with a different divisor: wave = threadIdx.x / warpSize, lane = threadIdx.x % warpSize for a one-dimensional workgroup. Listing 1 prints both cuts side by side (R1): work-item 100 is lane 4 of warp 3 on NVIDIA and lane 36 of wave 1 on a 64-wide target.

A workgroup whose size is not a multiple of the wave width leaves lanes idle in its last wave. The idle lanes are not free: the wave still issues every instruction, with those lanes switched off by the execution mask (HW301, F1-57). D2 Listing 1 shows the effect: a workgroup of 96 work-items is three full warps on NVIDIA, but two wavefronts on gfx90a, the second only half full, so 75.0 % of the lanes do useful work (R1). Choose workgroup sizes that are multiples of 64 when the code may run on CDNA; multiples of 64 are also multiples of 32.

Where the number 64 comes from in your program

In HIP 5.7's device headers, warpSize is declared as static constexpr int warpSize = __AMDGCN_WAVEFRONT_SIZE;. H1 The macro __AMDGCN_WAVEFRONT_SIZE is set by the compiler for each target. The targets step of the lab asked the compiler for four targets: gfx90a and gfx942 get 64, gfx1030 and gfx1100 get 32, and the kernel metadata (.wavefront_size) agrees each time (R2). So a single source file compiled for two targets contains two different values of warpSize, one in each code object.

On the host side, the number comes from the device: hipGetDeviceProperties fills hipDeviceProp_t::warpSize, documented in the header as "Warp size." H1 Host code that sizes buffers per wave (one result per wave, as in F7-12) must use this field, not a literal. Listing 2 does both: the kernel stores warpSize, t / warpSize and __lane_id() for every work-item, and the host prints the device's warpSize and CU count.

Note

Two meanings of "kernel" and of "thread". In this faculty a kernel is the GPU function every work-item runs, not an operating-system kernel (F3); a GPU work-item ("thread" in CUDA words) is not an operating-system thread (SP203, BR-03).

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

In the analogy: the row length is painted on the floor of each building when it is built, and the recipe card is printed separately for each building, with the number already filled in.

What the compiler made of warpSize

The lane_isa step compiled Listing 2's kernel for gfx90a and for gfx1100 (R3). Three differences tell the story:

  • out[3*t+0] = warpSize became v_mov_b32_e32 v0, 64 on gfx90a and v_mov_b32_e32 v0, 32 on gfx1100: a literal in the instruction stream, not a value read at run time.
  • t / warpSize became a shift: v_lshrrev_b32_e32 v1, 6, v0 (divide by 26 = 64) on gfx90a, v_lshrrev_b32_e32 v1, 5, v0 (by 25 = 32) on gfx1100. Because the width is a power of two known at compile time, division costs one instruction.
  • __lane_id() became two instructions on gfx90a, v_mbcnt_lo_u32_b32 and v_mbcnt_hi_u32_b32, and only the first on gfx1100. The HIP 5.7 header defines __lane_id() as __builtin_amdgcn_mbcnt_hi(-1, __builtin_amdgcn_mbcnt_lo(-1, 0)): "mbcnt" counts, for each lane, the set bits of a mask in the lanes below it; with an all-ones mask that count is the lane's own index. H1 D2 The "lo" half handles lanes 0–31 of the mask, the "hi" half lanes 32–63; a wave32 target needs only the low half. R3

CUDA does it differently. The ptx_warp step shows that in PTX for sm_80, warpSize is the symbolic constant WARP_SZ (mov.u32 %r2, WARP_SZ;), which the PTX-to-SASS assembler resolves. R4 D11 In both ecosystems the value is fixed per target; neither reads it from memory in the kernel.

Rules for portable code

  1. Never write 32 or 64 where you mean "lanes per wave": use warpSize in device code and prop.warpSize on the host. D1
  2. Loops over lanes start from warpSize / 2, not from 16 (the bug of the story hook; Listing 1 part 3).
  3. Lane masks are 64-bit: HIP 5.7's __ballot returns unsigned long long. H1 F7-12 is about this.
  4. Workgroup sizes: multiples of 64 run full waves on every target in this chapter.
  5. Per-wave buffers: size them with the device's warpSize at run time.
Not verified — check before relying on this

Newer HIP versions. This build has HIP 5.7. The authoring guide records that the HIP documentation announced API changes in HIP 7.0 to align more closely with CUDA; whether warpSize is still a constexpr in device code, and whether __lane_id(), __ballot and the __shfl family keep these names and types, must be checked in the HIP documentation (programming guide, warp cross-lane functions; API reference) for the ROCm version in your dossier. Check: D1.

How the hardware actually does it

When a workgroup is dispatched to a CU, its wavefronts are placed into wave slots of the CU's SIMD units, and they stay there until they finish (they are resident, as in F1-56). D2 D6 Each wavefront has its own program counter, its own execution mask (EXEC, 64 bits on wave64) and its own scalar and vector registers. D2 Each cycle, issue logic picks ready wavefronts and sends their next instruction to an execution unit, as F1-60 modelled; a vector instruction operates on all lanes whose EXEC bit is set. D2 All waves of one workgroup are on the same CU, which is why they can share the LDS and meet at a barrier (s_barrier, F7-11). D2

Besides the vector units, a wavefront has a scalar unit for values that are the same in every lane (addresses of kernel arguments, loop counters): in R3, the kernel argument is loaded by s_load_dwordx2 into scalar registers s[0:1] once per wave. R3 F7-10 is about the two register files.

Not verified — check before relying on this

How many SIMD units a CU has, how many lanes a SIMD processes per cycle and how many cycles a wave64 vector instruction therefore occupies it, how many wave slots each SIMD has, and how many CUs a named product has: none of these could be read from a product document in this build. The four SIMDs per CU and the eight wave slots per SIMD that F7-10's calculator uses are the values that reproduce this build's compiler's occupancy reports for gfx90a (R5); they are the compiler's model, not a datasheet. Check: the CDNA architecture whitepaper for the named product (D6) and the "AMD Instinct MI200" ISA reference guide, chapter on the program state and wavefronts (D2). Run rocminfo on the lab machine (F7-14) and write the product's numbers into the hardware passport (mini-project).

Worked example

Task. A kernel is launched with 1,000 work-items per workgroup (allowed by the default limit of 1,024 that the compiler records as .max_flat_workgroup_size, F7-13). (a) How many wavefronts does a workgroup have on gfx90a and how many warps on an NVIDIA GPU? (b) How many lanes are idle? (c) A reduction inside each wave uses offsets 16, 8, 4, 2, 1 and lane 0 writes the result. With the input value of lane l equal to l + 1, what does lane 0 write for wave 0 on gfx90a, and what was lost?

  1. (a) Waves = ⌈1000 / 64⌉ = ⌈15.6…⌉ = 16. Warps = ⌈1000 / 32⌉ = ⌈31.25⌉ = 32. Listing 1 prints 16 and 32 (R1).
  2. (b) 16 × 64 = 1,024 lanes for 1,000 work-items: 24 idle; 32 × 32 = 1,024: also 24 idle. Both use 97.7 % of the lanes (R1). Here the widths agree; at 96 work-items they do not (75.0 % versus 100.0 %).
  3. (c) With offsets starting at 16, lane 0 only ever adds values from lanes 0–31: the sum is 1 + 2 + … + 32 = 32 × 33 / 2 = 528. The full wave's sum is 64 × 65 / 2 = 2,080. The missing 1,552 = 33 + … + 64 has been collected into lane 32, which nobody reads. Listing 1 prints exactly these three numbers (R1). Starting at warpSize / 2 = 32 gives 2,080.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A row is not a fixed group of people. A wavefront exists only while its work-items run; when it finishes, its slot takes a wave of any other workgroup. There is no permanent "helper 37". D2
    • 

  • Idle helpers still occupy their places. In a kitchen, missing helpers leave room for others; in a wave, a masked lane still occupies its slot in every instruction (75.0 % lane use for a 96-item workgroup, R1).
    • 

  • The row length is not painted once per building. On RDNA the same hardware runs both wave32 and wave64 (F7-09); the length is chosen per compiled kernel, not per building.

Forensic lab


Scenario. A team ported a histogram post-processing kernel from CUDA with hipify. On their NVIDIA development machine the checksum is right. On the AMD test machine (a CDNA GPU) the total count is too small, but never zero, and always by the same amount for the same input. They suspect a race in the atomics.


Evidence pack. The team reduced the problem to one wave with values 1 to 64 and ran the university's model of their reduction (Listing 1, part 3, R1):




Their kernel's reduction loop, from the ported source: for (int offset = 16; offset > 0; offset /= 2) v += __shfl_down(v, offset); followed by if (__lane_id() == 0) atomicAdd(total, v);.

Summary


    

  • AMD's compute unit (CU) plays the role of NVIDIA's SM; its wavefront plays the role of the warp.
    • 

  • Wavefronts are 64 lanes on CDNA targets (gfx90a, gfx942) and 32 by default on RDNA targets (gfx1030, gfx1100) in this build's compiler.
    • 

  • In HIP 5.7, warpSize is a per-target constexpr; the compiler turns it into literals and shifts.
    • 

  • __lane_id() is built from mbcnt: two instructions on wave64, one on wave32.
    • 

  • Workgroup sizes should be multiples of 64; lane loops start at warpSize / 2; masks are 64-bit.
    • 

  • A hard-coded 32 fails silently and deterministically on wave64: 528 instead of 2,080.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Width 32: lane 0 holds 528, the expected value. Width 64: lane 0 holds 528 against an expected 2,080; lane 32 holds 1,552, and 528 + 1,552 = 2,080 (R1).


Hypotheses ruled out. (a) A race would give varying shortfalls; the shortfall is exact and repeatable, and it appears in a model with no atomics at all. (c) A wrong warpSize is excluded because the portable version in the same evidence (offset warpSize / 2) gives 2,080.


Root cause. The literal 16 in the loop's starting offset, copied from 32-lane code. On a 64-lane wave the first step must fold lanes 32–63 onto 0–31 (offset 32); without it, half the wave is summed into lane 32 and discarded. The error is deterministic because it depends only on the data in lanes 32–63.


Fix and verification. for (int offset = warpSize / 2; offset > 0; offset /= 2). The model's line "warpSize/2: lane 0 holds 2080" is the verification; on hardware, compare against a CPU sum.


Prevention. Review rule: no literal 32 or 16 near shuffles, ballots or lane ids (a grep in CI).
