Story hook

In the second building, every group of rows shares a table. The table has drawers along its edge, numbered in order, and each drawer can hand out one thing per moment. Ingredients are laid out in the drawers in order: the first in drawer 0, the next in drawer 1, and so on, round and round.

Lucía's group is turning a big square of numbers on its side: they take it in row by row and must give it out column by column. Taking a row in is easy: the helpers reach into neighbouring drawers. Giving a column out is a disaster: with 64 numbers in each row, every number of a column sits in the same drawer, so 64 helpers queue at one drawer while the others stand still.

Lucía knows the old trick from the first building: leave one empty slot at the end of every row, so that a column runs diagonally across the drawers. It works. But the next morning her group is told that their table is now slightly too big, and one group fewer fits in the hall. This chapter is about that table, AMD's Local Data Share: how it is banked, how to see its accesses in the ISA, how padding and swizzling remove conflicts, and what the extra bytes cost in occupancy.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what the LDS is, how HIP's __shared__ maps to it, and why only the waves of one workgroup can use the same allocation.
  • Compute with a bank model the number of passes for a wave-wide LDS access, for any stride, with the bank count and the number of lanes served together as parameters.
  • Read LDS instructions in AMD ISA: ds_write_b32, ds_write2st64_b32, ds_read2_b32, ds_read_b32, and the barrier s_barrier.
  • Remove a column-access conflict by padding (pitch 65) or by an XOR swizzle, and state what each costs.
  • Predict occupancy from LDS per workgroup, and show that padding a 64 × 64 tile drops occupancy from 4 to 3 waves per SIMD in this build's model.
Jargon box
LDS (Local Data Share)
Simple words: AMD's fast on-chip memory that the work-items of one workgroup share. Analogy: the table in the middle of a group of rows. Precise: a software-managed memory in each CU; HIP's __shared__ variables are placed there; it is accessed with ds_ instructions; its size per workgroup is recorded as .group_segment_fixed_size and reported as "LDS Size [bytes/block]"; AMD's name for what CUDA calls shared memory. D2 D5 R4
LDS bank
Simple words: one of several independent parts of the LDS, each able to serve one word at a time. Analogy: one drawer of the table. Precise: the LDS is divided into banks; consecutive 4-byte words are in consecutive banks; an access in which several lanes need different words of the same bank is split into several passes (a bank conflict); lanes reading the same word are served together (broadcast). The bank count and how many lanes are served together are product facts (D2). D2
Pass (of an LDS access)
Simple words: one round in which each bank hands out at most one word. Analogy: one moment at the drawers. Precise: in this chapter's model (Listing 1), the number of passes of one wave-wide access is the sum, over groups of lanes served together, of the largest number of distinct words any single bank must deliver; the minimum for 64 different words is 64 / banks.
Padding (of an LDS tile)
Simple words: adding unused words at the end of each row so that a column is spread over the banks. Analogy: one empty slot at the end of every row of drawers. Precise: declaring tile[64][65] instead of tile[64][64]: element (r, c) is at word 65r + c, so a column's words have stride 65, which in a model with 32 banks hits every bank (R1). Costs 256 bytes more per tile (R4).
Swizzle (XOR)
Simple words: storing each row's elements in a shuffled order, so that a column lands in different banks without extra space. Analogy: each row puts its ingredients in the drawers in a different order, by a rule everybody knows. Precise: storing element (r, c) at word 64r + (c XOR r); a column read then touches words 64l + (c XOR l), which in the model spread over the banks like padding does (R1), with no extra bytes (R4) but extra address instructions (R2).
s_barrier
Simple words: the instruction where all waves of a workgroup wait for each other. Analogy: everyone at the table stops until the whole group has put its ingredients down. Precise: the workgroup barrier instruction that HIP's __syncthreads() compiles to on gfx90a (R2); HIP 5.7 implements __syncthreads() as __barrier(__CLK_LOCAL_MEM_FENCE), a barrier with an LDS memory fence. H1 R2

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the column read of the transpose (Listing 2) in the bank model: the first eight lanes of a wave read column 0 of the tile. With pitch 64, lane l reads word 64l, and every word is in bank 0: the lanes are served one after another. With pitch 65, lane l reads word 65l, in bank l (modulo the bank count), and they are served together. The swizzled tile gives the same spread as padding without the extra column.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A group of rows shares one table with many drawers. Each drawer can hand out one thing at a time. If every helper wants something from a different drawer, everybody is served at once. If many helpers want different things from the same drawer, they queue, one after another.

How things are laid out in the drawers decides who queues. Lay a row of 64 numbers across the drawers, and the next row starts again at drawer 0: then all numbers in one column are in the same drawer. Leave one slot empty at the end of each row, and every row starts one drawer later. Now a column runs across all the drawers, and nobody queues. The price is a slightly bigger table.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What the LDS is in HIP and in the code object

A HIP variable declared __shared__ inside a kernel lives in the LDS. Each workgroup gets its own copy for its whole lifetime, and only the work-items of that workgroup can reach it. D1 D2 The compiler adds up the static __shared__ variables and records the total: Listing 3, which declares __shared__ float buf[BYTES / 4], reported LDS sizes of exactly 4,096, 8,192, 16,384, 32,768 and 65,536 bytes for its five instances, and the transpose tiles reported 16,384 bytes for tile[64][64] and 16,640 for tile[64][65] (R4). R4 HIP also has dynamically sized LDS (extern __shared__ with a size given at launch), as CUDA has; HP301 showed the launch syntax. D1

LDS is read and written with ds_ ("data share") instructions, and the workgroup meets at s_barrier, which is what __syncthreads() became in R2. R2 H1 The barrier matters: in Listing 2, wave 0 reads a column that waves 1–3 wrote. Without the barrier, a wave could read before the others have written.

The bank model

HW301 (F1-58) introduced banks with a model of 32 banks of 4 bytes. On AMD the questions are the same, and the answers are product facts: how many banks, how wide, and how many lanes of a 64-lane wave are served together. Listing 1 therefore keeps both numbers as parameters and prints three columns: 32 banks with all 64 lanes served together, 32 banks with 32 lanes at a time, and 64 banks with 64 lanes. D2

Some conclusions hold in all three columns (R1). A row access (stride 1) needs the minimum number of passes. A stride of 2 doubles it, 4 quadruples it; a stride equal to the bank count or a multiple of it puts every lane in the same bank. Stride 64, the column of a tile[64][64], is the worst case in every column: 64 passes. Strides 33 and 65 return to the minimum, and so does the XOR swizzle. Broadcast (every lane reads the same word) costs one pass per group of lanes served together. R1

LDS and occupancy

F7-10's calculator has an LDS rule: workgroups per CU = ⌊LDS per CU / LDS per workgroup⌋, and waves per SIMD = workgroups × waves per workgroup / 4, at most 8. With the model value of 64 KiB per CU, Listing 3's five instances give 8, 8, 4, 2 and 1 waves per SIMD, and the compiler reported exactly these (R4). R4 The transpose tiles show the price of padding: 16,384 bytes allow 4 workgroups per CU (4 waves per SIMD); 16,640 bytes allow only 3 (65,536 / 16,640 = 3.9), so occupancy falls from 4 to 3 (R4). The swizzled tile keeps 16,384 bytes and occupancy 4 (R4).

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

In the analogy: the shape of the request each helper makes is written on the recipe card, and the person who reads the card can tell in advance whether helpers will queue at one drawer.

The LDS instructions of the three transposes

The lds_isa step prints the ds_ and barrier instructions of Listing 2's three kernels for gfx90a (R2). Each workgroup is 64 × 4 work-items, so each wave is one value of threadIdx.y, and each work-item moves 16 elements.

  • Naive, writes: eight ds_write2st64_b32 v0, v20, v21 offset1:4-style instructions, each writing two words. The "st64" form scales its two offsets by 64 words: offset1:4 means 4 × 64 words = 4 rows further, which is exactly the loop step r += ROWS with 64 words per row. The compiler could pack two writes into one instruction because the pitch is 64. R2 D2
  • Padded, writes: sixteen ds_write_b32 with offsets 0, 1,040, 2,080, … bytes; 1,040 = 4 rows × 65 words × 4 bytes. With pitch 65 the st64 form no longer fits, so the compiler used one instruction per word (R2).
  • Naive and padded, reads: eight ds_read2_b32 v[4:5], v8 offset1:4-style instructions in both: two words 4 apart (offsets of ds_read2 count words), that is tile[tx][ty + r] and tile[tx][ty + r + 4]. The instructions are identical; the conflict is not in the instruction but in the address in v8, which is tx × 64 + ty words in one kernel and tx × 65 + ty in the other (R2). R2
  • Swizzled: sixteen ds_write_b32 and sixteen single ds_read_b32, each with its own address register, 16 v_xor_b32 instructions, and 385 instructions in total against 276 (naive) and 293 (padded) (R2). The swizzle buys conflict-free access without extra LDS, and pays in address arithmetic. R2

The lesson for reading ISA: a bank conflict is a property of the addresses the lanes compute, not of the opcode. To find one, follow the address register back to its arithmetic and work out the stride across lanes, as you do for coalescing of global loads (CU201).

Which is fastest cannot be read from this table. It depends on whether the kernel is limited by LDS throughput, by memory latency (where occupancy helps, F1-60) or by instruction issue (where 109 extra instructions hurt). A transpose reads and writes every element in global memory once and does no arithmetic, so global memory may well dominate; whether the LDS conflict matters on your GPU is measured, not assumed (F7-14). A third option is to pad less wastefully: the occupancy step for this tile lies between 13,107 and 16,384 bytes (5 workgroups need ≤ 13,107; 4 need ≤ 16,384), so any padding that stays at or under 16,384 bytes keeps occupancy 4, for example a tile of 63 × 65 words if the algorithm allows it. Other LDS users

The LDS hardware is also used by instructions that do not touch LDS memory: ds_bpermute_b32, which HIP 5.7 uses for __shfl (F7-12), and ds_swizzle, a fixed-pattern exchange. H1 D2 They are counted in the lgkmcnt counter of s_waitcnt, like LDS loads (F1-60, F7-13); whether they also compete with LDS accesses for throughput is a product fact (D2).

How the hardware actually does it

The LDS is a memory inside the CU. When a workgroup is dispatched, the hardware reserves a contiguous region of it of the size recorded in the kernel descriptor (.amdhsa_group_segment_fixed_size, plus any dynamic size given at launch), and all waves of the workgroup address that region from offset 0. D2 D5 A ds_ instruction sends one address per active lane; the LDS splits the request among its banks and returns the results; the wave continues and waits for the data with s_waitcnt lgkmcnt(…) only when it needs it. D2 When the workgroup's last wave ends, the region is free for another workgroup, which is why LDS per workgroup limits how many workgroups a CU can hold.

Worked example

Task. A reduction kernel for gfx90a stores partial sums in LDS as float part[16][32], with workgroups of 512 (8 waves). In the second phase, lane l of wave 0 reads part[l % 16][l / 16]. (a) What stride across lanes does that read have, and how many passes does Listing 1's model predict with 32 banks? (b) What is the LDS size and the occupancy limit from LDS, if VGPRs allow 8? (c) Propose a conflict-free layout.

  1. (a) Word address of lane l: 32 × (l mod 16) + ⌊l / 16⌋. For lanes 0–15 the words are 0, 32, 64, …, 480: stride 32, all in bank 0. Lanes 16–31 start at word 1, also stride 32, all in bank 1, and so on. Each group of 16 lanes hits one bank with 16 different words. In the 32 / 64 model, the 64 lanes form 4 groups in 4 banks: the busiest bank delivers 16 words, so 16 passes. (The model's stride-32 line gives 64 passes for 64 lanes all in one bank; here the extra ⌊l / 16⌋ term spreads the four groups over four banks.)
  2. (b) 16 × 32 × 4 = 2,048 bytes. 65,536 / 2,048 = 32 workgroups by LDS, × 8 waves / 4 SIMDs = 64 → capped at 8. LDS does not limit this kernel.
  3. (c) Declare float part[16][33] (2,112 bytes, still 8 waves): word = 33 × (l mod 16) + ⌊l / 16⌋, so lanes 0–15 hit banks 0–15, lanes 16–31 banks 1–16, and so on; each bank now serves at most 4 different words: 4 passes instead of 16 in the 32 / 64 model. Or change the phase-1 layout so that phase 2 reads consecutive words (stride 1 across lanes): 2 passes, the minimum. (Lab Engineer's check of both patterns with Listing 1's passes function in scratch space: 16 and 4 passes in the 32 / 64 model; not in the recorded output. Lab step 3 asks you to add them.)

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Drawers do not hold whole ingredients. A bank delivers one 4-byte word per pass in the model; an 8- or 16-byte access (ds_read_b64, ds_read2_b32) touches several banks and may be split, by rules that depend on the product (D2).
    • 

  • Helpers asking for the same thing do not queue. In a kitchen, two helpers wanting the same bowl must take turns; in the LDS, lanes reading the same word are served together (broadcast, R1).
    • 

  • The table is not "slightly too big" in a smooth way. Occupancy falls in steps: 256 extra bytes cost a whole wave per SIMD here (4 → 3, R4), while thousands of bytes below the edge cost nothing.
    •

Summary


    

  • HIP's __shared__ is the LDS, one region per workgroup; its size is in the metadata and the resource report.
    • 

  • LDS is accessed with ds_ instructions; __syncthreads() is s_barrier with an LDS fence.
    • 

  • Banks serve one word per pass; strides that are multiples of the bank count conflict; odd pitches and XOR swizzles spread a column.
    • 

  • Conflicts live in the addresses, not the opcodes: follow the address register.
    • 

  • Padding a 64 × 64 float tile adds 256 bytes and cost a wave per SIMD (4 → 3) in this build's model; swizzling keeps 16,384 bytes and adds instructions (276 → 385).
    • 

  • Bank count and service width are product facts to read in the ISA guide.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Model: column reads go from 64 passes to 2 (R1). LDS: 16,384 → 16,640 bytes; occupancy 4 → 3 (R4). Instructions: 276 → 293 (R2).


Hypotheses. (a) Not supported: the model and, in the scenario, the profiler agree that the conflicts are gone. (b) Supported: R4, by the LDS step. (c) Supported, mildly: 17 more instructions, mostly the 16 single ds_write_b32 replacing 8 ds_write2st64_b32.


Root cause. The expected "wrong way" metric is occupancy (or the profiler's measure of resident waves): one wave per SIMD fewer, because the padded tile crossed the 16,384-byte step. For a transpose, which does no arithmetic and waits mostly on global memory (to be confirmed with the profiler), fewer waves means less latency hiding, which can cancel the gain from removing conflicts.


Fix and verification. The XOR swizzle: 2 passes in the model, 16,384 bytes, occupancy 4 (R1, R4), at the cost of 385 instructions (R2). Or a padded tile that stays within 16,384 bytes. Verify on the GPU by timing all three under the profiler (lab step 6).


Prevention. For every LDS change, record the three views (bank passes, occupancy, instruction count) in the review, and run the resource report in CI to catch occupancy drops.
