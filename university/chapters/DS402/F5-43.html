Story hook

The club's letters go through three friends' houses, and the club's rule says that any of them may be away for a week without anyone noticing. Last spring Arjun really was away, and nobody noticed: his letters simply went to Mei and Kofi. So the club felt safe.

This autumn the club decided to check its rule on purpose, the way a school holds a fire drill: Leila would pretend to be ill for one afternoon. She did not leave. She stayed at home, opened her door to every letter, and then sat on it. Senders waited, gave up, and sent the same letter again, to anyone, up to three times. Mei and Kofi, who were already busy that afternoon because it was the week before the festival, received three times their usual post, fell behind, and soon every letter in the club was late. Leila got better at four o'clock; the post stayed jammed until somebody told every sender to stop resending.

A drill that goes wrong is still a better teacher than a real fire. This chapter turns "practise failure" into an experiment with a hypothesis, a measurement, a limit on the damage and a stop button; runs six experiments on a model service; and then examines a game day that took the service down.

What you will be able to do

  • Design a fault-injection experiment with a steady-state hypothesis, a fault, a blast radius and an automatic abort condition.
  • Explain why a slow component can hurt more than a dead one, and how retries amplify load.
  • Compute retry amplification and the success ratio of a retry policy, and check them against a model.
  • Recognise a metastable failure, in which a system stays down after the trigger is removed, and name its sustaining effect.
  • Diagnose a failed game day from metrics, logs and chat, and propose changes to the system and to the experiment process.
Jargon box
Fault injection
Simple words: breaking something on purpose to see what happens. Analogy: Leila pretending to be ill for an afternoon. Precise: deliberately introducing failures (process crashes, refused connections, delays, packet loss, disk errors, clock skew) into a system or its environment to test its behaviour under those failures. D10
Chaos experiment (steady-state hypothesis)
Simple words: a fault-injection test written like a science experiment: say what "normal" looks like, predict it stays normal, break something, measure. Analogy: "during the drill, every letter still arrives within a week". Precise: an experiment that defines the system's steady state by a measurable output (for example the SLI of F5-42), hypothesises that it continues in the experimental group while a real-world event is injected, and tries to disprove the hypothesis by comparison with a control. D10
Blast radius
Simple words: how much can go wrong if the experiment goes badly. Analogy: a drill for one class, or for the whole school. Precise: the share of users, requests, machines or data that an experiment can affect; experiments start with the smallest radius that can disprove the hypothesis and grow only as confidence grows. D10
Abort condition
Simple words: the rule that stops the experiment by itself when it hurts too much. Analogy: the drill ends the moment anyone actually smells smoke. Precise: a pre-agreed, automatically evaluated condition on the steady-state metric (for example success below 95 % in a 10 s window) that removes the fault and ends the experiment without waiting for a human judgement. R1
Retry storm (retry amplification)
Simple words: failed requests are sent again, which adds load, which makes more requests fail. Analogy: every sender resending each late letter three times, to anyone. Precise: a positive feedback loop in which client or intermediate retries multiply the offered load on a degraded service; amplification is the number of attempts per original request, which can reach the product of the retry limits of every layer. D5
Retry budget
Simple words: a cap on how many retries a client may send, as a share of its normal requests. Analogy: each sender may resend at most one letter in ten. Precise: a limit on retries relative to first attempts (for example retries ≤ 10 % of requests over a window), so that retries help with occasional failures but cannot multiply load during a widespread one. D5
Metastable failure
Simple words: a broken state that keeps itself going after the thing that caused it is gone. Analogy: the post stays jammed after Leila is better, because everyone keeps resending. Precise: a failure in which a trigger pushes a system from a stable good state into a stable bad state, sustained by a feedback effect (typically retries or work amplification) rather than by the trigger; recovery requires removing the sustaining effect, often by shedding load. D11
Game day
Simple words: a scheduled day when a team practises a failure on a real system, together. Analogy: the club's announced fire drill. Precise: a planned exercise in which engineers inject one or more faults into a staging or production system while observing it, to test both the system and the people and procedures that respond. D8

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

The club's fire drill. Mapping: the drill = a fault-injection experiment; "every letter still arrives within a week" = the steady-state hypothesis; Leila pretending to be ill = the injected fault; drilling one class instead of the whole school = a small blast radius; stopping the drill the moment real smoke appears = the abort condition; senders resending late letters to anyone = retries; three times the post at Mei's and Kofi's = retry amplification; "resend at most one letter in ten" = a retry budget; the post staying jammed after Leila is better = a metastable failure; the announced drill afternoon = a game day.

You cannot be sure a backup plan works until you have used it. Waiting for a real failure to find out is like waiting for a real fire to find out whether the doors open. So you practise, on purpose, at a time you choose, with someone watching and a way to stop.

The surprising lesson is that a friend who is slow can cause more trouble than a friend who is gone. If Leila's door is locked, senders notice at once and go elsewhere. If she opens the door and then sits on the letter, every sender waits, gives up, and resends, and the resending is what jams everyone else.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Experiments, not accidents

The chaos-engineering paper frames fault injection as an experiment D10: define the steady state by an output users care about (not by internal metrics such as CPU); hypothesise that the steady state continues during a realistic event; inject the event (a crashed process, a slow disk, a lost network link); and try to disprove the hypothesis by comparing with a control group. Its other principles are to vary real-world events, to run experiments where they are most representative (which can mean production), to automate them so they run continuously, and to minimise the blast radius D10. The steady state in this chapter is the SLI of F5-42: success of requests, measured in 10-second windows. Every experiment in Listing 1 states the same hypothesis ("success ≥ 99.5 % in every 10 s window") and the same abort condition ("success below 95 % in a 10 s window removes the fault") R1.

The model service

Listing 1 models three replicas r0, r1, r2, each able to serve 700 requests per second, behind a load balancer that spreads new requests evenly. A replica's response time is a fixed 2 ms plus a queueing delay; a client gives up after 250 ms and may retry; a replica still does the work for a request whose client has given up R1. The model computes how many requests per 100 ms step reach each replica and what share of their responses is late, which is enough to show the mechanisms of this chapter; it is not a measurement of any real system (Layer 3 lists what it leaves out). At 900 requests per second each replica runs at 43 % of capacity and every request succeeds (E0).

All numbers are from the run R1. E1 kills r2: its connections are refused, which the client sees at once, and the retry to another replica succeeds; after 3 seconds the health check removes r2 and the other two run at 64 %. The 10 % extra attempts in the first window are those 3 seconds of retries. A dead replica, detected fast, costs almost nothing. E2 makes r2 slow instead: every response from r2 takes more than the 250 ms timeout, but r2 still accepts connections and stays in rotation. A request fails only if all three of its attempts land on r2; with retries spread over all three replicas that happens to (1/3)³ = 3.7 % of requests, so success is 96.3 %, matching the printed 96.30 %. Attempts per request are 1 + 1/3 + 1/9 = 1.44. E3 sends retries to a different replica, so a request that met slow r2 once never meets it again: success 100 %, amplification 1.33. The policy, not the fault, decided the outcome.

E4 is E2 at the afternoon peak of 1,500 requests per second. Before the fault each replica is at 71 %. The retries add 1,500 × (1/3 + 1/9) ≈ 667 attempts per second, about 222 per replica, which takes each replica to about 722 per second: past its capacity of 700. In the model an overloaded replica answers everything late, so every attempt now fails and is retried, and attempts reach 3 per request: 4,412 per second, 210 % of capacity, 3.56 % success, aborted after 10 seconds R1. The same fault and the same clients that cost 3.7 % at 900 per second take the service down at 1,500. E5 adds a retry budget of 10 % of new requests: amplification stays at 1.10 and load at 79 %, so the healthy replicas stay healthy; success is 73.37 %, still bad (the budget cannot rescue every request that met r2), and the experiment aborts. A budget does not fix the fault; it stops the fault from spreading.

Slow is worse than dead

Comparing E1 and E2 gives the most useful lesson of fault injection: a component that fails fast is easy to route around; one that is slow consumes the caller's time, holds its resources (threads, connections, memory for queued requests), passes the health checks that test only whether it answers at all, and triggers timeouts and retries everywhere upstream D5. The SRE book's chapter on cascading failures lists the defences: limit retries (with budgets and backoff, and only at one layer), set deadlines and propagate them, shed load when overloaded, and keep enough spare capacity that losing one replica at peak does not overload the rest D5. The last one is simple arithmetic: at 1,500 requests per second, two replicas of 700 cannot carry the load even with no retries at all, so this service is not "N + 1" at peak, whatever the earlier experiments suggested.

When removing the fault is not enough

In E4 the model aborted after 10 seconds, so the question of what happens next did not arise. In the forensic lab it does: after the fault is removed, the service stays down for another minute, because the retries alone keep every replica past capacity, so every attempt is late and is retried again. Bronson and co-authors call such a state a metastable failure: a trigger (here, the slow replica) moves the system into a bad state that a sustaining effect (here, retry amplification) keeps it in, after the trigger is gone; recovery needs the sustaining effect removed, for example by stopping retries or shedding load D11. Metastable states are hard to find by testing the trigger alone, because they appear only at high enough load: the same reason the game day's earlier evidence, gathered at 900 requests per second, said nothing about 1,500.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Designing an experiment

A written experiment has: the hypothesis, as a measurable statement about the steady-state SLI; the fault, as precisely as possible (which process, which resource, how much delay or loss, for how long); the blast radius (which share of traffic or which hosts, staging or production, which hours); the abort condition, evaluated by a machine on the same SLI, with the action it takes; the expected observations, written before the run; and the people who must know, including on-call D10. Grow the radius in steps: a single host in staging, then a small share of production traffic, then a whole replica at off-peak load, then at peak. Each step either disproves the hypothesis (a finding, and the experiment stops there until it is fixed) or earns the next step.

Which faults to inject

Crash faults (kill a process, power off a machine) test failover and are the easiest to inject and to survive. Gray faults test more: added latency on one replica or one link, a disk whose writes take seconds, partial packet loss, a full disk, a clock that jumps, a dependency that returns errors for a share of requests. Faults that combine with load (the fault at peak traffic) and faults in the control plane (the health checker, the configuration service, the certificate that expires) find the problems that took down E4 and the forensic lab's service. The fault list should come from incident reviews: every past outage is a candidate for an experiment that shows it cannot recur.

What the model leaves out

Listing 1 is a fluid model: request counts are real numbers, not individual requests; a replica is either below capacity (a queue with exponential waiting times) or over it (everything late); there is no backoff between retries, no jitter, no connection limits, no load shedding and no autoscaling; the health check in E1 removes a dead replica after exactly 3 seconds. Real systems have all of these, and the details matter: backoff spreads retries in time, load shedding keeps some requests fast while dropping others, and connection limits can make a healthy replica refuse work. The model is right about the direction and the mechanism of every effect in this chapter, and is not a prediction of any real service's numbers.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The principles attributed to D10 (steady state as output, vary real-world events, run in production, automate, minimise blast radius) and the definition and examples of metastable failures attributed to D11 are recalled, not read, in this build; neither paper is in the guide's F5 source registry, both are proposed in NOTES.md, and neither was opened. The defences attributed to the SRE book's chapter on cascading failures are also recalled. The idea that retry amplification multiplies across layers (three layers each retrying three times give up to 27 attempts) is arithmetic; whether a particular RPC library retries by default, and how, must be checked in its documentation for a recorded version.

How the hardware actually does it

Faults are injected at different layers. A process is killed with a signal; a machine is powered off through its management controller or hypervisor. Network faults are added in the kernel's network stack of a host: delay, loss, reordering or a block of specific traffic, applied to one interface or one destination, which is how a partition between two replicas is made without touching cables. Disk faults are added below the file system, by a block device layer that delays or fails requests, or above it, by a file system shim that the service writes through. A slow disk is particularly harmful to a storage service because a write-ahead log's fsync (F5-41) blocks the thread that issued it; if every worker thread writes the log, a slow disk can block every thread, and the node stops answering reads that never touch the disk, which is what happens in the forensic lab. Health checks that only open a TCP connection are answered by the operating system's network stack, which completes the connection handshake even when every application thread is blocked.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on real hardware: no process was killed, no packet delayed and no disk slowed in this build; all experiments ran on a model in one process. The descriptions of kernel network-emulation and block-layer delay facilities above are general and recalled; the tools, their names and their options must be taken from the documentation of the operating system version you use, and none was run here. The claim that a kernel completes TCP handshakes for a listening socket while the application is blocked describes the usual behaviour of a listen backlog and was not tested here.

Safety

Fault injection on a real network or machine can cut off your own access: a rule that drops traffic on an interface also drops your remote session, and a disk delay on the system disk can stall the whole machine. Run the course labs on your own machines or virtual machines, never on shared, school or employer systems without written permission; keep a console or out-of-band way back in; and give every injected fault an automatic expiry. Experiments on production systems need the owner's approval, on-call's knowledge and an automatic abort.

Worked example

Predict E2's success and amplification, and decide whether E4 will overload, before running anything.

  1. Each attempt goes to a random replica of three. In E2 every attempt at r2 is late (r2 adds 300 ms; the timeout is 250 ms), every attempt elsewhere succeeds (load stays well below capacity).
  2. A request needs a second attempt if its first went to r2: probability 1/3. A third attempt: 1/3 × 1/3 = 1/9. Attempts per request: 1 + 1/3 + 1/9 = 13/9 ≈ 1.44.
  3. A request fails if all three attempts went to r2: (1/3)³ = 1/27 ≈ 3.70 %. Success ≈ 96.30 %. Listing 1 prints 1.44 and 96.30 % R1.
  4. Load per replica at 900/s: 900 × 13/9 / 3 = 433 per second, 62 % of 700, as printed. The retries are spread evenly, so r2 gets its share of them too.
  5. E4 at 1,500/s: 1,500 × 13/9 / 3 ≈ 722 per second per replica, above 700. Prediction: overload. Once a replica is overloaded, its attempts are late as well, so the share of failed attempts rises from 1/3 towards 1, and amplification rises from 1.44 towards 3: the model printed 2.94 and 210 % load in the first window R1.
  6. Conclusion before the run: the retry policy of E2 has no margin at peak; an experiment that passed at 900/s proves nothing about 1,500/s.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A fire drill rehearses people. A chaos experiment rehearses machines and people together, and the machine part runs much faster: the service in E4 collapsed within a single 10-second window, long before any person could react.
    • 

  • Letters are resent by people who might notice the jam and wait. Clients retry by program, instantly and identically; the jam is made of thousands of identical decisions, which is why a single configuration change (max_attempts = 1) ended the forensic lab's outage.
    • 

  • In a drill, pretending is safe. Injected faults are real faults: users of a production service really see the failures, so every experiment spends real error budget (F5-42).
    • 

  • Leila sitting on letters is visible to anyone who knocks. A blocked server still completes TCP handshakes, so a simple health check sees nothing wrong.
    •

Summary


    

  • A chaos experiment states a steady-state hypothesis on the SLI, injects a realistic fault, limits the blast radius and aborts automatically.
    • 

  • A dead replica, detected fast, cost almost nothing (E1); a slow one with retries to any replica cost 3.7 % of requests (E2), and nothing with retries to another replica (E3).
    • 

  • Retries amplify load: 1 + 1/3 + 1/9 = 1.44 attempts per request at 900/s; at 1,500/s that pushed every replica past capacity and the service collapsed (E4: 2.94, 3.56 % success).
    • 

  • A retry budget stops the spread (E5: 1.10, 79 % load) but does not fix the fault.
    • 

  • Past capacity, retries can sustain an outage after the trigger is gone: a metastable failure; recovery needs the sustaining effect removed.
    • 

  • Evidence from one load and one fault does not transfer to another load or another fault.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


1. Why every request failed on kv-2. The plan delayed every fsync of kv-2's write-ahead log by 2 s. The metrics show kv-2's fsync p99 at 2,004 ms and all 16 of its 16 threads busy from 10:40:00 R2: writes block their worker threads for 2 s each, and with every thread blocked, reads wait in the queue behind them. A disk fault became a whole-node stall: a slow node, not a dead one.


2. Why kv-2 stayed in rotation. The health check is a TCP connect to port 7000 every 5 s, and it reports "no state changes" for the whole period R2. Opening a connection does not need an application thread, so a blocked server passes. A health check through the request path, with a deadline, would have removed kv-2 within seconds, turning this into the kill experiment.


3. The 10:40:30 window. Clients have max_attempts = 3, retry_target = any_replica and no budget. Every attempt at kv-2 is late. At 1,500 requests per second the first retries alone push each replica to about 722 per second (worked example), past 700; then every attempt at every replica is late and every request uses all 3 attempts: 4,500 attempts per second, 1,500 per replica, 214 % of 700 R2. Success 0 %.


4. Why the evidence did not predict it. The evidence was a different fault at a different load: "kill kv-2" at 09:00, about 900 requests per second. A killed node fails fast and is removed by the health check; a stalled node fails slowly and is not. And 1,500 per second is past what two replicas can carry (750 each) even with no retries, so even the kill experiment would have failed at this load (Check yourself 6). The hypothesis "the service survives the loss of one replica" was true only at 900/s and only for fast failures.


5. Why the outage outlasted the fault. At 10:44:20 the fault was removed; from 10:44:30 kv-2's fsync p99 is back to 3 ms, but load stays at 214 % and success at 0 % for another minute R2: the retries alone keep every replica past capacity, so every attempt is late and retried again. That is a metastable failure; the sustaining effect is retry amplification. The configuration push of max_attempts = 1 at 10:45:30 removed it: attempts fell to 1,510 per second, load to 72 %, success to 99.00 % and then 100 %.


6. Page and abort timing. The fast-burn page (F5-42's rule C) fired at 10:41:05, 65 s after the fault. With success near 0 %, the 1-hour window's error ratio passes 14.4 × 0.1 % = 1.44 % after 1.44 % of an hour, about 52 s of total failure, so the page was as fast as its long window allows. The abort was manual and late: the plan said "the operator stops the experiment if the dashboard looks bad"; the dashboard refreshed once a minute and still showed 10:39 at 10:40:50; the operator believed only kv-2 was affected (10:42:20), and stopped at 10:44:18, more than four minutes after success fell to about 1 % R2. An automatic abort on success below 95 % in a 10 s window would have stopped it within about 10 s, before the overload became self-sustaining or soon after. Cost: the window at 10:40:00 (1.19 % success) and ten windows at 0 %, each of 30 s at 1,500 requests per second: 1,500 × 30 × (0.988 + 10) ≈ 494,000 failed requests; with the traffic and SLO of F5-42's model (budget about 86,400 failed requests a month), that is more than five months' error budget in six minutes.


7. Changes. Clients: a retry budget (about 10 % of requests), retries to a different replica, exponential backoff with jitter, and deadlines propagated so no layer retries a request whose caller has given up. Health checks: through the request path with a short deadline, plus outlier ejection on latency. Capacity: enough replicas that losing one at peak leaves headroom (four or more at 1,500 requests per second with 700 per replica). Process: a written hypothesis that names the fault and the load; start in staging, then a small share of production traffic, off-peak; an automatic abort on the SLI; the on-call engineer briefed and in the channel; a pre-agreed recovery action for "the fault is gone and the service is still down" (stop retries, shed load).


How the evidence was produced. gameday.cpp runs a compact copy of Listing 1's model at 1,500 requests per second with E2's client policy, with kv-2 completely late from 10:40:00 (the stall blocks all its threads), the fault removed at 10:44:20 and max_attempts = 1 from 10:45:30, and prints the metrics it computes. The plan, configuration, health checks, pager and chat lines are scripted to match the computed metrics. Output unedited.
