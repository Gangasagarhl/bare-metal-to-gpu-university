Story hook

The club's letters are slow this month, and everyone has a theory. Arjun thinks the post office in his town is the problem. Nia thinks it is Sofia, who answers letters only on weekends. Sofia thinks the letters are fine and people are impatient.

Dmitri settles it with three kinds of records. Every friend keeps a diary: one line for each letter received or sent, with the date and a few details. On the fridge, each friend keeps a tally sheet: how many letters came in this week, how many were answered, and how many took longer than three days. And on a few letters chosen at random, Dmitri clips a tracking slip that every post office and every friend stamps with the time the letter arrived and left.

The tally sheets show that slow letters started on a Tuesday, in all towns at once. The diaries show that on that Tuesday nobody wrote a single line for a whole afternoon in Arjun's house. And the tracking slips show letters waiting in Arjun's house, not in any post office. Arjun had spent the afternoon reorganising his whole filing cabinet, and nothing else in his house moved until he finished. Three kinds of evidence, each useless alone, together tell one story. This chapter builds all three into a service, and then uses them to solve exactly this kind of mystery.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what logs, metrics and traces each record, what each costs, and which questions each can answer.
  • Instrument a replicated key-value service with structured logs, counters, a latency histogram and spans, and read the result.
  • Compute latency percentiles exactly and from histogram buckets, and explain why averages and averaged percentiles mislead.
  • Explain trace context, sampling and label cardinality, and choose a sampling policy that keeps the traces you need.
  • Diagnose a latency spike by correlating metrics, traces and logs, and tell a process pause from a disk stall.
Jargon box
Observability
Simple words: being able to find out what is happening inside a running system from the records it produces. Analogy: the club's diaries, tally sheets and tracking slips together. Precise: the property of a system that its internal state and behaviour can be inferred from its external outputs (telemetry such as logs, metrics and traces), including for questions that were not anticipated when it was built; monitoring is the practice of collecting and acting on such telemetry. D5
Structured log
Simple words: a diary line written as named fields, so a program can read it. Analogy: each diary line has the same boxes: date, letter, from whom, how long. Precise: a timestamped record of a discrete event emitted by a program, with machine-parseable key-value fields (for example level, node, trace, dur_ms) rather than free text, so that events can be filtered, joined and counted. D5
Metric (counter, gauge, histogram)
Simple words: numbers sampled over time: a count that only grows, a value that goes up and down, or counts per range of values. Analogy: the tally sheet on the fridge. Precise: a numeric time series identified by a name and labels; a counter is monotonically increasing (rates are computed from its differences), a gauge is a current value, and a histogram counts observations into fixed buckets so that distributions can be aggregated across instances and time. D5
Trace and span
Simple words: a trace is the story of one request through all the computers it visited; each step is a span. Analogy: the tracking slip stamped at every post office and every house. Precise: a trace is a tree of spans sharing a trace identifier; a span records one timed operation (name, start, duration, node) with its own identifier and its parent's identifier, so that the causal structure and timing of a request can be reconstructed across processes. D7
Trace context propagation
Simple words: passing the tracking number along with the request. Analogy: the slip travels clipped to the letter. Precise: carrying the trace identifier and the current span identifier in every request a service sends (for example in RPC metadata), so that the receiver's spans become children of the sender's span. D7
Sampling (head-based, tail-based)
Simple words: keeping only some traces, decided either at the start or at the end of a request. Analogy: clipping slips to every tenth letter, or keeping the slips only of letters that turned out slow. Precise: recording a subset of traces to bound cost; head-based sampling decides when the root span starts (cheap, but blind to the outcome), tail-based sampling buffers spans and decides after the request completes (can keep all slow or failed requests, at the cost of buffering). D7
Percentile (p50, p99)
Simple words: the value below which a given share of measurements falls; p99 is "99 of 100 requests were at least this fast". Analogy: line up all letters by delay; the one at position 99 of every 100 is the p99 letter. Precise: the q-quantile of a distribution: the smallest value v such that a fraction q of observations is ≤ v; high percentiles describe the tail that averages hide. D5 D9
Cardinality (of labels)
Simple words: how many different time series a metric turns into. Analogy: one tally column per town is fine; one column per letter-writer's name fills the fridge. Precise: the number of distinct label-value combinations of a metric; each combination is a separate time series that must be stored and queried, so labels with many values (user ids, keys) multiply storage and query cost. R1
Stop-the-world pause
Simple words: a moment when a whole program stops doing its normal work. Analogy: Arjun reorganising his filing cabinet while nothing else in his house moves. Precise: an interval during which all application threads of a process are suspended, for example by a garbage collector or by a maintenance task holding a global lock; every operation in progress in that process is delayed by the length of the pause. D9

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Three kinds of records explain a slow month of letters. Mapping: each friend's diary = logs (one line per event, with details); the tally sheet on the fridge = metrics (counts per week, cheap to keep, no names); the tracking slip clipped to a few letters = a trace (one letter's whole journey, stamped at every stop); the slip's number copied onto every stamp = the trace id; stamping only every tenth letter = sampling; the afternoon Arjun's house stood still = a stop-the-world pause.

Tally sheets are what you look at first: they are small, and they tell you that something changed and when. Tracking slips tell you where one slow letter spent its time. Diaries tell you what happened at a place and moment, in words. You usually need all three, in that order: tally sheet to notice, slip to locate, diary to explain.

Each record also has a cost. Diaries of every letter take a lot of paper. Tally sheets are cheap but cannot tell you which letter was slow. Slips on every letter would slow the post down, so you clip them on only some letters, and then you must choose well which ones.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why distributed systems need telemetry

On one machine you can attach a debugger. A request to the DS302 service touches a gateway, a leader and two followers, each with its own clock, its own threads and its own failures, and the problem you are chasing may have ended before you look. The SRE book frames monitoring around two questions, what is broken and why, and distinguishes symptoms (what users experience: errors, latency) from causes (a full disk, a paused process) D5. It proposes four "golden signals" for a user-facing system: latency, traffic, errors and saturation D5. Listing 1's metrics are the first three; saturation (how full the most constrained resource is) appears in the forensic lab as CPU utilisation and busy threads.

Logs

A log line records one event with its context. Free text ("put of k348 took a while") is readable by people and useless to programs; a structured line with named fields can be filtered by node, joined with traces by trace id and aggregated by dur_ms D5. Listing 1 logs the first three requests and every request slower than 30 ms (lines 139–145), which is a policy, not an accident: logging every request at 400 requests per second costs storage and I/O, so services log errors and slow requests in detail, sample the rest, and count everything in metrics. Section 1 of the output has nine lines: three ordinary gets and six slow puts, each with the trace id that leads to its trace R1.

Metrics: counters and histograms

A counter counts events since the process started; a monitoring system samples it periodically and computes rates from the differences between samples D5. Listing 1 counts requests by operation: 1,586 gets and 414 puts R1. For latency, the listing uses a histogram with fixed buckets (upper bounds 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 ms, and a last bucket for everything above); each request increments one bucket, and the output prints them cumulatively, so the line for le="5" is the number of requests that took at most 5 ms (1,916 of 2,000) R1. Fixed buckets have a property that matters across machines: histograms from many instances can be added bucket by bucket, and percentiles computed from the sum; percentiles computed per instance cannot be averaged into a correct overall percentile.

The mean, 1.56 ms, describes no actual request: most gets take about half a millisecond and puts several, and the mean sits between them. The tail is a hundred times the median. The SRE book argues for percentiles rather than means for exactly this reason D5, and "The Tail at Scale" shows why the tail matters more as systems grow: a request that fans out to many servers waits for the slowest of them, so a rare slow response on each server becomes a common slow response for the whole request D9. The bucket estimates are within the right bucket but not exact: the histogram knows only how many values fell between 10 and 20 ms, not where, so the estimate interpolates linearly (the worked example does it by hand). Bucket boundaries should be placed where decisions are made: if the objective of F5-42 is "99 % under 20 ms", a boundary at 20 ms makes that question exact.

Traces

Dapper describes a trace as a tree of spans: every span has a trace id shared by the whole request, its own span id and its parent's span id, plus a start time, a duration and annotations D7. The ids travel in every request a service sends, so a downstream span can name its parent; this propagation is the one change every component must make, which Dapper achieved by instrumenting shared RPC and threading libraries rather than each application D7. Listing 1 keeps spans in a Tracer (lines 34–44): begin returns a span id that children use as their parent, end records the duration. Section 2 prints the slowest request's tree: the leader's fsync took 73.72 ms of the 74.01 ms total, while the replication to b and c finished within 4 ms R1. The log line for the same request said only "put done … dur_ms=74.01"; the metrics said only that one request fell in the 50–100 ms bucket. Only the trace says where.

Sampling and cardinality

Tracing every request costs CPU, memory and storage in every process; Dapper samples, and describes adaptive sampling rates D7. Section 5 of the output shows the trap of head-based sampling: keeping 1 trace in 10, decided when the request starts, keeps 200 traces, but only 3 of the 20 slowest requests R1. The decision was made before anyone knew which requests would be slow. Tail-based sampling (decide when the request ends; keep all slow and failed requests and a sample of the rest) keeps exactly the evidence you need, at the cost of buffering every span until the decision. The forensic lab's evidence pack says "all requests slower than 500 ms are kept": a tail-based policy.

Section 6 makes the matching point for metrics: a request counter labelled by operation is 2 time series; labelled by operation and key it could be up to 1,000 here, and millions in a real store R1. Per-key detail belongs in logs and traces; metrics carry low-cardinality labels (operation, status, node).

The SRE book also distinguishes white-box monitoring (metrics exposed by the system's internals) from black-box monitoring (testing externally visible behaviour as a user would) D5. The gateway's latency in the forensic lab is close to black-box: it is what users experienced. The leader's fsync timer is white-box: it explains why.

Time in telemetry

Every log line and span carries a timestamp from the clock of the machine that wrote it, and those clocks differ (F5-10). Spans of one trace are ordered by their parent links, not by comparing timestamps from different machines; a child that appears to start before its parent is clock skew, not causality violation. Durations measured on one machine (end minus start, from a monotonic clock) are reliable; differences between timestamps of two machines are not. When you correlate logs from several nodes, align them on events that both sides record (a heartbeat sent and received) before trusting sub-second ordering. The forensic lab's scenario states that clocks are synchronised so that you can focus on the method; in a real incident, check that first.

Overhead and the observer effect

Telemetry is code on the hot path. Taking a timestamp, formatting a log line, incrementing a shared counter and allocating a span all cost time, and a contended counter or a synchronous log write can become the bottleneck it was meant to reveal. Dapper reports designing for low overhead and measuring it D7. The engineering rules that follow: increment per-thread counters and sum them when sampled; write logs asynchronously with a bounded buffer and count what you drop; sample traces; never block a request on telemetry export. And measure the overhead of your instrumentation like any other change, with the method of F5-21: same workload, with and without, several runs.

Pauses, stalls and their signatures

Two causes of latency spikes recur often enough to have recognisable signatures. A stop-the-world pause (a garbage collector, or a maintenance task holding a global lock, such as the index compaction of the forensic lab) delays every operation in the process, including operations that never touch the disk, and stops the process's own heartbeats and log output; it ends everywhere at the same instant D9. A disk stall delays only operations that wait for the device (fsync, cache-miss reads); CPU-only work in the same process continues, logs keep flowing, and the device's own latency metric rises. "The Tail at Scale" lists background activities such as garbage collection and log compaction among the causes of latency variability in shared systems D9. Telling the two apart is the core of the forensic lab: look at a span that cannot touch the disk, and at the device metric that cannot see a process pause.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Real telemetry systems have their own data models and wire formats: the OpenTelemetry project specifies APIs, a data model for traces, metrics and logs, and context propagation formats; monitoring systems have their own exposition formats and query languages, and their own definitions of histogram and summary types. None of these documents was opened in this build. Listing 1's text format (name{label="value"} number) and its bucket naming were chosen to look familiar, not copied from a specification: do not assume any tool accepts them. Field names, header names for context propagation and API calls must be taken from the chosen tool's documentation for a recorded version.

How the hardware actually does it

A timestamp is a read of a hardware counter through the operating system (OS303); on Linux, the monotonic clock is the right source for durations and the real-time clock for wall time, and the cost of reading them depends on the platform's clock source. The fsync that dominates Figure 2 is a request to the storage device to make data durable, whose latency depends on the device and its write cache (HW205); the device's own latency, as the kernel sees it, is measured below the process and therefore keeps reporting normally while the process is paused, which is exactly the asymmetry the forensic lab exploits. A stop-the-world pause is the opposite case: the CPU is busy (often one core fully busy compacting or collecting), but the application's threads are not running. On Linux, per-device latency and per-thread CPU time are available from kernel statistics; the tools and their output formats are not covered in this build.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on real hardware: all latencies in this chapter come from an exercise model inside one process; no disk was synced and no network crossed. The forensic evidence is generated, not captured. To capture the same signals on a real service you need several processes (or machines), a real disk, and a monitoring pipeline, none of which this build ran. The course lab asks you to do that on your own machine and to label every number as your measurement (AH-23).

Worked example

Estimate p99 from the histogram of section 3 by hand, and explain the difference from the exact value.

  1. There are n = 2,000 observations. The p99 rank is 0.99 × 2,000 = 1,980: we need the value below which 1,980 requests fall.
  2. Cumulative counts from the output: le="10" 1,967 and le="20" 1,994 R1. Rank 1,980 is in the bucket (10, 20]. The bucket holds 1,994 − 1,967 = 27 requests.
  3. The rank is 1,980 − 1,967 = 13 requests into the bucket. Assuming values spread evenly across the bucket: 10 + (20 − 10) × 13 / 27 = 10 + 4.81 = 14.81 ms. The program's estimate is 14.81 ms, the same arithmetic (quantile, lines 64–78).
  4. The exact p99 is 11.39 ms R1. The 27 values in the bucket are not spread evenly: most are disk reads of gets, which the model draws between 4 and 12 ms, so they crowd the bottom of the bucket. Linear interpolation assumes they do not.
  5. Conclusion: a bucket estimate is only as precise as the bucket is narrow. The error is bounded by the bucket width (10 ms here); a boundary at 12 ms would make this p99 nearly exact. Put boundaries where your objectives and your distribution's shape are.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A friend writes her diary after the letter is handled, calmly. A service writes logs while handling requests, and the writing itself takes time and can fail or be dropped under load: the record changes what it records.
    • 

  • A tracking slip's stamps come from post-office clocks that someone set. Spans come from clocks on different machines that disagree; only parent links, not timestamps, give the order across machines.
    • 

  • Tally sheets on a fridge are read by people when they feel like it. Metrics are read by machines every few seconds and drive alerts: their names, labels and buckets are an interface that other people's dashboards and alerts depend on.
    • 

  • When Arjun stops to reorganise, someone could knock on his door and notice. A paused process cannot even report that it is paused; you see it only from outside (missing heartbeats, a gap in its logs, its peers' complaints).
    •

Expected observations. On one machine, fsync latency and scheduling will dominate the tail; the slowest traces will show either a slow fsync or a request waiting behind another one on the leader. Head-based sampling will miss most of the slowest requests.


Verification. For every trace, the spans form one tree (exactly one root, every parent exists); the sum of counter values equals the number of requests your client sent; your exact p99 is reproducible from the span file.


Troubleshooting.


    

  • Follower spans have no parent → the span id was not put into the AppendEntries message, or a batching path dropped it. One AppendEntries carries entries of several requests: give its span a list of trace ids, or one span per entry.
    • 

  • Durations are negative → you subtracted timestamps from two processes' wall clocks; measure durations with a monotonic clock in the process that owns the span.
    • 

  • The service becomes much slower with telemetry on → synchronous logging or a shared counter under a lock; buffer logs, use per-thread counters.
    • 



Extension. Implement tail-based sampling: buffer the spans of each trace until its root ends, keep all traces slower than your p99 and 1 % of the rest, and show that every slow request's trace is kept.

Method hints. Start from metrics to find the window; then pick traces in that window and ask of each span "could this operation be slowed by the disk?"; then read node a's log for gaps and for anything that started just before the spike. A disk stall and a process pause predict different things for CPU-only spans, for the device's await metric, and for the process's own heartbeats.

Summary


    

  • Logs record events in detail, metrics count everything cheaply over time, traces show where one request's time went across machines.
    • 

  • Structured logs with trace ids join the three signals together.
    • 

  • Latency is a distribution: the lab's mean was 1.56 ms, its p99 11.39 ms and its p99.9 70.54 ms. Watch percentiles, computed from aggregated histograms.
    • 

  • Histogram percentiles are estimates bounded by the bucket width; put boundaries where decisions are.
    • 

  • A trace is a tree of spans linked by parent ids; context must be propagated through every hop.
    • 

  • Head-based sampling misses the slow requests you most need (3 of 20 kept); tail-based sampling keeps them.
    • 

  • Labels with many values belong in logs and traces, not metrics.
    • 

  • A process pause slows everything in the process and silences it; a disk stall slows only disk operations. Look at a span that cannot touch the disk.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Logs: what happened at one place and moment (good); how often something happens across the fleet (expensive and incomplete if sampled). Metrics: whether and since when something changed, for all requests (good); which request or key (cannot, by design). Traces: where one request spent its time across services (good); how many requests are affected (only a sample is kept).
    2. 

  2. A histogram is a vector of counts; counts add, so the sum of ten instances' histograms is the histogram of all requests, and its percentile is the fleet's. A percentile is a single order statistic; the average of ten instances' p99 values weights each instance equally regardless of its traffic and ignores how the tails combine: an instance with 1 % of the traffic but terrible latency barely moves the fleet's p99, yet moves the average of p99s by a tenth of its value.
    3. 

  3. Good answers say: the decision was made at the start of each request, independently of how slow it would be, so it kept about one in ten of the slowest requests too (3 of 20 here, by chance); tail-based sampling decides after the request ends, when its latency and status are known, so it can keep every slow or failed request.
    4. 

  4. le="5" minus le="2": 1,916 − 1,505 = 411 requests.
    5. 

  5. 1 − 0.9950 ≈ 1 − 0.605 = 0.395: about 39.5 % of the fanned-out requests wait more than 10 ms, although each server is slow only 1 % of the time.

  6. p99.9 changes most: the 50–100 ms tail came from slow fsyncs (the leader's and, for the majority, both followers' at once is rare), so without slow leader fsyncs p99.9 falls to the disk-read range of gets. p50 does not change (gets from memory), and p99 changes little because it is set by the 5 % of gets that read from disk. (A scratch run during the build with the leader's slow-fsync probability at 0 % confirmed p50 unchanged and p99.9 falling below 20 ms; it is not in the lab logs.)
    7. 

  7. The number of time series grows with the number of keys (up to 1,000 here; millions in a real store), multiplying memory, storage and query time, and possibly overloading the monitoring system. Hot-key detection belongs in logs or traces (count keys in sampled requests) or in a dedicated top-k structure inside the service, exported as a small fixed-size report.
    8. 

  8. A memory read cannot be slowed by the disk; if it took 1.4 s, the process itself did not run (a pause, a lock held by something else, CPU starvation). A slow fsync alone is consistent with both a disk stall and a pause.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Only the window starting 14:02:00 is abnormal: 181 errors (504), p99 1000 ms (the timeout cap), max 1000 ms, CPU utilisation 0.35 instead of 0.31, and a.index_entries drops from 1,019,434 to 612,331 R2. Request rate is unchanged (401/s), and a.disk_await_p99_ms in that window is 3.00, lower than in several normal windows (which show 30–55 ms from occasional slow fsyncs).


Hypotheses. (1) The 14:01:30 config reload: ruled out, it is 37 seconds before the spike, the windows 14:01:30–14:01:50 are normal, and it touched only the gateway's rate-limit table. (2) A disk stall on node a: ruled out, the device-level await is normal in the window; trace 51000 is a store.read(memory) of 1,412.86 ms, an operation that never touches the disk; trace 50999's slow span is encode, CPU-only; and followers b and c report normal fsync p99 (2.94 ms, 2.97 ms). (3) A network problem: ruled out, trace 50998's replicate spans to b and c completed in 3.90 ms and 2.11 ms during the spike; the slowness is inside node a. (4) A pause of node a's whole process: confirmed: every span on node a that overlaps 14:02:07.400–14:02:08.820 is stretched by about 1.42 s, whatever it does (fsync, encode, memory read); node a's log has no line between 14:02:07.390 and 14:02:08.820; node a itself reports "1420 ms since last heartbeat sent"; followers b and c report "no heartbeat from leader a for 1000 ms" at 14:02:08.403 and 14:02:08.401; and the last gateway timeout is at 14:02:08.827, right after the pause ended.


What node a was doing. Its log says it started "compaction of the in-memory key index (entries 1019434)" at 14:02:07.390 and finished at 14:02:08.820 with 612,331 entries; the index gauge drop and the CPU rise in the 14:02:00 window match. The compaction held the process (a global lock over the index, in the scenario): a garbage-collection-like stop-the-world pause.


Why trace 50998 looks like a disk stall. Its fsync was issued at +0.30 ms, just before the pause; the device finished it in milliseconds, but the thread that waited for it could not run until the pause ended, so the span records 1,420.91 ms. A span measures how long the thread waited, not how long the device took. This is why one trace is not enough, and why a span that cannot touch the disk (trace 51000) decides between the hypotheses.


Fix and verification. Make the compaction incremental (compact in small slices, releasing the lock between slices) or build the compacted index aside and swap it in with a short critical section; schedule it with a limit on pause length; or trigger it before the index is large. Verify by replaying the same load with the new compaction and checking that no span on node a exceeds a bound (for example 50 ms) during compaction, and that followers log no missed heartbeats. Note also that the pause came within 500 ms of the election timeout (1500 ms): a slightly longer pause would have caused a leader election, turning 1.4 s of latency into an availability incident.


Monitoring change. Export a "longest stop of the process's request threads" metric (or a compaction-duration histogram) and log the duration at the end of compaction; alert on followers' missed-heartbeat warnings; and keep the tail-based sampler, which is what preserved trace 51000.


How the evidence was produced. spike.cpp runs the latency model of Listing 1 for 300 simulated seconds at about 400 requests per second, with node a's process paused from 127,400 ms to 128,820 ms after 14:00:00 (constants kP0, kP1): every operation on node a that would run in the pause is delayed to its end. Disk device times are drawn as usual and reported unchanged. The log lines of nodes a, b and c and the gateway are written by the generator from the same constants. Output unedited.
