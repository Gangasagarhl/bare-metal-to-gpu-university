Story hook

The club made itself a promise: "we answer 999 of every 1,000 letters within a week". Sofia pointed out what that also means: in a month of 30,000 letters, up to 30 may be late without breaking the promise. The club wrote "30" on a card and called it the allowance. Every late letter takes one from the allowance.

At first Kofi wanted a bell that rings whenever any letter is late. It rang twice a day, usually for a letter that turned up the next morning, and within a week everyone ignored it. Then the club tried the opposite: ring the bell only if more than 1 in 100 letters are late in a single day. It never rang. At the end of the month the allowance was gone: for twelve days, one letter in four hundred had been late, quietly, every day.

So Mei proposed a different bell: ring when the allowance is being used up fast enough that it will run out before the month ends, and ring loudly if it will run out within days. This chapter turns the club's promise into numbers, replays a month of traffic against several bells, and finds the one that rings when it matters and stays quiet when it does not.

What you will be able to do

  • Define SLIs, SLOs and SLAs for a service, with the measurement point, the window and the target stated.
  • Compute an error budget and how much of it an incident consumes, and decide what the remaining budget allows.
  • Write alert rules on error-budget burn rate over multiple windows, derive their thresholds, and compare them with threshold alerts on a replayed month.
  • Build a static HTML SLO report with an inline SVG chart and check it mechanically.
  • Diagnose why an error budget was exhausted without a page, and write the alert that would have caught it.
Jargon box
SLI (service-level indicator)
Simple words: a number that measures how well the service is doing for its users. Analogy: the share of letters answered within a week. Precise: a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service provided, usually a ratio of good events to valid events (for example requests answered without a server error, or requests answered within 100 ms), with the point of measurement stated. D5
SLO (service-level objective)
Simple words: the target for an SLI over a period. Analogy: "999 of every 1,000 letters within a week, each month". Precise: a target value or range for an SLI measured over a stated time window, for example 99.9 % of requests successful over 30 days. D5
SLA (service-level agreement)
Simple words: a promise to customers with consequences if it is broken. Analogy: the club promising members a refund of their stamps if it misses the target. Precise: an explicit or implicit contract with users that includes consequences (often financial) of meeting or missing the SLOs it contains; internal SLOs are normally stricter than any SLA. D5
Error budget
Simple words: how many failures the target still allows. Analogy: the allowance card: 30 late letters this month. Precise: one minus the SLO, applied to the window: the number (or fraction) of bad events the service may have in the window while still meeting its SLO; spending it on releases, experiments and incidents is a decision the SLO makes explicit. D5
Burn rate
Simple words: how fast the allowance is being used compared with spending it evenly. Analogy: using four letters of the allowance in a day when one a day would last exactly the month is a burn rate of four. Precise: the observed bad-event ratio over a window divided by the ratio the SLO allows (1 − SLO); a burn rate of 1 sustained for the whole SLO window consumes exactly the budget, a burn rate of b consumes it in 1/b of the window. D8
Multiwindow burn-rate alert
Simple words: an alert that checks a long and a short window together, so it fires for real trouble and stops soon after it ends. Analogy: ring the bell if the allowance shrank fast over the last day and is still shrinking over the last hour. Precise: an alert condition that requires the burn rate to exceed a threshold over a long window (for significance) and over a short window (so the alert resets quickly after recovery); several such pairs with different thresholds separate urgent pages from tickets. D8
Page and ticket
Simple words: a page wakes someone now; a ticket is a task for working hours. Analogy: ringing the bell at night versus writing a note on the fridge. Precise: the two urgencies of alert notifications: a page requires immediate human action, a ticket requires action within days; an alert that needs no human action should be neither. D5

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

The club's promise and its allowance. Mapping: "share of letters answered within a week" = the SLI; "999 of 1,000, each month" = the SLO; a refund of stamps if the club misses it = an SLA; the allowance card (30 late letters a month) = the error budget; using the allowance faster than one-thirtieth a day = a burn rate above 1; the bell = an alert; ringing the bell at night = a page; a note on the fridge = a ticket.

A promise of "always" cannot be kept, and trying makes everything slow and expensive. A promise of "999 in 1,000" can be kept, and it also tells you how much room you have: if the month has gone perfectly, you can afford to try something risky; if the allowance is nearly gone, you slow down and fix things first.

A good bell rings when the promise is in danger, not for every late letter, and not only when it is already too late. The trick is to watch how fast the allowance is shrinking. Very fast shrinking needs someone now. Slow but steady shrinking needs someone this week. Normal shrinking needs nobody.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Choosing SLIs

An SLI should measure what users experience, so it is usually a ratio: good events over valid events, for example "requests answered without a server error" or "requests answered within 100 ms" D5. Three decisions make it precise: what counts as good (status codes, latency threshold, correctness of the answer), which events are valid (exclude health checks, malformed requests from clients, load tests) and where it is measured (the server's own counters, the load balancer, the client, or a synthetic prober), because each point sees different failures D5. The forensic lab's SLI is measured at the gateway; a failure the gateway never sees (a client that cannot reach the gateway at all) is invisible to it. The F5-41 metrics are the raw material: a request counter by status gives the availability SLI, a latency histogram with a boundary at the threshold gives the latency SLI.

SLOs and the error budget

An SLO adds a target and a window: "99.9 % of requests succeed over a rolling 30 days" D5. The SRE book argues that 100 % is the wrong target for almost everything: users cannot tell the difference beyond some point (their own networks and devices fail more often), and each additional nine costs much more D5. The error budget is the complement: with 99.9 %, 0.1 % of valid requests may fail D5. The book describes using the budget as a shared decision rule between development and operations: while budget remains, releases proceed; when it is exhausted, releases slow down or stop in favour of reliability work D5.

Listing 2 makes this concrete for one model month R2: 86,393,796 requests, 70,852 failed, SLI 99.9180 %. The budget is 0.1 % of the requests, 86,394 failures, and 82.0 % of it was used: the SLO was met. The incidents' shares: the 20-minute outage of half the backends used 28.4 %, the 4-minute total outage at night 2.5 % (few requests at 02:00), and the three-day slow burn at 0.3 % errors 30.1 %. The rest, about 21 %, was the background error rate of 0.02 %, which alone uses a fifth of the budget every month. That last number deserves a decision of its own: an SLO that the service's normal behaviour already consumes a fifth of leaves little room for incidents and releases.

Alerting: what a page is for

The SRE book's guidance on alerting is that pages should be about symptoms users experience, should be urgent, actionable, and require human intelligence; anything else is a ticket, a dashboard, or nothing D5. An alert has four properties to judge it by: precision (what share of its alerts were real), recall (what share of real problems it caught), detection time and reset time D8. Listing 2 replays five rules over the month, minute by minute, and counts alerts, "noise" (alerts that started outside every incident and the hour after it), and minutes from each incident's start to the first alert R2:

Rule A, "page whenever the error ratio over five minutes exceeds what the SLO allows", catches everything instantly and pages 39 times for nothing: at night, with few requests, a handful of background failures in five minutes is enough to cross 0.1 %. That is Kofi's bell. Rule B, a high fixed threshold over an hour, is quiet and fast for big outages, and never sees the slow burn that used 30 % of the budget: Sofia's bell. Rules C, D and E are the burn-rate family. C and D page fast for outages that spend budget fast; E is a ticket that fires about 19 hours (1,161 minutes) into the slow burn, by which time it has used about 8 % of the budget, roughly two-thirds of a day at 10 % per day (Figure 1). Together C (or D) and E cover all three incidents with no noise in this month.

Burn rate, defined by arithmetic

If the SLO allows an error ratio of ε = 1 − SLO, and over some window the observed ratio is r, the burn rate is b = r / ε D8. A burn rate of 1 sustained for the whole 30-day window spends exactly the budget; a burn rate of b spends it in 30/b days. During the lab's slow burn, r = 0.3 % and ε = 0.1 %, so b = 3: that pace would empty a full budget in 10 days. During the outage of half the backends, r = 50 %, b = 500: a full budget in under an hour and a half. An alert on burn rate therefore asks the right question directly: "at this pace, how soon is the promise broken?" The worked example turns "page if 2 % of the monthly budget is spent within an hour" into the threshold 14.4.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Designing burn-rate alerts

A burn-rate page has two parameters: the fraction of the budget whose loss is worth waking someone (f) and the window over which that loss is measured (w). The threshold follows: spending f of the budget in w at burn rate b means b × w / T = f, so b = f × T / w, with T the SLO window. Short windows with high thresholds catch fast burns quickly; long windows with low thresholds catch slow burns that would otherwise exhaust the budget unseen. The second, shorter window in each rule exists for reset: a rule on the 1-hour window alone keeps firing for up to an hour after a 5-minute outage ends, because the outage stays inside the window; requiring the 5-minute window too makes the alert stop within minutes of recovery D8. Low-traffic services need care: when a window holds few requests, one failure is a large ratio, which is what made rule A noisy at night; options include synthetic traffic, longer minimum windows, or alerting on counts of failures as well as ratios.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The specific multiwindow, multi-burn-rate recommendation used for rules C, D and E (page at 2 % of a 30-day budget in 1 hour with a 5-minute short window; page at 5 % in 6 hours with a 30-minute short window; ticket at 10 % in 3 days with a 6-hour short window; short windows one-twelfth of the long ones) is recalled from "The Site Reliability Workbook", chapter on alerting on SLOs. It was not opened in this build. The thresholds 14.4, 6 and 1 are derived by arithmetic in the worked example from those fractions and windows, so the arithmetic is verified but the choice of fractions and windows is not. Check D8 before quoting the recommendation as the Workbook's.

Latency SLOs

A latency SLO is an availability-style ratio with a different definition of "good": "99 % of requests complete within 100 ms over 30 days" counts requests faster than 100 ms as good D5. Written this way, it uses the same error-budget and burn-rate machinery, and it needs a histogram with a bucket boundary exactly at 100 ms (F5-41); a percentile computed from buckets that straddle 100 ms would make the SLI an estimate. Two common mistakes: setting a latency SLO on the mean (which F5-41 showed describes no actual request), and setting it on the p99 of each minute averaged over the month (which weights quiet minutes like busy ones).

Error-budget policy

An SLO without a policy is a number on a dashboard. The SRE book's position is that the budget is a tool for a joint decision: product and reliability work compete for the same engineering time, and the budget makes the trade-off explicit and agreed in advance D5. A written policy says what happens at given levels of remaining budget (for example: releases continue; releases need extra review; only reliability fixes ship), who decides, and what is excluded (incidents caused by a dependency that has its own SLO, planned maintenance announced in advance). Writing it before the first incident is what keeps the conversation about engineering rather than blame.

From SLO to SLA

External agreements should be looser than the internal objective, so that the team acts on the SLO long before the SLA is at risk D5. An SLA's window and measurement point are usually fixed by contract (calendar months, measured by the provider or by a third party), which can differ from the rolling window used internally; the report should show both when they differ. The legal and financial terms of SLAs are outside this course.

How the hardware actually does it

An SLI is computed from counters that live in many processes on many machines; each machine's counter is read periodically, the readings are summed, and rates are computed from differences between readings. Counters reset when a process restarts, so the rate computation must detect resets; readings arrive late or not at all when a machine is in trouble, which is exactly when they matter, so the SLI pipeline must decide whether a missing reading means "no requests" or "unknown". The most robust SLIs are measured at a point that sees all requests and survives the failure of the servers behind it: a load balancer or gateway, as in the forensic lab. Storage for a month of minute-level data per SLI is small (43,200 points per series), which is why the lab can replay a month in a fraction of a second.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on real hardware: the month of request counts is generated by slo_model.hpp from exercise rates (2,000 ± 1,500 requests per minute on a daily cycle, 0.02 % background failures, scripted incidents); no production service was measured, and no monitoring system evaluated the rules. Real alert evaluation adds scrape intervals, delays and missing data that the replay ignores, so real detection times are longer than the replay's. The HTML report was checked by a parser, not opened in a browser, in this build.

Worked example

Derive the thresholds of rules C, D and E from "fraction of budget in a window", and check rule E's detection time for the slow burn.

  1. SLO window T = 30 days = 720 hours. Spending fraction f of the budget within window w at burn rate b means b × w / T = f, so b = f × T / w.
  2. Rule C: f = 2 %, w = 1 hour: b = 0.02 × 720 / 1 = 14.4.
  3. Rule D: f = 5 %, w = 6 hours: b = 0.05 × 720 / 6 = 6.
  4. Rule E: f = 10 %, w = 3 days = 72 hours: b = 0.10 × 720 / 72 = 1.
  5. Slow burn: error ratio 0.3 % starting at day 18, 00:00, after a background of 0.02 %. Rule E needs the 3-day ratio above 0.1 %. With roughly equal traffic per minute, after t hours of the burn the 3-day window holds t hours at 0.3 % and (72 − t) hours at 0.02 %: (0.3 t + 0.02 (72 − t)) / 72 > 0.1 gives 0.28 t > 5.76, so t > 20.6 hours.
  6. The replay fired after 1,161 minutes = 19.4 hours R2. The difference comes from traffic: the burn starts at midnight, so its first hours carry little traffic, but the hours of the afternoon carry most of it; weighted by requests, the burn's share of the window grows faster than its share of the hours. The estimate assumed equal traffic and is within two hours.
  7. Budget spent by then: the slow burn uses about 10 % of the budget per day (Figure 1), so about 19.4 / 24 × 10 % ≈ 8 % before the ticket. The ticket arrives with more than enough budget left to act; a page would have been the wrong urgency.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The club counts letters one by one. A service's SLI comes from counters sampled every few seconds on many machines; a sample that never arrives is not the same as "no failures", and the pipeline must say which it assumed.
    • 

  • The allowance card is spent by people who chose to be late. An error budget is mostly spent by things nobody chose (background failures used about a fifth of it in the lab), which is why the size of the budget and the normal failure rate must be compared before setting the SLO.
    • 

  • A bell in a house is heard by whoever is home. A page reaches one on-call person who did not cause the problem and may know little about this service: the alert's text and its link to evidence matter as much as its threshold.
    • 

  • A promise to friends can be renegotiated over dinner. An SLA is a contract, measured in the way the contract says, which may not match the SLO's window or measurement point.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F5-41 instrumented a service with counters, histograms, logs and traces. What carries over: request counters by status and latency histograms with deliberate bucket boundaries are exactly the inputs of SLIs; percentiles over aggregated data, not averages D5. What changes: most dangerous first: an alert is a decision to interrupt a person, so its cost is human attention, and both noise (rule A) and silence (rule B) are failures; the window of an SLO turns every incident into a share of a budget that someone must spend deliberately; and the numbers now carry policy (what happens when the budget is gone) R2. What comes next: F5-43 spends error budget on purpose: it injects faults to find weaknesses before users do, using the SLO as the experiment's success criterion.

Summary


    

  • An SLI is a ratio of good to valid events measured at a stated point; an SLO adds a target and a window; an SLA adds consequences.
    • 

  • The error budget, 1 − SLO, turns every incident into a share of what the month allows; in the lab, background failures alone used about 21 % of it.
    • 

  • Burn rate = observed error ratio / allowed ratio; the threshold for "fraction f of the budget in window w" is f × T / w (14.4, 6 and 1 for the rules C, D, E).
    • 

  • Threshold alerts at the SLO are noisy (39 noise alerts); high fixed thresholds miss slow burns; multiwindow burn-rate pages plus a long-window ticket caught all three incidents with no noise.
    • 

  • Pages are for urgent, actionable symptoms; slow burns deserve tickets.
    • 

  • Static HTML reports, generated and checked by programs, make SLO reviews reproducible and archivable without JavaScript.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. SLI: share of get and put requests answered without a 5xx status, measured at the gateway. SLO: that SLI ≥ 99.9 % over a rolling 30 days. SLA: a contract with a user team promising 99.5 % per calendar month, with a defined remedy if missed. (Any well-defined equivalents are fine; the SLA must be looser than the SLO.)
    2. 

  2. Budget: 0.05 % × 50,000,000 = 25,000 failed requests. The outage fails 0.6 × 10 × 10,000 = 60,000 requests: 240 % of the budget. One 10-minute outage at peak breaks the monthly SLO more than twice over, a reason to check that an SLO is achievable before adopting it.

Good answers say: rule A compares a five-minute error ratio with the SLO's allowed ratio, so ordinary random fluctuation of the background failures crosses it whenever traffic is low (at night, a few failures among few requests); it measures noise, not budget at risk. Rules C, D and E compare burn rates over windows long enough to contain many requests with thresholds that mean "a meaningful fraction of the month's budget", which background failures never reach.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Before day 14, a typical day has about 650 failures of 2.88 million requests (SLI about 99.977 %). From day 15 to day 26, every day has about 7,000–7,400 failures (SLI about 99.75 %): an error ratio of about 0.25 %, a burn rate of 2.5. Day 14 and day 27 are partial days (deploy at 09:12, rollback at 15:40) R5. The support ticket's "about 1 request in 400" is 0.25 %.


Budget. The month's budget is 0.1 % of about 86.4 million requests, about 86,400 failures. Before day 14 the background used about 650 per day, about 5,900 by day 9, plus about 5,150 extra on day 9 (the spike); by the end of day 13 about 13,600 (16 % of the budget). From day 14, each day adds about 7,200: the cumulative total passes 86,400 during day 24. The calculation in budget_key.out gives "budget exhausted at day 24 09:29", an error ratio of 0.251 % (burn rate 2.51) during the deploy period, which alone used 112.4 % of the budget; the month used 131.0 % R5.




Why no page. The deployed rule pages only when the five-minute error ratio exceeds 2 % for five minutes. The day-9 spike (25 % errors for six minutes) crossed it and auto-resolved. The deploy's 0.25 % is eight times below the threshold: a slow burn of 2.5 times the allowed rate, invisible to a rule designed only for outages. Rules C and D would not have fired either (their thresholds, 14.4 and 6, are above 2.5); rule E, the ticket, would have fired at day 15 08:49, 23.6 hours after the deploy, with about three-quarters of the budget still left R5.


Changes. Alerting: add a long-window, low-threshold burn-rate ticket (rule E) and route it to the owning team with an expected response within a working day; review the daily SLI report as part of the on-call handover. Release process: compare the SLI for a fixed period after every deploy with the period before (a canary or staged rollout with an automatic check would have caught a 12-fold increase in failures within hours); the change "retry once on upstream 503 removed" turned previously invisible upstream errors into user-visible failures, which a pre-release review of error-handling changes should flag.


How the evidence was produced. budget.cpp generates the month with forensicIncidents() (a 6-minute spike at 25 % on day 9, and 0.25 % failures from day 14 09:12 to day 27 15:40) and replays the deployed rule over it; budget_key.cpp computes the answers above from the same month. The deploy log, ticket and configuration are scripted to match. Output unedited.
