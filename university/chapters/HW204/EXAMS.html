Quizzes (Q)

Each chapter's "Check yourself" is its quiz: seven or eight questions per chapter, each set with at least one "explain in your own words", one "apply to a new case" and one "predict, then run" question (guide 11.3; the course is L2–L3, so the prediction questions are run against the chapter's lab listing). Learners answer in writing after reading the chapter and before the lab, run the prediction question, then mark themselves against the chapter's answers; a mentor marks the "in your own words" answers with the generic rubric. The nine quizzes together are 10 % of the course.

  7. F7 (4 points, F1-43, Analyse). A controller (line 0 most urgent, vector base 32) shows IRR = 0x1A, IMR = 0x08, ISR = 0x02. (a) Which lines are pending and not masked? Part C — forensic question: "The FIFO that was not enough" (18 points)

Scenario. After F1-42's "missing bytes" incident, Ana's team moved the firmware to a new board whose UART has a 4-byte receive FIFO instead of a one-byte holding register. "The FIFO fixes it," the project lead said, and the polling firmware was kept: it still polls the UART once per pass of its main loop. The new board also drives a small display, which the main loop refreshes now and then, at the same time as it flushes its log. Field reports of failed firmware updates over the serial console continue. Ana runs the same bench tool as in F1-42: it pastes 64 KiB at one byte every 10 ticks and prints four reports.

Practical (P) — decode a captured I2C transaction

Weight 25 % (guide 11.4). Time limit: 120 minutes. The course card's practical: "decode a captured I2C transaction". Candidates decode a logic-analyser capture of an I2C bus by hand, complete a decoder program, and explain what the driver that produced the capture did.

Allowed documentation: the HW204 chapters with their lab listings and recorded outputs (above all F1-47 and its i2c_sim.h), NXP UM10204 (the I2C specification cited in F1-47), a C++ reference, a calculator, and the practice capture i2c_decode_practice.in (the capture of F1-47 Listing 1, whose decoding is printed in that chapter). Not allowed: help from other people or code written before the exam other than the starting files.

The capture

The exam capture is the two lines of an I2C bus with one sensor on it, in the text format of F1-47 Listing 1: rows of 64 samples, - high, _ low, four samples per bit, the sample number at the start of each SCL row. It was recorded by the course's I2C bus model, which stands for a real I2C controller and a sigrok/PulseView capture (ruling A2); every address and value in it is an exercise value. It is the content of i2c_decode_start.in:

The task

  1. Decode by hand (on paper, about 30 minutes). Using the method of F1-47 Worked example 1, decode the first two transactions of the capture (up to and including the second STOP): mark every START and STOP with its sample number, write the nine bits of each byte, give each byte in hexadecimal with its ACK or NACK, and split each address byte into the 7-bit address and the R/W bit.
  2. Program (about 60 minutes). Complete the five functions marked TODO 1 to TODO 5 in i2c_decode_start.cpp: START detection, STOP detection, the sampling point (rising edge of SCL), splitting an address byte, and the summary (transactions, acknowledged and unacknowledged 7-bit addresses, data bytes written and read after an acknowledged address). Do not change anything else. Build with the course command g++ -std=c++20 -Wall -Wextra -Wpedantic -Werror -g -fsanitize=address,undefined i2c_decode_start.cpp -o i2c_decode_start, run it with ./i2c_decode_start < i2c_decode_start.in, and make its self-check report self-check: 0 failures. The self-check uses a tiny hand-built capture and two address bytes, not the exam capture; when it passes, the program's event list is your decoding of the whole capture.
  3. Explain (about 30 minutes). Compare the program's output with your hand decoding of the first two transactions (they must agree; explain any difference). Then write, in plain sentences, what the driver did on this bus, transaction by transaction: which address it probed and what the answer means; which register it wrote and with which value; which register it read and what the sensor returned. Say why the last data byte is NACKed and why that is not an error. Finally, a colleague's datasheet for this sensor says "address B4h/B5h": which value goes into a driver that expects a 7-bit address, and what would the capture have shown if the colleague had configured B4h instead (see F1-47's forensic lab)?

Course project — an interrupt-driven sensor reader with a logic-analyser trace

Weight 20 % (guide 11.4). The course card's project: "an interrupt-driven sensor reader on a microcontroller, logic-analyser trace attached". The brief follows guide 11.5; the firmware structure is the one of F1-48's mini-project section, which names this project.

Goal

Build firmware in which a timer interrupt at a rate you compute starts an I2C read of a sensor, the I2C peripheral's interrupts (or a DMA transfer) complete it, a ring buffer passes each reading to the main loop, and the main loop prints readings with sequence numbers over the UART; prove it with a logic-analyser trace of the I2C bus and a ten-minute log with no lost readings.

Requirements

  • Functional. A periodic timer interrupt at a chosen rate between 50 Hz and 500 Hz, with prescaler and reload computed and the real rate and error printed (F1-48 Listing 1's method); an I2C register read of at least 6 bytes from the sensor per tick, driven by interrupts or DMA, not by a busy wait in the timer handler (F1-42, F1-44, F1-47); a single-producer, single-consumer ring buffer in which the handler writes only the head and the main loop only the tail (F1-42 mini-project); UART output of one line per reading with a sequence number and a timestamp; an overrun or drop counter that is printed, never hidden.
  • Quality. All device registers through typed volatile accessors of the documented width, with read-modify-write for shared registers and W1C acknowledges where the reference manual says so (F1-41); every handler short, acknowledging its cause and never blocking (F1-42, F1-43); every time comparison wrap-safe (F1-48); the 7-bit sensor address stated as such (F1-47); every register name, interrupt number and clock setting cited to the microcontroller's reference manual or the sensor's datasheet, with section.

Constraints

Hardware: the reference kit (ruling D1): Raspberry Pi Pico 2 (RP2350) or ST NUCLEO-F446RE, the Raspberry Pi Debug Probe for programming and the UART output (the NUCLEO's ST-LINK serves both), the Adafruit LSM6DSOX breakout as the I2C sensor (3.3 V logic), and a sigrok-supported FX2 logic analyser with PulseView (alternative: Digilent Analog Discovery 3 with WaveForms). Safety: the F1-42 and F1-47 Part B safety boxes (USB-powered boards only, common ground first, analyser input range checked, power off before rewiring; adult supervision below L3). Software: the board vendor's SDK or Zephyr 4.4 (ruling D1); the course's host toolchain for any host-side tools. Time: about fifteen hours spread over the course. Honesty: without the kit, the project is done in the course's simulators (as the reference notes do) and every hardware claim is marked "untested on hardware".

Deliverables

  1. The firmware source with a build script, and the timer calculation (prescaler, reload, real rate, error) as printed by your own tool.
  2. Tests: a host-side unit test of the ring buffer (including wrap-around and full/empty), and of the wrap-safe time comparison with a counter preset near its maximum (F1-48 forensic method).
  3. The logic-analyser trace: a PulseView session or exported annotations with at least three decoded I2C transactions, and the measured spacing between the starts of consecutive reads compared with your computed period.
  4. A ten-minute UART log with sequence numbers, and a short script output showing the count of readings, missing sequence numbers and the drop counter.
  5. A two-page report: design (timer, I2C, ring, UART), the measurements, the sources for every register, and what was not tested; plus a five-minute demonstration.

Milestones

  1. After F1-42: UART output by interrupt and ring buffer; the 64 KiB paste test passes (curriculum C2).
  2. After F1-44: register accessors reviewed (F1-41 rules); DMA or interrupt-driven transfer path chosen and justified.
  3. After F1-47: one I2C register read of the sensor, captured and decoded with the logic analyser; the 7-bit address confirmed on the trace.
  4. After F1-48: the timer interrupt at the computed rate drives the reads; the ten-minute run, the trace with measured spacing, the report and the demonstration.
