Story hook

Analogy

Inside the kitchen, not every message needs a corridor. The thermometers in the cold room, the scales, the oven controls: they are all close to the head chef's station and say very little. So the kitchen uses simple speaking tubes and string telephones.

One shared tube runs past all the thermometers: you call a name into it, and only the thermometer with that name answers. A second kind of line is private: one wire to each scale, and you pull a cord to say which scale you mean. A third is the old serial line to the bar: two people agree in advance how fast they will talk, and if one speaks faster, the other hears nonsense. And in the delivery van, many gadgets share one wire, and the most important message always gets through first.

On Monday a new thermometer is installed. The technician copies its name from the label onto the chef's list, but it never answers. This chapter explains these four ways of talking, and why that thermometer is silent.

Jargon box
I2C
Simple words: a two-wire bus where one controller talks to many small chips, each with its own address. Analogy: the shared speaking tube where you call a name. Precise: a synchronous, half-duplex serial bus with a clock line (SCL) and a data line (SDA), both open-drain with pull-up resistors; transfers start with a START condition and a 7-bit (or 10-bit) address plus a read/write bit, each byte is followed by an acknowledge bit, and a STOP condition ends the transfer. D1 L2
Open-drain (wired-AND)
Simple words: every chip can only pull the wire low or let go; a resistor pulls it high when everyone lets go. Analogy: a rope that anyone can pull down, held up by a spring. Precise: an output that either sinks current or is high-impedance; with a pull-up the line is low if any device pulls it low, so the line is the logical AND of all outputs and no two drivers can fight. D1 R1
ACK and NACK
Simple words: the receiver's "got it" (pull SDA low) or silence (leave it high) after every byte. Analogy: a grunt down the tube after each word. Precise: the ninth clock of each I2C byte, during which the receiver pulls SDA low to acknowledge; a high level (NACK) means no device answered the address, the receiver refused the byte, or the controller signals the last byte of a read. D1 R1
SPI
Simple words: a fast four-wire link where the controller picks one chip with its own select wire and swaps bits with it. Analogy: private string telephones, one cord per scale. Precise: a synchronous, full-duplex serial interface with a clock (SCLK), controller-out data (MOSI, also COPI), controller-in data (MISO, CIPO) and one chip-select line per target; each clock shifts one bit each way, so a transfer exchanges the contents of two shift registers; clock polarity and phase (the "SPI mode") must match. D2 R3
UART
Simple words: a serial line with no clock wire: both sides agree on the speed in advance. Analogy: the line to the bar, where both agreed how fast to talk. Precise: a universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter sends frames of a start bit (low), 5–8 data bits least significant first, an optional parity bit and 1 or 2 stop bits (high) at a configured baud rate; the receiver resynchronises on each start bit and samples in the middle of each bit. D3 L1 R2
Baud rate
Simple words: how many signal changes (here, bits) per second the line uses. Analogy: the agreed talking speed. Precise: symbols per second; for a two-level UART line, one symbol is one bit, so 9600 baud is 9600 bit times per second including start and stop bits; both ends derive it from their own clocks with a divisor. D3 L1
CAN
Simple words: a robust two-wire bus for cars and machines, where every message has an ID and the lowest ID wins when two talk at once. Analogy: the van's shared wire where the most urgent message goes first. Precise: the Controller Area Network: a multi-controller, message-based differential bus with dominant (0) and recessive (1) levels; frames carry an 11-bit or 29-bit identifier, up to 8 data bytes (64 in CAN FD) and a CRC; bitwise arbitration on the identifier resolves collisions without destroying the winning frame. D4 L3 R4
Logic analyser
Simple words: a tool that records many digital wires over time and can decode protocols from the recording. Analogy: a recorder on every speaking tube. Precise: an instrument that samples digital signals at a fixed rate, stores the samples, and displays them as waveforms; protocol decoders (for example in sigrok's PulseView) turn the samples into START, addresses, bytes and ACKs. D5

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Inside a gadget, small chips such as temperature sensors, memory chips and displays talk to the main chip over very simple buses, with two to four wires. Each bus sends one bit at a time, one after another: that is what "serial" means.

  • I2C: two wires shared by many chips; each chip has an address, like a name.
  • SPI: faster; one extra wire per chip says "I mean you".
  • UART: no clock wire at all; both sides agree on the speed beforehand.
  • CAN: for cars and machines; many devices share two wires, and the most important message always wins.

A logic analyser records the wires, so you can see every bit and check what really happened.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

I2C, bit by bit

I2C has two open-drain lines, SCL (clock) and SDA (data), with pull-up resistors D1. Because every device can only pull a line low or release it, the line is the AND of all outputs. Listing 1's model implements exactly that (line 26 of i2c_sim.h: the bus's SDA is the controller's output AND the target's) R1.

The rules, as the model and its decoder use them D1:

  • Data may change only while SCL is low; the receiver reads SDA while SCL is high (the decoder samples at each rising edge).
  • START: SDA falls while SCL is high. STOP: SDA rises while SCL is high. These two are the only times SDA changes with SCL high, so they cannot be confused with data.
  • After START, the first byte is the 7-bit address followed by the read/write bit (0 write, 1 read), most significant bit first.
  • After every byte, the receiver drives the ninth bit: low = ACK, high = NACK.
  • A repeated START (a START without a STOP before it) lets the controller switch direction without releasing the bus.

Listing 1 reads two bytes from register 0x00 of a sensor at address 0x48: write the register pointer, repeated START, read two bytes (run R1). Its decoder, which sees only the samples, prints: START; address 0x48 WRITE (byte 0x90) ACK; data 0x00 ACK; START; address 0x48 READ (byte 0x91) ACK; data 0x19 ACK; data 0x40 NACK; STOP R1. The final NACK is not an error: the controller sends it to say "that was the last byte".

Linux's I2C interface describes each segment of such a transaction as a struct i2c_msg whose addr field is the "Slave address, either 7 or 10 bits" and whose flag I2C_M_RD marks a read L2. The read/write bit is not part of the address; software adds it. That detail is the forensic lab.

UART frames and baud rates

A UART line idles high. A frame is a start bit (low), the data bits least significant first, optionally a parity bit, and a stop bit (high) D3. The common setting "8N1" means 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit; on the 16550-style UART of F1-41, it is UART_LCR_WLEN8 (0x03) in the line control register with the parity and two-stop-bit flags off L1. There is no clock wire, so the receiver times the bits itself: it waits for the falling edge of the start bit and then samples each bit in its middle, at 1.5, 2.5, … bit times after the edge D3.

Listing 2 sends "Hi" and decodes it three times (run R2) R2. With the same bit time it reads [0x48][0x69], "Hi". With a bit time 25% too long it reads one wrong byte, 0x44; with 25% too short it reads 0x30 and then 0x8B with a framing error: it found a low level where the stop bit should be. The UART's line status register reports exactly these errors: UART_LSR_FE (frame error), UART_LSR_PE (parity error), UART_LSR_OE (overrun, F1-42) L1. Garbage characters on a serial console almost always mean mismatched baud rates.

SPI: a swap of two shift registers

SPI has a clock driven by the controller, two data lines and a chip select per target D2. The controller and the target each have an 8-bit shift register; on each clock both shift one bit out and one bit in, so after 8 clocks they have exchanged their bytes. Listing 3 shows it: the controller sent 0x9F and the target had 0xC2 loaded; after 8 clocks the controller holds 0xC2 and the target 0x9F (run R3) R3. To read from a device the controller must send something (often dummy bytes); to write, it receives something it may ignore. SPI has no addresses, no acknowledge and no flow control: the chip select is the address, and the datasheet defines the bytes. The clock polarity and phase, and the bit order, must match the device's datasheet.

CAN: arbitration without collisions

CAN connects many equal nodes on a differential pair. A 0 is dominant and a 1 recessive: if any node sends 0, the bus reads 0, the same wired-AND as I2C D4. Every frame begins with an identifier, 11 bits in the standard format (CAN_SFF_ID_BITS = 11, CAN_SFF_MASK = 0x7FF) or 29 in the extended format (CAN_EFF_ID_BITS) L3. Nodes send the identifier bit by bit and read the bus back; a node that sends a 1 but reads a 0 has lost and stops. Listing 4 runs three nodes, IDs 0x65A, 0x123 and 0x124 (run R4) R4: the engine drops out at bit 10, the wipers at bit 2, and the brakes (0x123, the lowest ID) win, and their frame continues undamaged. The identifier is therefore also the priority. Classic CAN frames carry up to 8 data bytes (CAN_MAX_DLEN), CAN FD up to 64 (CANFD_MAX_DLEN) L3.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Analogy first: on the shared tube, two cooks can start shouting at once; the one who hears a different word from the one he said stops. And a slow thermometer can hold the tube to say "wait".

I2C multi-controller arbitration and clock stretching. Because SDA is a wired-AND, two controllers that start together can each compare what they send with what they read; the first one to send 1 and read 0 lost and backs off, as in CAN D1. A target that needs time can hold SCL low after the controller releases it ("clock stretching"); a controller must wait until SCL really goes high. Some controllers handle this badly; if a capture shows SCL held low for a long time by the target, that is the reason.

Bus recovery. If a controller resets in the middle of a read, a target may still be driving SDA low, waiting for clocks. The bus then looks stuck: SDA low forever. The usual recovery is to toggle SCL until SDA is released, then send a STOP D1.

SPI modes. Clock polarity (CPOL: idle low or high) and clock phase (CPHA: sample on the first or second edge) give four modes; a mismatch shifts every byte by one bit, which looks like values doubled or halved D2. Many SPI devices also have a maximum clock and a minimum time between chip-select and the first clock.

UART tolerance. Because the receiver resynchronises only on the start bit, timing errors add up over the frame; the last sampling point (the stop bit) is the most sensitive. Listing 2's 25% error is far beyond what any receiver accepts; real tolerances are a few per cent, and both ends' errors add D3. The baud rate comes from dividing a clock (the 16550's divisor latch, UART_DLL/UART_DLM, L1), so some rates cannot be produced exactly from some clocks; F1-48 computes such errors for timers.

CAN error handling. CAN frames carry a CRC; every node checks every frame, and a node that sees an error destroys the frame with an error flag so it is retransmitted; nodes that cause too many errors go "error passive" and finally "bus off" D4. Linux handles CAN in its network layer, with sockets: the can.h header used in Listing 4 describes itself as "Definitions for CAN network layer (socket addr / CAN frame / CAN filter)" L3.

How the hardware actually does it

Each of these peripherals is a shift register with a small state machine (F1-17, F1-19). An I2C peripheral adds open-drain pad drivers, START/STOP detection and an address comparator for target mode; an SPI peripheral is little more than the shift register, a clock generator and a FIFO; a UART adds a baud-rate divider and an oversampling clock (often 16 samples per bit) to find the middle of each bit; a CAN controller adds bit timing, CRC, arbitration logic and acceptance filters, and talks to a separate transceiver chip that drives the differential pair D3 D4. Software sees them as MMIO registers (F1-41) with interrupts (F1-42) and sometimes DMA (F1-44). Many microcontrollers let the program drive pins directly instead ("bit-banging"), which is how Listing 1's model works and how I2C is often done on boards without a free peripheral.

Worked example

Decode a captured I2C transaction (exam task P)

Take the third row of Listing 1's capture (samples 128–191) and decode the first byte in it. The method:

  1. Find each rising edge of SCL (_ followed by - in the SCL row).
  2. Read the SDA character directly below the first - of each high SCL phase.
  3. Group bits in nines: eight data bits (most significant first) and the ACK bit.
  4. Watch for START (SDA falling while SCL stays high) and STOP (SDA rising while SCL stays high).

Done on the full capture, this gives the decoder's result: START, 0x90 ACK, 0x00 ACK, START, 0x91 ACK, 0x19 ACK, 0x40 NACK, STOP R1. For the byte 0x19: 0001 1001; for 0x40: 0100 0000. The address byte 0x91 = 1001 0001 splits into 1001 000 = 0x48 and the R/W bit 1, read W1.

Decode a UART frame

'H' = 0x48 = 0100 1000. Sent least significant bit first: 0 0 0 1 0 0 1 0. On the line: idle 1, start 0, then those eight bits, then stop 1 (run R2's first line). Ten bit times carry 8 data bits: at 9600 baud, 8N1 moves at most 960 bytes per second W1.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A speaking tube carries sound continuously; these buses carry bits on a clock or a timing agreement. Nothing in the analogy explains why a wrong speed turns words into nonsense rather than into slow words (run R2) R2.
    • 

  • A name called into a tube can be misheard; an I2C address is exact. One bit off and nobody answers (the forensic lab).
    • 

  • In a kitchen the loudest wins; on CAN and I2C the 0 wins. Priority is built into the electrical design, not the volume D4.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: every I2C access fails with NACK on the address. Cause: 8-bit address (with R/W bit) given where a 7-bit one is expected, wrong address pins, device unpowered, missing pull-ups. Find it: logic analyser: which address byte was sent? Fix: use the 7-bit address; check the board.
    • 

  • Symptom: I2C lines stay low. Cause: a stuck target after a reset, or no pull-ups. Fix: bus recovery (toggle SCL), add pull-ups.
    • 

  • Symptom: garbage characters on a serial console. Cause: wrong baud rate, data bits, parity or stop bits; TX and RX swapped gives silence instead. Fix: match the settings at both ends; cross TX and RX.
    • 

  • Symptom: SPI values look shifted by one bit or doubled. Cause: wrong SPI mode or bit order. Fix: set CPOL/CPHA from the datasheet; compare with a capture.
    • 

  • Symptom: a CAN node never gets its frames through under load. Cause: its identifier has low priority (high number) and the bus is busy. Fix: assign identifiers by urgency; reduce bus load.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: the capture shows START, address byte 0x20 = 0010 0000, which encodes 7-bit address 0x10, write, then NACK and STOP (R1). The sensor is at 0x48. Hypotheses: (1) hardware: sensor not powered, wrong address pins, broken wiring; would give a NACK too, but the byte on the wire would be 0x90; (2) software: the wrong address was sent; the capture proves it, since 0x20 is not the board's 0x90 either. Proof: the driver passes 0x90 as a 7-bit address; read_reg shifts it left by one to add the R/W bit (line 117 of i2c_sim.h): 0x90 << 1 = 0x120, which does not fit in a byte; the low 8 bits, 0x20, go on the wire. The board document gave the 8-bit form (address and R/W bit together): 0x90 >> 1 = 0x48. Fix: configure 0x48. Verify: capture again: the address byte must be 0x90 followed by ACK, then the data. Prevention: the configuration should accept only 7-bit addresses (reject values above 0x7F), and documentation should state "7-bit address 0x48". How the evidence was made: i2c_forensic.cpp on the same model. Wrong answers: "the sensor is broken" (the wire shows a different address than the board's, so the sensor was never asked); "add a delay" (timing is not involved).
