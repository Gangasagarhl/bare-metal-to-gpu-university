Story hook

Analogy

The kitchen runs on time. The oven timer rings when the bread is done; the dishwasher runs every twenty minutes; the head chef's watch decides when the soup has simmered long enough. All these timers count the ticks of something: a pendulum, a quartz crystal, a motor.

The kitchen clock is set from a crystal that ticks sixteen million times a second. To get one ring every thousandth of a second, the timer counts sixteen thousand ticks. A new apprentice sets it to count from 0 to 16,000 instead of 0 to 15,999. Nobody notices for a day; by then the kitchen clock is five seconds slow.

In another restaurant, the order system works for weeks and then, one afternoon, every order is marked "late" the moment it is placed. Nothing was changed. Both problems are about counting, and this chapter is about both.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain where a computer's time comes from: oscillators, crystals, PLLs, dividers, clock trees.
  • Configure a periodic timer interrupt: choose a prescaler and a reload value, compute the real frequency and error, and avoid the off-by-one.
  • Name the timer functions of a microcontroller (periodic, compare, capture, PWM, watchdog, real-time clock) and of a PC (PIT, HPET, local APIC timer, TSC).
  • Compare time values safely across counter wrap-around (the forensic lab).
  • Measure the resolution and the real behaviour of an operating system's clock and sleep.
Jargon box
Oscillator and crystal
Simple words: the part that makes a steady tick; a quartz crystal makes it very steady. Analogy: the pendulum of the kitchen clock. Precise: an oscillator is a circuit that produces a periodic signal; a quartz crystal resonator sets its frequency precisely, while an RC oscillator is cheaper and less accurate; the frequency's deviation is usually given in parts per million (ppm). D1 D2
PLL and clock divider
Simple words: a PLL makes a faster clock from a slower one; a divider makes a slower one. Analogy: gears between the pendulum and the hands. Precise: a phase-locked loop multiplies a reference frequency by a ratio by locking a controlled oscillator's divided output to the reference; dividers and prescalers divide a clock by an integer; together they produce the clocks of the CPU, buses and peripherals (the clock tree). D1
Hardware timer
Simple words: a counter driven by a clock that raises an interrupt when it reaches a set value. Analogy: the oven timer. Precise: a peripheral with a counter register clocked from a (prescaled) clock, a reload or compare register, and status and interrupt-enable bits; on reaching the compare value or overflowing it sets a flag, raises an interrupt and may reload. D2 R1
Prescaler and reload value
Simple words: the prescaler slows the counter's clock; the reload value sets how far it counts before it rings. Analogy: choosing "count every eighth tick" and "ring after 2000 counts". Precise: the prescaler divides the input clock; for a counter that counts 0 … reload and wraps, the period is (reload + 1) × prescaler / fin. R1 W1
Input capture, output compare and PWM
Simple words: capture writes down the time of an outside event; compare does something at a set time; PWM makes a square wave whose high part you choose. Analogy: a stopwatch button; an alarm; a light switched on for part of every second. Precise: input capture copies the counter into a register on a pin edge; output compare sets, clears or toggles a pin or raises an interrupt when the counter equals a register; pulse-width modulation uses a period and a compare value to produce a duty cycle. D2
Watchdog timer
Simple words: a timer that resets the computer unless the program "pats" it in time. Analogy: a dead man's switch. Precise: an independent counter that triggers a reset (or an interrupt first) on expiry; correct software restarts it periodically, so a hung program causes a reset. D2
Wrap-around
Simple words: a counter that reaches its biggest value goes back to zero. Analogy: a clock face going from 12 back to 1. Precise: an n-bit counter counts modulo 2n; time comparisons must use modular differences, not plain "greater than". R2 D3 [basic.fundamental]
Clock source (operating system)
Simple words: the hardware counter the operating system reads to tell the time. Analogy: the one clock on the wall everybody trusts. Precise: a free-running counter with known frequency (on x86, for example the TSC or HPET; in a virtual machine, a paravirtual clock) that the kernel reads to keep time; Linux lists the candidates and the current one in sysfs. R4 C1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every computer has a tiny "heartbeat": a crystal that wiggles millions of times a second. Circuits turn that heartbeat into faster clocks for the processor and slower ones for everything else. A timer is a counter that counts those beats. When it reaches a number you chose, it rings the doorbell of F1-42: an interrupt. That is how a computer knows that a millisecond has passed, how a game updates its screen at a steady rate, and how a microwave knows when to stop.

Counters have a largest number. When they reach it, they start again from zero, like a clock face. Programs that forget this can think that time went backwards.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Where time comes from

F1-18 introduced clocks for logic. A board's reference clock usually comes from a quartz crystal; a PLL multiplies it up for the CPU and buses, and dividers produce the slower clocks of peripherals D1. Every timer, baud rate (F1-47) and polling interval (F1-46) is derived from these clocks by division, so its accuracy is at best the crystal's, and its achievable values are those that integer divisors allow.

Programming a periodic interrupt

The model of Listing 1 is the common up-counter: clocked at fin / prescaler, it counts 0, 1, …, reload, wraps to 0 and raises its interrupt; one period is reload + 1 counts D2. For a 1 kHz tick from an exercise clock of 16 MHz (run R1) R1:

  • prescaler 1: 16,000 counts per period, reload 15,999: exact;
  • prescalers 8 and 64: reloads 1,999 and 249: exact;
  • prescaler 256: 62.5 counts, which is not an integer; reload 62 gives 992.063 Hz, −0.79%.

All four reloads fit a 16-bit register. The rule: choose the smallest prescaler whose reload fits the register, which gives the finest resolution, and check that the division is exact. The classic bug is writing the count instead of count − 1: a reload of 16,000 gives 999.9375 Hz. The error is only −0.0062%, but a clock that counts these ticks loses 5.40 seconds per day (run R1).

Not verified — check before relying on this

Whether a particular timer counts to the reload value inclusive, counts down instead of up, or reloads on the next clock, differs between microcontrollers and timer modes; the "period = reload + 1" rule is the model's, and common, but not universal. The 16 MHz clock is an exercise value, not a specific board (the kit's RP2350 and STM32F446 have their own clock trees). Check the timer chapter of your microcontroller's reference manual; no reference manual was opened in this build or in the verification pass.

The timer zoo

A microcontroller usually offers several kinds D2:

  • Periodic timers for ticks and sampling (Figure 1). Arm Cortex-M cores include a simple one, SysTick, for an operating system tick (curriculum H2) D4 C1.
  • Input capture to timestamp edges: measuring a pulse width or a frequency.
  • Output compare and PWM to produce pulses: motor speed, LED brightness, servo positions.
  • Watchdog to reset a hung system.
  • Real-time clock (RTC), often with its own low-frequency crystal and battery, to keep calendar time while the rest sleeps.

A PC has its own set, all visible in this course's QEMU runs: the legacy programmable interval timer at I/O port 0x40 (pit) and the HPET at 0xfed00000 (hpet) in F1-41's memory tree; the local APIC's own timer, which produces the "Local timer interrupts" (LOC) row of /proc/interrupts; and the time-stamp counter (TSC), a CPU register that counts cycles C1 D5. RISC-V's ACLINT timer appeared in F1-43 (riscv.aclint.mtimer).

What the operating system uses

Linux separates the clock source, a counter it reads to know the time, from the clock event devices that interrupt it. On the build machine, the current clock source is tsc, and kvm-clock is the other candidate, a clock provided by the hypervisor to virtual machines (run R4) R4. Counting the LOC row over one second showed local timer interrupts on every CPU, at different rates on different CPUs (run R5) R5; the counts change from run to run, so they are not quoted here.

Programs see time through the OS. Listing 2 asks C++'s steady_clock (a clock whose time points never decrease and which may not be adjusted D3 [time.clock.steady]) for its tick period, which the library reports as 1/1,000,000,000 s, and measures sleep_for(1 ms) 20 times: every sleep took at least 1 ms and some took noticeably longer (run R3) R3. A clock's resolution is not its accuracy, and a sleep's request is a minimum, not a promise: the thread wakes when the OS's timer fires and the scheduler runs it D6.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Analogy first: the kitchen clock is accurate only if its pendulum is; two kitchens with two pendulums slowly disagree; and the clock's hands go round, so "later" needs care.

Accuracy, drift and ppm. A crystal's frequency error is stated in parts per million. An error of e ppm accumulates e × 86,400 / 106 seconds per day: 1 ppm is 0.0864 s per day W1. The off-by-one of Listing 1 is a 62.5 ppm error (0.00625%), which is why it loses 5.40 s per day; a crystal's own tolerance adds to it. Networked computers correct drift by synchronising with time servers; a microcontroller without a network needs a trimmed crystal or an RTC.

Wrap-around arithmetic. An n-bit counter wraps after 2n ticks. A 32-bit millisecond counter wraps every 232 ms = 49.71 days (run R2), a well-known source of "fails after seven weeks of uptime" bugs. Comparing two times with now >= deadline breaks when the deadline has wrapped and the current time has not. Computing the difference in unsigned arithmetic (which wraps modulo 2n, as the C++ standard defines for unsigned types) and reading it as signed gives the right answer as long as the two times are less than 2n−1 ticks apart D3 [basic.fundamental] R2. Linux uses this idiom for its jiffies counter D6.

Reading a running counter. A 64-bit time value read as two 32-bit halves can tear: the low half wraps between the two reads. The usual fix reads high, low, high again and retries if the high halves differ. Reading a counter in another clock domain (a peripheral clocked separately from the CPU) has the same problem at bit level and is solved in hardware by synchronisers (F1-18).

Tick versus one-shot timers. A periodic tick is simple but wakes the CPU even when there is nothing to do, which costs energy. Operating systems and low-power firmware can instead program a one-shot timer for the next deadline that matters D6. Either way, timer interrupts are interrupts: their handlers must be short (F1-42), and their jitter adds to every timing in the system.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The behaviour of the TSC (constant rate across frequency changes, synchronisation between cores), of the HPET and the local APIC timer, and of the Cortex-M SysTick (width, clock source) was not checked against the Intel SDM or the Arm Architecture Reference Manual in this build or in the verification pass (neither manual's chapters could be opened). Linux's choice of tsc on the build machine (run R4) is evidence only for that machine.

How the hardware actually does it

A timer is a register of flip-flops with an adder (a counter, F1-17), clocked through a divider, plus a comparator against a compare register and some control logic (F1-19) D1. The prescaler is itself a small counter whose overflow enables the main counter. Capture is a register that loads the counter's value on a synchronised pin edge; PWM is a comparator that drives a pin. A crystal oscillator is an amplifier with the quartz resonator in its feedback path; a PLL contains a phase detector, a filter, a voltage-controlled oscillator and a feedback divider D1. The clock tree distributes the results across the chip with gating to switch unused blocks off, which is why many microcontrollers require software to enable a peripheral's clock before its registers respond.

Worked example

A 50 Hz servo pulse from a 16 MHz clock

Wanted: a 20 ms period (50 Hz) with a 16-bit counter. Counts per period at prescaler p: 16,000,000 × 0.020 / p = 320,000 / p.

  1. p = 1: 320,000: does not fit 16 bits (maximum 65,535).
  2. p = 8: 40,000: fits; reload 39,999; exact W1.
  3. A 1.5 ms pulse is 1.5 / 20 × 40,000 = 3,000 counts: compare value 3,000.
  4. Resolution: one count = 8 / 16 MHz = 0.5 µs.

When does a 32-bit microsecond counter wrap?

232 µs = 4,294,967,296 µs = 4,294.97 s ≈ 71.6 minutes. A microsecond timestamp therefore wraps several times a day; wrap-safe comparisons are mandatory, not optional W1.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A kitchen clock shows hours; a timer shows a number that wraps. Nothing on a clock face corresponds to "4294967265 is earlier than 19" (Figure 2) R2.
    • 

  • A pendulum is one clock; a chip has many derived clocks. Changing one PLL setting changes baud rates, timer periods and bus speeds together D1.
    • 

  • An oven timer rings exactly on time; a timer interrupt's handler runs later. Interrupt latency (F1-42) and the scheduler (R3) add delay.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a clock that drifts by seconds per day. Cause: off-by-one in the reload value, or an inexact division. Find it: compute the actual frequency (Listing 1). Fix: reload = counts − 1; choose an exact prescaler.
    • 

  • Symptom: timeouts fire immediately or never, after long uptime (forensic lab). Cause: naive comparison across wrap-around. Fix: compare signed differences; test with a counter preset near its maximum.
    • 

  • Symptom: a timer runs at the wrong speed after a clock change. Cause: the timer's input clock comes from a bus clock that was changed. Fix: recompute timer settings from the clock tree after any clock change.
    • 

  • Symptom: a timer peripheral's registers read zero and do not change. Cause: its clock is gated off. Fix: enable the peripheral clock first.
    • 

  • Symptom: measured durations jump or go backwards. Cause: using wall-clock time (which can be adjusted) for intervals. Fix: use a steady (monotonic) clock.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: the start is 4294967265 (0xFFFFFFE1), the deadline start + 50 wraps to 19. The naive check says "expired" at 0, 10, 20 and 30 ms, wrongly, then "no" at 40 ms (now = 9 after the wrap), then "yes" at 50 ms. The wrap-safe check says "no" until 50 ms, then "yes" (R2). Hypotheses: (1) a hardware timer fault: the counter values in the log are correct and continuous; (2) the comparison now >= deadline fails when the deadline has wrapped and now has not: fits exactly. Why after weeks: the counter starts at 0 at power-on and wraps after 232 ms = 49.71 days (R2); only timeouts that span the wrap fail, and here every timeout started near the wrap fails. In the field, controllers that are rarely rebooted hit it first; a reboot resets the counter. Why the fix works: now - deadline in unsigned arithmetic is the true distance modulo 232; read as int32_t, it is negative before the deadline and non-negative after, as long as the two times are less than 231 ms (about 24.9 days) apart. Test: preset the counter near its maximum, as Rafael did, in a unit test. Prevention: one helper for all time comparisons; never compare raw timestamps. How the evidence was made: wrap.cpp. Wrong answers: "make the counter 64-bit" (this works in practice, since it wraps only after an extremely long time, but it is a different fix and costs atomic-read care on 32-bit CPUs); "reboot weekly" (hides the bug).
