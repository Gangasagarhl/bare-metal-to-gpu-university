Story hook

Kofi has a head waiter at a desk by the door, who sees the whole room and tells him where to go next. On busy nights an assistant walks beside him, carrying a notepad, reading orders, and telling him "table 6, then the bar". The rule of the house is simple to say and hard to keep: if Kofi stops hearing from the person who is directing him, he goes back to the meeting point and waits.

One evening the head waiter's radio dies. Nobody notices, because a new trainee at the bar keeps clicking his own radio, and Kofi counts every click as "someone is still there". He keeps walking the old route for ten minutes with nobody directing him. This chapter is about the people who direct a drone from outside, the ground station and the companion computer, and about the rule that should bring the drone back when they go quiet.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe the roles of a ground station and a companion computer, and how they connect to a flight controller.
  • Explain how messages are routed between several links by system and component ID.
  • Write a companion-computer program that connects to a simulated vehicle over UDP, uploads a mission, commands it, and handles refusals.
  • Explain and test a link-loss failsafe based on heartbeats.
  • Reconstruct from heartbeat logs when a lost-link failsafe should have triggered, and why it did not.
Jargon box
Ground control station (GCS)
Simple words: the program on a laptop, tablet or phone that shows where the drone is and lets a person plan and command a flight. Analogy: the head waiter's desk. Precise: a MAVLink endpoint, usually with system ID reserved for ground stations, that displays telemetry, uploads missions and parameters, and sends commands over a telemetry radio, USB or a network link. D2 D3
Companion computer
Simple words: a second, bigger computer on the drone that does heavy jobs, like vision, and talks to the flight controller. Analogy: the assistant walking beside the waiter. Precise: a Linux-class computer on the vehicle connected to a flight controller serial port (or network), running programs that exchange MAVLink with the autopilot as a separate component of the same system, for example to send position targets in a guided mode. D2
MAVLink routing
Simple words: passing messages between several links so that each reaches the program it is meant for. Analogy: the waiter relaying a message from the kitchen to the head waiter. Precise: a node with several links learns on which link each system and component was heard, forwards broadcast messages to all other links and targeted messages only to the link of their target. D3
Guided mode (external control)
Simple words: a flight mode in which the drone flies wherever another computer tells it, one target at a time. Analogy: the assistant saying "table 6 now". Precise: a mode in which the autopilot accepts position, velocity or attitude targets over MAVLink from a ground station or companion computer, while keeping its own stabilisation and safety checks. D2
Lost-link failsafe
Simple words: what the drone does on its own when it stops hearing from whoever is directing it. Analogy: go back to the meeting point. Precise: a rule in the autopilot that, when no heartbeat has arrived from the controlling ground station (or companion) for a configured time while it matters, switches to a configured action such as return, land or continue. D2

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A drone is often directed by someone outside it. A person on the ground uses a program that shows a map and buttons: the ground station. Some drones also carry a second small computer that can see with a camera and decide where to go: the companion computer. Both talk to the flight controller with the message language of the last chapter.

Each one says "I am here" once a second. If the drone stops hearing that from the one in charge, it should do something safe on its own, such as fly home or land.

The tricky part: the drone must know who is in charge. If it listens to anyone who says "I am here", a different program can hide the fact that the one in charge has gone quiet.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Ground stations

The head waiter's desk sees the whole room. The ArduPilot documentation lists several ground-station programs for desktop and mobile computers, which plan missions, show telemetry, set parameters, calibrate sensors and send commands; they connect over a telemetry radio, USB or a network. D2 They all speak MAVLink, so the protocols of F10-30 (mission, parameter, command) are what they do underneath every button. D3 A ground station is not needed in the air: once a mission is uploaded and the vehicle is in an automatic mode, the autopilot flies it alone. That is why its silence must be handled by a rule, not assumed to be harmless or fatal.

Check each name, number and behaviour in D2, D3 and the software's own documentation at the versions in the dossier.

Companion computers

The assistant does the thinking the waiter has no time for. The documentation describes companion computers (single-board Linux computers and embedded GPU boards) connected to a flight-controller serial port, running programs that read telemetry and send commands or targets, for example for obstacle avoidance, visual navigation, or precision landing. D2 The companion is a component of the vehicle's system, so it uses the vehicle's system ID with its own component ID; a ground station can reach it through the flight controller's routing. D3 The division of labour is a safety design: the flight controller keeps stabilisation, failsafes and geofences on a real-time system (F10-27); the companion, running a general-purpose OS that can pause or crash, only proposes where to go. D2

Routing

Kofi relays messages he is not the target of. The MAVLink guide describes routing in a node with several links: learn on which link each system and component was heard; forward messages without a target to every other link; forward targeted messages only to the link where the target was heard; and do not send a message back on the link it came from. D3 Listing 1 implements these rules for a flight controller with USB, TELEM1 and TELEM2: heartbeats spread to all other links; a ground station's command to the companion (1/191) goes only to TELEM2; a message for the autopilot itself is used and not forwarded; a command for a system never heard goes nowhere. R1

A companion program against a simulated vehicle

The course's lab asks for a companion program speaking MAVLink to SITL. This build had neither, so Listings 2 and 3 do the same job with the university's own parts: a mini-SITL (a point-mass vehicle speaking U-link over UDP on the loopback interface) and a companion program. The companion waits for the vehicle's heartbeat, uploads a two-item mission with the microservice of F10-30, is refused when it asks for GOTO on the ground, switches to GUIDED, arms, takes off to 5 m, switches to AUTO, and after "Reached WP1" stops its own heartbeat to test the failsafe. The vehicle detects no companion heartbeat for about 1.5 s, switches to LAND, lands and disarms. R2 R3 The real programs differ in every name and number; the structure (wait for heartbeat, upload, check every acknowledgement, watch the vehicle's state, never assume) is the one the practical exam needs.

The lost-link failsafe

Go back to the meeting point when the director goes quiet. ArduPilot's documentation describes a ground-station failsafe: when heartbeats from the ground station stop for a configured time while the vehicle is armed (and, depending on settings, in certain modes), it takes a configured action such as return to launch, land, or continue a mission; parameters choose which ground station counts. D2 The forensic lab shows why the last part matters.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Recalled, not checked: ArduPilot parameters with names like FS_GCS_ENABLE (the action) and a timeout parameter, and SYSID_MYGCS (which ground-station system ID the vehicle obeys and listens to for this failsafe); a separate radio-control failsafe for the RC link; guided-mode behaviour when a companion stops sending targets. The parameter names, their defaults, which messages count as "heard from the ground station", and the failsafe's conditions differ between vehicles and releases: read the ArduPilot documentation (D2) for the vehicle and release in the dossier before configuring any of them.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

"Is the link alive?" is the wrong question

  • Ask "is the one in control alive?". A link can carry heartbeats from several programs. A failsafe that counts any ground heartbeat measures "is any ground program alive", which is not what the safety case needs. In the forensic evidence, the rule that counts any system-255 heartbeat never triggers; the rule that counts only the controlling laptop triggers at 32.10 s. R4
  • A heartbeat proves that the sender's heartbeat thread runs, not that the sender works. The companion in Listing 3 could have a crashed planner and a healthy heartbeat thread. Better liveness signals carry progress: a target stream with timestamps, a counter that the planner increments. R3
  • Both directions fail separately. Sequence numbers tell "sent but not received" (a link problem) from "not sent" (the sender stopped): in the evidence, the 4 s gap shows 3 lost heartbeats in each direction; the 10 s gap shows 9 vehicle heartbeats lost at the laptop but nothing sent by the laptop. R4

Choosing the timeout and the action

A short timeout reacts fast and fires on every radio fade (here a 4 s fade would trip a 3 s timeout); a long one tolerates fades but leaves the vehicle uncontrolled longer. The choice depends on what the vehicle does meanwhile: in an automatic mission, continuing may be safest; in guided mode driven by a companion, holding or landing within a second or two is (Listing 2 uses 1.5 s). R2 The action must be possible in the failure: returning needs a position estimate (F10-28, fallback chains); landing needs a safe place below.

Guided control from a companion

A companion sending targets is a control loop across a process boundary and a serial link. Its latency (camera, processing, link, autopilot task rate) adds phase lag to any outer loop it closes (F10-16): close only slow loops (position, path) through it, never attitude. Targets should carry a time, and the autopilot should treat stale targets as no targets. D2 Commands from the companion are checked by the autopilot like any other: in Listing 3, GOTO on the ground is refused, and the companion must handle that, not assume success. R3

Who may command the vehicle

Every program that can put a frame on any link can command the vehicle through routing. A phone app on the companion's Wi-Fi reaches the flight controller unless something filters it. Restrict which systems are accepted, use MAVLink 2 signing where the stack supports it (F10-30), and keep networks that carry commands closed. D3

How the hardware actually does it

A companion is wired to a flight-controller UART (TX to RX, RX to TX, common ground) at a bit rate both sides are set to, with receive DMA on the controller's side (F10-29); on the companion, the port is a device file read by a routing program, which also offers UDP endpoints to local programs and to the network. D2 Power deserves care: a companion with a GPU can draw several amperes, and a brown-out that resets the companion in flight is exactly the "heartbeat stops" case of this chapter. Power it from its own regulator, and test what the vehicle does when the companion is unplugged in flight-like conditions, on the bench without propellers. S1 Telemetry radios have their own buffers, so a fading link often shows as bursts and long gaps rather than isolated losses.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Wiring, voltage levels and the serial-port settings for a specific companion and flight controller are not given here; they depend on the boards. Use the board makers' documentation and ArduPilot's companion-computer pages (D2). No hardware was connected in this build.

Worked example

Task. From the forensic evidence's rows 26–38, decide when a 5 s lost-link failsafe should have triggered if only the laptop counts.

  1. Last laptop heartbeat before the gap. LOG B, column 255/190: 27.10 s, seq 27. Next one: 37.10 s, seq 28. R4
  2. Sent or lost? Sequence 27 → 28 is one step: the laptop sent nothing in between. Its program stopped; the radio was fine.
  3. Timeout. The vehicle's settings in the log: enabled, 5.0 s. 27.10 + 5.0 = 32.10 s. The gap lasts 10 s, so the condition holds.
  4. Check the other direction. LOG A at the laptop: vehicle heartbeat 27.30 s seq 162, then 37.30 s seq 222. Step 6 per second (the vehicle sends other frames between heartbeats), so 60 / 6 − 1 = 9 heartbeats were sent but not received: the laptop was not listening either, consistent with a stopped program.
  5. Why no failsafe? Column 255/1 (the phone) has heartbeats every second from 20.75 s. If the vehicle counts every system-255 heartbeat, its longest gap after 20 s is about one second, and the 5 s rule never fires. The checker confirms both outcomes. R4

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Kofi recognises the head waiter's voice; a vehicle recognises a number. Any program that sends heartbeats with system ID 255 sounds like "the ground station" unless the vehicle is told which one is in control. R4
    • 

  • An assistant who falls silent has usually stopped helping; a companion's heartbeat thread can keep clicking while its planner is dead. Silence detection catches crashes of the whole program, not of its useful part. R3
    • 

  • Going back to the meeting point is always possible for Kofi; returning home needs a working position estimate. The failsafe's action can itself be impossible (F10-28).
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the companion's commands are ignored. Cause: wrong target system or component, or the vehicle only accepts commands from its configured ground station. How to find it: the COMMAND_ACK (if any) and the router's view of who was heard. Fix: correct IDs; configure the accepted sender.
    • 

  • Symptom: the vehicle refuses to arm from the companion. Cause: pre-arm checks fail. How to find it: status texts. Fix: read and resolve them; never bypass checks from code.
    • 

  • Symptom: the failsafe never fires in tests. Cause: another program's heartbeats count (forensic lab), or the failsafe is disabled or limited to some modes. How to find it: list every heartbeat source on every link. Fix: configure which system counts; test by killing the real controller process.
    • 

  • Symptom: failsafe triggers during normal flights. Cause: timeout shorter than the radio's fades, or the companion's heartbeat sharing a thread with slow work. How to find it: heartbeat gap histogram. Fix: a dedicated heartbeat timer; a timeout above the measured fades.
    • 

  • Symptom: duplicate or looping messages. Cause: two routers forwarding to each other. How to find it: repeated sequence numbers from one sender. Fix: never forward back on the arrival link; one router per link pair.
    •

Summary


    

  • Ground stations plan, monitor and command; companion computers compute and propose; the flight controller decides and stays safe.
    • 

  • A companion is a component of the vehicle's system; routing by system and component ID connects all programs across links.
    • 

  • Every command is checked by the vehicle and acknowledged; client programs must handle refusals.
    • 

  • A lost-link failsafe must watch the program in control, not "any heartbeat".
    • 

  • Sequence numbers separate "lost on the link" from "sender stopped".
    • 

  • Timeouts trade false alarms against time out of control; the action must be possible in the failure.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. lostlink_gen.cpp generates heartbeat send times: vehicle at x.30 s (sequence +6 per second), laptop 255/190 at x.10 s, phone 255/1 at x.75 s from 20.75 s. Events: a radio fade in both directions from 12.4 to 15.9 s (heartbeats sent but not received); the laptop's program frozen from 27.6 to 36.6 s (laptop sends nothing and receives nothing). lostlink_check.cc reads the table and replays the rule. R4


Observation. Gaps: LOG A 12.30–16.30 s (seq 72→96: 3 lost) and 27.30–37.30 s (seq 162→222: 9 lost); LOG B laptop 12.10–16.10 s (seq 12→16: 3 lost) and 27.10–37.10 s (seq 27→28: nothing sent); phone: no gaps from 20.75 s. R4


Reconstruction. The 4 s fade is shorter than 5 s: no failsafe, correctly. The laptop's 10 s silence starts after 27.10 s: the failsafe should have triggered at 32.10 s. R4


Root cause. A second ground-station app on a phone, also system 255 (component 1), connected at about 20.75 s and sent heartbeats every second. A vehicle that counts any system-255 heartbeat saw no gap longer than about one second after 20 s; under that rule the failsafe never triggers, which matches "no failsafe event recorded". The laptop link was healthy in the second gap; the laptop's program stopped (sequence step 1).


Fix and verification. Make the vehicle count only the controlling ground station (configure its identity, give each ground program a distinct system ID); remove uncontrolled apps from the link. Verify in SITL: freeze the controlling ground station with a second app connected; the action must start 5 s after its last heartbeat.


Prevention. A pre-flight check listing all heartbeat sources on the link; the failsafe test of this chapter's mini-project in every release; log which system the failsafe is watching.
