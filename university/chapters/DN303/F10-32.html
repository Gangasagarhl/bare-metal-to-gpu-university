Story hook

Kofi keeps a diary. Every few seconds he writes a short line: time, where he is, what he carries, how heavy the tray feels. At the front of the diary is a legend: "line starting with T: time, table, number of glasses". Anyone can read the diary, even a new manager who has never seen it before, because the legend is in the book itself.

One night Kofi stops in the middle of the room, puts the tray down and waits. The manager blames the heavy tray. The diary says something else: two minutes earlier the lights in the room flickered, and Kofi could no longer see the tables. Flight logs are the vehicle's diary, and this chapter teaches how they are written, how to read them without knowing their contents in advance, and how to find what really happened.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how a self-describing binary log works: format records first, then data records decoded by those formats.
  • Read such a log with a reader that knows nothing in advance, including damaged and truncated files.
  • Analyse a flight with a script: timeline of modes, messages and errors, energy, attitude, sensors, estimator health.
  • Diagnose an unexpected landing by building an evidence chain in time order, and reject a plausible decoy.
  • Choose between onboard logs and telemetry logs for a question.
Jargon box
DataFlash log (onboard log)
Simple words: the file the flight controller writes during a flight, with everything it measured and decided. Analogy: the waiter's diary. Precise: a binary log written by the autopilot to onboard storage (an SD card or flash chip), containing records from sensors, estimator, controllers, modes, events and parameters at their own rates; ArduPilot calls these DataFlash logs for historical reasons. D2
Self-describing log
Simple words: a log file that contains its own explanation of how to read it. Analogy: the legend at the front of the diary. Precise: a format in which special format records declare, for every record type, its name, length, field types and field names; a reader decodes the rest of the file from those declarations without a built-in message list. D2 R2
FMT record
Simple words: one entry of the legend. Analogy: "line starting with T means: time, table, glasses". Precise: a record of a reserved type that defines another record type: its numeric type, total length, short name, one format letter per field, and comma-separated field labels; the FMT record also describes itself. D2
Telemetry log
Simple words: a recording, made by the ground station, of the messages it received over the radio. Analogy: the head waiter's notes of what Kofi said on the radio. Precise: a timestamped capture of the MAVLink stream at the ground station; it holds only what was sent at telemetry rates and received, while the onboard log holds what the autopilot recorded at its own rates, including during link loss. D2
Evidence chain (flight review)
Simple words: a list of facts from the log, in time order, that explains what happened. Analogy: "the lights flickered, then Kofi lost sight of the tables, then he stopped". Precise: a time-ordered sequence of logged observations, each linked to the next by a known mechanism (a parameter, a failsafe rule, a control law), ending in the observed outcome, with competing explanations checked against the same data. R5

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A drone writes a diary while it flies: many short lines every second, about where it is, how it is tilted, how full its battery is, what it decided. The diary is written in a computer code, not in words, to save space.

The first pages of the diary explain the code: "a line of kind 134 means time, volts, amps". So a reading program can understand the diary without knowing it beforehand.

After a strange flight, engineers read the diary from start to end and put the important lines in order of time. Often the first guess ("the battery was empty") is wrong, and the diary shows the real reason.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What the autopilot logs

The diary records more than the head waiter ever hears. The ArduPilot documentation describes onboard logs written to the board's storage, with record types for attitude, sensors (IMU, GNSS, barometer, compass), the estimator, controller targets and outputs, battery, modes, events and errors, messages sent to the ground station, and the parameter values at the start of logging; which records are written is controlled by parameters. D2 The logger writes from a buffer in a background thread, so the main loop does not wait for the SD card; when the buffer fills, records are dropped, and the documentation describes how to see that this happened. D2 That buffer is the logging task of F10-28: a starved logging task is a gap in the diary.

A self-describing format

The legend comes first. The documentation describes DataFlash files as self-describing: format records at the start of the file define every record type's name, field types and labels, and every record starts with marker bytes and its type number. D2 Listing 1 writes our UDF format on the same idea: two marker bytes (A5 5A), a type byte, and a payload; type 128 is the format record and describes itself first. Listing 2 flies a short simulated survey and writes 20,362 bytes; its first 96 bytes, dumped in hex, show the FMT record describing FMT (length 0x59 = 89) and the start of the next one. R1 R2

Reading without prior knowledge

A new manager can read the diary from its legend. Listing 3 knows only the layout of the FMT record; it learns every other type from the file. On the normal flight it finds 840 records of 9 types over 39 s. R3 On a damaged copy (7 junk bytes inserted at offset 4,000, file cut at 9,000 bytes), it skips the 7 bytes by searching for the next marker, reads 356 records, and reports the cut-off record at the end instead of decoding garbage. R4 Real logs end this way when power is removed during writing; a reader that stops at the first error loses the most interesting seconds.

A first-pass analysis

Listing 4 answers the questions in the order an engineer asks them: what did the vehicle think it was doing (modes, messages, errors), was energy fine, was attitude sane, were the sensors fine, and when, if ever, did the estimator lose position. For the normal flight: armed at 2 s, AUTO at 3 s, LAND at mission end at 34.44 s, landed at 38.42 s; battery minimum 14.74 V, above its 14 V threshold; at least 13 satellites; position valid throughout. R3 Tools such as those in the unverified box do the same with plots; a script is repeatable and can run on every flight automatically.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Design choices in a log format

  • Self-description costs little and saves years. The 9 FMT records of our log take 801 bytes, about 4 % of a 39 s flight and less in longer flights; in exchange, a reader written today can read a log from a firmware version with new record types. R2
  • Resynchronisation needs markers. Without the two marker bytes, one inserted byte would shift every later record. With them, the reader recovered after 7 bytes. Two bytes can also occur inside data; a reader should check that a candidate record's type is known and, ideally, that the next record also starts with a marker. Listing 3 checks only the first. R4
  • No checksum per record. UDF, like the format recalled in the unverified box, has no per-record checksum: a flipped bit in a value is read as a valid value. Plausibility checks (jumps in time, values out of range) are the analyst's job.
  • Timestamps are per record. Every UDF data record starts with microseconds since boot. Records of different types are aligned by time, not by position in the file; rates differ (attitude 10 Hz, GNSS 5 Hz, battery 1 Hz here). R1

Building an evidence chain

A flight review is a forensic method (the five-step method of the university's forensic labs) applied to a log: observations with times; hypotheses; for each, a prediction that the log can confirm or refute; root cause; fix and verification. Three rules prevent the usual mistakes. Order everything by time: a cause precedes its effect. Look for the mechanism: a failsafe has a parameter and a rule, and the log should show the condition, then the action. Test the obvious suspect against the data instead of accepting it: in the forensic lab the battery's lowest value (14.80 V) is above the threshold and higher than in the normal flight, which did not land early (14.74 V). R3 R5

Onboard log or telemetry log?

The onboard log has every logged record at its full rate and continues when the radio fails; it is lost if the vehicle and its card are lost. The telemetry log has only what was streamed at telemetry rates and only what arrived, but it is safe on the ground and records the ground station's own actions. For a link-loss question (F10-31) you need both: the onboard log for what the vehicle heard, the telemetry log for what the ground heard. D2

How the hardware actually does it

On a ChibiOS flight controller the logger fills a RAM buffer from the main loop's tasks; a lower-priority thread writes the buffer to a FAT file system on an SD card through the SDIO or SPI driver (F10-27), or to a flash chip on boards without a card. D2 SD cards have unpredictable write latency: an internal erase can stall a write for a long time, which is why the buffer exists and why a slow card causes dropped records. D7 If power is cut, the last buffered data and possibly the file system's metadata are lost; that is the truncated file of Listing 3's damaged case.

Worked example

Task. From the hex dump of Listing 2's log, decode the first record and the start of the second without any tool.

  1. Bytes 0–2: a5 5a 80: markers, type 0x80 = 128: FMT. R2
  2. Bytes 3–4: 80 59: this FMT defines type 128, length 0x59 = 89 bytes. It describes itself.
  3. Bytes 5–8: 46 4d 54 00: name "FMT". Bytes 9–24: 42 42 6e 4e 5a 00 …: format "BBnNZ" padded with zeros to 16 bytes. Bytes 25–88: "Type,Length,Name,Format,Columns" padded to 64.
  4. Check the length: 3 + B(1) + B(1) + n(4) + N(16) + Z(64) = 89. The next record starts at offset 89 = 0x59.
  5. Offset 0x59: a5 5a 80 81 1f 50 41 …: another FMT, defining type 0x81 = 129, length 0x1F = 31, name "PA…" (PARM). Check: 3 + Q(8) + N(16) + f(4) = 31.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Kofi writes what he thinks matters; the autopilot writes what its parameters select. A record type that was not enabled is not in the log, and its absence is not evidence that nothing happened. D2
    • 

  • A diary line is either readable or not; a log value with a flipped bit looks valid. There is no per-record checksum; plausibility is the reader's job.
    • 

  • The diary records what Kofi saw; the log records what the sensors and estimator believed. After 26.18 s the logged position (POS) is the estimator's dead-reckoning, not where the vehicle was. R5
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the log ends seconds before the crash. Cause: buffered data lost at power loss. How to find it: the reader's truncation report. Fix: none after the fact; use the telemetry log for the last seconds; check the logger's settings for future flights.
    • 

  • Symptom: gaps in one record type. Cause: dropped records (slow card, starved logging task). How to find it: time differences between consecutive records of one type. Fix: a faster card; fewer or slower record types; check the scheduler (F10-28).
    • 

  • Symptom: the analysis blames the first suspicious value. Cause: no time ordering. How to find it: build the timeline first. Fix: require a mechanism for every link of the chain.
    • 

  • Symptom: units are wrong by a factor (centi-degrees, millivolts). Cause: the field's unit was assumed. How to find it: the format's unit records or the documentation. Fix: convert at load time, once.
    • 

  • Symptom: a reader crashes on a log from a newer firmware. Cause: the reader has a built-in message list. How to find it: unknown type numbers. Fix: read the formats from the file, as Listing 3 does.

Summary


    

  • Onboard (DataFlash) logs record sensors, estimator, control, modes, events and parameters at their own rates.
    • 

  • The format is self-describing: format records define every record type; readers learn it from the file.
    • 

  • Marker bytes let a reader skip damage; truncated files must be reported, not misread.
    • 

  • A flight review orders events in time, links them by mechanisms, and tests the obvious suspect with numbers.
    • 

  • Onboard and telemetry logs answer different questions; use both for link problems.
    • 

  • In the forensic case, a GNSS fix loss, not the battery, triggered the landing at 26.18 s.
