Story hook

Analogy

Kenji is filling the bath and wants it warm, not hot. He cannot stand over it, so every half minute he looks at the thermometer, turns the hot tap a little according to what he sees, and walks away; between looks the tap stays exactly where he left it. If he looks often, this works as well as standing there. If he looks rarely, he must turn the tap more gently, because each turn acts for a long time before he checks again.

Now imagine Kenji learned his "how much to turn" rule while checking every 3 seconds, and this week he checks every 30 seconds but still turns as if 3 seconds had passed. Each turn is ten times too strong for the time it acts. The bath swings from too hot to too cold. A digital controller is exactly this: it samples, computes, and holds its output until the next sample. This chapter shows how to turn a controller designed with derivatives and integrals into code that runs once per period, how the choice of formula and period decides whether the loop is stable, and a robot joint that started buzzing when its loop period changed and one constant in the code did not.

What you will be able to do

  • Draw a sampled control loop with sampler, discrete controller and zero-order hold, and say what each block does.
  • Discretise a PI controller with forward Euler, backward Euler and Tustin's rule, and write each as a difference equation in positional and incremental form.
  • Predict stability with poles in the z-plane: map a continuous pole and find the closed-loop poles of a sampled loop.
  • Choose a sample period from the loop's time scales and check the choice in simulation.
  • Diagnose a controller whose code assumes a different period from the one it runs at (this chapter's forensic lab).
Jargon box
Sample period (sampling rate)
Simple words: how often the controller looks and acts. Analogy: the time between Kenji's looks at the thermometer. Precise: the time T between successive executions of a discrete controller; the sampling rate is 1/T. Measurements y[k] = y(kT) and outputs u[k] are indexed by the sample number k. B1
Zero-order hold (ZOH)
Simple words: keep the output constant until the next update. Analogy: the tap stays where Kenji left it. Precise: u(t) = u[k] for kT ≤ t < (k+1)T; implemented by a DAC or PWM register written once per period. Combined with the sampler it adds a delay of about T/2 to the loop. B1
Difference equation
Simple words: the discrete version of a differential equation: the next value from the last few. Analogy: "turn the tap by this much, given what the thermometer said now and last time". Precise: a recurrence such as u[k] = u[k−1] + q0e[k] + q1e[k−1]; its transfer function in the variable z (one-sample shift) plays the role that s plays for differential equations. B1
Discretisation rule (forward Euler, backward Euler, Tustin)
Simple words: a recipe for replacing derivatives and integrals by sums and differences of samples. Analogy: different ways of estimating "how much water has flowed" from occasional looks. Precise: forward Euler s → (z − 1)/T, backward Euler s → (z − 1)/(zT), Tustin (bilinear, trapezoidal) s → (2/T)(z − 1)/(z + 1). For an integral these are the left rectangle, right rectangle and trapezoid rules. B1D1
Pole in the z-plane (discrete stability)
Simple words: the number that each step multiplies a disturbance by. Analogy: if every look-and-turn leaves the bath error at 0.8 of what it was, the error dies out; at −1.2, it flips and grows. Precise: a root z of the denominator of a discrete transfer function; a linear discrete system is stable when every pole has |z| < 1. A continuous pole s maps to z = esT under exact sampling. B1B2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A controller compares what you want with what you have, and pushes to close the gap. In a textbook it watches all the time. In a robot it is a small program that wakes up, say, a thousand times a second: it reads the sensor, does a little arithmetic, sets the motor, and sleeps. Between wake-ups the motor keeps the last setting.

The arithmetic needs to know how long it slept. The "I" part adds up the error over time, so it multiplies by the time step; the "D" part divides the change in error by the time step. If the program thinks it slept 1 millisecond but it really slept 10, the "D" part pushes ten times too hard, and the motor shakes.

And the wake-ups cannot be too far apart. If the motor moves a lot between two looks, the controller always reacts to old news, overshoots, and can make things worse instead of better.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From a PI law to a difference equation

The continuous PI controller of RB202 is u(t) = Kpe(t) + Ki∫0te dτ. The proportional term needs no change: u[k] uses e[k]. The integral I[k] ≈ ∫0kTe dτ must be built from samples, and there are three common rules: B1B2

  • Forward Euler (left rectangle): I[k] = I[k−1] + T e[k−1]. The newest error enters the integral one sample late.
  • Backward Euler (right rectangle): I[k] = I[k−1] + T e[k].
  • Tustin (trapezoid): I[k] = I[k−1] + (T/2)(e[k] + e[k−1]).

Subtracting u[k−1] from u[k] gives the incremental (velocity) form u[k] = u[k−1] + q0e[k] + q1e[k−1], with (q0, q1) = (Kp, −Kp + KiT) for forward Euler, (Kp + KiT, −Kp) for backward Euler and (Kp + KiT/2, −Kp + KiT/2) for Tustin. The incremental form stores no growing integral and makes output limits easy to apply; the positional form makes the integral visible for anti-windup. Both give the same numbers in exact arithmetic, and run R4 shows them agreeing to 2.2 × 10−16 in double. D1R4

A derivative term is discretised the same way, as (e[k] − e[k−1])/T, followed in practice by a low-pass filter because differencing amplifies sensor noise by 1/T (RB202 F9-21). The 1/T is the place where a wrong T does the most damage. B2

The experiment: three rules, five periods

Listing 1 controls a model motor speed, τw′ = −w + Ku with τ = 0.5 s and K = 2 (lab numbers, not a real motor), with Kp = 2 and Ki = 8. The continuous loop overshoots by 7.19 % and settles (within 2 %) at 0.865 s. Run R1: R1

  • At T = 1 ms all three rules are within 0.0023 of the continuous response; the choice does not matter.
  • At T = 10 ms the differences are about 0.02, overshoot 7.3–7.9 %.
  • At T = 50 ms and 100 ms overshoot grows (up to 19.6 % for forward Euler at 100 ms) and the response is visibly different from the design.
  • At T = 200 ms forward Euler overshoots 51.9 %, Tustin 84.6 %, and backward Euler is unstable: 699 % and growing.

The closed loop's own time scale is about 0.2 s (its poles are at s = −5 ± 2.65i, run R5). The period stops mattering once there are tens of samples per such time scale, and becomes the dominant effect when there are only one or two. R5B1

Poles: a first look

For w′ = −w/τ alone, exact sampling gives the pole z = e−T/τ. Forward Euler gives 1 − T/τ, which leaves the unit disk when T > 2τ: a stable system turned unstable by the formula alone (Figure 2; F0-76 found the same factor for the integrator). Backward Euler gives 1/(1 + T/τ), inside the disk for every T; Tustin gives (1 − T/2τ)/(1 + T/2τ), also inside for every T, but negative for T > 2τ, which means sign-alternating ringing the real system never had. R2D1

Watch out

"Backward Euler is always stable" is true for discretising a stable system on its own. It is not a promise about a loop: in run R1 the backward-Euler PI loop is the one that diverges at T = 200 ms, because its larger immediate gain q0 = Kp + KiT acts on a measurement that is a whole period old. Layer 3 computes the loop's poles and shows why.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Exact discretisation of the plant. With a zero-order hold, the plant τw′ = −w + Ku is reproduced exactly at the sample instants by w[k+1] = a w[k] + b u[k], with a = e−T/τ and b = (1 − a)K: between samples u is constant, so the differential equation can be solved in closed form. The controller C(z) = (q0z + q1)/(z − 1) and this plant give the closed-loop characteristic polynomial z2 + (bq0 − 1 − a)z + (a + bq1) = 0. B1D1

Design directly in discrete time. Instead of discretising a continuous design, a controller can be designed for the exact discrete plant (a, b): choose desired poles inside the unit circle and solve for q0, q1. This handles slow sampling properly and is the approach of computer-controlled systems texts. B1


Precision of the integrator. I[k] = I[k−1] + T e[k] adds a small number to a growing one, the situation of F0-74. In float with T = 1 ms, once |I| is large enough, increments of T × e below half an ULP of I are lost entirely and the integral stops moving (F0-74 measured a float time counter going wrong this way). The incremental form avoids a large accumulator in the controller, but the actuator command it accumulates must be checked the same way. Use double for accumulators, or bound them (anti-windup does), and test with long runs. D1B3


Timing is part of the model. The equations assume samples exactly T apart and computation taking no time. Real loops have jitter (varying T) and a computation delay between reading the sensor and writing the output. Both add phase lag; a fixed delay of one sample can be included in the discrete model as an extra pole at z = 0. Measuring the actual period and delay on the target is part of testing a controller. B1



How the hardware actually does it


On a microcontroller, a hardware timer raises an interrupt every T; the handler reads an ADC conversion or an encoder counter (the sampler), runs the difference equation, and writes a PWM compare register (the hold: the duty cycle stays until rewritten). On a robot computer running Linux, a real-time thread wakes on a clock instead, with more jitter. In both cases T exists twice: once in the timer configuration and once in the controller's arithmetic, and nothing in the hardware checks that they agree. B1U1

Safety

When you move a controller onto a real motor (RB202 F9-22), start with the output limited to a safe value, the mechanism free to move without hitting anything, and a way to cut motor power within reach. A controller that is unstable on a real motor can drive it to full power in a few periods, as the forensic lab's model does in 30 ms.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Kenji sees the bath change between looks if he wants to; a controller sees nothing. Anything that happens between samples, including a fast oscillation, is invisible or appears as a slower false one (aliasing), which is why sensors are filtered before sampling. B1
    • 

  • Kenji knows roughly how long he was away. The code knows only the T written in it (run R3). R3
    • 

  • A bath changes slowly and forgivingly. A motor's time constant can be milliseconds, so "rarely" for a motor is a few milliseconds, and instability reaches full power within a few periods. R3
    • 

  • Turning a tap is smooth; real outputs saturate. A PWM duty cycle cannot exceed 100 %, so a linear analysis holds only while the output stays inside its limits; saturated loops need anti-windup (RB202). B2
    •

Summary


    

  • A digital controller samples, computes a difference equation, and holds its output for one period T (Figure 1).
    • 

  • Forward Euler, backward Euler and Tustin discretise integrals and derivatives; they agree when T is small compared with the loop's time scales and differ sharply when it is not (run R1).
    • 

  • Stability means |z| < 1. Forward Euler maps a stable pole outside the circle for T > 2τ; backward Euler and Tustin never do for a system alone, but a sampled loop must be checked with its own closed-loop poles (run R5).
    • 

  • T appears in the timer and in the arithmetic; when they disagree, I and D gains are wrong by the ratio (run R3).
    • 

  • Integrators are long sums: watch float accumulation (F0-74).
    •
