Story hook

Analogy

Amara, Kenji and Laila each went to the same market and lost the price list. They still have their receipts. Amara bought 1 apple bag, 2 loaves and 2 cheeses for 3 coins; Kenji 4, 4 and 2 for 4 coins; Laila 4, 6 and 4 for 6 coins. Three receipts, three unknown prices. Kenji's trick: use his receipt to remove apples from the other two (subtract a quarter of his receipt from Amara's, all of it from Laila's), then use one of the new receipts to remove bread from the last. One receipt is left with only cheese on it, which gives the cheese price; then bread; then apples.

The trick has one weak spot. To remove apples, you divide by the number of apple bags on the receipt you use. If that number were smudged and nearly zero, you would divide by almost nothing, and any small error on that receipt would be multiplied enormously. So you always choose the receipt with the most apples to work with. That choice is called pivoting, and this chapter shows a robot arm's calibration that silently produced "not a number" because its firmware skipped it.

What you will be able to do

  • Solve a linear system Ax = b by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting and back substitution, by hand for 3 × 3 and in code for n × n.
  • Explain why a small pivot destroys the answer in floating point, and show it with a 2 × 2 example.
  • Describe the LU factorisation PA = LU, its cost of about 2n3/3 operations, and why you factor once and solve many times instead of forming A−1.
  • Use the residual b − Ax as a check, and state what a small residual does and does not prove.
  • Write result checks that cannot be fooled by NaN (this chapter's forensic lab).
Jargon box
Linear system
Simple words: several equations, each a weighted sum of the same unknowns. Analogy: the receipts, with the prices unknown. Precise: Ax = b with A an n × n matrix, b a known vector and x the unknown vector; it has exactly one solution when A is nonsingular (det A ≠ 0). B4
Gaussian elimination
Simple words: subtract multiples of one equation from the others until each equation has fewer unknowns. Analogy: using Kenji's receipt to remove apples from the others. Precise: for k = 1 … n−1, subtract lik = aik/akk times row k from each row i > k, producing an upper triangular system Ux = c, then solve it by back substitution from the last row up. B2
Pivot and partial pivoting
Simple words: the number you divide by at each step, and the rule of picking the biggest one available. Analogy: the apple count on the receipt you work with. Precise: the pivot of step k is akk at that moment; partial pivoting first swaps row k with the row i ≥ k whose |aik| is largest, so every multiplier satisfies |lik| ≤ 1. B2B1
LU factorisation
Simple words: elimination written down so it can be reused for new right-hand sides. Analogy: keeping a note of every "subtract a quarter of Kenji's receipt" so that tomorrow's receipts with the same items take only seconds. Precise: PA = LU with P a permutation matrix (the row swaps), L unit lower triangular (the multipliers) and U upper triangular; then Ax = b is solved by Ly = Pb and Ux = y. B2
Residual
Simple words: how far the answer fails to satisfy the equations. Analogy: put the computed prices back into the receipts and see by how much the totals are off. Precise: r = b − A x̂ for a computed x̂. A small residual means x̂ exactly solves a nearby problem; it does not by itself mean x̂ is close to x (F0-78). B1
Backward stability (of an algorithm)
Simple words: the computed answer is the exact answer to a slightly changed question. Analogy: the prices you found are exactly right for receipts that differ from yours by a crumb. Precise: an algorithm for Ax = b is backward stable if its x̂ satisfies (A + ΔA) x̂ = b with ‖ΔA‖/‖A‖ of order the unit roundoff u; Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting has this property in practice, with a factor called the growth factor. B1B2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When you have three puzzles that share the same three unknown numbers, you can solve them one unknown at a time: use one puzzle to wipe an unknown out of the others, and repeat. At the end one puzzle has a single unknown left, which you can read off, and then you go backwards filling in the rest.

The wiping-out step divides by a number from the puzzle you are using. Dividing by a tiny number makes everything huge, and the computer's small rounding crumbs become huge too. So before each wiping step, swap the puzzles so that you divide by the biggest number you can. That one swap is the difference between the right answer and garbage in Figure 2.

At the end, put your answer back into the puzzles and see whether they come out right. That check is useful, but the robot arm in this chapter's forensic lab shows that a check can be fooled when the answer is "not a number" at all.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Elimination and back substitution

Write the system as an augmented matrix [A | b]. Step k removes the unknown xk from rows k+1 … n: for each such row i compute the multiplier lik = aik/akk and replace row i by row i − lik × row k (the right-hand side too). After n − 1 steps the matrix is upper triangular, U, and the right-hand side has become c. Back substitution then computes xn = cn/unn, and for i = n−1 down to 1, xi = (ci − Σj>i uijxj)/uii. Figure 1 does this for a 3 × 3 system. B2B4

Cost, and why not to invert

Step k updates (n − k) rows of (n − k) entries with one multiply and one subtract each. Summed over k this is about 2n3/3 floating-point operations; back substitution adds only about n2. If the multipliers are kept (they form L) together with the row swaps (P), a new right-hand side costs only about 2n2: two triangular solves. Computing A−1 costs about three times as much as the factorisation, and x = A−1b is in general no more accurate, often less. Rule: factor, then solve; do not invert. B2B1D1

Why a small pivot breaks it

Take [[e, 1], [1, 1]] x = (1, 2) with a tiny e. The exact solution is x1 = 1/(1 − e), x2 = (1 − 2e)/(1 − e), both very close to 1. Without pivoting, the multiplier is l = 1/e and the second row becomes (1 − 1/e) x2 = 2 − 1/e. For e = 10−16, 1/e = 1016, where the spacing of doubles is 2 (F0-73); 1 − 1016 rounds to −1016 and 2 − 1016 is exact, so x2 = (1016 − 2)/1016 = 0.9999999999999998. Then x1 = (1 − x2)/e = 2.2 × 10−16/10−16 = 2.22: a catastrophic cancellation (F0-74) divided by a tiny number. Run R2 prints exactly 2.22044605. The information in row 2 (the "1" in 1 − 1/e) was rounded away by the huge multiplier. D1R2

With partial pivoting, the rows are swapped first: the pivot is 1, the multiplier is e, and nothing large ever appears. Run R2 shows the residual staying at 1.1 × 10−16 or 0 for all e, while without pivoting it grows to about 1. Note the warning sign: the problem itself is perfectly well behaved (its answer barely depends on e); only the algorithm was bad. R2

What the residual tells you

The residual r = b − Ax̂ is cheap to compute and needs no knowledge of the true x. A small residual relative to ‖A‖‖x̂‖ means x̂ is the exact solution of a slightly perturbed system: the algorithm did its job. Whether x̂ is then close to x depends on how sensitive the problem is: the condition number of F0-78. And a residual check is only as good as its code: NaN compares false with everything (F0-73), so a check that is not written for it can wave NaN through. B1S1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Backward error of GEPP. Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting computes x̂ with (A + ΔA)x̂ = b, where |ΔA| is bounded entrywise by a modest multiple of nu times the entries of |L̂||Û|. With |lij| ≤ 1, the size of Û relative to A, the growth factor ρ, decides the bound. For partial pivoting ρ can reach 2n−1 for specially built matrices, but such growth is rarely met in practice; complete pivoting (searching rows and columns) bounds it much better at the cost of an O(n3) search. This is why libraries use partial pivoting by default. B1B2

Special structure. A symmetric positive definite matrix (a covariance, the normal equations of a calibration, a stiffness matrix) can be factored as A = RTR by Cholesky factorisation in about n3/3 operations, with no pivoting needed and guaranteed stability. Arms A, B and D of the forensic lab have such matrices; arm C's does not, which matters for the fix. Banded and sparse matrices (meshes, robot chains, factor graphs) have specialised elimination orders that avoid filling in zeros. B2

Iterative refinement. Given x̂, compute r = b − Ax̂ (in higher precision if available), solve A d = r with the existing factors, and set x̂ ← x̂ + d. One or two steps can recover accuracy lost in a low-precision factorisation; this is the basis of mixed-precision solvers that factor in FP32 or FP16 and refine in FP64 (F0-80). B1

NaN as data. IEEE 754 defines comparisons involving NaN as unordered, so x < t and x > t are both false. C's fmax treats a single NaN argument as missing data and returns the other argument. A loop worst = fmax(worst, |r_i|) therefore reports 0 when every ri is NaN. Robust checks test std::isfinite on the result, or are written as !(err <= tol) so that NaN fails. S1B3R3

How the hardware actually does it

The inner operation of elimination is "row i −= m × row k": a multiply and an add per entry, which compilers turn into vector instructions and, where allowed, fused multiply-adds (FMA, SP302). For large n, the 2n3/3 operations are reorganised into blocks so that most of the work becomes matrix–matrix multiplication (GEMM), the operation that caches and GPUs run fastest (the GEMM kernels of faculty F6). Small systems, like the 3 × 3 calibration of the forensic lab, cost a few dozen operations and fit in registers; there the danger is not speed but missing pivoting and missing checks. B2D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The names and behaviour of library routines were not checked in this build: LAPACK's general solver (dgesv, LU with partial pivoting), its Cholesky routines, Eigen's decompositions (for example PartialPivLU), and batched small-matrix factorisations in GPU libraries such as cuBLAS and cuSOLVER. Read each library's documentation for the version in your dossier before relying on its pivoting, its error reporting on singular matrices, or its performance.

Worked example

Solve by hand, with partial pivoting: x1 + 2x2 + 2x3 = 3, 4x1 + 4x2 + 2x3 = 4, 4x1 + 6x2 + 4x3 = 6.

  1. Step 1, pivot search in column 1: |1|, |4|, |4|. The first largest is row 2; swap rows 1 and 2. Pivot 4.
  2. Eliminate x1: row 2 (old row 1) − 0.25 × row 1: (1, 2, 2 | 3) − (1, 1, 0.5 | 1) = (0, 1, 1.5 | 2). Row 3 − 1 × row 1: (4, 6, 4 | 6) − (4, 4, 2 | 4) = (0, 2, 2 | 2).
  3. Step 2, pivot search in column 2 below row 1: |1|, |2|. Swap rows 2 and 3. Pivot 2.
  4. Eliminate x2: multiplier 1/2 = 0.5; (0, 1, 1.5 | 2) − 0.5 × (0, 2, 2 | 2) = (0, 0, 0.5 | 1).
  5. Back substitution: x3 = 1/0.5 = 2; x2 = (2 − 2 × 2)/2 = −1; x1 = (4 − 4 × (−1) − 2 × 2)/4 = 1.
  6. Check in the original equations: 1 − 2 + 4 = 3 ✓, 4 − 4 + 4 = 4 ✓, 4 − 6 + 8 = 6 ✓.
  7. Count (as the code of Listing 1 does it): step 1 computes 2 multipliers and updates 2 rows of 4 numbers (3 in A, 1 in b), 8 multiply–subtract pairs; step 2 computes 1 multiplier and updates 3 numbers; back substitution does 3 multiply–subtract pairs and 3 divisions. In total 14 multiply–subtract pairs and 6 divisions, 34 operations, against 2n3/3 = 18 for the leading term with n = 3: at this size the lower-order terms dominate. D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Receipts have whole numbers; matrices have rounded ones. In the story the arithmetic is exact and pivoting is a matter of comfort; in floating point it decides whether the answer is right (Figure 2). R2
    • 

  • A smudged receipt is visibly smudged. A tiny pivot can appear in the middle of elimination from numbers that all looked fine at the start, so the check must be in the algorithm, at every step. B2
    • 

  • Real receipts can be checked against the shop. A computed x has no shop: the residual is the check you have, and it says nothing about how sensitive the prices are to a small error on a receipt (F0-78). B1
    • 

  • Three friends make three receipts; real systems have thousands of unknowns. Then cost, memory layout and sparsity matter as much as the method. B2
