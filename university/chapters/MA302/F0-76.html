Story hook

Analogy

The power is out and Kenji walks from the sofa to the kitchen in the dark. He knows the hallway curves. His first plan: face the way the hallway points where he stands, take a big step, then look again. On a straight hallway this works; on a curve, every big step carries him a little too far towards the outside wall, and the errors add up until he bumps into it. His second plan: before each step, peek ahead at where the hallway points halfway along and at the end, average what he sees, then step. He takes the same number of steps and arrives in the kitchen.

A simulator moves a robot, a drone or a pendulum the same way: the equations tell it which way the state is changing now, and it must take a finite time step. Taking the "now" direction for the whole step is Euler's method; peeking a few times inside the step is a Runge–Kutta method. This chapter integrates a pendulum both ways, measures how the error shrinks as the step shrinks, and finds the game bug in which a swing pushes itself higher and higher with nobody pushing it.

What you will be able to do

  • Rewrite an equation of motion as a first-order system x′ = f(t, x) and step it with explicit Euler, semi-implicit Euler and the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4).
  • Integrate a pendulum with two methods and compare them against a reference (the MA302 lab).
  • Measure the order of accuracy of a method by halving the step, and explain local and global truncation error.
  • Explain why explicit Euler adds energy to an oscillator and why semi-implicit Euler does not.
  • Diagnose a simulation that gains energy and blows up at low frame rates (this chapter's forensic lab).
Jargon box
Ordinary differential equation (initial value problem)
Simple words: a rule that says how fast the state changes, plus where it starts. Analogy: "the hallway points this way here" and "you start at the sofa". Precise: x′(t) = f(t, x(t)), x(t0) = x0, where x is a vector (for a pendulum: angle θ and angular velocity ω); higher derivatives are turned into extra components (MA301 F0-67). B2
Time step (step size h)
Simple words: how far forward in time a simulator jumps at once. Analogy: the length of Kenji's stride. Precise: the increment h = tn+1 − tn of a one-step method xn+1 = xn + h Φ(tn, xn, h). B2
Explicit (forward) Euler method
Simple words: move along today's direction for the whole step. Analogy: Kenji's first plan. Precise: xn+1 = xn + h f(tn, xn); a first-order method. B2
Semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler method
Simple words: update the velocity first, then move the position with the new velocity. Analogy: turn first, then step. Precise: for x′ = v, v′ = a(x): vn+1 = vn + h a(xn), xn+1 = xn + h vn+1; first order, and symplectic for mechanical systems of this form, which keeps the energy error bounded over long times. B3
Runge–Kutta method (RK4)
Simple words: look at the direction several times inside a step and average. Analogy: Kenji's peeks along the hallway. Precise: the classical fourth-order method: k1 = f(t, x), k2 = f(t + h/2, x + (h/2)k1), k3 = f(t + h/2, x + (h/2)k2), k4 = f(t + h, x + h k3), xn+1 = xn + (h/6)(k1 + 2k2 + 2k3 + k4). B2
Order of accuracy (local and global truncation error)
Simple words: how fast a method's error shrinks when you shrink the step. Analogy: halve the stride: does Kenji end half as far off, or sixteen times closer? Precise: a method has order p if its local error per step is O(hp+1) and its global error at a fixed end time is O(hp); halving h divides the global error by about 2p. Euler: p = 1; RK4: p = 4. B2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A pendulum's rule is simple: the further it hangs to one side, the harder gravity pulls it back. The rule tells you how the speed changes right now. A computer cannot follow "right now" continuously; it jumps forward by a small time, say one hundredth of a second, and updates the angle and the speed.

The simplest way, Euler's way, uses the pull at the start of the jump for the whole jump. But the pull changes during the jump. For a swinging pendulum, Euler's mistakes all push the same way: the simulated pendulum swings a little higher each time. Run R1 shows it more than doubling its energy in ten seconds.

Two fixes work. Semi-implicit Euler changes the order: first update the speed, then move with the new speed. That tiny change stops the energy from creeping up. Runge–Kutta looks at the pull four times inside each jump and averages; it costs four times the work per jump but is millions of times more accurate here.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From an equation of motion to a first-order system

The ideal pendulum of length L obeys θ″ = −(g/L) sin θ. With ω = θ′ it becomes the first-order system B2B4

θ′ = ω, ω′ = −(g/L) sin θ

which is the function deriv in pendulum.hpp. This course uses g = 9.81 m/s2 and L = 1 m as model values. Without friction the energy per unit mass (divided by L2), E = ½ω2 + (g/L)(1 − cos θ), is constant; that gives a check that needs no reference solution. B4D1

Three one-step methods

Explicit Euler replaces the derivative by a forward difference: xn+1 = xn + h f(xn). Taylor's theorem says the true solution satisfies x(t + h) = x(t) + h x′(t) + (h2/2) x″(ξ), so one Euler step is off by about (h2/2)|x″|: the local truncation error is O(h2). Reaching a fixed time T takes T/h steps, so the errors add to O(h): the global error is first order. B2

Semi-implicit Euler uses the new velocity for the position update. Its order is still 1, but for mechanical systems x″ = a(x) it is symplectic: it preserves the area of small regions of (x, v) space, and as a consequence its energy error stays bounded instead of growing. B3

RK4 evaluates f four times per step and combines the slopes with weights 1, 2, 2, 1 (the Jargon box). Its local error is O(h5) and its global error O(h4). B2

Measuring the order

Listing 2 integrates to T = 2 s with h = 0.1, 0.05, …, 0.003125 and divides each error by the next. A method of order p should give ratios near 2p. Run R2: semi-implicit Euler's ratios are 2.05, 2.03, 2.02, 2.01, 2.01 (order 1); RK4's are 19.6, 18.2, 17.2, 16.6, 16.3, approaching 16 (order 4). Explicit Euler's first ratio is 0.84, not 2: at h = 0.1 the error is so large that its error at T happened to be smaller than at h = 0.05; only for small h do its ratios settle at 2.00. Orders describe the limit h → 0, so measure them with several small steps. R2B2

Why explicit Euler adds energy

For small angles sin θ ≈ θ and the pendulum is the linear oscillator θ″ = −ω02θ with ω02 = g/L. One Euler step maps (θ, ω) to (θ + hω, ω − hω02θ). Substituting into E′ = ω2 + ω02θ2 gives E′n+1 = (1 + h2ω02) E′n exactly: every step multiplies the energy by a factor slightly above 1, whatever h is. With h = 0.01 s and ω02 = 9.81 s−2, 1,000 steps multiply it by (1.000981)1000 ≈ 2.7. Run R1 measured 2.13 for a 1 rad swing, smaller because sin θ < θ makes the large-angle pendulum softer than the linear model. For semi-implicit Euler the same calculation gives a step matrix with determinant exactly 1, so the energy only wobbles. D1R1B3

Watch out

"Accurate" and "stable" are different questions. RK4 is far more accurate than semi-implicit Euler on this problem, but over very long runs RK4's tiny energy drift (run R1: about −10−8 in 10 s) keeps one sign, while semi-implicit Euler's larger wobble does not grow. For orbits, molecules and long robot simulations the choice depends on what must be preserved.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Stability regions. Apply a method to the test equation x′ = λx (λ complex). One step multiplies x by a stability function R(hλ): R(z) = 1 + z for Euler, and the degree-4 Taylor polynomial 1 + z + z2/2 + z3/6 + z4/24 for RK4. The numerical solution stays bounded only if |R(hλ)| ≤ 1. An undamped oscillator has λ = ±iω0, on the imaginary axis, where |1 + iω0h| = √(1 + h2ω02) > 1: explicit Euler is unstable for every h (the energy factor above is |R|2). RK4's stability region includes a segment of the imaginary axis around 0, so for small enough hω0 it is stable there. F0-79 meets the same mathematics when a controller is discretised. B2D1

Stiff systems. If some components decay much faster than the motion of interest (a stiff motor current next to a slow mechanical speed), explicit methods need h smaller than the fastest time constant merely to stay stable. Implicit methods (backward Euler, implicit Runge–Kutta, BDF) solve an equation at each step and remain stable with large h. B2

Adaptive step size. Embedded Runge–Kutta pairs compute two estimates of different order from shared evaluations of f; their difference estimates the local error, and the step is enlarged or shrunk to keep it below a tolerance. Production integrators do this by default. A real-time simulator or controller usually cannot: it must advance exactly one fixed period per tick, which is why fixed-step methods and the analysis of this chapter matter for robots and drones. B2

Rounding versus truncation. Each step also rounds (F0-74). With N = T/h steps, rounding error grows roughly like Nu while truncation error falls like hp, so, as with the finite difference of F0-74, there is a smallest useful step. In double precision with RK4 that limit is far below any step a simulator uses; in float it can be reached, and the time variable itself must not be accumulated as t += h (F0-74's forensic lab). B1D1

How the hardware actually does it

Nothing in the hardware integrates: a simulator is ordinary floating-point arithmetic repeated once per step, and its cost is the number of evaluations of f. RK4 costs four evaluations per step against Euler's one, so the fair comparison is error per evaluation: in run R2, RK4 with h = 0.1 (80 evaluations to reach 2 s) is already more accurate (2.1 × 10−4) than explicit Euler with h = 0.003125 (640 evaluations, 2.2 × 10−2). R2

On a robot or a flight controller the step is often tied to a hardware timer (the control loop period) or to a sensor's sample rate, and the state may be float to save time and memory on a microcontroller. Then the choice of method, step and precision is a design decision recorded in the software, not a free parameter. On a GPU, many independent simulations (one per thread) are integrated in parallel with exactly the same arithmetic. U1

Worked example

One step of h = 0.1 s from θ = 1 rad, ω = 0, with g/L = 9.81 s−2.

  1. Slope at the start: k1 = f(1, 0) = (ω, −9.81 sin 1) = (0, −8.254830).
  2. Explicit Euler: θ = 1 + 0.1 × 0 = 1, ω = 0 + 0.1 × (−8.254830) = −0.825483. The angle has not moved at all, because the start slope of θ is 0.
  3. Semi-implicit Euler: ω = −0.825483 first, then θ = 1 + 0.1 × (−0.825483) = 0.917452.
  4. RK4: k2 = f(1 + 0.05 × 0, 0 + 0.05 × (−8.254830)) = (−0.412742, −8.254830); k3 = f(1 + 0.05 × (−0.412742), −0.412742) = (−0.412742, −8.143696); k4 = f(1 + 0.1 × (−0.412742), 0.1 × (−8.143696)) = (−0.814370, −8.029094). Then θ = 1 + (0.1/6)(0 − 0.825484 − 0.825484 − 0.814370) = 0.958911 and ω = (0.1/6)(−8.254830 − 16.509660 − 16.287392 − 8.029094) = −0.818016.
  5. Compare with a reference (RK4 with 10,000 sub-steps): θ = 0.958910, ω = −0.818071. Euler is off by 0.04 in θ after one step; RK4 by 10−6.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Kenji can feel the wall; a simulator cannot. Explicit Euler never notices that it is drifting; nothing in the method checks energy or position. Only a separate check (energy, a reference, an order test) reveals it. R1
    • 

  • Peeking costs Kenji nothing; each RK4 "peek" costs a full evaluation of f. For a complex robot model, f can be expensive, so the comparison must be per evaluation, not per step. R2
    • 

  • A hallway is fixed; the pendulum's "direction" depends on where the simulation already is. Errors change the future slopes too, which is why errors can grow systematically (Euler's energy) rather than only add up. B2
    • 

  • Smaller steps are always safer in the dark; in floating point they are not without limit. Very small steps mean very many roundings (Layer 3). B1
    •

Lab


Goal


Integrate a pendulum with two methods (the MA302 course lab), compare both with a reference, measure their orders, and choose a method and step for a 60-second simulation with a stated accuracy.


Hardware and software needed


A computer with a C++20 compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0. No physical pendulum: the model is in pendulum.hpp.


Safety


None beyond normal computer use. (If you build a real pendulum to compare, use a small soft weight on a string and keep faces away from its path.)


Steps


    

  1. Build and run Listings 1 and 2; compare with runs R1 and R2.
    2. 

  2. Add the midpoint method (RK2): k1 = f(x), k2 = f(x + (h/2)k1), x + h k2. Measure its order with Listing 2.
    3. 

  3. Add linear friction ω′ = −(g/L) sin θ − cω with c = 0.2 s−1. Now energy should fall; check that semi-implicit Euler and RK4 agree on how fast.
    4. 

  4. Requirement: simulate 60 s of the undamped pendulum from 1 rad with |θ error| < 10−3 rad at the end, using as few evaluations of f as possible. Pick a method and h from your order measurements, predict the error, then run and check.
    5. 

  5. Repeat step 4 with all state variables in float. Does the same h still meet the requirement?
    6. 



Expected observations


RK2 shows ratios near 4 (order 2). With friction, all stable methods show decaying energy at similar rates for small h. For step 4, RK4 needs a much larger step than either Euler method for the same accuracy; your prediction from the order should be within a factor of a few. In float, the end error stops improving below some h, because rounding takes over (F0-74).


Verification


Order ratios within 10 % of 2p for the three smallest steps; the 60-second requirement met against a reference with h/100; energy drift documented for each method.


Troubleshooting


    

  • RK4 ratios near 2 instead of 16 → a stage uses k1 where it should use k2, or the weights are wrong → compare one step with the worked example.
    • 

  • Ratios jump around → errors are near the reference's own error → refine the reference or use larger h.
    • 

  • Everything diverges → h is far too large or the sign of the restoring term is wrong → check that small swings oscillate with period near 2π√(L/g).
    • 



Extension


Implement an adaptive RK4 by step doubling: take one step of h and two of h/2, use their difference as an error estimate, and adjust h to keep it below a tolerance. Count evaluations for the 60-second requirement and compare with your fixed-step choice.
