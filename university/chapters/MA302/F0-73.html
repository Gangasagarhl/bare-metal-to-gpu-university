Story hook

Analogy

Amara bakes for the whole street on Saturdays. Her kitchen drawer holds a set of measuring scoops: a tiny one, one twice as big, one twice as big again, up to a scoop the size of a bucket. Every scoop has the same eight marks on its side. To measure anything she picks the biggest scoop the amount fits in, then reads the nearest mark. A pinch of salt is measured to a fraction of a gram; a sack of flour only to the nearest cupful. Nobody in her kitchen minds, because a cupful is tiny compared with a sack.

Your computer measures numbers the same way. It picks a power of two (the scoop), then stores a fixed number of binary marks on it (the significand). That is a floating-point number. This chapter takes the 32 bits of a C++ float apart switch by switch, so that later chapters can predict exactly when "nearest mark" turns a correct program into a wrong answer.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how IEEE 754 binary32 and binary64 represent a number with a sign, a biased exponent and a fraction, including subnormal numbers, signed zeros, infinities and NaN.
  • Encode a short binary fraction such as 6.5 or −0.75 into its 32-bit pattern by hand, and check the result with a program.
  • Compute the spacing between neighbouring floats (the ULP) at any magnitude and the largest integer that a float stores exactly.
  • Explain why rounding to nearest makes 0.1 inexact and why two programs can print different digits for the "same" number.
  • Diagnose a counter that stops counting because it was stored in a float (this chapter's forensic lab).
Jargon box
Floating-point number
Simple words: a number stored as "this many marks, on a scoop of this size". Analogy: Amara picks a scoop, then reads the nearest mark. Precise: a value (−1)s × m × 2e, where the significand m has a fixed number p of binary digits and the exponent e lies in a fixed range; the binary point "floats" with e. S1S2
IEEE 754 binary32 / binary64
Simple words: the agreed 32-bit and 64-bit layouts for floating-point numbers; C++ float and double use them on this course's machine. Analogy: two standard scoop sets that every kitchen buys from the same catalogue. Precise: binary32: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 fraction bits, precision p = 24; binary64: 1, 11 and 52 bits, p = 53. On this build machine std::numeric_limits<float>::is_iec559 is 1 and digits is 24 (53 for double). S1R2
Significand (and fraction field)
Simple words: the digits of the number, without its size. Analogy: the marks on the chosen scoop. Precise: for a normal number the significand is 1.f in binary: a hidden leading 1 followed by the stored fraction field f (23 bits in binary32). Older texts say "mantissa". S1
Biased exponent
Simple words: the power of two, stored with a constant added so that it is never negative. Analogy: the scoop's number in the drawer, counted from the smallest. Precise: in binary32 the 8-bit field E stores e + 127 for normal numbers (E = 1 … 254); E = 0 and E = 255 are reserved for zeros/subnormals and infinities/NaN. S1R1
Subnormal number
Simple words: a tiny number below the smallest normal one, stored with fewer significant digits. Analogy: the smallest scoop, read with some of its marks covered up. Precise: E = 0 and f ≠ 0 encodes 0.f × 2−126 in binary32; subnormals fill the gap between 0 and 2−126 with equal spacing 2−149 ("gradual underflow"). S1R1
ULP (unit in the last place)
Simple words: the gap between a float and the next bigger float. Analogy: the distance between two neighbouring marks on the scoop you are using. Precise: for a binary32 normal number with exponent e, ulp = 2e−23; it doubles at each power of two. Measured here with std::nextafter. D1R2
Machine epsilon
Simple words: how far 1 is from the next number up. Analogy: the gap between marks on the "1 cup" scoop. Precise: ε = 21−p: 2−23 ≈ 1.19 × 10−7 for float and 2−52 ≈ 2.22 × 10−16 for double, as std::numeric_limits<T>::epsilon() printed in run R2. Do not confuse it with the unit roundoff u = ε/2 of F0-74. B2R2
Round to nearest, ties to even
Simple words: the default rule: pick the closest float; on an exact tie, pick the one whose last bit is 0. Analogy: read the nearest mark; if the level is exactly halfway, always choose the "even" mark so that ties do not all lean one way. Precise: the default rounding-direction attribute of IEEE 754 for binary formats; every basic operation (+, −, ×, ÷, square root) returns the exact result rounded by this rule. S1S2
NaN and infinity
Simple words: special patterns meaning "too big to store" and "not a number at all". Analogy: a note in the recipe saying "overflowed the bucket" or "this step made no sense". Precise: E = 255 with f = 0 encodes ±∞; E = 255 with f ≠ 0 encodes NaN; a NaN compares unequal to everything, including itself. S1R1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Write a big number in your head: three hundred and forty thousand. Scientists write it as 3.4 × 105: a short list of digits, and a power of ten that says how big. A floating-point number is the same idea in binary: a short list of binary digits, and a power of two. The power of two is Amara's choice of scoop. The digits are the marks she reads.

Because every scoop has the same number of marks, a big scoop has big gaps between its marks. A float near 1 can tell apart numbers about one ten-millionth apart. A float near ten billion can only tell apart numbers 1,024 apart (run R2). Both are equally precise relative to their size. That is the deal floating point offers: the same number of correct digits at every size, instead of the same number of correct decimal places.

A float cannot store most numbers exactly. 0.1 in binary is 0.000110011001100… forever, like ⅓ in decimal. The computer keeps the nearest float, which is 0.100000001490116119384765625 (run R1). Most of the time that tiny difference does not matter. The rest of this course is about the times when it does.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The three fields

A binary32 number is 32 bits: bit 31 is the sign s, bits 30–23 are the exponent field E, bits 22–0 are the fraction field f. For a normal number (1 ≤ E ≤ 254) the value is S1S2

value = (−1)^s × 1.f × 2^(E − 127)

where 1.f means "binary 1, binary point, then the 23 fraction bits". The leading 1 is not stored, because every normal number has it: that is the hidden bit, and it is why the precision is p = 24 bits although only 23 are stored. The constant 127 is the bias. Listing 1 extracts the fields with shifts and masks (F0-25) and rebuilds each value from them; for every normal number in run R1 the rebuilt value equals the original exactly. S1R1

Rounding to nearest

When a result is not a float, IEEE 754 delivers the float nearest to the exact result; on a tie it chooses the neighbour with an even last bit S1. The tie rule shows up in run R1: 16,777,217 = 224 + 1 lies exactly halfway between the floats 16,777,216 and 16,777,218, and the compiler stores it as 16,777,216, whose fraction field ends in 0 R1. Run R2 shows the same tie when adding: 224 + 1 gives 16,777,216 but 224 + 2 gives 16,777,218 R2. So every integer up to 224 = 16,777,216 is exact in float, and above it only every second integer is. For double the limit is 253 D1.

Rounding to nearest bounds the relative error of storing a number x in the normal range: |fl(x) − x| ≤ ½ ulp, which is at most 2−p|x|. F0-74 builds the whole error analysis of this course on that one inequality S2B1.

Watch out

Printed digits are not the stored number. 0.1f prints as 0.1 with the default six significant digits, but run R1 shows that it is 0.100000001490116…, and 0.1 as a double is 0.100000000000000005551… (run R2). To print a float so that it can be read back exactly, use at least max_digits10 significant digits (9 for float, 17 for double).

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What the standard specifies. IEEE 754 defines formats (binary16, binary32, binary64, binary128 and decimal formats), the values each encodes, rounding-direction attributes (round to nearest with ties to even as the default for binary, ties away from zero, and three directed roundings towards +∞, −∞ and 0), the operations that must be correctly rounded (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, square root, fused multiply-add, conversions), and five exceptions (invalid operation, division by zero, overflow, underflow, inexact) with their default results. Exact clause numbers differ between editions; this chapter names them by title and the dossier must record the edition read. S1

Correct rounding is the contract that makes analysis possible. Because each basic operation returns "the exact result, rounded", fl(a op b) = (a op b)(1 + δ) with |δ| ≤ u in the normal range. This is the standard model of F0-74. It holds per operation, not per expression: a + b + c is two operations with two roundings, and the order in which they happen changes the answer (F0-75). S2B1

Subnormals and gradual underflow. Without subnormals, the gap between 0 and the smallest normal 2−126 would be much larger than the gap between 2−126 and its neighbour 2−126 + 2−149. Subnormals fill it with equally spaced numbers, which keeps the property "x − y = 0 exactly when x = y". The price is lost relative precision: the smallest subnormal has one significant bit. Some hardware and compiler modes replace subnormals by zero ("flush to zero"); nvcc, for example, has an option for it (see the hardware section). S1S2R6

Signed zero, infinity and NaN. 1/(+0) = +∞ and 1/(−0) = −∞ in the default exception handling, which preserves the sign of an underflowed result. Overflow produces ±∞; 0/0, ∞ − ∞ and √(−1) produce NaN, and NaN propagates through later arithmetic. A NaN compares false with every comparison operator except !=, so x != x is true exactly for NaN; prefer std::isnan. This makes NaN dangerous in checks written as "fail if error > tolerance": a NaN error passes such a check. F0-77's forensic lab finds exactly that bug in a real run. S1B2

The language layer. C++ does not itself require IEEE 754; std::numeric_limits<T>::is_iec559 tells you whether the implementation claims to follow it (IEC 559 is the international twin of IEEE 754). On this build machine both float and double report 1 B2R2. std::bit_cast (C++20) reads a float's bits without undefined behaviour; older code used memcpy or a union, and the union version is not valid C++ B2. Compilers may also evaluate expressions in a wider format or contract a * b + c into one fused operation, depending on flags; F0-80 shows nvcc doing the latter by default.

How the hardware actually does it

On this course's x86-64 build machine, g++ compiles a float addition to the SSE instruction addss ("add scalar single") and a double addition to addsd (run R5, with -O2). These instructions implement the binary32 and binary64 additions of IEEE 754, with the rounding mode taken from a control register; the default is round to nearest B3R5.



A floating-point adder works on the fields of Figure 1: it compares the exponents, shifts the smaller operand's significand right until the exponents match, adds the significands, normalises the result back to the form 1.f, and rounds. The bits shifted out past the last place are what rounding error is made of; hardware keeps a few extra bits during the shift so that the final result is correctly rounded S2B1.


GPUs implement the same formats. nvcc's own help text, printed in this build, describes a switch that flushes single-precision subnormals to zero, off by default and switched on by --use_fast_math (run R6). The behaviour on a real GPU was not observed: there is no GPU in the build container. R6

Which GPU instructions are correctly rounded, how subnormals are handled by each instruction, and which intrinsics trade accuracy for speed are documented in NVIDIA's CUDA Programming Guide (its appendix on floating-point standard and mathematical functions) and in AMD's HIP and ISA documentation. Worked example


Encode 6.5 as a binary32 float.


    

  1. Binary: 6 = 110, 0.5 = 0.1, so 6.5 = 110.1 in binary (F0-21).
    2. 

  2. Normalise: move the binary point two places left: 110.1 = 1.101 × 22. The significand is 1.101, the exponent e = 2.
    3. 

  3. Sign: positive, so s = 0.
    4. 

  4. Biased exponent: E = 2 + 127 = 129 = 10000001.
    5. 

  5. Fraction: drop the hidden 1; f = 101 followed by twenty zeros.
    6. 

  6. Assemble: 0 | 10000001 | 10100000000000000000000 = 0100 0000 1101 0000 … = 0x40D00000 (F0-22 groups the bits into hex).
    7. 



Encode −0.75. 0.75 = 0.11 = 1.1 × 2−1; s = 1; E = −1 + 127 = 126 = 01111110; f = 1000…0. Pattern 1 | 01111110 | 100…0 = 0xBF400000.


Decode the neighbour. The next float above 6.5 has the last fraction bit set: 1.10100000000000000000001 × 22 = 6.5 + 22−23 = 6.5 + 2−21 ≈ 6.50000048. So ulp(6.5) = 2−21 ≈ 4.77 × 10−7, four times ulp(1), because 6.5 lies in the binade [4, 8). D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Real scoops are decimal and approximate; floats are binary and exact. Every float is an exact binary fraction. The "approximation" happens only when a result is rounded to the nearest float, and that rounding follows an exact rule. S1
    • 

  • Amara has a few scoops; float has 254 normal exponents plus special patterns. No kitchen has a scoop for "infinity" or "not a number", and no scoop is negative. Signed zeros, infinities and NaN have no kitchen counterpart. S1
    • 

  • The smallest scoop is not the end. Below the smallest normal number, floats do not jump to zero: subnormals keep the gap even, at the price of fewer marks. S1
    • 

  • Amara chooses the scoop; the hardware chooses for you. She could decide to measure flour more carefully. A float has no such choice: its precision is fixed by the format, and a computation that needs more must use a wider format or a better algorithm. D1
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F0-24 stored integers in two's complement, and SP102 used double without asking how it works. What carries over: bits, place values, masks and shifts (F0-25), and the habit of printing what the machine really stores. What changes: integer arithmetic is exact until it overflows; floating-point arithmetic rounds on almost every operation, its spacing grows with magnitude, and it has special values (±0, ±∞, NaN) that integers do not have. S1 What comes next: F0-74 turns "round to nearest" into an error model, fl(x op y) = (x op y)(1 + δ), and follows how those small errors grow through a computation.

Summary


    

  • A binary32 float is (−1)s × 1.f × 2E−127 with 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits and 23 fraction bits; the hidden bit gives precision p = 24.
    • 

  • E = 0 encodes zeros and subnormals, E = 255 infinities and NaN.
    • 

  • The ULP doubles at every power of two; relative spacing stays between 2−24 and 2−23 for normal floats.
    • 

  • Every integer up to 224 is exact in float (253 in double); above that, increments are lost.
    • 

  • Results are rounded to nearest, ties to even, so the stored error is at most half an ULP.
    • 

  • Printed digits are not the stored value: print with max_digits10 digits or in hex.
    •
