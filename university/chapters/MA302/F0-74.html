Story hook

Analogy

Amara needs ten cups of flour and has only a small scoop. Each time she levels the scoop with a knife, the amount is right to within a few grains, but never exactly right. One scoop's error is too small to matter. A thousand scoops' errors, added together, can be a spoonful. And if some scoops always come out a little heavy, because of how her scoop's marks fall, the error does not average out at all: it piles up in one direction.

Every floating-point operation levels a scoop: it rounds. This chapter measures one rounding, then follows thousands of them through a loop, and finds the two ways small errors turn into wrong answers: accumulation (many roundings add up) and cancellation (subtracting nearly equal numbers exposes the roundings they already carried). Its forensic lab is a robot clock that, after twelve hours, is sixteen minutes fast.

What you will be able to do

  • State the standard model fl(x op y) = (x op y)(1 + δ), |δ| ≤ u, and compute the unit roundoff u for float and double.
  • Bound the error of a loop that repeats an operation n times, and compare the bound with a measured error.
  • Recognise catastrophic cancellation and rewrite a formula to avoid it.
  • Choose a finite-difference step that balances truncation error against rounding error.
  • Diagnose a time stamp that drifts because it is accumulated in a float (this chapter's forensic lab), and fix it.
Jargon box
Unit roundoff
Simple words: the largest relative error one rounding can make. Analogy: the most a single levelled scoop can be off, as a fraction of the scoop. Precise: u = 2−p for round to nearest in a binary format of precision p: 2−24 ≈ 5.96 × 10−8 for float (run R1) and 2−53 ≈ 1.11 × 10−16 for double; u is half the machine epsilon of F0-73. B1R1
Standard model of floating-point arithmetic
Simple words: every basic operation gives the exact answer times a number very close to 1. Analogy: each scoop holds "the right amount, plus or minus a tiny fraction". Precise: fl(x op y) = (x op y)(1 + δ) with |δ| ≤ u for op ∈ {+, −, ×, ÷} (and square root), provided no overflow or underflow occurs; it follows from correct rounding (F0-73). B1S1
Absolute and relative error
Simple words: how far off an answer is, in its own units or as a fraction of the true value. Analogy: "two grams too much" versus "two grams in a kilogram". Precise: for an approximation x̂ of x: absolute error |x̂ − x|; relative error |x̂ − x| / |x| (for x ≠ 0). Floating point controls relative error. B1
Error accumulation
Simple words: the roundings of many operations adding up. Analogy: a thousand scoops' small spills. Precise: after k dependent roundings the relative error is bounded by γk = ku / (1 − ku) (for ku < 1); the bound grows linearly with k, while measured errors are often smaller because roundings partly cancel. B1
Catastrophic cancellation
Simple words: losing most correct digits by subtracting two nearly equal numbers that already carry errors. Analogy: weighing a cake by weighing the baker with and without it on a bathroom scale. Precise: if a and b carry relative errors up to ε, then a − b can carry a relative error up to ε(|a| + |b|) / |a − b|, which is huge when a ≈ b; the subtraction itself is exact or nearly so. B1S2
Truncation error
Simple words: the error of a formula that is only approximately right even with perfect arithmetic. Analogy: a recipe that says "about a cup" even before anyone scoops. Precise: the difference between the exact mathematical quantity and the exact result of the approximation formula, for example (f(x + h) − f(x))/h − f′(x) = (h/2) f″(ξ) for a forward difference. It is independent of rounding. B1D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When the computer adds two floats, the exact answer usually falls between two floats. The computer keeps the nearer one. The difference is tiny: for a float, less than one part in sixteen million. That is the rounding error of one operation.

A program does millions of operations. If each one is a tiny bit off, the errors can add up. Run R1 adds 0.1 to itself ten million times in float and gets 1,087,937 instead of 1,000,000: almost nine percent too much. Each single addition was off by less than one part in sixteen million; together they were off by nine parts in a hundred, because nearly all of them were off in the same direction.

The other danger is subtraction. If you weigh yourself holding a cat and then without it, and the scale is off by a little each time, the cat's weight can come out badly wrong, because the two big numbers were nearly the same. Computers have the same problem: subtracting nearly equal numbers leaves mostly the errors. The cure is often a different formula that gives the same answer without the subtraction.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

One operation

Correct rounding (F0-73) means that the computed result of a basic operation is the exact result rounded to nearest. Figure 1 shows that the rounding error is at most half an ulp, and half an ulp is at most u times the result. So B1S1

fl(x op y) = (x op y)(1 + δ), |δ| ≤ u = 2^−p

Run R1 checks it once: the floats a = 0.1f and b = 0.2f have the exact sum 0.30000000447034836 (exact because a double holds it), and the float sum is 0.30000001192092896. The relative error δ ≈ 2.48 × 10−8 is below u = 5.96 × 10−8. R1

Many operations

Take the running sum sk = fl(sk−1 + xk). Each step multiplies the error so far by a factor (1 + δk) and adds its own. Collecting the factors gives the bound B1

|ŝ_n − s_n| ≤ γ_(n−1) · Σ |x_k|, γ_k = k·u / (1 − k·u)

For n copies of the same positive x this is a relative error of at most about (n − 1)u. Run R1's table compares it with the truth: after 1,000 additions of 0.1f the measured relative error is 9.55 × 10−6 against a bound of 5.96 × 10−5; after ten million it is 0.0879 against 0.596. The bound holds, and it is not wildly pessimistic here, because the errors share a direction. R1

Why one direction? Between 2k and 2k+1 the running sum has one fixed ulp, and adding the same 0.1f rounds the same way each time. Once the sum passes 219 = 524,288 the ulp is 1/16 and 0.1 (1.6 ulps) rounds to 2 ulps = 0.125; above 220 the ulp is 1/8 and 0.1 rounds to one ulp, again 0.125. Either way every addition adds 25 % too much. That is the jump between the last two rows of run R1. With independent, randomly signed errors you would expect growth closer to √n · u, but a test tolerance must not rely on luck: the deterministic bound is the safe default (F0-75). B1D1

Cancellation

Subtracting two floats that are within a factor of two of each other is exact: the result fits without rounding (Sterbenz's lemma). Cancellation is therefore not an error of the subtraction. It is the moment when earlier errors, which were tiny relative to a and b, become large relative to the small difference a − b. If a and b carry relative errors up to ε, the difference can carry up to ε(|a| + |b|) / |a − b|. B1S2

Run R2 shows it on (1 − cos x)/x2, which tends to ½. In double, cos(10−4) ≈ 0.999999995 is rounded with an error up to about 10−16, and 1 − cos x ≈ 5 × 10−9 keeps that absolute error: relative error up to about 2 × 10−8. The printed value 0.49999999696 is wrong in the 9th digit. At x = 10−8, cos x rounds to exactly 1 and the formula returns 0: every digit is lost. The identity 1 − cos x = 2 sin2(x/2) gives a formula with no subtraction, and it stays correct to about 16 digits for every x in the table. R2D1

Truncation against rounding

MA301 (F0-66) approximated a derivative by f′(x) ≈ (f(x + h) − f(x))/h. Taylor's theorem gives a truncation error of about (h/2)|f″(x)|, which shrinks with h. But the numerator is a cancellation: each function value carries an error of about u|f(x)|, so the quotient carries about 2u|f(x)|/h, which grows as h shrinks. The total is smallest where the two are equal, near h ≈ 2√(u |f| / |f″|), which is about √u ≈ 10−8 in double for f = sin at x = 1. Figure 2 and run R2 show exactly this U-shape, with the smallest error 2.97 × 10−9 at h = 10−8. B1D1R2

Watch out

"Use a smaller step for more accuracy" is only true while truncation error dominates. Past the bottom of the U, a smaller step makes the answer worse. The same trade-off returns in F0-76 (integration step) and F0-79 (sample period), with extra effects of its own.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Forward and backward error. The bound for a sum can be read in two ways. Forward: the computed sum differs from the true one by at most γn−1Σ|xk|. Backward: the computed sum is the exact sum of slightly perturbed inputs xk(1 + θk) with |θk| ≤ γn−1. The backward view separates the algorithm (how much it perturbs the inputs) from the problem (how much the answer reacts to perturbed inputs). F0-78 develops it into conditioning and stability. B1

The γ notation. Products of k factors (1 + δi)±1 with |δi| ≤ u can be written 1 + θk with |θk| ≤ γk = ku/(1 − ku). This lemma turns every error analysis in this course into counting how many roundings a value passes through: the depth of its computation. F0-75 uses depth to bound a GPU reduction. B1

Statistical behaviour. If rounding errors behaved like independent random variables with mean zero, their sum would grow like √n rather than n. Rigorous probabilistic versions of this statement exist and give tighter tolerances with a stated failure probability; they need assumptions (independence, mean zero) that the forensic lab of this chapter violates. Use them only with their assumptions written down. B1B4

Error in the time variable. Simulations and controllers often advance time by t ← t + Δt. The worked example shows that this accumulates an error that depends on which binade t is in, and can even change sign. The robust pattern is to count integer ticks and compute t = k · Δt (one rounding, independent of k), or to keep time in integer nanoseconds as clocks of operating systems do (OS201 timing chapters). D1R3

How the hardware actually does it

The hardware rounds each operation in the format of its operands only if the compiler asks it to. C++ exposes the choice as FLT_EVAL_METHOD: 0 means every operation is rounded to its own type. On this build machine g++ reports 0 (run R4), so the float results of runs R1 and R3 are true single-precision roundings. The same run shows that long double here has a 64-bit significand: the x87 extended format, which older 32-bit x86 code used for intermediate results and which then rounded twice (once to 64 bits, once to 24), a classic source of "the same code gives different answers on different machines". B2R4



A second hardware effect is contraction: a multiply followed by an add can be executed as one fused multiply-add (FMA) with a single rounding. That changes results in the last bits, usually for the better, but it changes them. The listings of this course are built in ISO C++ mode without a -march option; what g++ does about contraction in that mode is in the unverified box below. nvcc contracts by default, as F0-80 shows from its generated code. B2B3

Worked example


Why does a float clock drift, and in which direction? A loop runs at 100 Hz and does t += 0.01f. Find the real step in each binade of t.


    

  1. The increment itself: 0.01f is not 0.01; it is 0.00999999977648…, because 0.01 is not a binary fraction (F0-73).
    2. 

  2. Near t = 3,000 s: t is in [2048, 4096), so ulp(t) = 211−23 = 2−12 ≈ 0.000244 s. The step is 0.01 / 0.000244 = 40.96 ulps. The sum t + 0.01 rounds to the nearest multiple of the ulp, so the real step is 41 ulps = 0.0100097656 s: 0.098 % fast.
    3. 

  3. Near t = 5,000 s: ulp = 2−11; 0.01 is 20.48 ulps, rounded to 20 ulps = 0.009765625 s: 2.34 % slow. The same happens in the next two binades (10.24 → 10, 5.12 → 5).
    4. 

  4. Near t = 40,000 s: ulp = 2−8 = 0.00390625; 2.56 ulps rounds to 3 ulps = 0.01171875 s: 17.2 % fast.
    5. 

  5. Near t = 70,000 s: ulp = 2−7; 1.28 ulps rounds to 1 ulp = 0.0078125 s: 21.9 % slow.
    6. 

  6. Above 217 = 131,072 s: ulp = 2−6 = 0.015625; 0.64 ulp rounds up to a whole ulp: 56 % fast. And once t passes 218 s (about 73 hours), 0.01 is less than half an ulp and t stops advancing altogether.
    7. 



So the drift is not a steady creep: it is a different rate in every binade, set by the rounding of 0.01 to a whole number of ulps. D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Spilled flour is random; rounding error is deterministic. The same operation on the same inputs rounds the same way every time. That is why the clock in the worked example drifts in a predictable direction in each binade instead of wandering. S1
    • 

  • Spills only add flour or lose it; cancellation has no kitchen counterpart. In the kitchen, a small spill stays small. In arithmetic, subtracting two nearly equal amounts turns an old, tiny error into a large fraction of the result. B1
    • 

  • Amara's error is in grams; float error is relative. A float's rounding error scales with the size of the number, so the same 0.01 s step is measured more coarsely when t is large. A kitchen scoop does not get coarser as the bowl fills. D1
    •

Summary


    

  • One correctly rounded operation obeys fl(x op y) = (x op y)(1 + δ), |δ| ≤ u = 2−p.
    • 

  • k chained roundings give a relative error of at most γk = ku/(1 − ku); a running sum's error is at most γn−1Σ|xk|.
    • 

  • Errors can share a direction when the same rounding repeats, so measured growth can be close to linear.
    • 

  • Cancellation exposes old errors: rewrite formulas to avoid subtracting nearly equal numbers.
    • 

  • Truncation error falls with the step, rounding error rises: the best finite-difference step is near √u.
    • 

  • Never accumulate time as t += Δt in float; count ticks.
