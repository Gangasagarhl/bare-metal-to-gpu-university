Story hook

Analogy

Amara carries a tray of six full glasses across a busy café. She cannot look at the tray all the time; she must watch the people around her. So how does she keep it level? Two senses help her. Her wrists feel every quick twist of the tray at once: a bump, a turn, a sudden tip. And her body knows, more slowly, which way is "down", from the steady pull on her arms.

Each sense alone would fail. Her wrists feel changes but not where she started, so tiny mistakes add up: after a long walk she would be sure the tray is level when it has slowly crept to a slant. Her sense of "down" never creeps, but it is fooled whenever she speeds up or slows down: when she brakes hard, the drinks lean forward as if the tray had tipped, although it has not.

A drone has the same two senses and the same two weaknesses. This chapter measures both of them on a recorded flight, and shows why a multirotor fools its sense of "down" far more than Amara does.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what attitude estimation is and why a flight controller cannot measure its attitude directly.
  • Compute roll and pitch from one accelerometer sample, and attitude by integrating gyro rates, and state the assumption behind each.
  • Explain why the accelerometer of a multirotor reads "level" while it accelerates, and when its tilt reading is trustworthy.
  • Measure on a recorded flight how fast gyro integration drifts and how wrong the accelerometer tilt becomes in each manoeuvre.
  • Diagnose a mounting misalignment from a four-heading bench test and predict its effect in hover.
Jargon box
Attitude estimate
Simple words: the flight controller's best guess of how the vehicle is tilted and which way it points. Analogy: Amara's feeling of how level her tray is. Precise: an estimate of the rotation between the body frame and the world frame (here roll, pitch and yaw, or a quaternion), computed from sensor readings because no sensor measures the rotation itself. B1U1
Specific force
Simple words: what an accelerometer really feels: every push on the vehicle except gravity, divided by its mass. Analogy: the pull Amara feels in her arms, which changes when she speeds up. Precise: f = a − gworld, the vehicle's acceleration minus the gravitational acceleration, expressed in the sensor's axes; at rest it equals +g pointing up, in free fall it is zero. B1D1
Gyro bias
Simple words: the small rate a gyro reports when it is not turning at all. Analogy: a wrist that always feels a faint twist that is not there. Precise: the slowly varying offset b in the gyro model ωmeasured = ω + b + noise; in this chapter's recording b = (0.004, −0.006, 0.003) rad/s, an exercise value. B1R1
Drift (of an integrated estimate)
Simple words: an error that keeps growing because small mistakes are added up again and again. Analogy: the tray that creeps to a slant during a long walk. Precise: the error of an estimate obtained by integrating a rate; a constant bias b grows the error linearly as b t, and white noise grows it like a random walk. D1U1
Accelerometer tilt
Simple words: roll and pitch worked out by assuming the accelerometer points at "up". Analogy: Amara judging level from the pull on her arms. Precise: roll = atan2(fy, fz), pitch = atan2(−fx, √(fy² + fz²)) in DN201's axes; correct only when the vehicle's acceleration is zero. D1B1
Mounting misalignment
Simple words: the sensor board sits slightly crooked on the frame. Analogy: a tray with one short leg on its stand. Precise: a fixed rotation between the IMU's axes and the vehicle's body axes; if it is not measured and removed, every estimate is off by it. D1U1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the key picture of this chapter: what the accelerometer of a multirotor feels in three situations. Only in A and C does the reading point "up". In B, the most common situation in flight (speeding up, slowing down, turning), the reading follows the vehicle's own z axis and the vehicle looks level, whatever its real tilt.

Figure 2 shows what that means on a real recording: the accelerometer misses the 12-degree pitch of the acceleration phase almost completely, while the gyro follows every change but wanders away from the truth.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A drone must know how it is tilted, all the time. If it does not know, it cannot stay level, and it cannot lean the right way to move. But no sensor tells it "you are tilted 7 degrees". It must work this out from two kinds of sensor.

The first is the gyroscope. It feels turning: how fast the drone rolls, pitches and yaws, right now. Add up all the small turns and you know how far the drone has turned since it started. The trouble is that every gyro has a tiny error, and adding up means the errors add up too. After a while the answer is wrong, and it keeps getting worse.

The second is the accelerometer. When the drone stands still it feels which way is down, like the pull on your arms. That never drifts. But it is fooled whenever the drone speeds up or slows down, and a drone that moves is speeding up or slowing down nearly all the time. The next chapter mixes the two senses so that each covers the other's weakness.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What has to be estimated

The attitude is the rotation R that turns body-frame vectors into world-frame vectors. DN201 described it with roll φ, pitch θ and yaw ψ, with R = Rz(ψ) Ry(θ) Rx(φ), axes x forward, y left, z up (F10-03), and the simulator stores it as a quaternion (F10-04). The controllers of F10-16 need it hundreds of times a second: every thrust direction, every tilt command and every rate correction is computed from the current attitude estimate. U1B1

Roll and pitch matter most: they decide where the thrust points. Yaw, the heading, needs a reference that gravity cannot give (a magnetometer, GNSS motion or both, F10-08), and this chapter leaves it aside. B1U1

Sense 1: the gyroscope, integrated

A rate gyro measures the body angular velocity ω = (p, q, r), plus a gyro bias and noise. Integrating it gives the attitude change. For the Euler angles of DN201 the body rates turn into angle rates as B1D1

dφ/dt = p + (q sin φ + r cos φ) tan θ
dθ/dt = q cos φ − r sin φ
dψ/dt = (q sin φ + r cos φ) / cos θ

Two things follow. First, a constant bias b becomes an angle error that grows like b t: the recording's pitch-axis bias of −0.006 rad/s gives −0.15 rad, −8.59°, after 25 s (run R4, W4). That is drift. Second, the equations mix the axes: an error in the pitch estimate lets the yaw rate r leak into the roll equation through tan θ. In Figure 2 and in run R2 the gyro-only roll jumps from +2.9° to −4.3° during the 90° yaw turn although the true roll stays zero, because the estimate believes the pitch is −4.4° at that moment. R2D1

Sense 2: the accelerometer and "down"

An accelerometer does not measure gravity. It measures specific force, f = RT(a − gworld) with gworld = (0, 0, −g): all the forces on the vehicle except gravity, divided by the mass, in body axes. When the vehicle is at rest, a = 0 and f = RT(0, 0, g), the "up" direction seen from the body. Writing out the third row of R gives f = g (−sin θ, cos θ sin φ, cos θ cos φ), so B1D1

φ = atan2(fy, fz) θ = atan2(−fx, √(fy² + fz²))

That is the accelerometer tilt. It has no memory, so it cannot drift. Its two problems are noise and its assumption a = 0. In the recording, accelerometer noise of 0.25 m/s² per sample is already 1.46° of tilt noise (run R4, W5). The assumption is the serious problem. R4

Why a multirotor fools its own accelerometer

Apply Newton's law to a multirotor: m a = R (0, 0, T) + m gworld + Fdrag. Then the specific force is f = RT(a − gworld) = (0, 0, T/m) + RT Fdrag/m. Without drag the accelerometer reads only the thrust, along body z, whatever the tilt: the formula above returns roll 0 and pitch 0 in every flight condition. A vehicle tilted 12° that holds its height accelerates sideways at g tan 12° = 2.085 m/s², and its accelerometer reads (0, 0, 10.029) m/s²: "level" (Figure 1 B; run R4, W2). D1R4B1

Drag is what gives the accelerometer any tilt information in flight. At a steady speed the drag exactly cancels the horizontal thrust, a = 0, and the reading is "up" again: at 12° the body reads (−2.040, 0, 9.596) m/s², which gives pitch 12.00° (Figure 1 C; W3). Between these two extremes the reading lies somewhere in between, depending on how much of the horizontal thrust is still accelerating the vehicle. The recording shows all three cases: in the roll doublet (2–6 s) the accelerometer roll stays near zero while the true roll is ±20°; in the 6–9 s acceleration the accelerometer pitch creeps from 0° up to 4.8° as speed and drag build up; and in the final "hover" (21–25 s), when the vehicle is level but still slowing down from its last manoeuvre, drag alone makes the accelerometer report a few degrees of tilt that is not there. R2D1

Note

A person walking with a tray is different: their feet push on the ground in any direction, so the "down" sense is fooled only by real accelerations. A multirotor can only push along its own z axis (it is underactuated, F10-02), which is exactly why its accelerometer reading stays glued to that axis. Fixed-wing aircraft have lift and side forces and behave differently again (F10-06). B1U1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

A sensor model you can write down

Every estimator in this course starts from explicit sensor models, because a filter is only as right as its model (RB302 F9-33, F9-34). The recording uses: gyro = ω̄ + b + ng, accelerometer = f̄ + na, where the bar means the average over the 10 ms sample interval, b is a constant bias, and ng, na are white Gaussian noise with standard deviations 0.003 rad/s and 0.25 m/s². Real MEMS sensors add scale-factor and cross-axis errors, bias that changes with temperature, vibration that can alias into the band of interest, and clipping (DN202 F10-09); their sizes come only from the datasheet of the named part and from your own measurements. R1B1U1

Integrating correctly

Listing 2 integrates Euler-angle rates with one Euler step per sample. This is fine for teaching at 100 Hz and moderate angles, and wrong in two known ways: the Euler-angle equations divide by cos θ and fail near θ = ±90° (gimbal lock), and a first-order step loses accuracy when the rotation per sample is large. Production estimators integrate a quaternion or a rotation matrix with the rotation increment per sample, and many IMUs deliver integrated angle increments rather than instantaneous rates for that reason. The simulator of F10-04 already integrates a quaternion with RK4. B1U1D1

What can and cannot be known

Gravity is a single direction, so it fixes roll and pitch but says nothing about rotation about the vertical: yaw and the z-gyro bias are invisible to the accelerometer. A second reference direction (the magnetic field) or the vehicle's motion over the ground (GNSS velocity, F10-15) is needed for them. And because a multirotor's accelerometer sees mainly thrust in flight, even roll and pitch are only weakly observable from it while manoeuvring; F10-15 shows how GNSS velocity restores them. B1B2D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

PX4 and ArduPilot use a north–east–down world frame and a forward–right–down body frame, not DN201's x-forward, y-left, z-up axes; signs of roll, pitch, yaw and accelerometer readings therefore differ from this chapter's. This was not checked against the documents in this build. Before comparing a real log with this chapter, read the frame conventions in the PX4 Autopilot User Guide and the ArduPilot documentation (registry 4.8) for the release recorded in the dossier, and convert. D2D3

How the hardware actually does it

A MEMS gyroscope senses rotation through the Coriolis force on a vibrating proof mass; a MEMS accelerometer senses the displacement of a proof mass held by springs. Both are read by the flight controller over SPI or I2C, usually with the sensor's own digital low-pass filter set by configuration registers (DN202 F10-07, F10-08). The proof mass of the accelerometer cannot tell gravity from acceleration: that is the physics behind "specific force", not a shortcoming of one product. B1U1

Where the IMU sits matters. Vibration from the propellers reaches it through the frame (DN202 F10-09); and if its board is not parallel to the body axes, every reading is rotated by that mounting misalignment. Autopilots offer a board-orientation setting for gross rotations (90°, 180°) and a level calibration for small ones. The forensic lab below shows what an uncalibrated offset does. U1D2D3

Not verified — check before relying on this

The names of the board-orientation and level-calibration settings, the sample rates at which PX4 and ArduPilot read their IMUs, and whether a given flight controller uses delta-angle outputs, were not checked in this build. Look them up in the PX4 Autopilot User Guide and the ArduPilot documentation for the release in the dossier, and in the datasheet of the IMU part fitted to your kit (DN202). Never copy them from this chapter. D2D3

Untested on hardware

No real IMU, flight controller or vehicle was available in this build. The recording is produced by the DN201 simulator with modelled sensor errors, and the bench forensic data by a program. Repeating the lab on hardware needs the DN202 bench (an IMU on a flight controller or breakout board, propellers removed), a logger at a known sample rate, a level reference surface and known tilt angles, and for in-flight data a vehicle with logging; the sensor errors of a real IMU will differ from the model. Worked example

Problem. A vehicle stands still. Its accelerometer reads f = (−1.20, 1.65, 9.60) m/s² in DN201's axes. (a) Find roll and pitch. (b) The same vehicle later flies with pitch 12° and roll 0 while accelerating forward and holding height, with no drag. What does the accelerometer read, and what tilt does the formula give? (c) How large is the pitch error of a gyro-only estimate after 25 s with a pitch-axis bias of −0.006 rad/s?

  1. Check the size. |f| = √(1.20² + 1.65² + 9.60²) = 9.814 m/s², close to g = 9.81: consistent with "at rest".
  2. Roll. φ = atan2(1.65, 9.60) = 9.75°. Positive roll: the right side is down (F10-03).
  3. Pitch. θ = atan2(1.20, √(1.65² + 9.60²)) = atan2(1.20, 9.741) = 7.02°. Positive pitch: the nose is down.
  4. (b) Accelerating. Holding height needs T cos 12° = m g, so T/m = 9.81 / cos 12° = 10.029 m/s², and the horizontal acceleration is g tan 12° = 2.085 m/s². The accelerometer reads (0, 0, 10.029) m/s²; the formula gives roll 0°, pitch 0°: an error of the full 12°.
  5. (c) Drift. −0.006 rad/s × 25 s = −0.150 rad = −8.59°. The measured gyro-only pitch error at 25 s in run R2 is −7.05°: the same order, smaller because the integrated noise and the axis coupling of step 2 in Layer 2 add their own contributions.

All numbers are printed by checks.cpp (run R4, lines W1, W2 and W4). R4R2D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Amara's sense of "down" is fooled only when she really speeds up or slows down. A multirotor's accelerometer is fooled by its own way of moving: it reads only thrust and drag, so even a steady tilt while accelerating looks level (Figure 1 B). The drone's "down" sense is much worse than a person's. D1
    • 

  • Amara can glance at the tray to check. The flight controller has no eyes on its attitude at all; every number comes from sensors with bias, noise and vibration, and from models of them. B1
    • 

  • A person's wrists do not "integrate" anything; the brain combines senses in ways we do not write down. The flight controller adds numbers 100 or more times a second, and every bias is added each time: drift grows exactly in proportion to time (Layer 2). D1
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: DN202 (F10-07 to F10-09) showed the IMU as hardware on a board; RB302 (F9-30 to F9-34) taught estimation as combining uncertain information; DN201 (F10-03, F10-04) defined attitude and built the simulator. U1 What carries over: the sensor is a device you read; every reading has noise and bias; estimates come with uncertainty; attitude is a rotation with DN201's axes. What changes: the quantity you need most (tilt) is measured by nothing; the accelerometer measures specific force, not gravity, and on a multirotor that means thrust; integration turns tiny biases into large errors over time. D1R2 What comes next: F10-14 combines the two senses in a complementary filter, using each where it is good.

Lab


Goal


Quantify, on recorded IMU data, how gyro integration and accelerometer tilt fail, and find the conditions under which each one is trustworthy.


Hardware and software needed


No hardware. A computer with a C++20 compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 through university/labs/run_lab.sh. Optional: a real IMU log from the DN202 bench flight controller (propellers removed): untested on hardware in this build.


Safety


Simulation only: none beyond normal computer use. If you log a real flight controller, do it on the bench with the propellers removed (faculty safety rules, safety page); never record IMU data by flying for this lab.


Steps


    

  1. From the repository root run university/labs/run_lab.sh university/labs/F10-13. Check that record_flight.out, tilt_two_ways.out and bench_four_ways.out match runs R1–R3 in this chapter.
    2. 

  2. Open imu_flight.csv. For the hover rows (t < 2 s), compute the mean of each gyro column. Compare with the true bias printed by R1.
    3. 

  3. Copy tilt_two_ways.cpp to tilt_bias.cpp and subtract your measured means from the gyro before integrating. Rerun and compare the gyro column of the RMS table.
    4. 

  4. Add a column that prints |f| − g for each second. Find the parts of the flight where |f| differs from g by more than 0.5 m/s². Is |f| ≈ g a good test for "the accelerometer can be trusted" on a multirotor? (Hint: Figure 1 B.)
    5. 

  5. Change the script in record_flight.cpp so that the vehicle flies at a constant 12° pitch for 8 s instead of 3 s. Rerun and watch the accelerometer pitch approach 12° as the speed settles. Restore the file afterwards.
    6. 



Expected observations


The hover means of the gyro should be close to the true bias (0.004, −0.006, 0.003): the standard error of a 200-sample mean at 0.003 rad/s noise is 0.0002 rad/s (run R4, L1), although the start of the recording is not perfectly still. Removing them shrinks the gyro-only error. The |f| test fails on a multirotor: holding a 20° roll at constant height needs T/m = g / cos 20°, so |f| exceeds g by only 0.63 m/s² (R4, L2), close to the noise of one sample, while the accelerometer tilt is 20° wrong. With a longer steady pitch the accelerometer pitch rises toward the true 12° as the drag builds up. These are the results of this build's runs and of the formulas; your own numbers are the check.


Verification


Your bias-corrected run has a smaller gyro RMS error than run R2 in all six parts, and you can point to the rows where |f| ≈ g although the accelerometer tilt is wrong.

Method hints


Think of the table tilt as a fixed arrow in the room and the mounting offset as a fixed arrow on the vehicle. Turning the vehicle turns the first arrow as seen from the sensor and leaves the second unchanged. Averages over opposite headings cancel one of them; differences cancel the other.

Summary


    

  • No sensor measures attitude; the flight controller estimates it from a gyro and an accelerometer.
    • 

  • Integrated gyro rates are accurate over short times and drift over long ones; a bias b gives an error b t, and axes couple through the Euler-angle equations.
    • 

  • An accelerometer measures specific force, not gravity; its tilt formula is right only when the vehicle does not accelerate.
    • 

  • A multirotor's accelerometer reads mainly thrust along body z, so it looks level while accelerating; drag is what brings back tilt information at steady speed.
    • 

  • On the recording: accelerometer error up to 12.20° RMS in manoeuvres, gyro error growing to 7.10° RMS by the end.
    • 

  • Gravity fixes roll and pitch only; yaw and the z-gyro bias need another reference.
    • 

  • A four-heading bench test separates a mounting offset (constant) from a table tilt (turns with the vehicle).
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Roll is a rotation between two frames, not a force or a rate. The gyro measures angular rate (roll is its integral, which drifts), the accelerometer measures specific force (it gives roll only when the vehicle does not accelerate). Neither measures the rotation itself.
    2. 

  2. Roll = atan2(−0.50, 9.76) = −2.93° (left side down). Pitch = atan2(−0.85, √(0.50² + 9.76²)) = atan2(−0.85, 9.773) = −4.97° (nose up). |f| = 9.810 m/s², consistent with rest (run R4, Q2).
    3. 

  3. Your words may differ. The accelerometer feels every force except gravity. A multirotor's only large non-gravity force is its thrust, which always points along its own z axis. So the reading points along body z, exactly as it would for a level vehicle at rest; the formula cannot tell the difference. Only drag adds a small sideways part.
    4. 

  4. (c): 0.004 × 60 = 0.24 rad = 13.75° (run R4, Q4). (a) forgets to multiply by time; (b) confuses radians and degrees; (d) is wrong: a bias is a constant, it does not average out, it adds up.
    5. 

  5. During the yaw turn the vehicle is level and not accelerating sideways, so the specific force points up and the formula is right (2.08° RMS, mostly noise). During the diagonal acceleration the vehicle tilts to accelerate, the specific force follows body z, and the formula reads nearly level (10.98° RMS).
    6. 

  6. No. In a steady banked turn the aircraft accelerates toward the centre of the circle; lift supplies that, and the specific force points along the aircraft's own vertical axis (for a coordinated turn), so the accelerometer again looks "level" relative to the wings. The bank angle must come from integrated rates corrected by another reference.
    7. 

  7. Roll and pitch: gravity defines "down". Yaw, and the bias of the z gyro, need another reference direction, such as the magnetic field (F10-08) or the direction of motion from GNSS (F10-15).

Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. Every row would show (0, 0, 9.81) m/s² with roll 0° and pitch 0°, apart from noise (about 0.25 m/s² per sample, 0.011 m/s² for the 500-sample mean).
    2. 

  2. The mean over the four headings is roll 2.513°, pitch −0.823°: the constant part. Half the difference between opposite headings is the turning part: 0° versus 180° gives roll 0.363°, pitch 0.453°; 90° versus 270° gives roll 0.442°, pitch −0.445°; its size is about 0.58°–0.62° (run R4, K1–K3).
    3. 

  3. The constant part turns with the vehicle, so it is fixed to the vehicle: an IMU mounting misalignment of about roll +2.5°, pitch −0.8°. The part that changes with heading is fixed to the room: the table is tilted by about 0.6°. Rotating the vehicle by 180° flips the sign of the table's contribution in body axes and leaves the mounting offset unchanged.
    4. 

  4. The estimator believes the vehicle is rolled +2.5° (right side down) when it is level. The attitude controller drives the estimate to zero, so the true roll becomes −2.5°, left side down; the thrust then has a component to the left, g tan 2.5° = 0.43 m/s² (R4, K4), and the vehicle slides left. The pilot's right stick commands +2.5° of roll, which brings the true attitude back to level. The pitch offset of −0.8° also makes it creep forward slowly.
    5. 

  5. Fix: re-seat the IMU board so it is parallel to the frame, or run the autopilot's level calibration on a known-level surface (its setting names: unverified box in "How the hardware actually does it"). Verify: repeat the four-heading test; the mean over headings must be close to zero. Then hover and check that no stick trim is needed.
    6. 



How the evidence was produced: bench_four_ways.cpp computes the specific force of a vehicle on a table tilted by roll 0.4°, pitch 0.45° (line 50) with an IMU mounted at roll 2.5°, pitch −0.8° (line 51), at four headings, and adds noise of 0.25 m/s² per sample. Wrong answers and why: "the table is crooked, nothing to fix" — the table part changes sign with heading, the 2.5° part does not; "the gyro has a bias" — this is an accelerometer log on a still vehicle, and a gyro bias would make the attitude drift, not sit at a constant offset; "a motor is weak" — the bench test has no motor running, yet shows the offset.
