Story hook

Analogy

Amara's hands are the last link: they feel the tray start to tip and push back. If they react weakly, the tray sags before they catch it. If they react hard to the slightest tremor, they overshoot and the drinks slosh the other way. If they react to how fast the tray is starting to tip, they can catch it early and smoothly — but then every jitter of the floor makes them twitch, and after a long shift her wrists ache. And if one glass is heavier, a slow habit of leaning a little more on that side keeps the tray level without her thinking about it.

Those are the P, D, filter and I of the rate loop. This chapter changes them one at a time in the simulator, measures what each change does, and then meets a vehicle whose hands twitch so hard that its motors come down hot.

What you will be able to do

  • Measure a rate loop's rise time, overshoot, disturbance rejection and motor-command noise in a repeatable test.
  • Tune P, D, the D-term filter and I one change at a time, and justify each change with a number.
  • Predict the steady rate error of a P-only loop, the noise that P and D pass to the motors, and the time I needs to remove a disturbance.
  • Recognise D-term noise in a log and explain why it heats motors without moving the vehicle.
  • Describe how the same procedure is carried out in a software-in-the-loop simulator of an open flight stack, and what remains unverified.
Jargon box
Rate controller
Simple words: the innermost loop, which makes the vehicle spin at the speed it is asked for. Analogy: Amara's hands reacting to the tray starting to tip. Precise: a PID per body axis from rate error (setpoint minus gyro) to angular acceleration, multiplied by the inertia to give torque (Listing 1 of F10-16, lines 165–169); its output goes through the mixer to the motors. D1U1
Rise time and overshoot
Simple words: how fast a loop reaches a new setpoint, and how far it goes past it. Analogy: how fast the hands level the tray, and whether they tip it the other way. Precise: rise time is the time from 10 % to 90 % of a step; overshoot is the peak above the step, in percent of the step. D1U1
D-term filter
Simple words: a smoother on the derivative part, so that sensor noise does not reach the motors amplified. Analogy: Amara ignoring the floor's tiny tremors and reacting only to real tipping. Precise: a low-pass filter (here first-order, time constant 5 ms, corner 31.8 Hz) on the differentiated measurement; without it, white gyro noise of σ is amplified by about kd √2 σ/dt. D1R3
Disturbance rejection
Simple words: how well the loop holds its setpoint when something pushes it. Analogy: keeping the tray level when one glass is heavier. Precise: the remaining rate error under a constant disturbance torque; a P-only loop leaves (disturbance angular acceleration)/kp, an integrator drives it to zero over a time of order (disturbance)/(ki × error). D1R1
Software in the loop (SITL)
Simple words: running the real flight software on a computer, flying a simulated vehicle. Analogy: Amara practising the route with an empty tray in an empty café. Precise: the flight stack compiled for the host computer and connected to a physics simulator in place of sensors and motors, so its controllers, parameters and logs can be exercised without hardware. D2D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The rate loop is the drone's fastest helper. It checks five hundred times a second: "Am I spinning as fast as I should?" If not, it tells the motors to push harder on one side.

It has three ways to decide how hard to push. The first: the bigger the mistake, the harder the push. The second: if the mistake is shrinking fast, ease off early so you do not go too far. The third: if a small mistake stays and stays, slowly push a bit more until it is gone.

The second way has a catch. The drone's spin sensor is a little shaky. Looking at how fast the mistake changes makes those shakes look huge. If you do not smooth them first, the motors get told to speed up and slow down hundreds of times a second. They cannot really do it, so the drone flies fine — but the motors get hot.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A repeatable test

Tuning without a fixed test is guessing. Listing 1 runs the same 2 s experiment for every gain set: the rate controller alone (outer loops off), a roll-rate step of 1 rad/s from 0.1 to 0.6 s, a step back, and from 1.2 s a disturbance: motor 1's propeller gives 5 % more thrust than the mixer assumes. The gyro has white noise of 0.02 rad/s. Four numbers come out: rise time and overshoot of the step, the mean rate error under the disturbance (1.6–2.0 s), and the standard deviation of motor 1's command in that window. R1D1

The disturbance in numbers: 5 % of the hover thrust of one rotor is 0.05 × 9.81/4 = 0.1226 N, at a 0.15 m lever arm 0.01839 N·m, which on Jx = 0.010 kg·m² is an angular acceleration of 1.839 rad/s² (run R3, W1). R3

A P-only rate loop behaves like a first-order system with time constant 1/P as long as the motors are fast compared with it; the motors' 30 ms lag is what produces overshoot once P approaches 1/0.03. Under a constant disturbance the loop settles where the P term balances it: error = 1.839/P (W2), which the measured errors follow closely. The motor noise grows with P, because P passes gyro noise straight through: J P σ = 0.0040 N·m of torque noise at P = 20 (W4). R1R3D1

A steady rate error is worse than it sounds: −0.089 rad/s held for 10 s is 51° of roll. In the full cascade the attitude loop sees that angle and raises its rate setpoint until the rate error is cancelled, so the vehicle ends up with a small steady attitude error instead. D1U1

D and its filter

Trial 5 keeps P = 20 and adds D = 0.3: overshoot drops from 7.8 % to 0.7 %, the rise time grows from 0.075 to 0.100 s, the disturbance error is unchanged (D does nothing against a constant push). The price is noise: motor 1's command spread rises from 0.66 to 2.11 rad/s. The derivative of a noisy signal is noisier: differencing two samples with independent noise σ gives √2 σ/dt, so kd = 0.3 at dt = 2 ms would add J kd √2 σ/dt = 0.0424 N·m of torque noise, ten times the P term's (W3, W4). R1R3D1

The D-term filter (5 ms, corner 31.8 Hz, R3 Q6) removes most of that. Trial 6 switches it off: overshoot barely changes (0.7 → 1.1 %), but the motor spread jumps from 2.11 to 7.34 rad/s. The filter costs a little phase near its corner frequency, which is why it cannot be made arbitrarily slow; its corner has to stay well above the loop's own bandwidth (22.7 rad/s ≈ 3.6 Hz in F10-16). R1D1

I: removing the steady error

Trial 7 adds I = 5: the disturbance error after 0.4 s improves only from −0.089 to −0.078 rad/s. An integrator removes an error of e at a rate of about ki e per second, so cancelling 1.839 rad/s² at e = 0.09 rad/s takes about 1.839/(5 × 0.09) = 4.1 s (W6). Trial 8 with I = 40 does it in about 0.5 s (−0.047 after 0.4 s) but brings the step's overshoot back to 12 %. In the full cascade the attitude loop already removes the effect of a steady rate error (above), so the rate loop's I term needs to be only large enough to remove the attitude offset over a few seconds. R1R3D1

The procedure

The trials suggest an order that matches common tuning guides for multirotors (unverified box below): with I at zero and D at zero, raise P until the step overshoots noticeably; add D to remove the overshoot; check the motor noise and set the D-term filter as slow as the response allows; add I until a steady disturbance is removed in a few seconds; repeat the step test after every change, and stop when it passes. Only one gain changes per trial, and every trial is recorded. D1R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The loop transfer function

With the mixer and inertia normalised, the plant from rate command to body rate is the motor lag 1/(τms + 1) followed by an integrator (torque → rate); the loop with PID C(s) = kp + ki/s + kds/(τds + 1) is L(s) = C(s)/(s (τms + 1)) plus the sample-and-hold delays. The motor pole at 1/0.03 = 33 rad/s (5.3 Hz) is the main phase thief: P alone at 60 crosses over well above it, hence 31 % overshoot. D adds phase lead near crossover, which is why it reduces overshoot; its filter takes some of that lead back. F10-19 adds delay to this picture and measures the gain margin directly. D1U1B1

Gyro filters before the controller

Real flight stacks filter the gyro signal before the rate controller as well: a low-pass on all terms and often notch filters at the frequencies of motor and propeller vibration, whose frequencies move with motor speed. Every filter adds phase lag to the whole loop, not only to D. The art is to remove the vibration bands while keeping the lag at the loop's crossover small; logs with spectra of the gyro signal (DN202 F10-09) are the evidence. D1U1

Software in the loop

Software in the loop runs the real flight stack against a simulator. It tests the stack's controllers, parameters, logging and failsafes, not the real vehicle's physics: a SITL model's motor lag, inertia and noise are whatever the model says. The value of SITL for tuning is the workflow — change a parameter, run a step test, read the log — and the confidence that the parameters and log fields you will use on the vehicle exist and behave as expected. Gains from SITL are a starting point for a real vehicle at best. D1D2

How the hardware actually does it

The rate loop's plant is mostly hardware: the ESC and motor's response time (30 ms in the course quad's model, DN201), the propeller's inertia, the frame's stiffness and the IMU's own filters. A stiff frame with small propellers responds faster and tolerates higher P; a large, flexible frame with big propellers needs a slower loop. The gains of this chapter belong to the simulated course quad only. D1U1

Noise on the motor command is not harmless even when the motor cannot follow it. In the simulator the effect stops at the motor model; on a real vehicle, the ESC drives current to chase every change of command, and current that does not change the speed ends as heat in the motor windings and the ESC. How much heat depends on the motor, ESC and command rate, and was not measured in this build (untested on hardware); hot motors after a tuning change are a common field report and are the forensic lab's scenario. D1R2

Safety

Tuning on a real vehicle is done only after the simulator work, with propellers on only for flight tests in a safe area, small gain changes, and the kill switch in reach. Check motor temperature by hand only with the battery disconnected. A vehicle that oscillates after a gain change is landed and disarmed before anyone approaches it (safety page).

Untested on hardware

No motor, ESC or vehicle was available in this build; motor heating was not measured, and the PX4 SITL part of the lab was not run (PX4 and Gazebo are not installed in the build container). A real tuning session needs the vehicle, a restraint or a safe flight area, motor temperature or ESC current logging, and logs of rate setpoint against gyro. D1

Worked example

Problem. The course quad's motor 1 propeller gives 5 % more thrust than modelled. (a) What angular acceleration does that cause about the roll axis at hover? (b) What steady roll-rate error does a P-only rate loop with P = 20 leave? (c) How long does an integrator with ki = 5 need to cancel it, starting from that error? (d) With an unfiltered D term kd = 0.3 at 500 Hz and gyro noise 0.02 rad/s, what torque noise results?

  1. Force. Hover thrust per rotor 9.81/4 = 2.4525 N; 5 % is 0.1226 N.
  2. Torque and acceleration. × 0.15 m = 0.01839 N·m; / 0.010 kg·m² = 1.839 rad/s².
  3. (b) Steady error. The P term must supply 1.839 rad/s²: e = 1.839/20 = 0.092 rad/s. The simulation measured 0.089 (R1, trial 3).
  4. (c) Integrator time. 1.839/(5 × 0.09) ≈ 4.1 s; with ki = 40, 0.51 s.
  5. (d) D noise. 0.010 × 0.3 × √2 × 0.02/0.002 = 0.0424 N·m.

Printed by checks.cpp (run R3, W1–W3, W6). R3D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Amara's hands get tired and she notices. The motors heat up silently; nothing in the controller sees it unless temperature or current is logged. D1
    • 

  • Hands react continuously; the rate loop reacts in steps every 2 ms, and its sample delay matters (F10-19). U1
    • 

  • "Reacting harder" in the analogy is one knob. In the loop P, D and I trade different things — speed, damping, noise, steady error — and a better result in one column of R1 often costs another. R1

Summary


    

  • Tune with a fixed test that measures rise time, overshoot, disturbance error and motor noise.
    • 

  • P speeds the loop, leaves a steady error of (disturbance)/P and passes noise as J P σ; overshoot appears as P approaches the motors' speed.

  • D damps overshoot but amplifies noise by about √2 σ/dt; the D-term filter cut the motor spread from 7.34 to 2.11 rad/s here.

  • I removes a steady error over a time of about (disturbance)/(ki e); large I brings overshoot back.
    • 

  • Noise on the motor command can heat motors without moving the vehicle.
