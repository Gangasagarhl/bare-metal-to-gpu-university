Story hook

Analogy

The café has a system. The manager says "table 7" and never thinks about trays. The team leader turns that into "walk briskly to the left". The worker — Amara — turns "briskly to the left" into how far to lean the tray so the drinks do not slide when she speeds up. And her hands turn "lean this far" into tiny, fast twitches of the wrists. Nobody does anybody else's job, and each one checks only their own result, many times faster than the person above them gives new orders.

When the manager starts shouting a new table every second, the system falls apart: Amara is still leaning for the last order when the next one arrives, and the tray swings back and forth. This chapter builds that chain of command for a quadcopter, measures how fast each level is, and shows what happens when a level above becomes too impatient for the levels below.

What you will be able to do

  • Draw the cascade of position, velocity, attitude and rate loops with its estimator, and say what each loop's input, output and update rate is.
  • Compute the attitude setpoint and thrust from a wanted acceleration (the thrust vector), including the heading frame and a tilt limit.
  • Tune a cascade from the inside out with step tests, and check that each loop is several times slower than the one inside it.
  • Diagnose an outer-loop oscillation from setpoint-versus-actual logs at every level.
  • Explain why an attitude estimator that is fooled by acceleration can make the closed loop unstable.
Jargon box
Cascaded control
Simple words: controllers stacked inside each other, where each one gives the next one its target. Analogy: manager → team leader → worker → hands. Precise: a nested arrangement of feedback loops in which the output of an outer controller is the setpoint of the inner one; for a multirotor: position → velocity → attitude → body rate → torque. B1D1
Setpoint versus actual
Simple words: what a loop was asked to do, plotted next to what it achieved. Analogy: the order Amara was given next to what she did. Precise: the pair (reference, measured state) logged for each loop of a cascade; the loop whose actual value fails to follow its own setpoint is the one at fault. D1R1
Time-scale separation
Simple words: each inner loop must be clearly faster than the loop around it. Analogy: the hands react many times before the worker changes the lean. Precise: the design rule that the closed-loop bandwidth of an inner loop is several times (often quoted as 3 to 10) that of the outer loop, so the outer loop can treat the inner one as nearly ideal. B1D1
Thrust vector
Simple words: the direction and size of the push the rotors must give to produce the wanted acceleration. Analogy: how Amara must lean the tray to speed up without spilling. Precise: F = m (asp + g ez); its direction is the wanted body z axis, which together with the yaw setpoint fixes the attitude setpoint, and its component along the current body z axis is the thrust command. D1B1
Tilt limit
Simple words: the most the controller is allowed to lean the vehicle. Analogy: Amara never tips the tray more than a set angle, whatever the team leader asks. Precise: a bound on the angle between the thrust vector and the vertical (35° in this chapter's code), applied by scaling the horizontal acceleration setpoint to at most (g + az) tan(limit). D1R3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A quadcopter cannot push itself sideways. Its rotors only push "up" from the drone's own point of view. To fly forward it must first tip its nose down, so part of the push points forward. To tip, it must spin a little. To spin, the rotors on one side must turn faster than on the other.

So flying to a spot is a chain of jobs. "Go to that spot" becomes "move forward at this speed". That becomes "tip this much". That becomes "spin this fast". That becomes "these rotors faster, those slower". Each job has its own little boss who checks, again and again, whether its job is done.

The bosses near the rotors must be very quick. The boss for "go to that spot" can be slow. If a slow boss tries to be as quick as the boss below it, they start fighting, and the drone swings back and forth.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why a cascade

The quadcopter's dynamics (DN201 F10-03) form a chain of integrations: torque changes the body rate, rate changes the attitude, attitude tilts the thrust and so sets the horizontal acceleration, acceleration changes the velocity, velocity changes the position. Cascaded control closes one loop around each link of the chain. Each loop needs only a simple controller, each can be tested alone, and each output can be limited (a maximum rate, a maximum tilt, a maximum speed), which a single controller from position to motors would make hard. From acceleration to attitude: the thrust vector

The velocity loop asks for an acceleration asp. The rotors can only push along the body z axis with thrust T, so T ez,body = m (asp + g ez) = F, the thrust vector. Its direction is where the body z axis must point; its length is the thrust. With the yaw setpoint, which the thrust vector does not fix, this gives the attitude setpoint. D1B1

Example: asp = (2, 0, 0) m/s² for the 1 kg quad at yaw 0. F = (2, 0, 9.81) N, |F| = 10.012 N, and the body must pitch atan(2/9.81) = 11.52° nose down (positive pitch in DN201's axes). At yaw 90° the same world acceleration needs roll 11.52° instead: the code expresses F in the heading frame first (line 145) (run R3, W1, W2). R3

The tilt limit keeps the angle of F from the vertical below 35°: the horizontal part of asp is scaled down to at most (g + az) tan 35° = 6.869 m/s² in level flight (W3). The thrust command is the part of F along the current body z axis (line 150), so a vehicle still tilting towards its setpoint does not climb or sink while it does so. R3D1

Attitude error without Euler angles

The attitude loop compares the estimated quaternion q with the setpoint qd through the error rotation qe = q−1 ⊗ qd, expressed in the body frame. For a rotation by angle θ about axis n, the vector part of qe is n sin(θ/2), so 2 × (vector part) is close to the rotation vector θ n for small errors, and choosing the sign of qe,w turns the shorter way round. The rate setpoint is attP times that: a 10° error gives 6 × 2 sin 5° = 1.046 rad/s (W3, K3). No Euler angles, no gimbal lock, and the rate setpoint is already in body axes, where the gyro measures. D1R3U1

Reading setpoint versus actual

Figure 2 and the second half of R1 print setpoint versus actual at every level during the position step. At 0.2 s the position loop asks for 1.99 m/s; the velocity is 0.17 m/s; the velocity loop asks for 21.49° of pitch; the pitch is 13.81°, catching up; the attitude loop asks for 0.81 rad/s, the rate is 1.61 rad/s. At each level the actual value chases its setpoint with a lag, and the inner loops catch up faster. The rule for diagnosis follows: find the outermost loop whose actual value does not follow its own setpoint; the loops inside it are innocent if they follow theirs. R1D1

(The position RMS includes the time spent travelling to each new corner, which is why it is above 1 m even with the true state.) With τ = 0.3 s the vehicle was lost. The mechanism is a positive feedback loop: to accelerate, the controller tilts the vehicle; the accelerometer then reads "level" (F10-13); a fast filter believes it and reports less tilt than there is; the attitude loop tilts further to reach its setpoint; the vehicle accelerates harder. The estimator closed a loop of its own around the controller. With τ = 3 s the same estimator is harmless, with a 6.5° error. R2D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

How much separation, and why it matters

The loop analysis behind the rule: seen from the velocity loop, the attitude-and-rate cascade is not an ideal "tilt instantly" block but a low-pass with bandwidth ωatt. Its phase lag at the velocity loop's crossover frequency ωv is about atan(ωv/ωatt) for a first-order inner loop: 21° at a ratio of 1/2.7, 45° at a ratio of 1. That lag eats directly into the outer loop's phase margin (MA301 F0-72). Successive loop closure in Beard and McLain designs each loop with the inner closed loop as part of the plant; the ratio of bandwidths is the design variable. The forensic lab shows what happens at a ratio of 1.16. D1B1U1

Feed-forward

Each loop of Listing 1 reacts only to errors. When the setpoints come from a planned trajectory (DN302), the planned velocity, acceleration and even jerk can be fed forward: the velocity setpoint becomes vplan + posP (pplan − p), the acceleration setpoint gets aplan added, and so on. The feedback loops then correct only the small remaining errors, and tracking improves without raising any gain. D1B1

Other formulations

The thrust-vector step is a geometric controller in miniature: it works directly with the wanted body z axis. Fully geometric controllers on SO(3) and model-predictive controllers that handle position and attitude together exist and are studied in research and in some products; the cascade remains the structure most open flight stacks describe, because it is easy to tune and test loop by loop. B1D2

Not verified — check before relying on this

PX4's multicopter control is described in its documentation as a cascade of position (P), velocity (PID), attitude (P on a quaternion error) and body-rate (PID) controllers, and ArduPilot's copter control as a similar cascade with its own naming. The loop rates, gain names and units, default values, the way each stack converts thrust vector to attitude and the details of its tilt limit were not checked in this build, and the gains of this chapter are not portable to either. Read the PX4 controller diagrams and the ArduPilot copter tuning documentation (registry 4.8) for the release recorded in the dossier. D2D3

How the hardware actually does it

On a flight controller the loops run at different rates because their inputs arrive at different rates and their cost differs. The rate loop runs at or near the gyro rate, often in the same task as the gyro filtering, because its job is to react before the vehicle has rotated far; the position and velocity loops run at the estimator's output rate. This chapter's 500/250/50 Hz are a course choice that fits the simulator's 1 ms step. Each loop adds a sample of delay to the one outside it; F10-19 measures what delay does to the rate loop. D1U1

The limits in the cascade are hardware facts in disguise: the tilt limit protects the thrust margin (at 35° the vertical part of the thrust is cos 35° = 82 % of the total), the rate limit protects the motors' ability to produce torque, and the speed limits protect the estimator and the airframe. Their right values come from the vehicle's measured thrust and motor data (DN201 F10-02), not from this chapter. D1U1

Untested on hardware

No vehicle was flown in this build. The cascade flew only the DN201 simulated course quad, whose motors, inertia and sensors are models. Flying a cascade on a real vehicle needs a flight controller running it at known loop rates, measured mass, inertia and thrust curve, and the safety procedure of the club; the gains of this chapter are not transferable. D1

Worked example

Problem. The course quad (1 kg) hovers with yaw 0. The velocity loop asks for asp = (2, 0, 0) m/s². (a) Compute the thrust vector, the thrust and the attitude setpoint. (b) Repeat for yaw 90°. (c) What is the largest horizontal acceleration the tilt limit allows in level flight? (d) The current pitch is 1.52°, 10° short of the setpoint. What rate setpoint does the attitude loop produce?

  1. Thrust vector. F = m (asp + g ez) = (2, 0, 9.81) N; |F| = 10.012 N.
  2. Attitude. Body z must point along F: pitch = atan2(2, 9.81) = 11.52° (nose down, positive in DN201's axes), roll 0.
  3. (b) Yaw 90°. In the heading frame F becomes (0, −2, 9.81): roll = asin(2/10.012) = 11.52°, pitch 0. The world-frame acceleration is the same; which body angle produces it depends on the heading.
  4. (c) Tilt limit. 9.81 × tan 35° = 6.869 m/s².
  5. (d) Attitude loop. 2 sin(10°/2) = 0.1743 rad; × attP 6 = 1.046 rad/s, below the 4 rad/s rate limit.

All values are printed by checks.cpp (run R3, W1–W3, K3). R3D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • In the café, each person understands the order. In the cascade, an inner loop only sees a number; it cannot tell that its setpoint is impossible or that the loop above is oscillating. D1
    • 

  • The team leader does not need to know how Amara's wrists work. The thrust-vector step does need a model: mass, gravity and the fact that thrust points along body z. A wrong mass makes every level slightly wrong. D1
    • 

  • All café staff look at the same room. The loops all look at the estimator, and an estimator that is wrong in the right way can close a loop of its own and make the whole chain unstable (Layer 2, R2). R2

Expected observations


Raising an inner gain shortens its rise time until overshoot grows; the outer loop's overshoot rises when the ratio of rise times falls toward 1. A set of gains with ratios of 3 or more gives outer loops with little overshoot, but slower ones. The τ = 3 s run stays close to the true-state run, as in R2. Your numbers depend on your gains; R1 and R2 are the reference for the defaults.

Summary


    

  • A multirotor's cascade follows the chain of its dynamics: position → velocity → (thrust vector) → attitude → rate → torques → mixer.
    • 

  • The thrust vector F = m (asp + g ez) gives the attitude setpoint (with yaw) and the thrust; a tilt limit caps the horizontal acceleration at (g + az) tan(limit).
    • 

  • The attitude error is computed from quaternions: rate setpoint = attP × 2 vec(q−1 qd).
    • 

  • Tune from the inside out; each loop should be clearly slower than the one inside it (here ratios 2–2.7, with 7–8 % outer overshoot).
    • 

  • Diagnose by reading setpoint against actual at each level: the outermost loop that fails to follow its own setpoint is the suspect.
    • 

  • The estimator is part of the loop: a complementary filter with τ = 0.3 s made the vehicle unstable; τ = 3 s was harmless.
