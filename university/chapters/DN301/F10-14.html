Story hook

Analogy

Amara has learned a trick. For anything quick — a bump, a sudden turn — she trusts her wrists completely. But every few seconds she lets her slow sense of "down" nudge the tray back, a little at a time, never in one jerk. When she brakes hard, that slow nudge is wrong for a moment, but it is so gentle that the tray hardly moves before the braking is over. And the creeping slant her wrists would let build up never gets the chance: the slow nudge keeps pulling it back.

That is the whole complementary filter: one sense for fast changes, the other for the long run, and one number — how slow the nudge is — that decides where the hand-over happens. This chapter finds that number for a multirotor from a recorded flight, and shows why it must be much slower than for a person.

What you will be able to do

  • Derive the complementary filter as a high-pass filter on integrated gyro rates plus a low-pass filter on accelerometer tilt, with weights that add up to one.
  • Implement the angle-blending filter and the vector (Mahony-style) filter with a bias integrator, and run them on recorded IMU data.
  • Choose the crossover time constant from measured errors, and predict the steady error a gyro bias leaves behind.
  • Explain what the filter can and cannot estimate (yaw, z-gyro bias) and why accelerometer gating is weak on a multirotor.
  • Diagnose an estimator whose accelerometer correction has silently stopped working.
Jargon box
Complementary filter
Simple words: a way to mix two sensors so that each one is used where it is good. Analogy: trusting your wrists for quick tips and your sense of "down" for the long run. Precise: an estimator x̂ = H(s) x1 + (1 − H(s)) x2 whose two filters add up to one at every frequency; for attitude, a high-pass on the integrated gyro and a low-pass on the accelerometer tilt. B1B2D1
Low-pass and high-pass filter
Simple words: a low-pass keeps slow changes and smooths fast ones away; a high-pass does the opposite. Analogy: the slow nudge toward "down" and the quick wrist reaction. Precise: first-order filters 1/(τs + 1) and τs/(τs + 1); below the corner frequency 1/τ rad/s the low-pass passes the signal, above it the high-pass does (MA301 F0-72). U1D1
Crossover time constant (τ)
Simple words: how slow the nudge toward "down" is. Analogy: how many seconds Amara waits before her sense of "down" wins. Precise: the time constant shared by the two filters; at sample period dt the discrete weight on the gyro path is α = τ/(τ + dt). D1
Accelerometer gate
Simple words: a rule that ignores the accelerometer when its reading looks suspicious. Analogy: not trusting your sense of "down" while you are clearly braking. Precise: a test on the measurement (here |f| between 0.9 g and 1.1 g) that decides whether the accelerometer correction is applied to this sample. D1B2
Bias integrator (integral correction)
Simple words: a slow memory that learns the gyro's constant error and subtracts it. Analogy: Amara noticing that her wrists always feel a faint twist to the left, and ignoring it. Precise: the ki term of the vector filter, b̂ ← b̂ − ki e dt, which removes a constant gyro bias from the attitude error at the cost of slower, coupled dynamics. B2D1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows both filters of this chapter as data flow. Row A is the form most people meet first: two weighted paths and a sum. Row B is the vector form published by Mahony, Hamel and Pflimlin, which works on a quaternion and can learn the gyro bias.

Figure 2 is the result that decides the design: whole-flight error against τ. Too fast (small τ) and the filter believes the fooled accelerometer; too slow (large τ) and it inherits the gyro's drift.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

You have two helpers. One is quick but forgetful: it notices every small turn at once, but its mistakes pile up. The other is slow but steady: it always knows where "down" is in the long run, but it gets confused whenever the drone speeds up or slows down.

So you let the quick helper do the work, moment by moment. Then, very gently, you let the slow helper pull the answer back toward its idea of "down". The pull is so gentle that a short confusion of the slow helper hardly matters. But it never stops, so the quick helper's mistakes never pile up for long.

How gentle should the pull be? That is the one number you must choose. For a drone it has to be gentler than you might think, because a drone confuses its slow helper every time it moves.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Two filters that add up to one

Write the gyro-integrated tilt as θg and the accelerometer tilt as θa. In Laplace terms (MA301 F0-69, F0-70) the gyro measures sθ plus a bias, and the accelerometer measures θ plus disturbances. The complementary filter is D1B1

θ̂(s) = τs/(τs + 1) · θg(s) + 1/(τs + 1) · θa(s)

The two transfer functions add up to exactly one, so a perfect signal passes unchanged at every frequency: the filter adds no lag of its own to the true attitude. What each sensor contributes is filtered: the gyro's slow errors (bias, drift) go through a high-pass filter that removes them below 1/τ rad/s, and the accelerometer's fast errors (noise, short manoeuvres) go through a low-pass filter that removes them above 1/τ. The crossover time constant τ sets the hand-over frequency. D1U1

In code: blend after every gyro step

Discretised with sample period dt, the filter becomes: predict with the gyro, then blend with the accelerometer tilt, D1

θ⁻ = θ̂k−1 + (rate from gyro) · dt
θ̂k = α θ⁻ + (1 − α) θa,k, α = τ/(τ + dt)

For τ = 1 s and dt = 0.01 s, α = 0.990099: each sample moves the estimate one percent of the way toward the accelerometer. For τ = 3 s, α = 0.996678. The crossover frequency is 1/τ rad/s, 0.159 Hz for τ = 1 s (run R3, W1). The sum of the two weights is one, which is the discrete form of "the filters add up to one". R3

What each error source leaves behind

Gyro bias. A constant bias b in the gyro path is integrated (ramp b t) and then high-passed. The result settles at a constant error τ b. With the recording's biases and τ = 1 s: 0.229° in roll and −0.344° in pitch (R3, W2). The bench log of the forensic lab shows these offsets: the estimate of a level, still vehicle sits at about +0.2° roll and −0.35° pitch. R3R2D1

Accelerometer noise. White tilt noise of standard deviation σ is averaged by the low-pass; for small dt the output noise is about σ √(dt/(2τ)). The recording's 1.46° per sample becomes about 0.10° for τ = 1 s (R3, W3). R3D1

Manoeuvres. A manoeuvre of duration T that fools the accelerometer by an angle Δ moves the estimate by roughly Δ (1 − e−T/τ): about Δ T/τ when T ≪ τ. On a multirotor Δ is the whole tilt of the manoeuvre (F10-13, Figure 1 B). To keep it small, τ must be several times longer than the longest manoeuvre, and the bias error τ b then grows. That is the U-shape of Figure 2. D1R1

The vector form and the bias integrator

Angle blending works on roll and pitch numbers and inherits the problems of Euler angles. Mahony, Hamel and Pflimlin describe a filter on the rotation itself: measure the "up" direction v = f/|f|, predict it from the estimate as v̂ = R̂T(0, 0, 1), and use their cross product e = v × v̂ as the error. The estimate is turned by the corrected rate ωgyro − b̂ + kp e, and the bias integrator b̂ ← b̂ − ki e dt learns the gyro bias. B2D1

For small errors, kp plays the role of 1/τ: run R1 shows "B kp 1.0 ki 0.00" at 5.15° overall, the same as the angle filter with τ = 1 s. With ki = 0.05 the filter learned −0.0058 of the true −0.0060 rad/s pitch-axis bias in 25 s, but only 0.0014 of 0.0040 on the roll axis, and nothing about the z bias (0.0000 of 0.0030): gravity says nothing about rotation about the vertical. Its overall error got slightly worse (5.29°), because the integrator also learns the manoeuvre errors as if they were bias. A bias integrator is worth having on long, gentle flights and needs a slow ki; it is not a cure for a fooled accelerometer. R1B2

Gating the accelerometer

A common rule skips the accelerometer correction when |f| is far from g, because then the vehicle is clearly accelerating. On a multirotor this accelerometer gate is weak: as F10-13 showed, |f| = T/m, and a vehicle that holds its height at 20° of roll has |f| only 0.63 m/s² above g, inside a ±10 % gate, while the tilt reading is 20° wrong. Gates catch hard climbs, free fall and impacts; they do not catch a steady tilted acceleration. And a gate can fail silently in the other direction, as the forensic lab shows. D1U1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Filters on the rotation group

Mahony et al. formulate the filter directly on SO(3), the group of rotation matrices, and give stability results for it, including for several vector measurements (gravity and the magnetic field) and for the bias-estimating version; the conditions and proofs are in the paper (title only in this build). Two practical consequences matter here: the filter needs no Jacobians and no covariance, so it runs in a few dozen arithmetic operations per sample; and adding a second reference vector, such as the magnetic field, is one more cross-product term in e, which also makes yaw and the z-gyro bias observable. B2D1

Tuning beyond one τ

Engineers extend the basic filter in several directions: a different τ for roll/pitch and for yaw; a gain that drops when the gate suspects acceleration, instead of an all-or-nothing gate; correcting the accelerometer for the vehicle's known acceleration (for example from GNSS velocity) before using it, which turns the complementary filter into a simple aided filter; and freezing the bias integrator during manoeuvres. Each extension needs its own evidence on recorded data, as in this lab: one change at a time, judged per part of the flight. B1D1

When to move on

The complementary filter has no idea of its own uncertainty, so it cannot tell when it is unsure; and it has no model of the vehicle's motion, so it cannot use GNSS velocity to separate tilt from acceleration. Both are what the extended Kalman filter adds in F10-15, at the price of matrices, Jacobians and tuning of noise models. On the same recording, F10-15's GNSS-aided filter reaches 0.44° RMS overall, about seven times better than the best τ here. U1D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

How the hardware actually does it

On a flight controller the filter runs in the same task that reads the IMU, once per sample, in floating point on the microcontroller (DN202 F10-07). Its cost is tiny compared with the rest of the flight stack. Three hardware facts shape it more than the algorithm does: the IMU's own digital low-pass filter adds delay before the samples reach the filter; vibration that the IMU's filter does not remove ends up in the accelerometer tilt (DN202 F10-09); and the accelerometer's measurement range sets whether hard manoeuvres clip, which a gate on |f| may or may not catch. The datasheet of the IMU on your kit gives its filter options and ranges (DN202). U1B1

Units and scaling are hardware facts too. A driver delivers accelerometer counts converted with a scale factor from the datasheet; whether the result is in m/s² or in units of g is a decision of the driver's author. A filter that assumes one unit and receives the other does not crash: it quietly computes something else. The forensic lab is about exactly that. U1R2

Untested on hardware

No real IMU and no flight controller were available in this build. The filters ran only on the simulated recording of F10-13. Running them on a real IMU needs the DN202 bench (IMU on a flight controller or breakout board, propellers removed), a logger at a known sample rate, and a reference attitude (a level table and known tilts) to grade against; the delays and vibration of a real IMU will change the best τ. D1

Worked example

Problem. The angle-blending filter runs at dt = 0.01 s with τ = 1 s. Its previous roll estimate is 2.0°. The gyro gives a roll rate of 0.10 rad/s (and zero pitch and yaw rates), and the accelerometer tilt gives roll 3.0°. (a) Compute the new estimate. (b) What constant roll error does a gyro bias of 0.004 rad/s leave with this τ? (c) How does it change with τ = 3 s?

  1. Weight. α = 1/(1 + 0.01) = 0.990099.
  2. Predict. 0.10 rad/s × 0.01 s = 0.001 rad = 0.0573°; θ⁻ = 2.0573°.
  3. Blend. θ̂ = 0.990099 × 2.0573 + 0.009901 × 3.0 = 2.0666°. The accelerometer moved the estimate by only 0.0093°.
  4. (b) Bias. Steady error = τ b = 1 × 0.004 = 0.004 rad = 0.229°.
  5. (c) Slower filter. τ = 3 s gives 0.688°: three times larger. The bias error grows in proportion to τ, the manoeuvre error falls roughly as 1/τ; that is the trade-off of Figure 2.

Steps 1–4 are printed by checks.cpp (run R3, W1, W2, W6); step 5 is 3 × 0.229°. R3D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Amara can feel when she is braking and stop trusting her "down" sense right then. The simple filter cannot: it trusts the accelerometer by the same small amount all the time, and a gate on |f| does not see a multirotor's steady tilted acceleration (Layer 2). D1
    • 

  • A person's slow sense of "down" is good after a few seconds; a multirotor's needs a τ of several seconds to stay below 3° RMS on this recording, and still fails in long manoeuvres. The analogy suggests a faster hand-over than a drone can afford. R1
    • 

  • Amara's senses have no "units". The filter's accelerometer path is a formula with an expected magnitude; feeding it numbers in the wrong unit breaks it without any visible sign (forensic lab). R2

Your exact numbers depend on your choices; the R1 rows are the reference for the settings they list.

Summary


    

  • A complementary filter high-passes the integrated gyro and low-passes the accelerometer tilt; the two filters add up to one.
    • 

  • In code: predict with the gyro, then blend with α = τ/(τ + dt).
    • 

  • Gyro bias leaves a steady error τ b; accelerometer noise is averaged to about σ √(dt/(2τ)); manoeuvres pull the estimate by about Δ T/τ.
    • 

  • On the recording the best τ is 3–10 s (2.98–2.99° RMS overall); small τ is good only when the vehicle does not accelerate.
    • 

  • The vector (Mahony-style) filter works on the rotation, with kp ≈ 1/τ and a bias integrator; gravity alone cannot reveal yaw or the z-gyro bias.
    • 

  • Gating on |f| is weak on a multirotor and can fail silently, for example when units change.

Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. Log 1 receives |f| ≈ 9.81 and accepts 100 % of samples. Log 2 receives |f| ≈ 1.000 and accepts 0 %: the accelerometer arrives in units of g, while the gate expects m/s² (8.83 to 10.79, R3, W4).
    2. 

  2. A gyro-only integration. With the biases 0.004 and −0.006 rad/s, 120 s give 27.5° of roll and −41.3° of pitch (R3, W5); log 2 shows 23.95° and −44.17°. The difference comes from the z bias (0.003 rad/s) leaking between roll and pitch through the Euler-angle coupling of F10-13 as the pitch error grows.
    3. 

  3. The steady complementary-filter error τ b with τ = 1 s: 0.229° roll and −0.344° pitch (R3, W2). Log 1 sits near +0.2° and −0.35° with noise.
    4. 

  4. Root cause: the new driver changed the accelerometer unit from m/s² to g without the estimator being changed. The gate turned the unit error into "the vehicle is always accelerating", so the accelerometer correction was switched off for every sample; the filter degraded into a drifting gyro integrator. No error appeared because a rejected sample is normal behaviour for a gate, and nothing counted rejections.
    5. 

  5. Fix: one documented unit for the accelerometer interface (m/s² here), converted in the driver; the estimator checks it at start-up. Tests: (1) a bench test that a still IMU reports |f| within a tolerance of 9.81 m/s² (catches the unit); (2) a monitor that raises a warning when the gate rejects more than a set fraction of samples for more than a few seconds (catches any future reason for silent rejection).
    6. 



How the evidence was produced: creep_log.cpp runs the same gated angle filter (τ = 1 s, gate 0.9 g to 1.1 g in m/s²) on 120 s of a still vehicle with the F10-13 gyro biases and noise; for log 2 every accelerometer value is divided by 9.81 before the filter sees it (the second run call). Wrong answers and why: "the new gyro driver added a bias" — log 1's offsets are the expected τ b and log 2's drift rates match the same biases integrated without correction; "the IMU is mounted crooked" (F10-13) — a mounting offset is constant, it does not grow with time; "the gate threshold is too tight" — the threshold is reasonable for m/s²; it is the unit of the input that is wrong.
