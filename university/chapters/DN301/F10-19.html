Story hook

Analogy

Two things went wrong at the café this week. On Monday a new waiter was told to "react faster" and started to shake: every time the tray tipped a little, his hands pushed back hard — but a moment late — so they pushed past level, and then back again, and the tray buzzed in his hands. On Tuesday Amara set her tray on a sloping counter and kept pressing the low side to level it, harder and harder; the counter did not let it move. When she lifted it, all that pressing came free at once and the tray tipped the other way.

Both stories are in this chapter, as a quadcopter that buzzes after a tuning session and one that leans the wrong way on take-off. Both are found the same way: by putting what each loop was asked for next to what it did.

What you will be able to do

  • Read setpoint-versus-actual logs of a cascade and locate the loop that oscillates.
  • Estimate an oscillation's frequency from a log and relate it to loop delay and gain.
  • Measure the ultimate gain of a loop in simulation and the gain margin of a tuning, with and without delay.
  • Recognise integrator windup before take-off and in saturation, and specify the rule that prevents it.
  • Write a diagnosis that names the gain or rule at fault and the evidence for it.
Jargon box
Ultimate gain (Ku)
Simple words: the gain at which a loop stops calming down after a kick and keeps swinging. Analogy: how hard the waiter's hands can react before the tray starts to buzz on its own. Precise: the smallest proportional gain at which the closed loop no longer returns to rest after a disturbance; the oscillation frequency there is the ultimate frequency. The gain margin of a tuning is Ku divided by its gain (MA301 F0-72). D1U1
Loop delay
Simple words: the time between something happening to the vehicle and the controller's answer reaching the motors. Analogy: how late the waiter's hands react. Precise: the sum of sensing, filtering, computation and actuation delays in a loop; a delay T adds a phase lag of 360° × f × T at frequency f without changing the gain, which lowers the ultimate gain. D1R2
Lightly damped oscillation
Simple words: a swing that dies out only slowly, so any noise keeps it going. Analogy: the tray that keeps buzzing because the floor keeps trembling. Precise: the response of a stable loop with closed-loop poles close to the imaginary axis; with noise or repeated inputs it looks like a sustained oscillation at the loop's resonance, without the loop being unstable. D1R1
Landed-state integrator hold
Simple words: not letting the controllers' memories build up while the vehicle sits on the ground. Analogy: Amara not pressing against the counter while the tray rests on it. Precise: a rule that keeps the integrators of the attitude-related loops at zero (or frozen) while a landed detector reports ground contact, and releases them only after lift-off. D1R3

Related terms from earlier courses: Integrator windup, Anti-windup, Limit cycle, Delay margin and Gain margin.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When a drone shakes, you want to know who is shaking it. The drone's helpers are stacked: one says where to go, one how to lean, one how fast to spin. Each helper writes down what it asked for and what happened. So you look through the notes, helper by helper. If a helper asked for "stay still" and the drone kept shaking anyway, that helper is the one making the shaking.

Shaking usually means a helper reacts too hard, too late. It pushes, the drone moves, but the helper only notices a moment later, so it is already pushing too far. Then it pushes back too far. Back and forth.

A different problem happens on the ground. A helper that tries to level the drone on a slope gets nowhere, so it keeps pushing harder and harder in its memory. At take-off all that pushing comes out at once. The fix is simple: tell the helpers not to remember anything until the drone has left the ground.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A method for logs

The rule of F10-16 becomes a procedure: D1U1

  1. Describe the symptom in numbers: which axis, which frequency, how large, when (hover, after inputs, at take-off).
  2. Walk the cascade from the outside in: for each loop, plot setpoint against actual. A loop whose setpoint is steady while its actual value swings is producing the swing; a loop whose setpoint swings is following orders from outside.
  3. Compare axes: a problem on one axis only points to a per-axis setting; a problem on all axes to something shared (estimator, vibration, delay, mixer).
  4. Relate the frequency to the loop: rate-loop oscillations are fast (several to tens of hertz on a small vehicle); position-loop swings take seconds (F10-16's forensic: 0.5 Hz).
  5. Reproduce in simulation with the suspected setting, and confirm that changing it back removes the symptom.

Gain, delay and the ultimate gain

F10-17 tuned the rate loop with no delay in the loop except the motor response and the 2 ms sample. Real loops have more: gyro filtering, computation, ESC update and motor response. A pure delay T leaves the loop's gain unchanged and adds phase lag 360° f T: 18.0° at 8.33 Hz for 6 ms, 22.7° at 10.5 Hz (run R4, W2). That lag comes straight off the phase margin. R4D1

R2D1

Below Ku the loop is stable, but close to it the response is a lightly damped oscillation: a kick rings for many cycles, and continuous noise keeps it ringing. That is what a "buzz" in hover usually is. D1

Integrator windup on the ground

Listing 3 puts the course quad on ground sloping 8°, motors at low thrust, with the attitude and rate loops already running and a level setpoint. The attitude loop asks for −0.837 rad/s of roll rate (W4); the ground does not allow any rotation, so the rate error stays and the rate integrator charges at ki e per second: after 4 s it holds −16.74 rad/s², or −0.1674 N·m of torque (W5). Run R3 shows the torque command growing from −0.1883 to −0.3347 N·m on the ground. At lift-off that torque throws the vehicle 7.74° the other way; it takes seconds to return (−5.58° at 5.5 s). With the integrators held at zero until lift-off, the same take-off leans at most 0.07° the wrong way. The difference in torque at 4.00 s, −0.1672 N·m, is the wound-up integral (W6). R3R4

The landed-state integrator hold prevents this. It depends on a reliable decision "the vehicle is on the ground", which flight stacks make from thrust, vertical speed and acceleration (unverified box); the simulation here is told directly. The same mechanism appears in flight whenever a loop is saturated (F10-18): the integrator charges against a limit the loop cannot pass. Anti-windup for that case stops or limits integration while the output is saturated (RB202). D1U1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

From logs to frequency responses

Setpoint-against-actual time plots identify the loop; frequency-domain analysis of the same logs quantifies it. With enough excitation (stick inputs or a deliberate frequency sweep), the ratio of the spectra of actual and setpoint estimates the closed-loop frequency response, and the ratio of the spectra of the loop's output and its error estimates the open-loop one; from these come bandwidth, resonance and margins without a model. The method needs care with noise, windowing and the correlation between noise and control, and is a topic in its own right; Ku testing as in Listing 2 is the simulation counterpart. D1B1

Using Ku for tuning

Classical rules (Ziegler–Nichols and its successors, RB202) derive PID gains from Ku and the ultimate period. They tend to give aggressive loops, and finding Ku on a real vehicle means making it oscillate, which is acceptable only on a test stand or in simulation. Their use here is as a check: a tuning whose P is a large fraction of Ku is fragile, and any added delay (a new filter, a slower ESC protocol, a heavier computation) may push it over. D1U1

Other oscillation sources

Not every oscillation is a gain problem. Frame and arm resonances seen by the gyro, propeller imbalance, a loose IMU mount (DN202) and estimator problems all appear as oscillations in the logs. They are told apart by frequency (structural modes are usually higher than the rate loop's), by whether the setpoint is involved (vibration appears with steady setpoints on all axes), and by whether changing the gain changes the frequency. D1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

PX4 writes ULog files and ArduPilot DataFlash logs; both record controller setpoints and estimates, and both projects document log-analysis tools and a landed-state detector that resets or freezes integrators on the ground. The names of the log topics and messages (for example the rate-setpoint and angular-velocity topics in ULog, or the rate messages in DataFlash), the fields, the logging rates, the landed-detector criteria and the integrator-reset behaviour were not checked in this build, and the forensic logs of this course are produced by its own programs in its own text format, not by either stack. Read the PX4 log-analysis documentation and the ArduPilot log documentation (registry 4.8) for the release recorded in the dossier before analysing a real log. D2D3

How the hardware actually does it

Each part of the chain contributes delay: the IMU's internal filter, the flight controller's gyro filters (F10-17), the time from computing a command to sending it, the ESC protocol and update rate, and the motor and propeller response. Their sum is a hardware property of a specific vehicle; this chapter's 6 ms is an exercise value, not a measurement. A filter or protocol change that adds two milliseconds can cut the margin more than any gain change, as Figure 2 shows. D1R2

Logging is hardware too. A flight log sampled more slowly than twice the oscillation frequency cannot show it correctly (DN202, sampling); a log written at a rate that the storage cannot sustain drops samples. Before trusting a log's frequency content, check its sample rate and gaps. D1U1

Safety

A vehicle that oscillates after a gain change is landed and disarmed at once; it is not flown again until the change has been understood in simulation. Never search for Ku on a free-flying vehicle. Test take-off integrator behaviour with the vehicle restrained or propellers removed (safety page).

Untested on hardware

No real flight log, vehicle or loop-delay measurement was available in this build. The forensic logs are produced by the course's own programs in its own text format, and the 6 ms delay is an exercise value. Applying the method to a real vehicle needs PX4 ULog or ArduPilot DataFlash logs with controller setpoints at a sufficient rate, a measured loop delay, and a restrained test for take-off behaviour. D1

Worked example

Problem. A log shows the roll rate crossing zero at 1.516 s and again at 1.578 s, with a nearly steady setpoint. The vehicle's loop delay is 6 ms and its roll-rate P is 120. (a) Estimate the oscillation frequency. (b) How much phase does the delay cost at that frequency? (c) Using R2, how close to instability is the roll-rate loop, and how much margin would the course default P = 20 have?

  1. (a) Frequency. Two successive zero crossings are half a period apart: 0.062 s, rounded to 0.060 s → period 0.12 s → 8.33 Hz (R4, W1).
  2. (b) Phase. 360° × 8.33 Hz × 0.006 s = 18.0° (W2).
  3. (c) Margin. At 6 ms Ku = 143.5 (R2). P = 120 is 0.84 of Ku: a gain margin of 1.2. P = 20 has 7.2 (W3).

Printed by checks.cpp (run R4). R4R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A shaking waiter feels it and slows down. A controller has no idea it is oscillating unless something measures it; the log is read afterwards. D1
    • 

  • The waiter's lateness is fixed. A vehicle's delay changes with filters, protocols and software versions, and with it the safe gain (Figure 2). R2
    • 

  • Amara knows when the tray is on the counter. The controller must be told by a landed detector, and that detector can be wrong — for example on a soft surface or in a slow, gentle take-off. D1

Summary


    

  • Diagnose by walking the cascade from the outside in: the loop whose actual value swings while its setpoint is steady is the source.
    • 

  • One-axis problems point to per-axis settings; all-axis problems to shared causes such as vibration, delay or the estimator.
    • 

  • Delay adds phase lag 360° f T and lowers the ultimate gain steeply: 975 at 0 ms, 144 at 6 ms, 93 at 10 ms for the course quad's roll-rate P.
    • 

  • A gain close to Ku gives a lightly damped oscillation that noise keeps alive: the forensic vehicle's roll-rate P = 120 had a margin of 1.2.
    • 

  • Integrators charge on the ground against a slope and release at lift-off (7.74° lean); holding them until lift-off removes it (0.07°).
