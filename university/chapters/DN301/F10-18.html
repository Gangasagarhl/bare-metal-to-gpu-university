Story hook

Analogy

In DN201, Amara held the tray on four fingertips: press harder at the front to tip it back, harder on the left to tip it right. Fingertips can only push up, and each one only so hard. Now the café is busy. She has to lift the tray over a chair, tip it to keep the drinks level, and turn it round to face a guest — all at once. Her fingertips cannot do everything. Something must give.

A sensible waiter keeps the tray level first, because a tipped tray spills everything. Height comes next. The turn to face the guest can wait a moment. A careless one pushes as hard as each finger can and lets the tray do whatever results. This chapter shows what each choice does to a quadcopter: the careless one makes it leap a metre into the air when it is only asked to turn.

What you will be able to do

  • Decide whether a wanted thrust and torque set can be produced by four rotors with force limits, and find the exact rotor forces when it can.
  • Explain what naive clipping of each rotor does to thrust and torques, including torques nobody asked for.
  • Implement a prioritised allocator (roll and pitch first, then thrust or yaw), with thrust shift and yaw reduction.
  • Compute yaw and roll authority at hover and their dependence on thrust.
  • Diagnose a yaw-induced climb from motor-speed and thrust logs.
Jargon box
Allocation priority
Simple words: the rule that says what to give up first when the motors cannot do everything asked. Analogy: keep the tray level first, then its height, then turn it. Precise: an ordering of the wrench components (here roll and pitch torque, collective thrust, yaw torque) such that an allocator meets each component fully before the next, and reduces only the lowest-priority components when the rotor limits are reached. D1D2
Thrust shift (desaturation)
Simple words: raising or lowering all four motors together so that the differences between them fit. Analogy: lifting or lowering the whole hand so every fingertip can still press its share. Precise: adding the same force to all rotors (a change of collective thrust only) so that the torque-producing differences lie inside [0, fmax]; it trades thrust accuracy for torque accuracy. D1R1
Yaw authority
Simple words: how much turning torque the rotors can make, which is small for a quadcopter. Analogy: turning the tray by twisting fingertips: possible, but weak. Precise: the largest yaw torque available while other demands are met; it comes from the rotors' reaction torques (coefficient c = kQ/kT = 0.015 m for the course quad), giving 4 c fhover = 0.147 N·m at hover thrust if thrust is kept. D1R3
Clipping cross-coupling
Simple words: when cutting each motor at its limit creates a push or a turn nobody asked for. Analogy: one fingertip that cannot press harder makes the tray tilt sideways. Precise: the error in the produced wrench, including non-zero components that were zero in the request, caused by clipping rotor commands independently after an exact inverse mixer. D1R1

The mixer itself and control authority were defined in DN201: see Mixer (control allocation) and Control authority; actuator saturation in MA301 and RB202: Saturation (actuator).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A quadcopter has four motors, and every motor can only spin between "stopped" and "as fast as it can". The flight computer works out how fast each one should spin to lift, tip and turn the drone. Usually the answer fits. Sometimes it does not: one motor would have to spin faster than it can, or slower than stopped.

Then the drone has to choose what to give up. The most important thing is to stay the right way up, so tipping comes first. Then staying at the right height. Turning round is the least urgent: it can happen a bit later.

If the drone does not choose — if it only cuts each motor at its limit — strange things happen. Asked only to turn, it can shoot upwards, because cutting the motors that wanted to go "slower than stopped" leaves the others pushing far too hard.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The feasible set

The mixer of DN201 F10-02 maps four rotor forces to thrust and three torques. For the X quad with its motors at distance a from both body axes and reaction coefficient c = kQ/kT, each rotor force is D1U1

fi = T/4 + (roll part)i + (pitch part)i + (yaw part)i
roll part = ±τx/(4a), pitch part = ±τy/(4a), yaw part = ±τz/(4c)

with signs from each motor's position and spin direction (allocation.hpp, lines 47–48). A request is feasible if every fi lies in [0, fmax]; for the course quad fmax = kT ωmax² = 10 N per rotor. Note the different denominators: with a = 0.15 m and c = 0.015 m, one newton-metre of yaw needs ten times the force difference of one newton-metre of roll. A quadcopter's yaw authority is small. D1R1

Prioritised allocation

An allocator with an allocation priority builds the answer in order (Listing 1, lines 57–103): D1

  1. Roll and pitch first. Compute their force differences. If their spread alone exceeds fmax, scale them down together (keeping the direction of the wanted tilt torque).
  2. Then yaw or thrust. Find the largest fraction s ∈ [0, 1] of the yaw differences that still fits — either at the requested thrust ("RP > T > Y": thrust before yaw) or at any thrust ("RP > Y > T": yaw before thrust).
  3. Thrust last: thrust shift. Add the same force to all four rotors, as close to T/4 as the range allows.

The two orders differ only in what yaw costs: RP > T > Y keeps the height and gives up yaw; RP > Y > T keeps the yaw and changes the height. R1D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Allocation in general

For vehicles with more rotors or with control surfaces, allocation is a constrained optimisation: find actuator commands u in their box that make B u as close as possible to the wanted wrench, with weights or priorities. The exact inverse (a pseudo-inverse for more actuators than wrench components) solves the unconstrained problem; the constraints turn it into a small quadratic or linear program, which flight software solves with fixed-iteration methods such as redistributed pseudo-inverse or active-set iterations so that the run time is bounded. Listing 1's three steps are the special case for a quadrotor with the structure of its mixer used directly. D1B1

Idle, airmode and low thrust

Case E shows a choice that matters on descent: near zero throttle the vehicle has almost no torque authority unless the allocator may raise thrust to keep it (here from 2.00 to 2.67 N). Raising thrust at idle keeps the vehicle controllable in fast descents and flips, at the price of not reaching the lowest throttle exactly; flight stacks offer this as a mode with its own name (unverified box). A minimum rotor speed above zero (ESC idle) is a further hardware limit the allocator must respect; the course quad's model has none. R1D1

Feedback to the loops

An allocator that gives up part of a request should tell the controllers. If the rate loop's yaw integrator keeps integrating an error that the allocator refuses to correct, it winds up and overshoots when authority returns (F10-19). The usual remedy is to report saturation per axis back to the rate controller, which then stops integrating in the saturated direction. D1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Both PX4 and ArduPilot are described as allocating with roll and pitch above yaw, and as offering a mode (called "airmode" in PX4) that keeps attitude authority at low throttle by raising thrust. Which order each stack uses between thrust and yaw, how it shifts thrust, how it handles motor idle, how saturation is reported to the rate controllers, and the names of the parameters involved were not checked in this build. Read the PX4 control allocation documentation and the ArduPilot motor documentation (registry 4.8) for the release recorded in the dossier. D2D3

How the hardware actually does it

The limits the allocator respects are the motor and ESC's: the largest speed at the present battery voltage (which falls during a flight, DN202), and the smallest speed at which the ESC keeps the motor running reliably. Neither is a fixed number; the course quad's 0 and 1000 rad/s are model values. Allocators on real vehicles work in normalised commands and rely on a thrust curve measured on a thrust stand (DN201 F10-04) to map them to forces. A wrong curve makes "feasible" wrong too. D1U1

Yaw authority depends on the propellers' reaction torque, which is set by propeller shape and speed; tilting motors slightly (a common frame design choice) adds a component of thrust to yaw control. Both are physical facts of the airframe to look up or measure, not values to assume. D1B1

Untested on hardware

No vehicle or ESC was available in this build. Rotor limits, idle speed and the thrust curve are model values of the course quad. Checking allocation on a real vehicle needs measured thrust curves (DN201 thrust stand), logged motor outputs during hard yaw and roll inputs, and a tethered or propeller-off test of any new mixer. D1

Worked example

Problem. Course quad at hover (T = 9.81 N). The rate loop asks for τx = 0.5 N·m and τz = 0.2 N·m (case C). (a) Find the parts of each rotor force and the exact solution. (b) Find the largest yaw fraction that fits with thrust kept, and the yaw torque that results. (c) With yaw kept instead, what thrust results?

  1. Parts. Base T/4 = 2.4525 N; roll part 0.5/(4 × 0.15) = 0.8333 N; yaw part 0.2/(4 × 0.015) = 3.3333 N (R3, W1).
  2. Exact. Applying the signs of the four motors: 6.6192, −1.7142, 4.9525, −0.0475 N (W2). Two rotors would need negative force: infeasible.
  3. (b) Yaw fraction. The worst rotor is motor 2: 2.4525 − 0.8333 − s × 3.3333 ≥ 0 gives s = 0.4857, so τz = 0.4857 × 0.2 = 0.0972 N·m (W3); forces 4.9050, 0, 3.2383, 1.6667 N (W4).
  4. (c) Thrust shift. Keep all of roll and yaw; the most negative part is −(0.8333 + 3.3333) = −4.1667 N, so the base must rise to 4.1667 N: thrust 16.667 N, 70 % above hover (W5).

Printed by checks.cpp (run R3). R3D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Amara can lift the tray with her whole arm when her fingertips are at their limit. The quad has no other actuator: every change of thrust, tilt and turn comes from the same four rotors. D1
    • 

  • Turning the tray is easy for a hand. Turning a quadcopter is its weakest axis: the yaw part needs ten times the force difference of the roll part for the same torque. R3
    • 

  • A waiter decides what to save in the moment. The allocator's priorities are fixed in advance and apply the same way in every situation; choosing them is a design decision with safety consequences. D1
