Story hook

Analogy

Amara's slow sense of "down" lets her down whenever she speeds up: it feels the push of her own steps as a slant. Now a friend walks beside her and calls out her walking speed every second: "faster… steady… slowing". That is a strange thing to help with balance, until you think it through. If Amara knows how her speed is changing, she knows how hard she is pushing forward, and she can subtract that push from what her body feels. What remains is the true "down", even while she brakes.

The friend is the GNSS receiver, the speed calls are its velocity measurements, and the bookkeeping that turns "speed is changing" into "this much of what I feel is my own push" is an extended Kalman filter. In this chapter you build one, see why the version without the friend is no better than F10-14's filter, and find out what happens when the friend shouts a wrong number.

What you will be able to do

  • Formulate attitude estimation as an extended Kalman filter: state, prediction from the gyro, accelerometer measurement model, Jacobians, and check the Jacobians numerically.
  • Show from the innovation statistic (NIS) when a filter's noise model is wrong, and why a gravity-only measurement model cannot beat a complementary filter on a multirotor.
  • Build a 12-state GNSS-aided inertial navigation filter that uses the IMU as process input, and explain how GNSS velocity makes tilt, yaw and gyro bias observable.
  • Configure an innovation gate from the innovation covariance, and judge from a log whether bad measurements were fused.
  • Read an estimator log for test ratios, innovations and rejections.
Jargon box
GNSS-aided inertial navigation (INS)
Simple words: working out attitude, speed and position mostly from the IMU, and correcting the result with satellite fixes. Analogy: Amara walking by feel, with a friend calling out her speed. Precise: a filter whose prediction integrates gyro rates into attitude and rotated specific force plus gravity into velocity and position, and whose updates use GNSS position and velocity; here an EKF with 12 states including the gyro bias. B1D1
IMU as process input
Simple words: feeding the IMU readings into the prediction step instead of treating them as measurements. Analogy: counting your own steps to know where you are, instead of asking someone. Precise: the gyro and accelerometer enter the process model xk = f(xk−1, uk) as the input u; their noise appears in the process noise Q, and no model of the vehicle's forces or motor thrust is needed. B1D1
Innovation test ratio
Simple words: how surprising a measurement is, in units of how surprising it is allowed to be. Analogy: the friend's speed call compared with what Amara expected, judged by how sure both of them are. Precise: for innovation component yi with predicted variance Sii, the ratio |yi|/√Sii; in this chapter's code a ratio above the gate rejects the sample. Flight stacks define their own ratios (unverified box). D1U1
Innovation gate
Simple words: the limit above which a measurement is thrown away as nonsense. Analogy: ignoring the friend if he suddenly shouts that you are running at 40 km/h. Precise: a threshold on the test ratio (here 5) or on the normalised innovation squared; it protects the state from outliers, at the risk of rejecting good data after the state itself has gone wrong. D1R3
Observability (of a state)
Simple words: whether the measurements carry any information about a state at all. Analogy: the friend's speed calls tell Amara about her push, but nothing about which way she faces while she stands still. Precise: a state is observable if different values of it produce different measurement histories; for the INS, yaw and the z-gyro bias are observable only while the vehicle accelerates horizontally. D1B1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The drone keeps a guess of how it is tilted, how fast it moves and where it is, and next to each guess it keeps a note of how unsure it is. Every hundredth of a second it moves its guesses forward using what the IMU feels, and it becomes a bit more unsure. Every tenth of a second the satellite receiver tells it where it is and how fast it moves. Then the drone compares: "I thought I was here, the satellites say there." It moves its guess part of the way, more if it was very unsure, less if the satellites are known to be rough.

Here is the clever part. If the drone's guess of its tilt is a little wrong, it pushes its speed guess the wrong way, and after a second the satellites notice the wrong speed. So the satellites, which know nothing about tilt, end up correcting the tilt.

And if the satellites suddenly say something crazy, like "you jumped 12 metres", the drone checks how surprising that is. Far too surprising: it ignores that message.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

First attempt: roll, pitch and two biases

The direct translation of F10-14 into an EKF has four states, x = [φ, θ, bx, by]. The prediction integrates the bias-corrected gyro with the Euler-angle rates of F10-13; the measurement is the accelerometer, modelled as gravity only: D1B1

h(x) = g (−sin θ, cos θ sin φ, cos θ cos φ)

The Jacobians F = ∂f/∂x and H = ∂h/∂x are written out by hand in Listing 1 and checked against central differences: the largest difference is 3.71 × 10−10, so the hand derivation is right. The rest is the EKF of F9-34. R1U1

Read the NIS column first. The normalised innovation squared yTS−1y has mean 3 for a three-component measurement when the filter's model is right (F9-34). With the honest sensor noise it is 33.92: the innovations are about √(33.92/3) ≈ 3.4 times larger than the filter expects. The filter is inconsistent, because the model "the accelerometer measures gravity" is wrong whenever the vehicle accelerates (F10-13). It also learns a wrong bias (−0.0119 instead of −0.0060): it explains the manoeuvres as gyro bias. R1D1

Inflating σ hides the model error and helps: σ = 4 m/s² reaches 2.99°, the same as the complementary filter with τ = 3 s (F10-14). That is no coincidence: a Kalman filter with a fixed, inflated R settles to a fixed gain, and a fixed-gain filter for this problem is a complementary filter. Matrices alone do not help; a better model does. R1U1D1

Second attempt: the IMU as process input

The better model stops treating the accelerometer as a measurement of gravity. Instead, the IMU is a process input: the measured specific force, rotated into the world frame with the current attitude estimate, plus gravity, is the acceleration, which is integrated into velocity and then position (F10-13: f = RT(a − gworld), so a = R f + gworld). D1B1

attitude ← attitude + (gyro − b) dt
v ← v + (R(attitude) f − (0, 0, g)) dt
p ← p + v dt

The measurements are GNSS position and velocity at 10 Hz. The state has 12 numbers: roll, pitch, yaw, three velocities, three positions and three gyro biases. This is a GNSS-aided inertial navigation filter (Figure 3). It needs no model of the motors or the airframe: whatever pushes the vehicle, the accelerometer feels it. D1R2

How GNSS velocity corrects tilt

Suppose the roll estimate is 1° wrong. The filter then rotates the measured specific force (about g, upwards) 1° the wrong way, so it believes in a sideways acceleration of g sin 1° = 0.171 m/s² that does not exist. After 1 s the predicted velocity is 0.171 m/s off, and the GNSS velocity (σ = 0.1 m/s in the recording) sees it; after 2 s the position is 0.342 m off (run R3, W2 and W3). The filter's covariance P holds the correlation between tilt error and velocity error that the prediction creates, so the velocity update corrects the tilt. This is observability through the dynamics: GNSS measures no angle at all. R3D1

Yaw works the same way, but only when the vehicle accelerates horizontally: a yaw error rotates the horizontal acceleration. A 1° yaw error during 3 m/s² of acceleration produces a 0.052 m/s² sideways error; in hover it produces nothing (R3, W6). Yaw and the z-gyro bias are therefore observable during manoeuvres and drift in a long hover. R3D1

The largest tilt error is in part 1, the first two seconds: the filter starts with 0.1 rad of uncertainty and needs a few GNSS samples to converge. After that, the errors in the manoeuvres that fooled every earlier method are below 0.5°. R2

The innovation gate

Before fusing a GNSS sample, the filter computes each innovation component yi and its predicted variance Sii = (H P HT + R)ii. The innovation test ratio |yi|/√Sii says how many standard deviations the surprise is. If any ratio exceeds the innovation gate (5 in ins.hpp), the whole sample is rejected. During the clean flight, the largest ratio reached 3.36 (R2, at 20.0 s) and no sample was rejected. R2D1

The gate's danger is the other side: if the state has gone wrong (for example after a long GNSS outage), honest measurements look surprising too and are all rejected, and the filter never recovers. Flight stacks therefore add rules for resetting the state to the measurement after a period of rejections; the details are theirs (unverified box). D1D2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Error-state filters

Listing 2 keeps roll, pitch and yaw in the state, which breaks down near ±90° pitch, and it differentiates propagate() numerically: 24 calls per IMU sample. Production filters for flight usually estimate a small error state (a three-component rotation error, velocity and position errors, sensor biases) next to a quaternion nominal state; the error is reset into the nominal state after every update. The error stays small, so its linearisation is accurate, and the rotation has no singularity. The structure is the same as here; the bookkeeping differs. B1D1

Delayed measurements

A GNSS fix describes the vehicle at the time of measurement, which reaches the flight controller some time later; the recording here pretends it arrives instantly. A filter that fuses a delayed measurement against the current state makes a systematic error proportional to the delay times the velocity. A common remedy is to run the filter on a delayed time horizon, buffer the IMU data, fuse each measurement at its own timestamp, and predict forward to the present for the controllers. D1B1

Tuning by consistency

The NIS check of Layer 2 is the main tool for tuning Q and R: in a consistent filter, the mean NIS equals the measurement dimension and the test ratios mostly stay below 2–3. A filter with mean NIS far above that is overconfident (it will reject good data or follow bad model assumptions), one far below is underconfident (it ignores useful data). Hand-tuning against truth, as this chapter can do on a simulated recording, is not available on a real vehicle; consistency is. U1D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

PX4's estimator is called EKF2 and ArduPilot's current one EKF3; both are described in their projects' documentation as error-state or extended Kalman filters that fuse IMU, GNSS, magnetometer, barometer and other sensors on a delayed time horizon with innovation consistency checks. The number and order of their states, how their test ratios are defined, the names of their gate and noise parameters, their reset rules and their default values were not checked in this build. Read the PX4 EKF2 documentation and the ArduPilot EKF documentation (registry 4.8) for the release recorded in the dossier before quoting any of it. D2D3

How the hardware actually does it

An EKF of this size is heavy for a microcontroller: a dense 12 × 12 covariance prediction is a few thousand multiply-adds per IMU sample, and production filters with more states run it at a rate below the IMU rate, on IMU samples that the driver has already integrated over a short interval (delta angles and delta velocities). Hand-derived and generated code that exploits the sparsity of F replaces the general matrix products of mat.hpp. D1B1

The GNSS receiver brings its own facts: an update rate (10 Hz in the recording), a latency, and an accuracy estimate it reports with each fix; the receiver's datasheet and the DN202 sensor chapters give them for your kit. Filters commonly use the receiver's reported accuracy to set R per sample instead of a fixed σ. Barometer and magnetometer (DN202) are further measurements of the same kind: each with a model h(x), a gate and its own failure modes. U1D1

Untested on hardware

No GNSS receiver, IMU or flight controller was available in this build; both filters ran only on the simulated recording, whose GNSS has no latency, no outages and Gaussian noise. A real test needs a logged flight with IMU and GNSS timestamps on one clock, the receiver's reported accuracy per fix, and an independent reference (for example a survey-grade receiver) to grade against. D1

Worked example

Problem. Shortly before the forensic lab's glitch, the filter's position variance along x is P⁻ = 0.08² = 0.0064 m² (an exercise value of the size the filter shows there), and GNSS position noise is σ = 0.5 m (R = 0.25 m²). A sample arrives with innovation y = 10.62 m. Treat x as a scalar. (a) Compute S, K and the correction if the sample were fused, and the variance afterwards. (b) Compute the test ratio and decide with a gate of 5. (c) Above which innovation does this gate reject?

  1. S. S = P⁻ + R = 0.0064 + 0.25 = 0.2564 m².
  2. K. K = P⁻/S = 0.02496: the filter trusts its prediction about 40 times more than one GNSS sample.
  3. Correction. K y = 0.265 m in one sample. Small, but every following glitched sample adds another, and the velocity state is pulled along too (log A of the forensic lab).
  4. Variance. P⁺ = (1 − K) P⁻ = 0.006240 m²: barely changed, as one sample should.
  5. (b) Ratio. 10.62/√0.2564 = 20.97 > 5: reject.
  6. (c) Threshold. 5 √S = 2.532 m. A 12 m jump is 23.7 standard deviations (12/0.506).

All values are printed by checks.cpp (run R3, W1, W4, W5). R3D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The friend calls out only speed; the GNSS receiver also gives position. Position alone would correct tilt too, but more slowly, through two integrations. D1
    • 

  • Amara can tell whether her friend is joking. The filter can only compare the call with its own prediction; if its prediction is already wrong, it may reject the truth and accept a lie (Layer 2, gate). D1
    • 

  • The analogy suggests the friend is needed only while braking. In the filter, GNSS velocity is fused all the time and also learns the gyro biases and yaw, which have nothing to do with braking. R2

Lab


Goal

Expected observations


Step 2 makes the check value large (order 10−3 or more) and usually worsens the errors. Step 3 improves the gravity-only filter only a little: no σ can fix a wrong model. Step 4 keeps the tilt error well below the complementary filter's but larger than with velocity, because position reveals tilt only through a second integration. Step 5 shows the position error growing during the outage and recovering when GNSS returns, provided the gate does not reject the returning samples. The exact numbers depend on your changes; the R1 and R2 values are the reference for the unchanged code.

The Jacobian check prints a value below 10−6 for the unchanged code; your report gives the mean NIS for every filter setting you claim is "tuned".

Summary


    

  • An EKF for attitude with the accelerometer as a gravity measurement is inconsistent on a multirotor (NIS 33.9 instead of 3) and, once R is inflated, no better than a complementary filter (2.99°).
    • 

  • Treat the IMU as process input: rotate specific force into the world, add gravity, integrate to velocity and position; fuse GNSS position and velocity.
    • 

  • Tilt becomes observable through velocity (1° → 0.17 m/s²); yaw and the z-gyro bias only during horizontal acceleration.
    • 

  • On the recording the GNSS-aided EKF reaches 0.44° tilt, 0.98° yaw and 0.12 m position RMS, and learns all three gyro biases.
    • 

  • The innovation test ratio |yi|/√Sii and a gate protect the state from outliers; too wide a gate lets a glitch in, too tight a gate can lock out good data.
    • 

  • Check Jacobians numerically and filters by consistency (NIS, 1-σ against errors).
