Story hook

Amara's table group in F6-23 works fast: every helper holds a square of bowls and cooks sixteen or sixty-four dishes from each handful of jars. But watch the rhythm of the evening. The runners leave for the pantry; everyone waits. The jars arrive; everyone cooks; the runners stand idle. Then the runners leave again, and everyone waits again.

Yusuf suggests a second tray. While the group cooks from the first tray, the runners are already on their way to fill the second. When cooking ends, the second tray is (with luck) already full, and the runners turn around to refill the first. Neither the stove nor the road should ever stand still for long.

There is a new danger. With two trays, it is easy to start cooking from a tray that only looks full because nobody checked that the runners are back. A tray of last round's jars looks exactly like a tray of this round's jars. This chapter builds the two-tray kitchen with the GPU's asynchronous copies, models how much it can gain, and shows on the university's emulator what happens when someone skips the check.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain double buffering as software pipelining: the load of tile t + 1 overlaps the computation of tile t, and predict its best-case gain with a timeline model.
  • Write a double-buffered SGEMM with __pipeline_memcpy_async, __pipeline_commit and __pipeline_wait_prior, with the waits and barriers in the right places.
  • Read the PTX (cp.async, commit_group, wait_group) and SASS (LDGSTS, LDGDEPBAR, DEPBAR) of an asynchronous-copy kernel, and recognise the fallback for older targets.
  • Account for the cost: twice the shared memory, more registers, lower occupancy.
  • Diagnose a missing wait from a test log in which only some shapes fail.
Jargon box
Double buffering
Simple words: using two buffers so that one can be filled while the other is used. Analogy: two trays: cook from one while the runners fill the other. Precise: a scheme with two shared-memory stages per operand; in iteration t the kernel computes from stage t mod 2 while the copies for tile t + 1 fill stage (t + 1) mod 2. D2 D5
Software pipelining
Simple words: starting the next piece of work before the current one is finished, so that different stages overlap. Analogy: runners already on the road while the cooks cook. Precise: restructuring a loop so that operations of iteration t + 1 (here, its loads) are issued during iteration t; with S stages, up to S − 1 tiles are in flight. D5
Asynchronous copy (cp.async)
Simple words: a copy from global memory straight into shared memory that the thread starts and does not wait for. Analogy: sending a runner without standing at the door until she is back. Precise: the PTX instruction family cp.async, exposed in CUDA C++ through __pipeline_memcpy_async(dst_shared, src_global, size) with sizes 4, 8 or 16 bytes (header cuda_pipeline_primitives.h of CUDA 12.0); the data does not pass through the thread's registers. H1 D4
Commit group
Simple words: a bundle of copies that you will later wait for together. Analogy: all the runners sent out for one tray. Precise: __pipeline_commit() closes the copies issued since the previous commit into one group (PTX cp.async.commit_group); groups complete in order. H1 D4
Wait prior
Simple words: wait until all bundles except the newest N have arrived. Analogy: "I can cook as soon as every runner except the ones for the next tray is back." Precise: __pipeline_wait_prior(N) (PTX cp.async.wait_group N) blocks the calling thread until at most N of its most recent committed groups are still pending; it covers only the calling thread's own copies, so a block-wide barrier must follow before other threads read the data. H1 D4 D1
Stage
Simple words: one of the buffers in the pipeline. Analogy: one tray. Precise: one set of shared-memory tiles (here As[s] and Bs[s], s ∈ {0, 1}); a kernel with S stages needs S times the shared memory of a single-buffered one. R3

Terms from earlier chapters: latency hiding, occupancy and Little's law (F6-20); register tiling (F6-23).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Stage = a tray on the table; double buffering = two trays; asynchronous copy = sending the runners and not waiting at the door; commit = "these runners belong to the next tray"; wait = "are the runners for this tray back?"; barrier = "is everybody's part of the tray here?"

With one tray, the kitchen either cooks or waits for jars. With two trays, the runners fill one tray while the cooks use the other. If cooking and fetching take the same time, the evening can take about half as long. If one of them takes much longer, the longer one decides, and the second tray helps less.

Two checks keep it safe. Before cooking, everyone checks that the runners for this tray are back. After cooking, everyone checks that everybody has finished with the tray before the runners refill it. A tray from last round looks just like a full tray, so skipping the first check gives wrong dishes, quietly.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The loop, restructured

Listing 1 keeps F6-23's register tiling and changes only how the tiles get into shared memory. Before the loop, it issues the copies for tile 0 into stage 0 and commits them. In iteration t it issues the copies for tile t + 1 into stage (t + 1) mod 2 (when there is a tile t + 1), commits them (an empty group on the last iteration keeps the counting uniform), waits until all of its groups except the newest have completed, synchronises the block, computes from stage t mod 2 exactly as F6-23 did, and synchronises again. D5 H1

The copies are 16 bytes each: __pipeline_memcpy_async(&As[stage][m][k], &A[r * K + c], 16). The header asserts that the size is 4, 8 or 16 bytes and that both addresses are aligned to that size, and that the destination is shared and the source global. H1 Hence the same rule as F6-23's float4 loads: K and N must be multiples of 4, and the launcher falls back to F6-23's scalar kernel otherwise.

A layout consequence

An asynchronous copy moves bytes as they are; it cannot transpose. F6-23 stored A transposed (As[k][m]) by splitting each float4 into four scalar stores. Listing 1 stores A as it is in memory (As[s][m][k]), 16 bytes at a time, and reads a thread's A values with index [tRow * TM + i][k]. Does that cost shared-memory instructions? The SASS answers: the compute part of the 64 × 64 / 4 × 4 kernel has 128 FFMA and 16 LDS.128 in both versions — the compiler reads each thread's eight consecutive k values of a row with two 16-byte loads instead. R6 (The same listing contains 72 lines @!PT LDS RZ, [RZ]: PT is the always-true predicate, so @!PT instructions never execute; they are not loads, and the count excludes them.)

How much can it gain?

Listing 4 models one block with T tiles, a load time L and a compute time C per tile (model inputs, not GPU measurements). Single buffering takes T(L + C). Double buffering takes L + T·C when compute dominates and T·L + C when loads dominate, i.e. about T·max(L, C). The speed-up is (L + C) ÷ max(L, C) for large T: at most 2, reached when L = C. The run prints 1.25 for L = 400, C = 100 and for L = 100, C = 400, 2.00 for L = C = 400, and 1.40 for L = 400, C = 1,000. R4 In a real SM, other resident blocks already overlap one block's loads with another block's compute; double buffering adds overlap inside a block, which matters most when few blocks are resident — exactly the situation that register tiling created (25–37.5 % theoretical occupancy in F6-23).

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What the primitives compile to

The CUDA 12.0 header cuda_pipeline_helpers.h, read in this build, defines the hardware path only when __CUDA_ARCH__ >= 800; there, a 16-byte copy emits cp.async.cg.shared.global, a 4- or 8-byte copy emits cp.async.ca.shared.global, __pipeline_commit() emits cp.async.commit_group, and __pipeline_wait_prior(N) emits cp.async.wait_group N (N capped at a default maximum of 8 stages). H1 For older targets the header selects another implementation without these instructions. The PTX of Listing 1 for sm_80 contains 24 cp.async.cg.shared.global, 2 cp.async.commit_group, 1 cp.async.wait_group and 2 bar.sync. R5 The SASS for sm_80 shows the copies as 24 LDGSTS.E.BYPASS.128, the commits as 2 LDGDEPBAR and the wait as 1 DEPBAR.LE; the SASS for sm_75 of the same source has no such instructions: 24 LDG.E.128.SYS and 48 STS.128, i.e. ordinary loads into registers and stores to shared memory. R5 The code is correct on both; only the newer target gets the asynchronous hardware path. Reading the SASS is the only way to be sure which you got.

What .cg and .ca mean for caching, and exactly what BYPASS denotes in the SASS mnemonic, are defined in the PTX ISA's cp.async section and the SASS instruction reference; this build could not open them (unverified box below).

The correctness argument

In iteration t, a thread has committed groups for tiles 0 … t + 1 (the last one possibly empty). wait_prior(1) returns when at most the newest group (tile t + 1) is pending, so all of this thread's copies for tile t are complete. H1 Other threads copied other parts of the tile; the first __syncthreads() makes sure each of them has passed its own wait and makes their writes visible. D1 After the compute, the second __syncthreads() guarantees that nobody still reads stage t mod 2 when iteration t + 1 issues copies of tile t + 2 into it. Zero-filled edge elements are written with ordinary stores in loadTile, which the first barrier also orders. The header also offers a zfill argument that copies fewer source bytes and fills the rest with zeros (it is the fourth parameter of __pipeline_memcpy_async in CUDA 12.0); Listing 1 does not use it, so the edge rule is visible in the source. H1

Testing an asynchronous copy without a GPU

A missing wait is a race: on a GPU the copy usually lands before a few hundred FMAs finish, so a test can pass for a long time. The university's emulator (cuda_shim.hpp of F6-21) is deliberately pessimistic: each __pipeline_memcpy_async is queued and performed only when the thread waits for its group (or ends), the latest moment the primitives allow. A kernel that reads a stage before waiting therefore reads stale data every time, deterministically. R1 This is a model of the specification's worst case, not of any GPU's timing.

The cost side

Two stages double the shared memory: 8,192 bytes for 64 × 64 (against 4,096) and 16,384 for 128 × 128 (against 8,192). The compiler report gives 72 registers for the 64 × 64 double-buffered kernel (78 for F6-23's vector version) and 143 for 128 × 128 (124 before). R3 On F6-20's example device that is 37.5 % and 12.5 % theoretical occupancy (F6-23's Listing 4). R7 Deeper pipelines (three or four stages) are common in library kernels and cost proportionally more shared memory; the CUTLASS designs of F6-27 make the stage count a parameter. D5

How the hardware actually does it

Without asynchronous copies, moving a value from global to shared memory takes two instructions per thread: a global load into a register, then a shared store from that register, and the store cannot issue until the load's data has arrived — so the warp stalls there unless the compiler moves the load far ahead (which needs registers to hold the data in flight). D1 The sm_75 SASS shows exactly this pair: LDG.E.128 then STS.128. R5 With cp.async, one instruction (LDGSTS in the sm_80 SASS) names both the global source and the shared destination, and the thread continues; the memory system writes shared memory when the data arrives, and a dependency barrier (DEPBAR) is what the thread waits on. R5 D4 That is why double buffering became cheap on these GPUs: the prefetched data occupies shared memory, which is already allocated as a stage, instead of registers, which register tiling has already used up.

Worked example

Task. A block of the 128 × 128 / 8 × 8 kernel at K = 4,096 runs 512 tiles. Suppose (as model inputs) one tile's copies take L = 400 cycles to arrive and its compute takes C = 400 cycles. Compute the single- and double-buffered times, the speed-up, and what changes if C = 1,000.

  1. Tiles: 4,096 ÷ 8 = 512.
  2. Single: 512 × (400 + 400) = 409,600 cycles.
  3. Double: the model gives L·T + C = 400 × 512 + 400 = 205,200 cycles; speed-up 409,600 ÷ 205,200 = 1.996, printed as 2.00. R4
  4. C = 1,000: single 512 × 1,400 = 716,800; double L + T·C = 400 + 512,000 = 512,400; speed-up 1.40. R4
  5. Interpretation: the compute-bound case gains only the hidden load time. Real L and C come from your GPU: C from the FFMA count per tile and the issue rate, L from the profiler's memory latency. Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A tray in the kitchen is filled by a few runners; a shared-memory stage is filled by every thread of the block, each copying its own 16-byte pieces, and each thread can only wait for its own copies. "Is the tray full?" therefore needs two steps on a GPU: each thread's wait, then the block barrier.
    • 

  • The story suggests the runners are separate people. The copies are started by the same threads that compute; what is separate is the hardware that completes them while the threads continue.
    • 

  • Two trays at most halve the evening in the story. On a GPU other resident blocks may already overlap loads and compute, so the measured gain of double buffering can be much smaller than the model's single-block prediction — or larger, if it lets a low-occupancy kernel keep its loads in flight.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Missing wait. Symptom: some shapes wrong, others right (the forensic lab); on a GPU, results that depend on timing. Find it: the emulator, which lands copies at the latest moment; on a GPU, Compute Sanitizer's racecheck (D6). Fix: __pipeline_wait_prior before the barrier.
    • 

  • Wait without barrier. Symptom: rare wrong results. Cause: a thread reads elements copied by another thread whose copies are not complete. Fix: __syncthreads() after the wait.
    • 

  • Wrong count in wait_prior. Symptom: wait_prior(2) with two stages lets tile t be pending. Rule: with S stages, one group per tile, and the newest tile issued before the wait, use wait_prior(S - 1).
    • 

  • Skipping the empty commit. Symptom: last tile wrong. Cause: on the last iteration no group is committed, so "all but the newest" excludes tile t itself. Fix: commit unconditionally.
    • 

  • Built for an old target. Symptom: correct but no gain. Find it: no LDGSTS in the SASS (compare the sm_75 output above). Fix: build for your GPU's architecture.
    • 

  • Misaligned 16-byte copies. Symptom: an error on the GPU (the header asserts alignment in debug builds). Fix: the launcher's fallback or padded rows.
    •

Summary


    

  • Double buffering overlaps the copy of tile t + 1 with the compute of tile t, using two shared-memory stages.
    • 

  • In the single-block timeline model the best gain is (L + C) ÷ max(L, C): 2.00 for L = C, 1.25 for a 4 : 1 imbalance.
    • 

  • __pipeline_memcpy_async / commit / wait_prior become cp.async, commit_group, wait_group in PTX and LDGSTS, LDGDEPBAR, DEPBAR in sm_80 SASS; sm_75 gets ordinary loads and stores.
    • 

  • Correctness needs the thread's wait (wait_prior(1) with two stages) and a barrier before reading a stage, and a barrier before refilling it.
    • 

  • Costs: twice the shared memory, 143 registers for the 128 × 128 version, 12.5 % theoretical occupancy on the example device.
    • 

  • A missing wait is invisible to most GPU runs; the emulator lands copies at the latest legal moment and fails deterministically.
