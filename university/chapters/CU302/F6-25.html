Story hook

The great kitchen hall measures everything with tall glass cups that have many fine marks. They are accurate, but they are heavy, and a runner can carry only a few at a time. One morning a delivery of light cups arrives: smaller, with fewer marks. Twice as many fit on a tray. Some cups have marks very close together but can hold only a little; others hold a lot but have marks far apart.

Chen measures flour with the light cups and pours everything into one big, finely marked bowl. Each cupful is a little off, but the bowl keeps the total exact to the line. Bilal measures with light cups and adds the cupfuls up in a light cup too. After a hundred cupfuls, his total is visibly wrong: the light cup's marks are too far apart to notice a small extra spoon.

The head cook now has to decide what "close enough" means for the evening's dishes. "Within one spoon" is not a rule: one spoon is nothing in a pot of soup and everything in a cup of tea. This chapter measures the new cups, derives a rule for "close enough" from the cups themselves, and shows how a lazy rule can pass a broken recipe.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe FP32, TF32, FP16, BF16 and the two FP8 formats by precision (significand bits) and range, and verify those properties with a program instead of memory.
  • Derive an error bound for a K-term dot product with inputs rounded to a narrow format and FP32 accumulation, and use it as a per-element test tolerance.
  • Explain why accumulation precision matters more than input precision for long sums, with measured evidence.
  • Design a test set for a mixed-precision GEMM that combines a derived bound with exact tests able to catch logic errors.
  • Diagnose a fixed absolute tolerance that fails correct kernels and passes broken ones.
Jargon box
Precision (significand bits, p)
Simple words: how many binary digits a number format keeps. Analogy: how many marks a cup has. Precise: the number of significand bits including the implicit leading bit; a format with p bits rounds a real number to nearest with relative error at most u = 2−p (in the normal range). FP32 has p = 24, TF32 and FP16 p = 11, BF16 p = 8, FP8 E4M3 p = 4, FP8 E5M2 p = 3. D1 R1
Range (exponent bits)
Simple words: how large and how small a number the format can hold. Analogy: how much a cup can hold, and the smallest amount it can show. Precise: set by the exponent field; the largest finite and smallest positive (subnormal) values of each format, as decoded by NVIDIA's conversion functions in this build, are printed by Listing 1. R1
Unit roundoff (u)
Simple words: the largest relative error of one rounding. Analogy: half the distance between two marks, relative to the amount. Precise: u = 2−p for round-to-nearest; for FP32 u = 2−24 ≈ 5.96 × 10−8. D2
Mixed precision
Simple words: using a narrow format for the inputs and a wider one for the sums. Analogy: light cups for measuring, one finely marked bowl for the total. Precise: a computation whose operands are stored (and multiplied) in one format and accumulated in a wider one, e.g. FP16 or BF16 inputs with FP32 accumulation, as milestone E7 specifies. C1
TF32
Simple words: an NVIDIA format with FP32's range and FP16's precision, used inside tensor cores. Analogy: a big cup with few marks. Precise: TensorFloat-32, an NVIDIA format with an 8-bit exponent and 10 explicit fraction bits (p = 11) stored in a 32-bit container; the CUDA 12.0 header mma.h converts FP32 to it with the PTX instruction cvt.rna.tf32.f32. H1 D3
FP8 (E4M3, E5M2)
Simple words: two 8-bit formats, one with a bit more precision, the other with more range. Analogy: tiny cups: one with a few more marks, one that holds more. Precise: 1 sign bit, 4 exponent and 3 fraction bits (E4M3) or 5 and 2 (E5M2); in NVIDIA's cuda_fp8.h of CUDA 12.0, E4M3 has no infinities and its conversion from float saturates to the largest finite value. R1 D4
Tolerance (test)
Simple words: how far a computed result may be from the reference and still count as correct. Analogy: the head cook's "close enough" rule. Precise: a per-element bound |ĉ − c| ≤ τ, which for floating-point kernels should be derived from the formats and the length of the computation, and scaled by Σ|akbk|, not chosen as a constant. D2

Terms from earlier chapters: SGEMM and its FP64 reference with the bound γKΣ|ab| (F6-21); floating point and rounding (MA302).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Number format = a measuring cup; precision = how many marks it has; range = how much it holds; input rounding = reading a light cup to its nearest mark; accumulation = adding cupfuls into the bowl; tolerance = the head cook's "close enough" rule.

A cup with fewer marks makes each measurement a little wrong. That is fine, as long as you know how wrong it can be. With 8 marks per doubling, the error is at most half a mark: about one part in 256.

The big danger is not measuring with a light cup. It is adding up in a light cup. A small amount poured into a full light cup may not even reach the next mark, so it disappears. Add up in the finely marked bowl, and the small amounts count.

"Close enough" must grow with the size of the pot. Two hundred cupfuls can be off by more than two. A rule that says "within one spoon" will call a perfect big pot wrong and a sloppy small one right.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The formats, measured

A binary floating-point format with p significand bits represents numbers as ±m × 2e with an integer m of p bits; its exponent field bounds e. Rounding to nearest gives relative error at most u = 2−p in the normal range; below the smallest normal number, subnormals keep a fixed spacing and the relative error grows. D1 D2 FP32 and FP16 are IEEE 754 binary32 and binary16. D1 BF16 keeps FP32's 8-bit exponent with 7 fraction bits; TF32 keeps the 8-bit exponent with 10 fraction bits; the FP8 formats are vendor formats. D3 D4

Listing 1 does not ask you to believe any of that. Part 1 decodes every bit pattern of FP16, BF16, E4M3 and E5M2 with NVIDIA's own conversion functions and prints what it finds: FP16 has 63,488 finite values, 2 infinities and 2,046 NaN patterns, largest finite 65,504 and smallest positive 5.960464 × 10−8; BF16 has largest finite about 3.389531 × 1038 and smallest positive 9.18355 × 10−41; E4M3 has 254 finite values, no infinities and largest 448; E5M2 has 2 infinities, 6 NaN patterns and largest 57,344. R1 Part 2 checks the university's rounding model lowp.hpp against NVIDIA's float-to-format conversions on a million random values per format: they agree on all of them, and the largest relative error found is just below u for each (0.000487805 against 2−11 = 0.000488281 for FP16; 0.00389069 against 2−8 for BF16). R1 TF32 has no host conversion in the headers used, so its model (p = 11 with FP32's range) is not checked by Listing 1; the PTX instruction that converts to it appears in mma.h and is used in F6-26's lab extension.

Part 3 shows range at work: 70,000 becomes infinity in FP16 but 70,144 in BF16; in both FP8 formats it saturates to the largest finite value (448 and 57,344); 10−8 underflows to 0 in FP16 and FP8 but survives in BF16 as 1.0011718 × 10−8. R1 Same 16 bits, opposite trade-offs: FP16 has 8 times finer precision than BF16 (p = 11 against 8), BF16 has FP32's range.

The error bound of a mixed-precision dot product

Let c = Σ akbk be the exact dot product of the FP32 inputs, and let the kernel round each input to the input format (â = a(1 + δ), |δ| ≤ uin), multiply exactly, and accumulate in a format with unit roundoff uacc. The rounded product is akbk(1 + δ1)(1 + δ2), whose relative error is at most 2uin + uin2. Summing K terms in floating point, in any order, adds at most γK = K uacc ÷ (1 − K uacc) relative to the sum of absolute values. D2 Together:

|ĉ − c| ≤ [(2uin + uin2)(1 + γK) + γK] · Σk|akbk|.

For BF16 inputs (uin = 2−8), FP32 accumulation and K = 1,024, the factor is 0.00788928 (printed by Listing 3). With FP32 inputs (uin = 0) it reduces to F6-21's γK. R3 Two properties make it a good test tolerance. It is per element, scaled by that element's Σ|ab|, so large and small outputs are judged fairly. And it has no free parameter: every symbol comes from the formats and K.

Measured against the bound

Listing 2 runs 200 dot products per row on the CPU model, for each input format with FP32 accumulation (and FP16 inputs with FP16 accumulation), for K = 16, 256, 4,096, with inputs in [−1, 1] and in [0, 1]. Three observations stand out in the output. R2

  • With FP32 accumulation, every measured error is below the bound; the largest ratio is 0.341 (TF32 and FP16 inputs, K = 16, inputs in [0, 1]). The bound is a worst case, not a prediction.
  • TF32 and FP16 inputs give identical rows: both have p = 11, and values in [−1, 1] stay inside FP16's normal range almost always. Precision, not the name of the format, sets the error.
  • FP16 accumulation is different in kind. For K = 4,096 the bound does not exist (K uacc = 2 ≥ 1), and with positive inputs the measured error relative to Σ|ab| reaches 0.08613: about 8.6 % of the total, against 1.509 × 10−5 for the same FP16 inputs accumulated in FP32. That is Bilal's light-cup total from the story.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why the measured error shrinks with K in [−1, 1]

For inputs of mixed sign, the ratio error ÷ Σ|ab| falls as K grows (BF16: 0.001981 at K = 16, 0.0001153 at K = 4,096), while for inputs in [0, 1] FP32 accumulation errors grow (FP32 inputs: 1.576 × 10−7 to 2.333 × 10−6). R2 Input-rounding errors are independent with random signs, so their sum grows roughly like √K while Σ|ab| grows like K; accumulation errors with positive terms, in contrast, are applied to a growing partial sum. This is the content of probabilistic error analysis; the deterministic bound remains the safe test because it holds for every input, including the adversarial ones a test should contain. D2

What the bound cannot catch

A tolerance tells you whether a result could have come from correct arithmetic in the stated formats. It cannot tell you that every term was included. Listing 3 (the forensic evidence) models a kernel that drops the last of 1,024 terms: with random BF16 data the dropped term is smaller than the bound, and the bound passes the broken kernel (0 of 64 failures, worst 0.468 of the bound). R3 Three complementary tests close the gap:

  • Exact tests. Choose data on which the correct result is exact in every format involved: small integers (exact in BF16; partial sums below 224 exact in FP32). Then demand equality. Listing 3's integer data set catches the dropped term in 52 of 64 rows (the other 12 rows' last product is 0). R3
  • Structured tests. Identity matrices, single non-zero entries at the tile edges, a K that is one more than a multiple of the tile depth: each catches a class of index error with an exact expected answer.
  • Statistical sanity. The ratio error ÷ bound over a large random test should be far below 1 (Listing 2's ratios); a kernel whose ratios are close to 1 on random data deserves suspicion even when it passes.

Tensor cores do not promise this model

The bound assumes that each addition rounds to FP32 to nearest, in some order. A tensor-core instruction computes a whole small matrix product; the programming guide documents its input and accumulator types, but the internal order of additions, intermediate precision and rounding behaviour of the hardware are not something this build could verify. Treat the bound as a hypothesis for tensor-core results: test it, and if your GPU's results exceed it on some inputs, the documentation for your architecture (or a measurement) must tell you why. D3

Not verified — check before relying on this

(1) The accumulation behaviour inside tensor-core matrix instructions (order, internal precision, rounding mode, handling of subnormals) for FP16, BF16, TF32 and FP8 inputs: check the CUDA C++ Programming Guide's warp matrix functions section and the PTX ISA's mma / wmma sections for your architecture. (2) Whether cuBLAS SGEMM may use TF32 tensor cores by default for your library version and environment (math modes, environment variables): check the cuBLAS documentation. (3) That the device-side conversion functions round identically to the host-side ones measured here: run Listing 1's comparison in a kernel on your GPU. (4) The FP8 scaling conventions (per-tensor scale factors) used by libraries: check the library documentation and the FP8 paper in the Sources.

FP8 needs scaling

With p = 4, one E4M3 rounding can be off by up to 6.25 % (measured maximum 5.88 % in Listing 1), and the format holds nothing above 448 or below about 0.00195. R1 Data must therefore be scaled into range before conversion, with the scale kept in higher precision and applied after accumulation; E5M2 trades a bit of precision for range and is the usual choice where values span many magnitudes. D4 The bound of this chapter still applies to the scaled values; the scale factors add their own rounding if they are not powers of two. Listing 2's FP8 row shows the bound factor of 0.1289: a test tolerance of about 13 % of Σ|ab|, which is why FP8 GEMMs are judged by application-level accuracy as well as by element-wise bounds.

How the hardware actually does it

A narrower format moves fewer bytes per element (2 for FP16 and BF16, 1 for FP8 against 4 for FP32), so the same tile in shared memory holds more elements and the same DRAM bandwidth delivers more of them: the arithmetic intensity of a GEMM in elements stays the same, in bytes it doubles or quadruples. R4 The arithmetic units that take these formats as inputs are the tensor cores of F6-26: in the SASS of this course they appear as HMMA instructions whose mnemonics name the accumulator type (HMMA.16816.F32, HMMA.16816.F32.BF16). R4 Conversions are instructions too: the CUDA 12.0 mma.h converts FP32 to TF32 with cvt.rna.tf32.f32, where rna names a rounding mode of the PTX ISA (round to nearest, ties away from zero, per the PTX naming; unverified here, D5). H1 D5 Peak throughput per format is a product fact: take it from the architecture whitepaper of your GPU, never from a general rule.

Worked example

Task. A BF16-input, FP32-accumulate GEMM has K = 4,096 and inputs in [−1, 1]. Derive the per-element tolerance factor, compare it with Listing 2's measured worst ratio, and find the tolerance for an element with Σ|ab| = 1,000.

  1. uin = 2−8 = 0.00390625; uin2 = 1.5259 × 10−5; 2uin + uin2 = 0.0078278.
  2. uacc = 2−24; K uacc = 4,096 × 5.9605 × 10−8 = 2.4414 × 10−4; γK = 2.4414 × 10−4 ÷ (1 − 2.4414 × 10−4) = 2.4420 × 10−4.
  3. Factor = 0.0078278 × 1.00024420 + 0.00024420 = 0.0080739. Listing 2 prints the bound 0.008074 for this row. R2
  4. Measured worst error ÷ Σ|ab| = 0.0001153: ratio 0.014 of the bound (Listing 2). R2
  5. For Σ|ab| = 1,000: tolerance 8.07. An absolute tolerance of 0.01 would be 800 times too strict for this element; for an element with Σ|ab| = 0.5 it would be 2.5 times too lax.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Cup marks in the story are evenly spaced. Floating-point "marks" are evenly spaced only within one power of two; every doubling of the value doubles the spacing. That is why errors are relative, not absolute.
    • 

  • A real cup's reading error is random. Rounding to nearest is deterministic: the same input always gives the same output, and adversarial inputs can make every error point the same way. Tests rely on that determinism (exact integer tests).
    • 

  • In the story, a light cup that overflows spills. In FP16, a value above 65,504 becomes infinity; in E4M3 conversion it silently becomes 448 (Listing 1). The two formats "spill" differently, and neither shows a mess on the table.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • One absolute tolerance. Symptom: a correct kernel fails on large data and a broken one passes on small data (forensic lab). Fix: the per-element derived bound plus exact tests.
    • 

  • Reference in the same low precision. Symptom: a test that passes everything. Cause: comparing against a BF16 CPU result with the same roundings. Fix: FP64 reference from the original FP32 values.
    • 

  • Accumulating in FP16. Symptom: errors growing with K, visible at a few thousand terms (Listing 2: 8.6 % of Σ|ab| at K = 4,096). Fix: FP32 accumulator.
    • 

  • Overflow in FP16 inputs. Symptom: infinities or NaN in C for data that was fine in FP32. Cause: values above 65,504. Find it: count non-finite inputs after conversion. Fix: scale the data, or use BF16.
    • 

  • FP8 without scaling. Symptom: many zeros or saturated values. Cause: data outside E4M3's range. Fix: per-tensor scale factors (check the library's documented convention).
    • 

  • Bound with K of the wrong matrix. Symptom: failures only for tall-skinny shapes. Cause: using M or N instead of the dot-product length K.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. Good kernel: (a) fails 61 of 64 on "large" (worst 117× the tolerance), passes "small" and "integers"; (b) passes everything (worst 0.036 of the bound); (c) passes. Drops-last: (a) fails all of "large" and 52 of "integers" but passes "small"; (b) passes everything (worst 0.468); (c) fails 52 of 64.
    2. 

  2. Column (b): the good kernel's large-data errors are 0.036 of what correct BF16 arithmetic allows, so the arithmetic is fine and the tolerance does not scale. The bug hypothesis would show as (b) or (c) failures for the good kernel; there are none.
    3. 

  3. The dropped term |aKbK| is at most scale² (0.0025 for "small", 16 for "large"), while the bound is 0.0079 × Σ|ab| with Σ|ab| ≈ K × scale²/4 for uniform data: about 2 × scale². The dropped term is below the bound, so the bound cannot see it (worst 0.468).
    4. 

  4. Tolerance 1.0 still fails the good kernel on some "large" rows (its worst error is 117 × 0.01 = 1.17) while the broken kernel keeps passing every "small" row (worst error 0.0023). It moves the line without making it right: a constant cannot be both loose enough for large sums and tight enough for small ones.
    5. 

  5. (1) Per-element derived bound on random data (row "good large" shows a fixed constant is wrong). (2) Exact integer data with equality (row "drops-last integers" column (c) is the only one that catches the bug on its own terms). (3) Structured edge cases (single non-zero entries, K one more than a tile multiple) for index errors.
