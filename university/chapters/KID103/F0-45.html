Story hook

Analogy

Rosa and her little brother Leo play a game in the hallway. A long ribbon lies on the floor. It curves a little to the left, then to the right. Leo must walk along it while looking only at his feet.

"Ribbon under my feet: walk straight," Leo says. "Ribbon a bit to my right: step right. Ribbon a bit to my left: step left." He follows it all the way to the kitchen.

Then Rosa puts a sock over one of Leo's eyes for fun. He gets mixed up between left and right. He steps the wrong way, and the ribbon is gone. "I lost it!" he laughs.

Leo was doing exactly what a line-following robot does: look, decide, step, again and again. In this chapter you run that robot in our text simulator. You will also see what happens when its "eyes" are mixed up.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how a line-following robot works: sense, decide, act, repeat.
  • Trace the robot's steering rule by hand, row by row, on a text track.
  • Run the course's line-following simulator and change the track.
  • Diagnose a robot that loses the line, from its log, and find the swapped sensors.

Jargon box
Line follower
Simple words: a robot that drives along a line drawn on the floor. Analogy: Leo walking along the ribbon, looking at his feet. Precise: a mobile robot whose program uses downward-facing sensors to detect a line and steers to keep the line under its sensors. D2
Control loop
Simple words: look, decide, act, and do it again. Analogy: riding a bicycle and correcting your balance all the time. Precise: a repeated cycle that measures the system, compares the measurement with what is wanted, and applies a correction. D1
Feedback
Simple words: using what you sense now to choose what you do next. Analogy: Leo looks at the ribbon after every step. Precise: using a measurement of a system's output to adjust its input. D1
Error (control)
Simple words: how far you are from where you want to be. Analogy: how far the ribbon is from Leo's feet. Precise: the difference between the wanted value and the measured value; here, which side of the robot the line is on. D1 Not the same as a bug or a compiler error.
Grid world
Simple words: a pretend world made of squares, printed as text. Analogy: a floor of square tiles. Precise: a simulation in which positions are whole-number cells of a grid and the robot moves one cell per step; our track is a list of text rows. R1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the robot on the text track. It has three sensors in a row under its front: left, middle, right. Each one says 1 if it sees the line and 0 if it sees floor.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A line-following robot has sensors that look down at the floor. Each sensor can tell line from floor. Our robot has three: one on the left, one in the middle, one on the right.

The robot's program is a short rule, like Leo's. If the middle sensor sees the line, drive straight. If only the left one sees it, steer left. If only the right one sees it, steer right. If none sees it, the robot has lost the line.

The robot does this again and again. Look. Decide. Move. Look again. This repeating cycle is a control loop. Using what you see to choose what you do next is called feedback.D1

Our robot lives in a grid world made of text. The floor is dots. The line is # signs. The robot is the letter R. It drives down one row each step. At each row it can move one square left or right. The program prints every row, so you can watch its path.R1

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A control loop has three parts. It measures where the system is. It compares that with where it should be. It applies a correction. The difference between wanted and measured is called the error.D1

In our robot, the wanted state is "line under the middle sensor". The three sensors measure a rough error: line to the left, line in the middle, or line to the right. The correction is one step in the direction of the line. That is why the rule "steers back to the line".

This is the simplest kind of correction: a fixed step, whatever the size of the error. A smoother rule is possible, where a bigger error gives a bigger correction. RB101 (F9-05) and later RB202 build that idea step by step, up to the PID controller.D1

The rule depends on the sensors being where the program thinks they are. If the left and right sensors are swapped, every correction goes the wrong way. A small error becomes a bigger one, and the robot drives away from the line. That is the forensic lab.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. One common design steers with two driven wheels, one on each side. Turning the right wheel faster than the left makes the robot curve left, and the other way round.D2 Each wheel's speed is set through a motor driver (F0-44). A real robot also moves continuously: it can overshoot the line and swing back and forth. How strongly to correct is a trade-off between reacting fast and swinging. RB101's forensic lab "the robot that zig-zags" studies exactly that.D1

How the hardware actually does it

On a real robot, each line sensor is a small light sensor pointed at the floor; some designs add a small light source next to it. A dark line and a light floor reflect different amounts of light, so the sensor's reading differs, and a threshold (F0-43) turns it into "line" or "floor".D3 The program reads the sensors, decides, and sets the two motor speeds through the driver, many times per second. Which sensors your kit has, and their thresholds, come from its datasheets (D4). This chapter uses only the simulator.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. Leo can see the whole ribbon ahead; the robot cannot. Leo can look up and plan. Our robot sees only three cells in its own row. It reacts; it does not plan.R1
    2. 

  2. Real robots do not move in tidy squares. Our robot jumps exactly one column. A real robot rolls, turns smoothly, can overshoot and swing, and its wheels can slip.D1 D2
    3. 

  3. Leo notices when something is odd. With a sock over one eye, Leo laughed and stopped. A robot with swapped sensors keeps following its rule, even as it drives away from the line.R2

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: rows 0 and 1 are straight. In row 2 the line is in column 5 (to the right), but the log says sensors 100 and "steer left"; the robot moves to column 3. From row 3 the sensors read 000 and the robot reports "LOST the line"; in row 8 a sensor sees the line again, but the robot steers away from it once more. Hypotheses: "the track changed" is ruled out: the # cells printed in every row are the same as in Listing 1's log. "Sensors swapped" fits: in row 2 the line was on the right but the left sensor reported it. Root cause: lines 25 and 27 of line_follow_swapped.cpp read the wrong cells: left reads col + 1 and right reads col - 1. Fix: swap them back, as in Listing 1; on a real robot, check which physical sensor is wired to which input. Verification: the fixed program produces Listing 1's log (R1). Prevention: a test track whose first bend goes to the right (and another to the left), checked at the first steering row. How the evidence was made: Listing 1 with the two sensor lines deliberately swapped, run in this build (R2).
