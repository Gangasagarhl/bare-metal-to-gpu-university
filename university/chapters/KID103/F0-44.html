Story hook

Analogy

Yusuf's family has a big water tank on the roof. A thick pipe brings the water down to the garden. The water in that pipe pushes hard enough to spin a little water wheel Yusuf built.

Yusuf is small, and the main valve is stiff. He cannot turn it alone. So his father fitted a clever valve: a little lever opens it. Yusuf pushes the little lever with one finger, and the big flow from the tank does the heavy work.

A microcontroller is like Yusuf. It is very good at deciding, but its pins are weak. A motor is like the water wheel: it needs a big flow. In this chapter you meet the "clever valve" that lets a weak pin control a strong motor. It is called a motor driver.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a motor does and how to make it turn the other way.
  • Explain why a microcontroller pin must not drive a motor directly, and what a motor driver does instead.
  • Predict the motor's direction from the four switches of an H-bridge, and spot the forbidden combination.
  • Compare a pin's current limit with a motor's needs, using clearly labelled pretend numbers.
  • Diagnose a short circuit from a driver's command log.
Jargon box
Motor
Simple words: a part that turns electricity into spinning. Analogy: a muscle; or Yusuf's water wheel. Precise: an electric machine that converts electrical energy into rotation; in a simple DC motor, reversing the current reverses the direction. D1 D2
Motor driver
Simple words: a helper circuit that lets a weak signal switch a strong motor current. Analogy: a tap opened by a small control valve. Precise: a circuit (it can be a single chip) that uses a low-current control input to switch the motor's higher current from a separate supply. D2
Transistor
Simple words: an electronic switch with no moving parts, worked by a small signal. Analogy: a tap opened by a small control valve. Precise: a semiconductor device in which a small current or voltage at one terminal controls a larger current between two others. D2
H-bridge
Simple words: four switches around a motor that let it turn either way. Analogy: four valves around the water wheel that choose which side the water enters. Precise: a circuit of four switches (in practice transistors) arranged in an H, with the motor as the crossbar; closing opposite corners drives current through the motor in one direction or the other. D2
Short circuit
Simple words: a path that lets current rush from + to − without going through anything useful. Analogy: a burst pipe straight from the tank to the drain. Precise: an unintended low-resistance connection between points at different voltages, which lets a large current flow. D1
Current limit
Simple words: the most current a part may safely give or take. Analogy: the most water a small pipe can carry before it bursts. Precise: a maximum rating stated in the part's datasheet; exceeding it can damage the part. D3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows an H-bridge. The four switches are S1 to S4. The motor M sits in the middle. Closing S1 and S4 sends current one way. Closing S3 and S2 sends it the other way.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A motor turns electricity into spinning. It is the robot's muscle. Connect a simple motor to a battery one way, and it spins one way. Swap the two wires, and it spins the other way.D1

Can the microcontroller from F0-42 run a motor straight from a pin? No. A pin is weak. It can only give a small current, like Yusuf's finger. A motor needs a much bigger current, like the water wheel. Asking the pin to do it can damage the chip.D2 D3

So we use a motor driver. It is like Yusuf's clever valve. The pin sends a small "go" signal. The driver then lets the big current flow from the battery to the motor. The pin decides; the driver does the heavy work.D2

How does the driver choose a direction? Picture four switches around the motor, in the shape of the letter H. Close the top-left and the bottom-right, and the current crosses the motor one way. Close the other two corners, and it crosses the other way. That is an H-bridge.

There is one rule you must never break. Never close both switches on the same side. That would join the battery's + straight to its −. That is a short circuit. Parts can get hot and be ruined.D1

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A microcontroller's output pin has a current limit. The datasheet states it. A motor's datasheet states how much current it draws, and that number rises when the motor starts or is held still.D2 D3 When the second number is bigger than the first, the pin must not drive the motor.

A motor driver contains transistors. A transistor is a switch with no moving parts. A small signal at its control terminal lets a bigger current flow through it. That is the registered analogy: a tap opened by a small control valve.D2

Four transistors arranged as an H-bridge give direction control. Closing S1 and S4 makes current flow left to right through the motor. Closing S3 and S2 makes it flow right to left. Closing S1 and S2 (or S3 and S4) makes a short from + to −, so a driver's control rules must forbid it.D2

There is a second reason for a driver. A motor contains coils of wire. When the current through a coil is switched off suddenly, the coil can produce a brief high voltage. Drivers are designed to deal with this so it does not reach the microcontroller.D2 F0-39's "where the analogy breaks" warned that coils (inductors) have no simple pipe partner; here is one place where that matters.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. To control speed, a driver can switch the motor on and off very quickly. The motor's own inertia smooths the pulses. The share of time it is on (the duty cycle) sets the average push. This technique is called pulse-width modulation (PWM).D2 When a real H-bridge changes direction, it must make sure the switches on one side are never on together, even for a moment, during the change.D2 The forensic lab shows what happens in our model when a program forgets that.

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a simple DC motor, current flows through coils of wire that sit near magnets. The current makes the coils act like magnets too. Pushing and pulling between the coils and the magnets turns the shaft. Reversing the current reverses the push, so the shaft turns the other way.D1 D2 In the driver, transistors replace our four switches, and the motor's current comes from the battery pack, not from the microcontroller's pin.

Worked example

Part 1: does the pin need help? (pretend numbers)

In this exercise, pretend the board datasheet says a pin may give at most 10 mA, and the motor datasheet says the motor wants 300 mA.

  1. Compare: 300 mA is more than 10 mA. The pin must not drive the motor.
  2. How many times more? 300 ÷ 10 = 30. The motor wants 30 times what the pin may give.
  3. Conclusion: use a motor driver. The pin only sends the control signal.

Part 2: read the H-bridge

  1. S1 = 1, S2 = 0, S3 = 0, S4 = 1. Opposite corners, left top to right bottom: forward.
  2. S1 = 0, S2 = 1, S3 = 1, S4 = 0. The other opposite corners: backward.
  3. S1 = 1, S2 = 1, S3 = 1, S4 = 0. S1 and S2 are both closed on the left side: short circuit, whatever the others do.
  4. All 0: no path through the motor: not driven.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. A transistor is not an instant valve. The "clever valve" picture hides that a transistor takes a short time to switch and has thresholds, which is why direction changes need care (Layer 3).D2
    2. 

  2. Coils fight back. Turning off a water wheel's valve does not send a spike back up the pipe to Yusuf's finger in the same way. A motor's coils can produce a brief high voltage when switched off; pipes have no simple partner for this.D2
    3. 

  3. Muscles get tired and tell you; motors do not. An overloaded motor draws more current and gets warm. Only measurements and datasheets tell you its limits.D2
    4.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: at 1.5s the log shows S1=1, S2=1, S3=1, S4=0 and "SHORT CIRCUIT". The wish was "backward". At 1.6s the setting is the correct backward pair (S3+S2) with S1 open. Root cause: the program closed the new pair (S2, S3) before opening S1 from the forward pair, so S1 and S2 were closed together on the left side. Hypotheses: "driver broken" is ruled out: the simulated driver is the same code that gives correct results at every other step, and the bad line has a bad command in it. Fix: forward (S1+S4) → all open → backward (S3+S2). Verification: run the fixed command list through hbridge_log.cpp; no SHORT CIRCUIT line appears. Why it matters more for real: on a real driver, a short joins battery + to − through the switches; large current flows and parts can overheat (D1, D2); our simulator only prints a warning. How the evidence was made: hbridge_log.in was written by the author with the wrong order at 1.5s, then replayed by hbridge_log.cpp in this build (R2).
