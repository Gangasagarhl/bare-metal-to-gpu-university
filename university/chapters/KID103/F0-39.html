Story hook

Analogy

Lina is watering the garden with her grandmother. The hose is connected to a tap. When Grandma opens the tap wide, water rushes out fast. When she opens it a little, the water only trickles.

Lina squeezes the end of the hose with her thumb. The opening gets narrow. Now less water gets through. Her thumb is in the way of the flow.

"What pushes the water?" Lina asks. "Pressure," says Grandma. "The water behind pushes the water in front."

Later that evening, Lina's torch will not switch on. She wonders: is there "pressure" inside a battery too? Is there a "thumb" inside the torch? This chapter says yes, a little. Then it shows where the water story stops being true.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain voltage, current and resistance with the water picture: pressure, flow and a narrow pipe.
  • Calculate a current from a voltage and a resistance with Ohm's law, using exercise numbers.
  • Explain why electricity needs a closed loop, and why an open loop has no current.
  • Name at least two places where the water picture is wrong.
  • Run a tiny C++ program that does the Ohm's-law arithmetic and predict its output.
Jargon box
Electricity
Simple words: tiny charged particles moving through a material, carrying energy to a lamp or a motor. Analogy: water moving through the pipes of a house. Precise: the flow of electric charge (here, through metal wires, where the charge carriers are electrons) driven by a voltage. D3 §9.1
Voltage
Simple words: how hard the battery pushes. Analogy: water pressure. Precise: the electrical potential difference between two points; it is measured in volts (V). D3 §9.4
Current
Simple words: how much electricity flows past a point each second. Analogy: how much water flows through the pipe. Precise: the rate of flow of electric charge; it is measured in amperes (A, "amps"); one milliampere (mA) is one thousandth of an ampere. D3 §9.1
Resistance
Simple words: how much something gets in the way of the flow. Analogy: a narrow part of the pipe (Lina's thumb on the hose). Precise: the ratio of voltage to current for a part; it is measured in ohms (Ω). D3 §9.4
Circuit
Simple words: a complete loop that electricity can travel around. Analogy: a closed loop of pipe with a pump. Precise: a closed path of conductors and parts through which current can flow from one terminal of a source back to the other. D3 §9.1
Battery
Simple words: a store of energy that pushes electricity around a loop. Analogy: a pump that makes pressure. Precise: one or more cells in which chemical reactions keep a voltage between two terminals, marked + and −. D3 §10.1
Ohm's law
Simple words: a rule that links push, flow and narrowness. Analogy: more pressure gives more flow; a narrower pipe gives less flow. Precise: for a resistor, V = I × R, so I = V ÷ R. D3 §9.4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Think about the water pipes in a home. Water moves when something pushes it. In a garden hose, the push comes from the pressure behind the tap. More push means more water flows.

Electricity also needs a push. A battery gives that push. We call the push voltage. A bigger voltage pushes harder.

The flow of water is how much water goes past each second. The flow of electricity is called current. When the battery pushes harder, more current flows. When something gets in the way, less current flows.

What gets in the way? In a hose, a narrow part. Lina's thumb made the hose narrow. In electricity, a part called a resistor gets in the way on purpose. How much it gets in the way is its resistance.

So we have three ideas and three partners:

  • water pressure is like voltage (the push);
  • water flow is like current (how much moves);
  • a narrow pipe is like resistance (what gets in the way).

There is one more big idea. Electricity only flows around a closed loop. The loop goes out of the battery, through the parts, and back into the battery. We call that loop a circuit. If the loop has a gap anywhere, the flow stops everywhere. A light switch makes a gap on purpose. That is how it turns a lamp off.D3 §9.1

Here water and electricity are different. A broken water pipe spills water on the floor. A broken wire does not spill electricity. The flow stops. You will see this again in "Where the analogy breaks".

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Engineers measure the three ideas with units. Voltage is measured in volts, written V. Current is measured in amperes, written A. In this course, small currents are written in milliamperes, mA. One ampere is 1,000 milliamperes. Resistance is measured in ohms, written Ω.D3 §9.1, §9.4

The three are linked by Ohm's law. For a resistor, voltage equals current times resistance: V = I × R. We can turn it around to find the current: I = V ÷ R.D3 §9.4

Look at what the rule says. If you double the voltage, the current doubles. If you double the resistance, the current halves. That matches the water picture. More pressure, more flow. Narrower pipe, less flow.

You can use the law with any numbers. In this chapter we use exercise numbers. They are not taken from any real part. For example: a 6 V push across 300 Ω gives 6 ÷ 300 = 0.02 A. That is 20 mA. The worked example below does this slowly.

Note

Ohm's law is a rule for resistors. Some parts, such as the LED in the next chapter, do not follow it in a simple way. F0-40 shows how we still use it for the resistor next to the LED.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. In a metal wire, the moving charges are electrons. Electrons carry negative charge, so they drift from − towards +. Engineers still draw current flowing from + to −. That habit is called conventional current, and every diagram in this course uses it.D3 §9.1 Energy is delivered to a resistor as heat. The power is P = V × I, measured in watts. HW101 builds on these ideas with real measurements.D3 §9.5

How the hardware actually does it

A metal wire is full of electrons that can move. Before the switch closes, they are already there, all along the wire. When the loop closes, the battery's push acts on the whole loop. Electrons all around the loop start to drift together. That is why a lamp lights straight away when you press the switch. Nothing has to travel all the way from the battery first.D4 §20.1

Worked example

These are exercise numbers only. They are not from any real battery or resistor.

Loop A: 6 V and 300 Ω

  1. Write the law: I = V ÷ R.
  2. Put in the numbers: I = 6 ÷ 300.
  3. Work it out: 6 ÷ 300 = 0.02. The unit is amperes, so I = 0.02 A.
  4. Change to milliamperes: multiply by 1,000. 0.02 × 1,000 = 20. So I = 20 mA.

Loop B: twice the resistance

Now use 600 Ω with the same 6 V. I = 6 ÷ 600 = 0.01 A = 10 mA. The resistance doubled, so the current halved.

Loop C: half the voltage

Now use 3 V with 300 Ω. I = 3 ÷ 300 = 0.01 A = 10 mA. The push halved, so the current halved.

Loop D: the loop is open

Same battery and resistor as Loop A, but a switch is open. There is a gap, so there is no closed loop. The current is 0 mA. Ohm's law for the resistor still holds: zero current means zero volts across the resistor.

Where the analogy breaks


The water picture is useful. It is also wrong in some places. Here are three.


    

  1. Electricity needs a closed loop. Water from an open tap pours out and falls on the ground. It does not need to come back. Electricity only flows if the path goes all the way back to the battery.D3 §9.1
    2. 

  2. An open wire does not leak. Cut a water pipe and water sprays out. Cut a wire and nothing comes out. The current stops everywhere in the loop.D3 §9.1
    3. 

  3. Some parts have no pipe partner. Later courses meet parts called capacitors and inductors. An inductor is a coil of wire that fights any change in the current through it. No simple pipe does that. HW101 explains these parts with their own pictures.D3 §14.2
    4. 



A fourth, gentle warning: water pressure can hurt nobody at home. The same is not true of all electricity. Wall sockets are dangerous. This university never uses them in a lab. See F0-46.D4 §20.6 S1
