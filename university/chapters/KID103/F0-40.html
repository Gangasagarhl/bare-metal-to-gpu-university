Story hook

Analogy

At Kofi's home, the bathroom sink has a one-way drain. Water goes down it. It never comes back up. If you could turn the drain upside down, nothing would go through at all.

Kofi's mother also put a water saver on the kitchen tap. It makes the opening narrow. Without it, water splashes everywhere when the tap is opened wide.

Today Kofi wants to light a tiny lamp called an LED. It turns out the LED is a little like that one-way drain. It also needs a "water saver" next to it. Kofi will build it first on the computer, where nothing can break. Then, with his mother watching, he will build it with real parts.

Jargon box
LED
Simple words: a tiny lamp that lights when electricity flows through it the right way. Analogy: a one-way drain that glows when water goes through. Precise: a light-emitting diode: a diode (a one-way part) that gives off light when current flows through it in the forward direction. D1 D2
Diode
Simple words: a part that lets electricity through in one direction only. Analogy: a one-way drain. Precise: a two-terminal part that conducts current mainly from its anode to its cathode and blocks it the other way. D2
Anode and cathode
Simple words: the two legs of an LED: the one for the + side and the one for the − side. Analogy: the top and the bottom of the drain. Precise: the anode is the terminal current enters in the forward direction; the cathode is where it leaves. D2
Resistor
Simple words: a small part that gets in the way of electricity on purpose. Analogy: the water saver that makes the tap narrow. Precise: a part made to have a stated resistance, used here to limit the current through an LED. D1
Forward voltage
Simple words: the part of the battery's push that the LED needs before it lights. Analogy: the drain needs a little water pressure before it starts to flow. Precise: the voltage across a diode when it conducts in the forward direction; its value comes from the part's datasheet. D2 D3
Datasheet
Simple words: the instruction sheet a maker writes for a part, with its true numbers. Analogy: the label on a food packet that says what is inside. Precise: the manufacturer's document that states a part's ratings, limits and behaviour. D3
Simulator
Simple words: a program that pretends to be a real thing so you can try ideas safely. Analogy: practising a recipe on paper before using the stove. Precise: software that models a system's behaviour; this course uses its own small C++ text simulators. R1
Multimeter
Simple words: a tester that measures voltage, current and resistance. Analogy: a pressure gauge you can hold against any part of the pipe. Precise: a measuring instrument with several ranges; in its voltage mode its two probes show the voltage between two points. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

An LED is a tiny lamp. It is not like a normal bulb. It is a one-way part. Electricity can go through it in one direction only. Turn it around and it blocks the flow, like a drain upside down.D1

So an LED has a + leg and a − leg. The + leg is called the anode. The − leg is called the cathode. The anode must face the battery's + side.D2

An LED also needs a helper: a resistor. Without one, too much current can rush through. That can ruin the LED. The resistor is like the water saver on Kofi's tap. It keeps the flow gentle.D1

Before you touch any parts, you build the loop in a simulator. This course has its own tiny simulator, written in C++. You describe a loop in one line of text. The program tells you if the LED lights, and if the current is too high. Nothing can break inside a simulator.

Only after the simulator works do you build the real loop. A grown-up watches. You use the battery-powered kit chosen for this course, and nothing else.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

When an LED conducts, a certain voltage appears across it. This is its forward voltage. The rest of the battery's voltage appears across the resistor. So the resistor's share is: battery voltage − forward voltage.D2

Now Ohm's law from F0-39 works on the resistor alone. The current through the resistor is its voltage divided by its resistance. In a single loop, the same current flows through every part, so this is also the LED's current.D1 D2

Written as a formula: I = (Vbattery − Vforward) ÷ R. To choose a resistor for a wanted current, turn it around: R = (Vbattery − Vforward) ÷ I.

Where do the forward voltage and the largest safe current come from? From the datasheet of the exact LED in your kit. No book number and no number in this chapter replaces it.D3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. A diode does not follow Ohm's law. Its current rises very steeply once the voltage across it passes a certain level. That is why an LED connected straight to a battery has almost nothing to limit its current, and why the resistor matters.D2 Our simulator uses a simpler model: a fixed forward voltage, and either "conducts" or "blocks". HW101 replaces it with the real curve and real measurements.

How the hardware actually does it

Inside an LED is a tiny piece of a special material called a semiconductor. It has two regions joined together. When current flows the forward way, energy is given off as light at the joint. The other way round, the joint blocks the current. The colour and the forward voltage depend on the material, which is why each LED's datasheet gives its own values.D2 D3

Worked example

In this exercise, pretend the datasheet says: forward voltage 2 V, maximum current 20 mA. Pretend the battery pack gives 6 V. These are not real-part numbers.

  1. The resistor's share of the push: 6 V − 2 V = 4 V.
  2. Try a 200 Ω resistor. I = 4 ÷ 200 = 0.02 A = 20 mA. That is exactly the pretend maximum. Our course rule: stay below the maximum, not at it.
  3. Try a 1,000 Ω resistor. I = 4 ÷ 1,000 = 0.004 A = 4 mA. Well below the pretend maximum. The LED lights more dimly.
  4. Try a 100 Ω resistor. I = 4 ÷ 100 = 0.04 A = 40 mA. That is twice the pretend maximum. Not allowed: choose a bigger resistor.
  5. Smallest resistor for the pretend maximum: R = 4 ÷ 0.02 = 200 Ω. Anything bigger keeps the current lower.

The simulator run below shows the same three currents (20, 4 and 40 mA) for Amara's, Ben's and Chen's loops.R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. A drain does not need a full loop. Water goes down a drain and away. The LED's current must come back to the battery through wire W2. Remove W2 and the LED stays dark, even though "the drain" is the right way up.D1
    2. 

  2. A drain lets a little water through at low pressure. Our simple model says an LED either blocks or conducts. A real diode's behaviour changes smoothly with voltage. The simulator's sharp "on/off" is a simplification.D2
    3. 

  3. A water saver does not get warm. A resistor turns electrical energy into heat. With a big current, it can get hot. Pipes do not do that.D2
    4.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: in P the resistor has 4 V and the LED 2 V, with 20 mA. In Q and R the current is 0 mA and the resistor has 0 V. In Q the whole 6 V is across the LED; in R the whole 6 V is across wire W2. Root cause Q: the LED is reversed; it blocks, so the full push appears across it. Fix: disconnect the battery, turn the LED round. Root cause R: wire W2 is missing (a gap between the LED and the battery's − side); the full push appears across the gap. Fix: disconnect the battery, add the wire. Hypotheses ruled out: "flat battery" — no, the full 6 V is measured somewhere in Q and R; "LED broken" in R — the LED shows 0 V, so the break is elsewhere. Prevention: check LED direction against the kit's instructions; trace the loop with a finger before connecting the battery. How the evidence was made: meter.cpp with meter.in, which marks P as "none", Q as "reversed" and R as "gap"; ideal model, pretend numbers (R1).
