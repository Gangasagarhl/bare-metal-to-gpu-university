Story hook

Analogy

When Zainab cooks with her uncle, they have habits. They wash their hands first. They turn pan handles inwards, so nobody knocks them. They use oven gloves for hot trays. And her uncle is always in the kitchen when the stove is on.

Zainab does not think of these as rules any more. They are habits. She does them without being told, like brushing her teeth.

Making things with circuits and motors also needs habits. Some dangers you can see, like a spinning wheel. Some you cannot see, like a battery whose two ends are touching the same piece of metal. This chapter turns the safety rules of the whole course into habits, and gives you a small program that will not say "go" until every habit is checked.

What you will be able to do

  • State the faculty's safety rules: adult supervision, kits designed for children, simulator first, no mains, no LiPo batteries, no propellers.
  • Explain the reason behind each habit on the checklist.
  • Run the course's safety-checklist program and explain why a missing answer counts as "no".
  • Investigate a "warm battery" incident from its checklist record and say which habits would have prevented it.
  • Write your own maker checklist for the course project.
Jargon box
Supervision
Simple words: a responsible adult is with you, watching, the whole time. Analogy: Zainab's uncle staying in the kitchen while the stove is on. Precise: in this university, an adult present for every physical activity at levels L0–L2, able to stop the activity at once. S1
Mains electricity
Simple words: the electricity in the wall sockets of a building. Analogy: the high-pressure water main under the street, not the garden hose. Precise: the building's alternating-current supply from the electricity network; its voltage is dangerous, and this university has no mains-voltage labs at any level. S1 D4 §20.6
LiPo battery
Simple words: a kind of rechargeable battery used in drones and some robots, which can catch fire if mistreated. Analogy: a pressure cooker: useful, but only with strict rules. Precise: a lithium polymer battery; it can catch fire if punctured, over-charged, over-discharged, short-circuited or charged unattended. It is not used in F0. S1 D6
Short circuit
Simple words: a path that lets current rush from + to − without going through anything useful. Analogy: a burst pipe straight from the tank to the drain. Precise: an unintended low-resistance connection between points at different voltages, which lets a large current flow (defined in F0-44). D4 §20.6
Checklist
Simple words: a list you go through, item by item, before you start. Analogy: checking you have every ingredient before you cook. Precise: a written list of conditions that must all be confirmed before an activity begins. R1
Fail-safe default
Simple words: if you are not sure, choose the safe answer. Analogy: if you do not know whether the stove is off, treat it as on. Precise: a design rule: when information is missing or a part fails, the system goes to its safe state; our checklist treats a missing answer as "no". R1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

In this course, some rules are never broken. An adult watches every physical activity. You use only the battery-powered kit chosen for the course, designed for children. You try every idea in the simulator first.S1

Three things are never used in this part of the university. No mains electricity: nothing from a wall socket. No LiPo batteries: the kind that can catch fire if mistreated. No propellers: they can cut skin and eyes.S1

Then come the habits. Disconnect the battery before you change any wire. Keep the table clear of metal things, drinks and loose parts. Keep fingers and hair away from wheels and gears. Test motors with the wheels in the air. If anything gets warm or smells, take your hands away and call your adult.S1 D4 §20.6

A good way to build habits is a checklist. You answer every question before you start. If one answer is "no", you stop and fix it first. Surgical teams in hospitals use a checklist before an operation, for example.D7 Makers can use checklists too.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Engineers who build machines that can hurt people use the same ideas, in more detail. One is the fail-safe default: when information is missing, the system chooses the safe state. Our checklist program does this on lines 21–22 of Listing 1: a missing answer counts as "no". Another is an interlock: a machine that will not start unless a guard is closed. Faculty F11 (security and safety-critical engineering) studies these ideas properly.R1

How the hardware actually does it

Why does a short circuit make a battery warm? A short circuit has almost no resistance, so by Ohm's law a large current flows. That current passes through the battery itself and the wires. Every part with some resistance turns electrical energy into heat, and with a large current, that heat builds up fast.D4 §20.6 D5 §9.4, §9.5 Nothing glows or moves to warn you first. That is why the habits must come before the danger, not after.

Worked example

Elif answers the six questions of Listing 1: yes, yes, no, yes, yes, yes.

  1. Count the "no" answers: one (question 3, the table is not clear).
  2. The rule: start only if every answer is yes. One "no" means stop.
  3. The program prints "STOP: 1 check(s) not passed".
  4. Elif clears the table and runs the checklist again: six yes answers.
  5. Now the program says she may start, with her adult watching.

A second case: Ari's answer file has only five answers. The sixth question gets no answer. The program counts it as "no" and says STOP. Missing information never counts as safe.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. A hot stove warns you; a short circuit may not. A stove glows or a pan steams. A shorted battery can look exactly the same as a safe one until it is already warm. You cannot see current.D4 §20.6
    2. 

  2. Kitchen water is safe to touch; electricity is not always. The water picture of F0-39 makes electricity feel friendly. Our kit's battery is chosen to be low voltage, but mains electricity can kill. The water picture must never make you curious about a wall socket.S1
    3. 

  3. Kitchen habits are learned at home; kit rules come from documents. Your uncle can show you how to hold a knife. The safe way to use a specific battery or charger is written by its maker, and only that document is the rule.D2
    4.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: two [NO] lines: "battery pack disconnected while you change wires" and "table clear of metal objects, drinks and loose parts"; the program printed "STOP: 2 check(s) not passed". Hypotheses: (1) a faulty battery; (2) a short circuit across the battery terminals. The adult's note says the paperclip was lying across both terminals and the LED loop was correct, which supports (2); a faulty battery is not needed to explain the evidence (it could be checked later with the battery maker's instructions). Root cause: a short circuit through the paperclip, made possible by a connected battery while wiring and a metal object on the table. Habits that prevent it: disconnect the battery while wiring; clear the table of metal. What Ravi did wrong: he started even though the checklist said STOP. Prevention: the adult reads the checklist output before the kit box is opened (a human gate as well as the program's). How the evidence was made: safety_check_incident.in was written by the author with "n" for questions 2 and 3 and run through the same checklist code (R1); the adult's note is a story written for this lab, not a record of a real event.
