Story hook

Analogy

When Ama walks into the kitchen at night, she does three things without thinking. Her eyes notice it is dark, so she reaches for the light. Her hand feels the table edge before she bumps into it. And when she carries a bowl of soup, she feels if it starts to tip.

Light, distance, tilt. Her body senses each one, and her brain decides what to do.

A robot cannot see, feel or balance like Ama. But it can have small parts that measure light, distance and tilt. These parts turn the world into numbers. A program reads the numbers and decides. This chapter is about those parts, the sensors, and about one tricky problem: what to do when a number keeps wobbling around the line between "dark" and "bright".

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a sensor does, with the senses picture and precise words.
  • Describe in words how a light sensor, a distance sensor and a tilt sensor work, and what their datasheets must tell you.
  • Use a threshold to turn a sensor reading into a yes/no decision.
  • Run the course's sensor simulator on a scripted table of readings.
  • Diagnose a flickering night-light from its log and fix it with two thresholds.
Jargon box
Sensor
Simple words: a part that measures something about the world and turns it into an electrical signal. Analogy: a sense, like eyes or touch. Precise: a device that converts a physical quantity (light, distance, tilt …) into an electrical signal that a circuit or program can read. D1 D2
Reading
Simple words: one number the program gets from a sensor at one moment. Analogy: one look at the thermometer. Precise: a single sampled value of a sensor's output, in units stated by its datasheet (or, in our simulator, our own made-up units). R1
Threshold
Simple words: the line you choose between "yes" and "no". Analogy: "if it is darker than this, switch on the hall light". Precise: a chosen value that a reading is compared with to make a two-way decision. R1
Noise
Simple words: small random wobbles in a sensor's numbers. Analogy: a shaky hand reading a ruler. Precise: unwanted variation in a measured signal that does not come from the quantity being measured. D2
Hysteresis
Simple words: using two lines instead of one, so a decision does not flip back and forth. Analogy: you put a jumper on when it is clearly cold and take it off only when it is clearly warm. Precise: a switching rule with separate "turn on" and "turn off" thresholds, leaving a dead zone between them. D2
Analog and digital signal
Simple words: analog is a number that can take many values; digital here means only two, yes or no. Analogy: a dimmer knob versus a light switch. Precise: an analog signal varies continuously; a digital signal has two levels (high and low, F0-42). A microcontroller turns an analog voltage into a number with an analog-to-digital converter. D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A sensor is a part that measures something. It is like one of your senses. A light sensor is like eyes. A distance sensor is like your hand reaching out in the dark. A tilt sensor is like the balance sense that tells you if you are leaning.D1

Each time the program asks a sensor, it gets a reading. Some readings are numbers, like "light level 52". Some are only yes or no, like "tilted: yes".

A number on its own does not tell the program what to do. So you choose a line, a threshold. For example: "if the light is below 30, it is dark". Now the program can decide.

Real sensor numbers wobble a little. This wobble is called noise. If the light is close to your line, the number may jump above and below it again and again. A night-light would flicker on, off, on, off. You will find and fix this problem in the forensic lab.D2

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Light

One kind of light sensor is a part whose resistance changes with the light that falls on it. Put it in a loop with a fixed resistor, and the voltage between them changes with the light. A microcontroller can turn that voltage into a number.D1 D2 Other light sensors are small chips that send the number directly. Which kind your kit has, and what its numbers mean, is in its datasheet.

Distance

One way to measure distance is like an echo. The sensor sends out a short pulse of sound or light. The pulse bounces off an object and comes back. The sensor measures how long the round trip took. The longer the time, the farther away the object.D2 The pulse travels there and back, so the distance is half of (speed × time). The speed and the sensor's range come from its datasheet, not from this chapter.

Tilt

A simple tilt sensor is a switch that closes or opens when it leans. Inside, a small moving part touches or leaves two contacts. So it gives a yes/no signal, like the buttons of F0-41.D1 More advanced motion sensors give numbers for how much the board is tilting; RB101 and HW302 meet them.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. A microcontroller reads an analog voltage with an analog-to-digital converter (ADC). The ADC gives a whole number in a fixed range. How many steps that range has, and which voltage matches the top step, are in the microcontroller's datasheet.D2 D3 Every reading also carries noise. Two common defences are to average several readings and to use two thresholds (hysteresis). MA202 (F0-62) measures sensor noise properly with statistics.

How the hardware actually does it

A sensor changes something electrical when the world changes: a resistance, a voltage, a time, or whether a contact is closed. The microcontroller measures that change through one of its pins. A yes/no sensor uses a digital input pin, read as high or low (F0-42). A number sensor either uses an analog input pin with the ADC, or sends its number as a message of bits.D2 Which way your kit's sensors work is stated in their datasheets (D3).

Worked example

All numbers here are scripted exercise values in our simulator's own units.

One threshold

Rule: light below 30 means "dark". Readings: 80, 52, 31, 29, 10.

  1. 80 < 30? No → bright.
  2. 52 < 30? No → bright.
  3. 31 < 30? No → bright. Close to the line!
  4. 29 < 30? Yes → dark.
  5. 10 < 30? Yes → dark.

Why one line flickers

At dusk the readings wobble: 31, 29, 31, 28, 30, 29. With one line at 30 the decision flips: bright, dark, bright, dark, bright, dark. That is six changes in six readings.

Two lines: hysteresis

New rule: switch on only below 26; switch off only above 34. Starting off, the same wobbling readings never go below 26, so the lamp stays off. When the readings later drop to 24, it switches on once, and stays on. The forensic answer key shows this in a real run.R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. Your senses fill gaps; sensors do not. Your brain mixes what you see, hear and remember. A sensor gives only its one number. If it is pointed the wrong way or covered with dust, the program still trusts it.D2
    2. 

  2. Sensors sample; senses feel all the time. The program only knows the world at the moments it takes a reading. Between two readings, it knows nothing.R1
    3. 

  3. Sensor errors are specific and measurable. Your eyes are "a bit tired". A sensor's noise and limits are written in its datasheet and can be measured (MA202).D2
    4.

Between minutes 3 and 10 the readings wobble between 27 and 31, around the single threshold of 30. Hypotheses: a broken lamp would not follow the readings so exactly; the log shows the lamp following the rule at every minute, so the rule is the cause.
