Story hook

Before the kitchen is built, Mei draws three sketches. In the first, the window is above the sink: washing up has daylight, but there is no wall for the tall cupboard. In the second, the window is above the table: meals are bright, the sink is in a corner. In the third, there are two small windows: both places have some light, and the wall costs more to build. No sketch is best at everything. Every choice gives something and takes something.

The team gives each sketch points for light, storage, cost and how hard it is to change later. The second sketch wins by a little. Then Tomás notices that "storage" and "space for the cupboard" are on the list twice. Remove the double, and the result changes. Ayasha asks the useful question: "How much would we have to care about light before the answer flips?" If the answer is "only a little", the choice is close, and the team should say so out loud, decide, and note what would make them change their mind.

Designs are like that. This chapter is about drawing more than one sketch, comparing them honestly, and writing down why one was chosen.

What you will be able to do

  • Generate at least three real alternatives for a design decision, including "change nothing" and "change the requirement".
  • Separate facts that can be measured from judgements that people make, and label each in the design document.
  • Measure a criterion when it is measurable, with a build and a method that make the measurement meaningful.
  • Compare alternatives with a weighted decision matrix, and test the decision with a sensitivity check, a dominance check and a double-counting check.
  • Record the decision with its reversibility and the evidence that would make you revisit it.
  • Diagnose a decision record whose numbers were arranged to support a conclusion chosen in advance.
Jargon box
Alternative (design)
Simple words: another way to meet the same goals. Analogy: another sketch of the same kitchen. Precise: a complete candidate design for the goals of a design document, described well enough that its costs and risks can be judged; the "Alternatives considered" section lists each with the reason it was not chosen, or the evidence that would choose it later. Course definition; D1 discusses alternatives in design documents D1.
Trade-off
Simple words: getting more of one good thing by accepting less of another. Analogy: a bigger window gives more light and leaves less wall for shelves. Precise: a relation between two or more criteria such that, among the available alternatives, improving one criterion requires giving up some of another; a design decision is an explicit choice of a point on that relation, justified by the goals.
Decision matrix
Simple words: a table that scores each option on each thing you care about, with a weight for how much you care. Analogy: the team's points for each kitchen sketch. Precise: a table of criteria c with weights wc and scores sc,o for each option o, combined here as the weighted sum To = Σc wc sc,o; the weights and most scores are human judgements, and the table's value is to make them explicit and checkable, not to compute a truth. Listing 1 implements it.
Sensitivity analysis
Simple words: asking how much an input would have to change before the answer changes. Analogy: "how much more would we have to care about light before the other sketch wins?" Precise: for a weighted-sum matrix, for each criterion c and each challenger o, the weight change d at which Twinner − To + d (sc,winner − sc,o) = 0; the smallest |d| that keeps the weight non-negative shows how robust the decision is to that weight. Derived in Layer 3; Listing 1 computes it.
Dominated option
Simple words: an option that is no better than another option on anything, and worse on something. Analogy: a sketch with less light, less storage and a higher cost than another sketch. Precise: option a is dominated by option b if sc,a ≤ sc,b for every criterion c and sc,a < sc,b for at least one; whatever the non-negative weights, a cannot beat b, so a can be removed before any weighing.
Reversible decision
Simple words: a choice that is cheap to undo later. Analogy: where the table stands (reversible) versus where the drain goes through the floor (not). Precise: a decision whose cost of reversal (code to change, data to migrate, callers to update, hardware state to recover) is small compared with the cost of deciding slowly; reversible decisions can be made quickly with a recorded revisit trigger, irreversible ones deserve more alternatives, more evidence and more reviewers (the "how much plan" table of F12-01).

Terms from earlier chapters used here: design document, non-goal, measurable requirement (F12-02); microbenchmark and warm-up (benchmarking); median and percentile (MA202); AddressSanitizer (SP202).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): the kitchen sketches = alternatives; "more light, less wall" = a trade-off; the team's points table = a decision matrix; "how much more would we have to care about light?" = a sensitivity check; a sketch that is worse in every way than another = a dominated option; where the table stands versus where the drain goes = a reversible versus an irreversible decision.

If you only draw one sketch, you cannot know what it costs, because you have nothing to compare it with. So you draw at least three, and one of them is always "keep what we have". Then you ask what each one gives and what each one takes.

Some answers can be measured, like how long something takes. Others are opinions, like how much you care about simplicity. Both are allowed in a design, but you must label which is which, and you must check how much the decision depends on the opinions.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Generating real alternatives

The "Alternatives considered" section is where reviewers learn whether the author looked for the best design or defended the first one. Four sources of alternatives work for almost any decision:

  • Change nothing. What happens if we do not build this? Sometimes the honest answer is "nothing bad yet", and the document is postponed.
  • The smallest change. What is the least code that meets the goals? For the receive path: a larger ring, or polling more often.
  • Reuse instead of build. Is there a class driver, a library or an earlier course's component that already does it (the curriculum asks drivers to check for class drivers first) D2?
  • Change the requirement. Is a goal stronger than its users need? "No byte ever lost" might become "losses are counted and reported", which admits simpler designs.

An alternative that nobody would choose ("rewrite the kernel in another language") is not an alternative; it is decoration, and reviewers recognise it. Each listed alternative must be complete enough that someone could argue for it.

Criteria come from goals

Criteria are not invented at comparison time. They come from the Goals of the design document (F12-02), plus the costs every design has: effort to build, effort to test, risk, and how hard it is to reverse. For the receive path the worked example uses four: no data loss (goal G3), idle CPU (a cost the team cares about), simplicity (effort and risk), and "testable now" (can it be tested with the harness the team already has?). Each criterion is stated so that two people could agree on what a score means: "testable now: 5 = host tests and QEMU scenario both exist today; 1 = needs hardware we do not have".

Facts and judgements

Every entry in a comparison is one of two kinds, and the document labels which:

  • Facts can be measured or looked up with a source: how many nanoseconds a lookup takes on this machine, whether the UART has a DMA engine (datasheet), whether QEMU models a device (its documentation). A fact without its measurement or source is an open question, not a score.
  • Judgements are people's evaluations: how much simplicity matters, whether a design "feels" risky. They are legitimate; they must be written as judgements, by named people, before the totals are computed.

The weights of a decision matrix are always judgements. Many scores are judgements too. Listing 1 does not turn judgements into facts; it makes them explicit and checks how much the decision depends on them.

Measure what can be measured

When a criterion is measurable, measure it, with the build you will ship. Listing 2 compares two ways to keep a small table "device id → handler": a std::vector of pairs searched from the front (A) and a std::unordered_map (B). Arguments for either are easy to make. The measurement on the build container, with -O2 and no sanitizers, printed per-lookup minima of 3.0 ns for A and 6.9 ns for B at 4 entries, 3.0 and 6.0 at 16, 7.7 and 5.9 at 64, and 429.9 and 6.4 at 4,096 entries R3. On that machine the vector was faster for the smallest tables and the hash map from somewhere between 16 and 64 entries upwards. A driver with a handful of entries and a cluster service with thousands would choose differently, and both would be right.

The same listing built with the university's sanitizer flags printed a different story: the vector was slower at every size, 298.8 ns against 198.4 ns at 4 entries R2. Sanitizers instrument memory accesses and change the relative costs; a decision based on that run would be wrong. Test correctness with sanitizers; measure with the build you ship (the same rule as in DS401 and SP302).

Watch out

These numbers come from one shared cloud container with 4 visible CPUs, on one date, as the minimum of five repetitions. They are evidence about this comparison on that machine, not a property of std::vector, std::unordered_map or any CPU. Run Listing 2 on the machine your code will run on; the crossover can move.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The weighted-sum matrix and its sensitivity

With criteria c, non-negative weights wc and scores sc,o, the total of option o is To = Σc wc sc,o, and the winner W has the largest total. Change one weight wc by d and keep the others fixed: every total changes by d · sc,o, so the gap between the winner and a challenger o becomes

(TW − To) + d · (sc,W − sc,o).

The gap is zero, a tie, at d = −(TW − To) / (sc,W − sc,o), provided the scores differ and wc + d ≥ 0. The smallest such |d| over all challengers is how far this one judgement can move before the decision changes. This is what Listing 1 prints, line by line (lines 95–119). If no d exists (the scores are equal, or the tie needs a negative weight), the decision does not depend on that weight at all.

Read the result against how sure you are of each weight. In the worked example the decision survives the idle-CPU weight moving from 2 to just under 7, but survives the simplicity and testable-now weights only down to 0.5 R1: the choice of the interrupt-plus-ring design rests on the team caring about simplicity and testability. That is a fine reason, and now it is a visible one.

Three checks before trusting a matrix

  1. Dominance. Remove options that are no better than another option on any criterion and worse on one; no non-negative weights can make them win (Listing 1, lines 79–93). If the "chosen" option is dominated, the matrix is wrong or the choice is.
  2. Independence. Two criteria that measure the same property double its weight. "Idle CPU" and "CPU load" for the same receive path are one property; listing both lets one alternative collect the same advantage twice. Before scoring, ask for every pair of criteria: could an option score high on one and low on the other? If not, merge them.
  3. Order of work. Weights are agreed before anyone scores, ideally by two people independently, then compared. Weights written after the totals are visible tend to confirm the totals; the forensic lab shows the result.

What a weighted sum cannot do

  • Scores are ordinal, the sum treats them as cardinal. A score of 4 is "better than 3", but the sum assumes it is better by exactly as much as 3 is better than 2. Small total differences are therefore within the noise of the method. The worked example's margin is 5.0 points, 10.2 % of the winner's total R1; the corrected forensic matrix's margin is 2.0 points, 4.0 % R5. Call the second one what it is: a close call.
  • Hard constraints are not weights. "Must not lose input silently" is not worth 5 points; an option that violates it is out, before the matrix. Apply constraints first, then weigh what remains.
  • Interactions are invisible. A sum cannot express "DMA is fine if and only if the device is cache-coherent". Such conditions become separate alternatives (DMA on a coherent platform; DMA with cache maintenance) or open questions.
  • Uncertainty is not in the table. A score of 2 for "testable now" for DMA hides whether the team could build a test harness in a week or never. Where a score is uncertain, run the matrix with the optimistic and the pessimistic score; if the winner changes, reduce the uncertainty first (a spike, a datasheet read, a measurement).

Reversibility and the revisit trigger

A decision record ends with two lines that the matrix does not produce. Reversibility: what it would cost to switch to the runner-up later (for the receive path: the ring's interface rx_pop/rx_wait hides the mechanism, so switching to DMA later changes the driver, not its callers; that makes the decision reversible, which is itself a design goal). Revisit trigger: the evidence that would reopen it ("if rx_dropped() is non-zero in normal use", "if a second UART with a DMA engine is added"). A close, reversible decision is made quickly and watched; a close, irreversible decision is not made until the uncertainty is reduced. The curriculum's runbook says the same for its agent: when a design decision affects later milestones, stop, present options with consequences, and let the learner decide D2.

Measuring an alternative fairly

Listing 2 is deliberately small, and still needs care to be fair: both alternatives look up the same keys in the same order (line 36 and line 43), each is timed as a whole batch to keep clock overhead out of the per-lookup figure, every result feeds a checksum that is printed so the compiler cannot remove the work, and the reported number is the minimum of five repetitions, which estimates the cost without interference rather than the typical cost. What it does not do: vary the order of keys, measure misses (keys not in the table), measure insertion, or control CPU frequency and other tenants of the machine. A decision that depends on those needs them measured; SE401 (F12-13) treats benchmark methodology in full.

How the hardware actually does it

The lookup measurement has a hardware explanation, and the explanation is what makes it a fact you can reason about rather than a number you memorise. A std::vector stores its elements contiguously D4; a small table is therefore a short stretch of neighbouring memory, and a scan from the front reads elements that the caches of a CPU bring in together, a whole cache line at a time (the cache chapters of F1, HW203) D6. A hash map computes a hash, finds a bucket and compares the key stored there: a fixed amount of work per lookup that the scan of a tiny table does not need, so for a table of a few entries the fixed work costs more than scanning everything. As the table grows, the scan's work grows with the number of entries and the hash map's does not, so there is a crossover, and where it lies depends on the CPU, its caches and the element size. That is why the chapter reports a measured crossover range on one machine and asks you to measure yours.

The receive-path alternatives have hardware explanations too, and here the facts must come from documents, not from a matrix: whether the UART has a DMA engine, whether the platform's DMA is cache-coherent (curriculum 5.4: "PC devices usually are; many SoC devices are not and need explicit cache clean and invalidate") D2, and what the interrupt costs on your CPU (measure it). A decision matrix holds the team's judgement of these facts; the facts themselves belong in the Design and Open questions sections with sources.

Worked example

Task. Write the "Alternatives considered" section of the receive-path design document (F12-02, Listing 3) with a decision matrix, a sensitivity check and a decision record.

Step 1 — constraints first. G3 says losses must be counted. Polling loses bytes silently whenever the task is busy and does not know it lost them: it violates G3 unless the UART reports overruns, which the team cannot yet answer from a document: they add it as a new open question, Q2, for the datasheet. The team keeps polling in the matrix to show the comparison, and marks it "fails G3 unless Q2 answers yes".

Step 2 — criteria and weights, before scores. Two team members write weights independently on a 0–5 scale for no_data_loss, idle_cpu, simplicity and testable_now, compare, and agree on 5, 2, 3, 3. Their reason for the low idle_cpu weight: the console receives bytes only when someone types.

Step 3 — scores, labelled. All twelve scores are judgements of the two team members, on the 1–5 scale; "testable_now" is based on a fact (the host tests and the QEMU paste test of F4-03 exist for the ring; nothing exists for DMA).

Listing 3 — decide.in: the matrix as input to Listing 1.



Step 4 — run and read. Totals 42.0, 49.0 and 44.0; the interrupt-plus-ring design wins by 5.0 points, 10.2 % of its total; no option is dominated R1. Sensitivity: the decision survives any idle-CPU weight below 7, and survives simplicity or testable-now weights down to 0.5; lowering no_data_loss to 1.5 would make polling tie (because the ring's advantage over polling is mostly loss avoidance).


Step 5 — check by hand. irq_ring: 5×4 + 2×4 + 3×3 + 3×4 = 20 + 8 + 9 + 12 = 49. DMA: 25 + 10 + 3 + 6 = 44. For the idle-CPU weight: d = −(49 − 44) / (4 − 5) = +5, so the tie is at 2 + 5 = 7, as printed and as Figure 2 shows.


Step 6 — the decision record. "Chosen: B, interrupt + ring. Rejected: A, polling (fails G3 unless the UART reports overruns, Q2); C, DMA (not testable with our harness; larger setup; its advantage, idle CPU, matters little for a console). Close on simplicity and testability weights, robust on idle CPU. Reversible: the rx_pop/rx_wait interface hides the mechanism. Revisit if rx_dropped() is non-zero in normal use or if the serial line starts carrying bulk data."

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Software alternatives can be built and measured cheaply. Nobody builds three kitchens to compare them. In software, a prototype or a benchmark of two alternatives often costs an afternoon (Listing 2), so "measure instead of argue" is available far more often than the analogy suggests.
    • 

  • Software alternatives can be combined or switched later. A window cannot be moved without rebuilding the wall; an interface that hides the mechanism lets you switch from ring to DMA without touching callers. The analogy overstates how irreversible most software decisions are, and understates the cost of the irreversible ones (formats, public interfaces), which spread to other people's code.
    • 

  • The matrix is not the decision. A points table for kitchen sketches feels like arithmetic deciding. In engineering the table only records judgements; constraints, uncertainty and reversibility decide as much as the totals (Layer 3).

Troubleshooting.


    

  • Listing 1 prints "criterion X: 2 scores for 3 options" and exits with code 2 → a score is missing on that line; every criterion needs one score per option, in option order.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. decide_team.in was written to reproduce a matrix with a double-counted criterion, a vague criterion and a missing goal; it was run through Listing 1 by run.sh R4. The corrected matrix decide_fixed.in was run the same way R5.


Observation. idle_cpu and cpu_load have identical score rows (1 4 5) and equal weights: one property counted twice, adding 3 × (5 − 4) = 3 points to DMA's lead over the ring. future_proof (1 3 5) has no agreed meaning and no test; it adds 2 × (5 − 3) = 4 points. Together 7 of DMA's 11-point margin come from these two rows. No criterion represents G3 (testability), although it is a goal; simplicity has weight 2.


Why the sensitivity looks reassuring. Four of five criteria favour DMA in their scores, so increasing any of their weights only helps DMA and decreasing one alone cannot overcome the inflated margin; only simplicity can flip it, at weight 7.5. The robustness is produced by the duplicated and vague rows, not by the designs.


Corrected matrix. Remove cpu_load and future_proof, add testable_now 3 with the scores of the worked example (5 4 2). Result: polling 38, ring 50, DMA 48; the ring wins by 2.0 points, 4.0 %, and a weight change of 1.0 on simplicity or testable_now, or of 2.0 on no_data_loss or idle_cpu, ties DMA R5:




What the document should have said. "Close call between interrupt + ring and DMA. DMA's advantage is idle CPU, which matters little for a console; the ring is testable today (G3). Choose the ring; revisit if the line carries bulk data." Alternative wrong answer: "the team should have chosen polling": polling fails G2 unless the UART reports overruns, and scores lowest in every version.


Prevention. In review: map every goal to a criterion and every criterion to a goal or a stated cost; reject criteria without an agreed meaning; check pairs of criteria for identical rows; require weights written before scores; and require the margin to be stated as printed, with the word "close" when it is small.
