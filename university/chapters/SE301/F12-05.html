Story hook

Kofi's note is pinned to the window frame: "What is the lintel resting on now? I think it was sized for the old opening." Tomás reads it at the end of a long day, and his first feeling is not gratitude. He made a good frame. Then he reads the note again. It does not say his frame is bad; it says the lintel might not hold. He measures. Kofi is right.

The next morning Tomás fits a longer lintel and does three more things. He pins an answer under Kofi's note: "You were right, the old lintel rested on 6 cm of brick. Replaced with a longer one; photo attached; I also checked the other two windows, which are unchanged." He answers Kofi's second note, about the paint colour, differently: "I would like to keep this colour, because it matches the door; happy to change if you feel strongly." And he asks Kofi to come and look again, rather than assuming the note is closed.

A week later, on another wall, someone pins "fixed" under a note and moves on. The fix was real, but it was on the window next to the one the note was about. Nobody looked. This chapter is about receiving review: answering every comment, disagreeing well, and making sure "fixed" means the thing the reviewer pointed at is fixed.

What you will be able to do

  • Prepare a change for review: one purpose, a description that says what, why, how it was tested and what it does not do, and a self-review against the checklist.
  • Answer every review comment with one of four responses: fixed (with the commit and the test), answered, not changed (with a reason), or follow-up (with an issue).
  • Disagree with a reviewer using evidence, and escalate a disagreement without making it personal.
  • Verify that a fix addresses the exact line and scenario of the comment, with a test that reproduces the reviewer's scenario.
  • Track a review in a ledger, and say what a ledger can and cannot prove.
  • Diagnose a comment that was marked fixed but was not, from a thread, a diff and a run.
Jargon box
Author (of a change)
Simple words: the person who wrote the change and asked for review. Analogy: the carpenter whose frame is being checked. Precise: the person responsible for a change until it is merged and afterwards: they describe it, answer every review comment, make and verify the fixes, and keep the design document and tests consistent with it. Course definition.
Review response
Simple words: the author's answer to one review comment. Analogy: the note Tomás pins under Kofi's note. Precise: in this course, exactly one of: fixed (with the commit that changes the code and, for a defect, the test that would have failed before it), answered (the reviewer asked a question; here is the answer, with evidence), wontfix (not changed, with the reason), or followup (agreed but out of scope; with the id of the issue that tracks it).
Review ledger
Simple words: a list of every comment and its answer, so nothing is forgotten. Analogy: the board where every pinned note and its answer stay until both carpenters agree. Precise: a record, one line per comment, of its label, location, response, reference (commit or issue) and the reviewer's agreement where needed; it checks bookkeeping (every comment answered, every "fixed" names a commit, every blocking comment closed by a change or by agreement), not whether a fix is correct. Listing 1 is one.
Verified resolution
Simple words: a comment is closed only after someone checked that the fix fixes the thing the comment pointed at. Analogy: Kofi comes back and looks at the new lintel. Precise: the closing of a blocking comment after the reviewer (or the author, with evidence the reviewer accepts) has confirmed that the referenced change modifies the commented location or its cause and that a test reproducing the comment's scenario fails before the change and passes after it.
Follow-up issue
Simple words: a written task for later, made when a good comment does not belong in this change. Analogy: "repaint the hallway" goes on the list for next week. Precise: an entry in the project's issue tracker (or the design document's open questions) with an id, created during review for an agreed improvement outside the change's scope, referenced in the response so the comment can be closed without losing the point.
Escalation (review disagreement)
Simple words: asking a third person to decide when author and reviewer cannot agree. Analogy: asking the most experienced builder on site to look at the lintel with both of you. Precise: a step taken after both sides have stated their reasons and evidence in the thread: a named third person (another reviewer, the design document's approver, the teacher or mentor) decides, and the decision and its reason are recorded in the thread or the design document; escalation is about the decision, never about the person.

Terms from earlier chapters used here: code review, blocking comment, comment label, unified diff (F12-04); regression test and commit (SP202); bounded wait and surprise removal (DR401); design document, open question (this course).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; registered: code review = a second carpenter checks your joints; the rest proposed in NOTES.md): Kofi's pinned notes = review comments; Tomás's answer under each note = a review response; the photo of the new lintel = a commit and a test that show the fix; "I would like to keep this colour, because…" = a reasoned wontfix; "repaint the hallway next week" = a follow-up issue; asking Kofi to look again = re-requesting review; the board of notes and answers = the review ledger.

When someone reviews your work, they are spending their time to make your work better. The first feeling can still be "but I worked hard on this". That feeling is normal. Wait until it passes, then read the comment as information about the code, not about you.

Then answer every comment, even the small ones. Say what you changed and show it, or explain why you did not change it. And before you say "fixed", look at the exact line the reviewer pointed at and check that your change really touches it.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Before review: make the reviewer's job possible

Most of what makes a review go well happens before the reviewer opens it. (Large engineering organisations write down practices of this kind; D1 is one such book, listed by title only and not opened for this chapter D1.)

  • One purpose per change. A fix, a refactoring and a feature are three changes. Mixed changes hide defects and make every comment harder to answer.
  • A description with four parts. What the change does; why (link the design document, the issue, the incident); how it was tested (which tests are new, which were run, on what: host, QEMU, hardware); what it does not do or what is untested. The forensic lab of F12-04 shows the cost of a description that hides a changed test.
  • A self-review. Read your own diff with the reviewer's checklist of F12-04. Search for other places that encode any value you changed. Run the old tests against the new code. Most authors find something in their own change this way.
  • Tests in the change. A defect fix comes with a regression test that fails before the fix and passes after it (SP202) D2.

Reading the comments

Read the whole review before answering anything; a later comment often explains an earlier one. Sort by label: blocking first, then questions, suggestions, nits. For each comment, find the exact line and reproduce the reviewer's scenario in your head or, better, in a test. If a comment is unclear, ask: "Do you mean the loop on line 39 or the one on line 32?" is a perfectly good first response.

Separate the comment from its tone. A badly worded comment can carry a real defect; a kindly worded one can be wrong. Answer the content. If the tone hurt, say so privately and briefly later, about the wording, not about the person; that helps the reviewer improve as much as their comment helps you.

The ledger

It enforces this course's rules: every comment has a response; "fixed" names a commit id; "wontfix" gives a reason; "followup" names an issue; a blocking comment answered or declined needs the reviewer's agreement. Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Make "fixed" verifiable

A "fixed" response is a claim, and in this university claims need evidence. For a blocking comment about a defect, the evidence is a test that reproduces the reviewer's scenario: it fails on the reviewed version and passes on the fixed one. Write that test first, run it against the old code and see it fail, then fix. This is the curriculum's "tests first" habit applied to review D2, and it protects against the forensic lab's failure: had the author written "device removed before waitIdle" as a test, it would have hung on the "fixed" version and the mismatch would have been found by the author, not by an incident.

Three more habits make a fix easy to check: reference the comment in the commit message or a code comment next to the change (the forensic diff does this, on the wrong loop, which is how the mismatch becomes visible); keep the fix in its own commit so the reviewer's re-review is one small diff; and re-request review explicitly instead of assuming silence means agreement.

Disagreeing well

Reviewers are sometimes wrong, and authors often know things reviewers do not. Disagreement is part of review; the aim is to make the disagreement about evidence.

  1. Restate the comment in your own words, so both of you know you are discussing the same thing.
  2. Give your reason and your evidence: a test, a measurement, a datasheet section, the design document's goal or non-goal. "I prefer it" is a reason for nits only.
  3. Say what would change your mind. "If the chip can return month 13, I will add the check; the datasheet section on the date registers would settle it."
  4. Look at reversibility. If the disputed choice is easy to change later (F12-03), accept the reviewer's version or yours and move on with a follow-up; time spent arguing about reversible choices is rarely well spent.
  5. Escalate if still stuck. Ask a named third person, record the decision and the reason in the thread or in the design document. Escalation is a normal step, not a defeat; the curriculum's runbook does the same with the agent and the learner ("present options with consequences" and record the learner's decision in the log) D2.

Never resolve a disagreement by weakening a test silently. The curriculum's rule for acceptance tests is explicit: never skip, weaken or postpone one without the approver's explicit agreement, and record the decision D2. In review terms: a test changed to make a change pass is a blocking topic, and the author raises it first.

When review reveals a design problem

Some comments are not about the change but about its design: "should this be a polling loop at all?" If the design document was approved, answer with a pointer to the decision record (F12-03) and, if the comment brings new evidence, open the decision again through the document, not through the code review. A design changed inside a code review thread leaves the document stale and the next reader confused; the document is a living document precisely so that design changes go there.

Receiving review as a skill

Two habits compound over years. Keep a short list of the comments you receive more than once (unbounded waits, missing units, edited tests) and add them to your self-review; after a few months most reviewers stop finding them. And review others: the lab of F12-04 makes you better at receiving review, because you learn what the reviewer needs from your description and your responses.

How the hardware actually does it

Comments on driver code often describe a hardware scenario the author cannot reproduce at their desk: a device removed during a transfer, an interrupt arriving before initialization completes, a status bit that never clears. "Fixed" for such a comment needs a way to create the scenario. The curriculum's test plan names the tools: emulation first, host unit tests for pure logic, trace replay, and fault injection ("unplug during transfer, cable pulls, timeouts, corrupted descriptors, … interrupts arriving before initialization completes") D2. The forensic lab uses the simplest of these: a simulated device in host code whose status reads as all ones after removal, the curriculum's "surprise removal" convention D2. With such a test the reviewer's scenario becomes a run with an exit code, and the hang becomes visible as exit code 124 from a time limit rather than as a frozen robot.

When the scenario can only be produced on real hardware, the response says so honestly: "fixed in e41c9b7; tested with the simulated device (test X); untested on hardware: needs the board with a removable sensor". That sentence keeps the comment open in the right way: the reviewer can accept the simulator evidence, ask for the hardware run, or record the gap, and nobody mistakes a host test for a hardware result.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The notes stay forever. A pinned note is thrown away when the wall is finished; a review thread is kept with the code's history and read years later by someone asking "why is this loop bounded at 1000?". Write responses for that reader too.
    • 

  • Fixes can be tested, not only looked at. Kofi checks the lintel by looking. A reviewer can run the test that reproduces the scenario; the analogy suggests that "looking again" is the best possible check, when a failing-then-passing test is better.
    • 

  • The same mistake is copied. A wrongly placed lintel affects one window. A pattern ("bound the first loop you see") is copied into other code; the forensic lab's other loop shows that a fix applied to the wrong place leaves the defect and adds false confidence.
    • 

  • Code review also shares knowledge (the registered note): answering a comment well teaches the reviewer about your code as much as the comment teaches you D3.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. dev_v2.cpp has two unbounded waits (lines 32 and 39); dev_v3.cpp bounds only the one in resetDevice. run.sh produced the diff (R4), ran Listing 1 on the thread, ran the author's scenarios and ran the field scenario under a 3-second limit R4 R5 R6.


Observation. C2 points at dev.cpp:39, which in the reviewed version is while (d.readStatus() & kBusy) { inside waitIdle. The diff's hunk @@ -26,12 +26,15 @@ covers old lines 26–37: it adds kMaxPolls and bounds the loop of resetDevice (old line 32); waitIdle appears only as the last context line and is unchanged. The comment "review C2: bounded wait" sits next to the wrong loop.


Why the author's test passed. Its scenarios were "normal" and "device removed before reset": the second exercises exactly the loop that was fixed, and the scenario's main loop skips the wait after a failed reset. C2 described a device that disappears while the driver waits for it to become idle: removed after a successful reset and before waitIdle. No test created that scenario.


Exit code 124. timeout stopped the program after 3 s. The removed device reads 0xFFFFFFFF, whose BUSY bit is set, so waitIdle spins forever and never prints its line; "reset: ok" was flushed before the wait began, so it survives the kill R5.


The ledger. Not wrong: it reported what it checks, that C2 had a "fixed" response with a commit-shaped id. Nobody checked that the commit touched line 39, and nobody ran C2's scenario. The reviewer's agreement was not required by the tool for a "fixed" response, and none was given; the change was merged on the ledger's word.


What should have happened. The author writes the test first: "device removed after reset, before waitIdle", runs it on version 2 (it hangs: exit code 124 under a time limit), then bounds waitIdle (and, while there, the loop in resetDevice), and answers "fixed <commit>: both waits bounded by kMaxPolls; test 'unplug-before-wait' hung on the reviewed version and now reports a failure". The reviewer, before agreeing, opens the commit and checks that line 39's loop changed and that the test exists and passes. Alternative wrong conclusions: "the watchdog should be faster" (it hides the hang, does not fix it); "1000 polls is too few" (irrelevant: the loop at fault has no bound at all).


Prevention. Require a test name in every "fixed" response to a blocking comment (the lab's extension), and let only the reviewer close blocking comments; add "does the fix touch the commented line or its cause?" to the reviewer's re-review checklist.
