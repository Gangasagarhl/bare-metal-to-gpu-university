Story hook

A visitor arrives at the school office with a box of files. She speaks only from a phrasebook, and only in a fixed order: you must first say "hello" (phrase 0), then ask "which edition of the phrasebook do you use?" (phrase 8), then repeat "are you ready?" until she says yes, then ask her name, give her a desk number, ask the size of her box, and only then may you ask for "file number 2070".

The office clerk who learned the routine with the small boxes always asked for files by their position in centimetres from the front of the box: "the file 1059840 millimetres... no, bytes, in". Small-box visitors understood that. One day a visitor arrived with a big box and the newer phrasebook, in which you ask for files by their number, not by their distance. The clerk asked for "file 1059840" as usual. The visitor, who counts files, walked to file one million and fifty-nine thousand, eight hundred and forty, took it out and gave it to him. He put new papers in the same place later, asked for them back with the same number, got them, and wrote "all correct" in his log. Somebody else's files at that position were gone.

That is the SD card protocol and the bug of this chapter's forensic lab. The card answers only in its protocol's fixed sequence, and two generations of cards understand the same address differently. A driver that confuses them passes its own read-back test and destroys data it never looks at.

What you will be able to do

  • Program an SD Host Controller (SDHCI): reset, power, clock, command and response registers, interrupt status, and the data port for programmed I/O.
  • Initialise an SD card with the command sequence CMD0, CMD8, ACMD41, CMD2, CMD3, CMD9, CMD7, CMD16, and decode its OCR, CID and CSD (versions 1.0 and 2.0).
  • Read and write single blocks with CMD17 and CMD24, with the address in bytes for standard-capacity cards and in blocks for high-capacity cards, and verify every write from the host side.
  • Route the card's pins to the right controller through the pin provider of F4-35, and recognise the symptom of a wrong pin function.
  • State how eMMC initialisation differs from SD, and what milestone D9 still requires beyond this lab.
  • Diagnose a driver that corrupts data outside its test area while its own read-back test passes.
Jargon box
SDHCI (SD Host Controller Interface)
Simple words: a standard set of registers for the chip-side controller that talks to SD cards. Analogy: the office counter with a fixed form for talking to visitors. Precise: the SD Association's standard register interface for SD host controllers (argument, command, response, present state, host and power control, clock control, interrupt status, buffer data port, capabilities, version), implemented with quirks by many SoC vendors. D2
SD command and response
Simple words: a numbered request sent to the card, and the card's reply. Analogy: a numbered phrase from the phrasebook and the visitor's answer. Precise: a 48-bit command token (index CMD0–CMD63 plus a 32-bit argument) on the CMD line; responses are 48 bits (R1, R3, R6, R7) or 136 bits (R2); application commands (ACMDn) are preceded by CMD55. D1
OCR, CID, CSD and RCA
Simple words: four things the card tells you about itself: its voltage and readiness, its identity, its size and timing, and the short address you give it. Analogy: "I am ready", the visitor's name card, the size of her box, her desk number. Precise: Operation Conditions Register (voltage window, busy bit 31, Card Capacity Status bit 30), Card Identification register (manufacturer, product name, serial), Card-Specific Data (structure version, capacity fields), Relative Card Address (16 bits, published by the card in reply to CMD3). D1 R2
SDSC and SDHC/SDXC addressing
Simple words: older small cards are addressed in bytes; newer large cards are addressed in 512-byte blocks. Analogy: files asked for by distance from the front of the box, or by their number. Precise: for Standard Capacity cards (CCS = 0) the data command argument is a byte address; for High and Extended Capacity cards (CCS = 1 in the OCR) it is a block number; the driver must read CCS after initialisation and choose. D1 R3
eMMC
Simple words: an SD-like storage chip soldered onto the board, with its own version of the protocol. Analogy: a visitor who lives in the building and speaks a dialect of the same phrasebook. Precise: embedded MultiMediaCard, specified by JEDEC (JESD84 series): an MMC-protocol device with a controller, hardware partitions (boot areas, RPMB, user area) and an extended CSD register; it shares SDHCI host controllers with SD but initialises differently. D3
Programmed I/O (PIO)
Simple words: the processor itself copies every word between memory and the device. Analogy: the clerk carrying each page by hand instead of using the delivery truck. Precise: data transfer through a device's data register by CPU loads and stores, as opposed to DMA (for SDHCI: SDMA or ADMA2), which D9 requires as the second step. D2 C1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

An SD card is a little computer of its own that keeps your files. You cannot reach in; you have to talk to it in its own language, one sentence at a time, in the right order: wake up, tell me about yourself, here is your number, now give me page 2070. Old small cards count pages in tiny steps, new big cards count whole pages, and if you mix them up you take the wrong page, and you might write over someone's homework.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

SoC boards boot from SD cards and eMMC chips, not from NVMe or SATA disks. Both are managed flash: a small controller inside the card hides the flash chips and offers numbered 512-byte blocks. Between the CPU and the card sits a host controller on the SoC. Many SoCs use a controller that follows the SD Association's SD Host Controller Specification, SDHCI, with vendor quirks; others use different designs (Synopsys DesignWare MMC, Allwinner's own). C1 D2

The driver has two layers. The host controller layer writes SDHCI registers: reset the controller, turn on bus power, set a slow clock for identification, then send commands and read responses. The card protocol layer sends the right commands in the right order: CMD0 puts the card in its idle state; CMD8 asks whether it understands version 2 of the physical layer; ACMD41 (CMD55 followed by CMD41) negotiates voltage and waits until the card is ready, and its answer, the OCR, says whether the card is standard capacity (SDSC, byte addresses) or high capacity (SDHC/SDXC, block addresses); CMD2 reads the card's identity; CMD3 gives it a short address (RCA); CMD9 reads its size; CMD7 selects it; and then CMD17 and CMD24 read and write single blocks. D1

The lab runs the driver on QEMU's Raspberry Pi 3 model with two card images: 64 MiB, which QEMU presents as SDSC, and 4 GiB, which it presents as SDHC. Both initialise, both pass a self-test that writes 32 blocks in a scratch area and reads them back, and a host-side checker confirms each block landed exactly where intended. QEMU's own trace shows every command the driver sent. Routing the card's pins to the other SD controller (function 4 instead of 7) makes the card disappear. R2 R3 R8

The forensic lab uses the same pieces to show a bug that the driver's own test cannot see: on the 4 GiB card, a branch build always uses byte addresses. Its self-test reads back exactly what it wrote, because reads and writes make the same mistake; only the host-side check, which looks at the card image from outside, finds the data inside partition 1. R9 R10

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The host controller: registers and a command

SDHCI's registers are a 256-byte window. The ones this driver uses: block size and count (0x04), argument (0x08), transfer mode and command (0x0C, written as one 32-bit word), four response words (0x10 to 0x1C), the buffer data port (0x20), present state (0x24: command line busy in bit 0, card inserted in bit 16), host control and power control (0x28), clock control, timeout and software reset (0x2C), normal and error interrupt status (0x30), status enable (0x34), capabilities (0x40) and the host controller version (0xFE, read here as the top half of the word at 0xFC). D2

Sending a command: wait until the command line is not busy; clear all status bits by writing ones; write the argument; write the command register with the index in bits 13:8 and the response type and check flags in the low byte (48-bit, 136-bit, 48-bit with busy; CRC check; index check; data present); then poll the status register for "command complete" or an error. The response is then in the response registers. For a data command, the driver also sets the block size and count, the transfer direction, and after "command complete" waits for "buffer read ready" or "buffer write ready", moves 128 words through the data port, and waits for "transfer complete". D2

Before any command, the controller must be reset, the bus powered and the SD clock started: write the software-reset bit and wait for it to clear; set power control to 3.3 V and bus power on; program a clock divider for a slow identification clock, enable the internal clock, wait for "internal clock stable", then enable the SD clock to the card. This driver writes a divider of 0x80 and does not compute the resulting frequency; on real hardware the identification clock must be at most 400 kHz, which depends on the controller's base clock from the capabilities register or the devicetree. D1 D2

Two CSD layouts, two size formulas


The CSD's top two bits give its structure version. For version 1.0 (SDSC), capacity = (C_SIZE + 1) × 2C_SIZE_MULT + 2 × 2READ_BL_LEN bytes, with C_SIZE in CSD bits 73:62, C_SIZE_MULT in 49:47 and READ_BL_LEN in 83:80. For version 2.0 (SDHC and SDXC), capacity = (C_SIZE + 1) × 512 KiB, with C_SIZE in bits 69:48. The driver applies the formula of the version it reads; the runs give 131072 blocks (64 MiB) and 8388608 blocks (4096 MiB), equal to the image sizes. D1 R2 R3


A subtlety of SDHCI makes the bit positions harder than they look: the controller stores a 136-bit response without its 8-bit CRC and without the start bits, so the response registers hold CSD (or CID) bits 127:8 shifted down by 8. A field the specification places at bits 69:48 is at register bits 61:40, which is bits 29:8 of the second response word. The driver's comments state the shift for each field. Getting it wrong by one byte gives sizes that are wrong by orders of magnitude, which is why the lab compares the decoded size with the image size. D2

The card's pins belong to one controller at a time


QEMU's Raspberry Pi 3 model, like the BCM2837 it imitates, has two SD controllers: an SDHCI-compatible one at bus address 0x7e300000 and a Broadcom-specific "sdhost" at 0x7e202000. The SD card's pins (GPIO 48 to 53) are routed to one of them by the GPIO function select: function 7 for the SDHCI controller, function 4 for sdhost. The course's devicetree disables sdhost and gives the SDHCI node the course's pin property dr403,sd-pins = <&gpio 48 6 7>; the driver asks the pin provider of F4-35 to apply it before it touches the controller. R2 D4


Changing the 7 to a 4 in the tree routes the pins to the other controller. The SDHCI controller resets and clocks normally, but its present-state register says "card NOT inserted", and the probe fails with "no card". The register values show the cause directly: GPFSEL4 = 0x24000000 and GPFSEL5 = 0x924 are six 3-bit fields of value 4 (0b100). R8

Testing a block driver without trusting it


The driver's self-test writes 32 blocks at pseudo-random places in a scratch area (blocks 2048 to 2175, outside the partition that mksd.py created from block 8192), reads each back and compares CRC-32s. A test that reads back through the same driver can only prove that reads and writes agree with each other. It cannot prove that the data went to the right place. So the lab adds a check from outside: verify_image.py recomputes the same pseudo-random block numbers and data patterns on the host and inspects the card image file directly. In both good runs, 32 of 32 blocks are where they should be, and block 0 still carries the partition table's 0x55AA signature. R4 R5




The image layout above: partition 1 from block 8192, the scratch area at blocks 2048 to 2175 before it. R7






Writing is opt-in: the self-test runs only if the devicetree's /chosen node carries dr403,sd-selftest = <first count>. A storage driver that writes during probe without being asked is a data-loss bug waiting for the wrong card.



eMMC: the same controller, a different conversation


eMMC devices use the MMC protocol, specified by JEDEC, and share SDHCI host controllers with SD on many SoCs. The initialisation differs: CMD1 (SEND_OP_COND) instead of CMD8 and ACMD41, with its own OCR and an access-mode field for sector addressing on devices larger than 2 GB; CMD3 in which the host assigns the RCA; an Extended CSD of 512 bytes read with CMD8 (which means something else than SD's CMD8), holding the sector count, the supported bus widths and speeds, and the partition configuration; and CMD6 (SWITCH) to change settings such as bus width or the active partition. eMMC devices also have hardware partitions (two boot areas, an RPMB area for authenticated storage, and the user area), and boot ROMs on many SoCs read their first stage from a boot area. D3

How the hardware actually does it


An SD card is a flash memory with its own controller: it maps logical blocks to flash pages, erases in large units, levels wear and corrects errors, all invisibly. The bus between host and card has a clock line, a command line and one, four or eight (eMMC) data lines. At power-up the card must be talked to at a low clock and 3.3 V signalling; faster modes (high speed, UHS-I) are negotiated afterwards, and UHS modes need the I/O voltage switched to 1.8 V through a regulator and a tuning procedure, which is why curriculum's storage catalog warns about regulators and tuning on SoCs. C1 D1


Card detection is a mechanical switch in the slot, wired either to the controller's card-detect input (the present-state bit) or to a GPIO, which the devicetree names; some boards wire neither and rely on polling. Write protection on full-size cards is a sliding tab read by another switch. Removal during a transfer leaves the controller waiting for a response that never comes: the driver must time out every wait, report an error and reset the controller, which is D9's third acceptance test. C1


Many SoC SDHCI implementations deviate from the standard: registers that accept only 32-bit accesses, missing or wrong capability bits, clock dividers in a different format, extra vendor registers for tuning or voltage switching. Linux carries these as "quirks" per compatible string; the driver for your board's controller is the best list of what yours does wrong. C1

Safety
A storage driver that writes can destroy data in one command. Develop against card images in QEMU first, as this lab does. On a board, use a card you may erase, keep the self-test's scratch area outside every partition, never point a write test at the card or eMMC that holds the board's only boot firmware unless you have practised recovery, and remove other storage from the board. Never write to eMMC boot partitions or RPMB while learning. See the university safety rules.



Worked example


Question. From the driver's output and the trace, work out (a) why the 64 MiB card is SDSC and the 4 GiB card SDHC, (b) the argument of the first CMD24 on each card, and (c) where the first self-test block landed on each.


(a) OCR. 64 MiB card: OCR 0x80ffff00. Bit 31 = 1 (power-up done), bit 30 = 0: CCS clear, SDSC, byte addressing. 4 GiB card: OCR 0xc0ffff00, bits 31 and 30 set: SDHC/SDXC, block addressing. QEMU's card model chose the type from the image size; the threshold between the two was not tested in this build (a card larger than 4 GiB could not be addressed in 32-bit byte addresses at all). R2 R3


(b) The first written block. The self-test's generator starts from seed 12345: seed = 12345 × 1103515245 + 12345 mod 232, and the block is 2048 + (seed >> 8) mod 128. The trace shows the first CMD24 argument 0x00102c00 = 1059840 on the SDSC card, which is 2070 × 512: block 2070, as a byte address. On the SDHC card the driver sends 2070 (0x816) itself. R6


(c) Where it landed. SDSC: byte 1059840 = block 2070, in the scratch area. SDHC: block 2070, the same place. verify_image.py confirms 32 of 32 blocks in place on both images. R4 R5


(d) The size, from the CSD. For the 4 GiB card, version 2.0: 8388608 blocks = (C_SIZE + 1) × 1024, so C_SIZE = 8191; capacity (8191 + 1) × 512 KiB = 4 GiB. For the 64 MiB card, version 1.0: 131072 blocks × 512 = 64 MiB; one combination that gives it is READ_BL_LEN = 9, C_SIZE_MULT = 7 and C_SIZE = 127 ((127 + 1) × 29 × 29 = 67108864 bytes); the driver printed only the product, so which combination QEMU used is not known from this run.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A visitor who misunderstands a request may hesitate. A card executes whatever valid command arrives, at once; there is no "are you sure?" in the protocol.
    • 

  • The phrasebook visitor speaks only when spoken to. A real card also has states the driver cannot see (wear levelling, background erase) that change its timing, and after a power cut its internal mapping may need recovery before it answers.
    • 

  • Asking a visitor for "file 1059840" from a box with four thousand files would get a "no such file". An SD card with a large capacity has that many blocks, so the wrong address is valid and succeeds.
    • 

  • The office counter is one desk. An SoC may have several SD controllers sharing one set of pins, and the pin multiplexer, not the card, decides which counter the visitor stands at.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Ignoring CCS. Byte addresses on a high-capacity card write somewhere else, silently. Read OCR bit 30 after ACMD41 completes and keep it with the card. (Forensic lab.)
    • 

  • Self-tests that only read back through the same driver. They prove consistency, not placement. Check from outside: the image file in QEMU, or a different reader (another OS) on a board.
    • 

  • Checking the CRC of R3. The OCR response has no valid CRC; asking the controller to check it fails every ACMD41.
    • 

  • Forgetting the 8-bit shift of R2 responses in SDHCI. CID and CSD fields come out shifted by one byte. Symptom: absurd sizes or names.
    • 

  • Wrong pin function. "Card NOT inserted" with a card in the slot: check the GPIO function select of the card's pins and which controller owns them (the pin-mux run).
    • 

  • Waits without time-outs. A removed card or a missing clock turns every poll into a hang. Every wait in Listing 1 ends; D9 requires "errors, not a hang".
    • 

  • Writing during probe by default. Make every write test opt-in and confine it to a scratch area outside every partition.
    • 

  • Debugging blind. D5 QEMU's -trace sdhci_send_command (and other sdhci_* and sdcard_* trace points) shows what the controller model received; on hardware, a logic analyser on CMD and CLK shows the same.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F4-35 built the driver model with a pin provider; F4-05 and F4-06 wrote block drivers for virtio and AHCI; OS304 built the block layer and file systems (B14–B16). What carries over: block devices as numbered 512-byte blocks, command/response/data phases, polling with time-outs, verifying data from outside the driver. What changes: the host controller is an SoC block with clocks and pins of its own; the device is a card with a negotiated protocol, two addressing modes and several generations; boot media must be handled with the care of the only copy of the firmware. Summary


    

  • SoC boards boot from SD and eMMC through host controllers that are often SDHCI with vendor quirks; the driver has a controller layer (registers) and a card protocol layer (commands).
    • 

  • SD initialisation is CMD0, CMD8, ACMD41 until ready, CMD2, CMD3, CMD9, CMD7, CMD16; the OCR's CCS bit decides between byte addresses (SDSC) and block numbers (SDHC/SDXC).
    • 

  • CSD versions 1.0 and 2.0 have different size formulas; SDHCI stores 136-bit responses shifted by 8 bits.
    • 

  • The card's pins must be routed to the controller that drives it; a wrong pin function looks like "no card".
    • 

  • A self-test that reads back through the same driver proves consistency, not placement; verify writes from outside.
    • 

  • eMMC shares controllers with SD but initialises differently (CMD1, host-assigned RCA, Extended CSD, CMD6, hardware partitions).
    • 

  • D9's ADMA2, eMMC, B14 registration and acceptance tests remain open in this build.
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Good answers say: the card reports its capacity class (CCS, OCR bit 30) only in the final ACMD41 response, the one with the busy bit 31 set; before that the bit is not valid, and the addressing mode follows from it. Deciding earlier means guessing.
    2. 

  2. OCR 0x80ff8000: bit 31 set, bit 30 clear: SDSC, byte addressing. CMD17's argument for block 100 is 100 × 512 = 51200 (0xc800).
    3. 

  3. (b). Present state bit 16 is "card inserted" when the controller's card-detect input is used. (a) Capabilities describe the controller. (c) The OCR is the card's answer to ACMD41, which needs a card. (d) The CID is the card's identity, also only after it answers.
    4. 

  4. The test sends a CMD24 and a CMD17 per block: 64 commands for 32 blocks. Initialisation: CMD0, CMD8, CMD55, CMD41 (once, if the card is ready at the first try, as in the trace), CMD2, CMD3, CMD9, CMD7, CMD16: 9 commands; plus CMD17 for block 0: 10. 10 + 64 = 74, the number in the trace.
    5. 

  5. The read uses the same wrong address as the write, so it reads back what was written, from the same wrong place. The comparison checks the data, not the location.
    6. 

  6. As written, it fails with "no card". It should honour the devicetree: if the node has cd-gpios (or a property saying the card is not removable, or that the controller cannot detect), read the card-detect GPIO through the GPIO framework, or assume a card and handle a CMD0/CMD8 time-out as "no card".
    7. 

  7. Any three of: CMD1 instead of CMD8/ACMD41 for the operating conditions; the host assigns the RCA with CMD3; the Extended CSD read with CMD8 (a different command than SD's CMD8); CMD6 SWITCH for bus width, speed and partition; sector addressing by the OCR's access mode; hardware partitions (boot areas, RPMB).
    8. 

  8. PCI enumeration on Arm: find the PCIe host's ECAM window in the devicetree (/pcie@10000000's reg), enumerate (DR301), find the SDHCI class device, map its BAR (assigning one if firmware did not), and hand that base address to the same SDHCI code; plus the interrupt mapping if interrupts are used.
    9. 



Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. OCR 0xc0ffff00: bits 31 and 30 set, so the card is SDHC/SDXC and requires block addressing. The branch printed "SDSC (byte addressing)" for it: the label contradicts the value printed on the same line. The good run prints "SDHC/SDXC (block addressing)" for the same OCR.
    2. 

  2. The driver sent 2070 × 512 = 1059840 (0x102c00) as the argument; the SDHC card took it as block 1059840, which is byte 1059840 × 512 = 0x20580000 (about 517.5 MiB into the card). Partition 1 starts at block 8192 (4 MiB), so the data landed inside partition 1, and verify_image.py found all 32 blocks at block number × 512.
    3. 

  3. Reads and writes used the same wrong argument, so each read returned the block it had written at the wrong place. Alternative hypothesis: "a file-system bug corrupted the partition". Ruled out for these 32 blocks: the host check finds exactly the self-test's patterns at block × 512, inside the partition, which only a misaddressed self-test write can produce.
    4. 

  4. Line 135, g_card.block_addressing = (ocr & (1u << 30)) != 0;, was replaced with g_card.block_addressing = false;. Fix: restore the line. How the fault was injected: run.sh makes the branch with sed and runs it on a 4 GiB image.
    5. 

  5. In the driver: keep the OCR with the card and check, at the first transfer, that the addressing mode equals its CCS bit. A range check alone would not catch this case: argument 1059840 is below the card's 8388608 blocks, so the wrong address is valid. Outside: run the self-test on both an SDSC-sized and an SDHC-sized image in CI and verify placement from the host with verify_image.py; this lab's run.sh does both.
    6. 

  6. Run a file-system check (read-only first) on the user's partition, list the files whose clusters overlap the 32 overwritten blocks (the verifier gives their positions), and tell the user which files may be damaged, that the cause is known and fixed, and how to restore them from a backup. Remove the self-test from release builds or keep it opt-in with a scratch area the user chose.
    7.
