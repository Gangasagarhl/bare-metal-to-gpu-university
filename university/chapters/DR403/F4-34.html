Story hook

Two students each pick a school for their teaching placement. Amira picks the one with the new glass building and the best results; it looks wonderful in the brochure. When she arrives, the office cannot give her a timetable, nobody knows which key opens the science lab, and the head says the building plans are "confidential". She spends her six weeks finding out things that should have been written down.

Ben picks an ordinary school in an older building, because its website has the staff handbook, the timetable, the floor plans with every room numbered, and a page called "known problems" that says, for example, "the fire door on the second floor sticks; push hard". On his first day he knows where everything is, and when the fire door sticks, he pushes hard.

Choosing a board for operating-system work is Ben's choice, made on purpose. The fastest SoC with no public manual will teach you the art of guessing. A slower one with a full technical reference manual, an errata list, open boot firmware and a USB recovery mode will teach you to write drivers. This chapter compares the families the curriculum lists, turns the comparison into a procedure you can defend in writing, and ends with the course's exam task: a bring-up plan written from documentation before you write any code.

What you will be able to do

  • Compare the SoC families of curriculum 11.4 (Raspberry Pi, Rockchip, Allwinner, NXP i.MX, TI Sitara and AM6x, Qualcomm, Apple Silicon, RISC-V boards, Amlogic, NVIDIA Jetson) by boot chain, open documentation, open firmware and mainline status, and state the honest scope of each.
  • Assemble the documentation set for one board: architecture manual, core TRM, SoC TRM, datasheet, errata, board schematic, devicetree source, mainline drivers; and judge what is missing.
  • Apply gates (debug console, recovery path, a boot chain that runs your code) before scoring, and explain why a gate must accept only known-good values.
  • Write a bring-up plan for a board from its documentation, in the order of curriculum 11.5, with a reference boot, a recovery drill and a list of open questions.
  • Diagnose a selection tool that recommended a board whose boot chain cannot run your code.
Jargon box
SoC family
Simple words: a group of chips from one vendor that share a design, documentation style and boot method. Analogy: schools run by the same trust, with the same handbook layout. Precise: a vendor's line of SoCs (for example Rockchip RK33xx/RK35xx, NXP i.MX 8M) that share the boot ROM design, IP blocks, vendor tools and the shape of their TRMs and Linux support. C1
Mainline support
Simple words: drivers for a chip that are in the official versions of Linux or U-Boot, not only in a vendor's own copy. Analogy: the school's procedures published in the county handbook, not in a private folder. Precise: support merged into the upstream repositories (Linux, U-Boot, TF-A), maintained and reviewed there; contrasted with vendor ("downstream") trees that may be old, unreviewed or never merged. C1
Binary blob (firmware)
Simple words: a piece of firmware you must use but cannot read the source of. Analogy: a sealed envelope you must deliver without opening. Precise: vendor-supplied executable code without source (for example DRAM training, device-management or security firmware) that is loaded in the boot chain or by a driver; it limits what you can audit, change or fix. C1
Locked (fused) boot chain
Simple words: a board whose chip only runs early boot code signed by the vendor, so you cannot replace it. Analogy: a school where only the trust's own staff may open the building in the morning. Precise: a boot ROM configured by one-time-programmable fuses to verify the first stage's signature against a vendor key; on such devices your code can run only as a payload of the vendor's later stages, if at all. C1
Board schematic
Simple words: the wiring diagram of a board: what is connected to which pin. Analogy: the plumbing and wiring plan of the building. Precise: the circuit diagram of the board, showing the SoC's pin assignments, the debug UART connector and its voltage, boot-mode straps, regulators, and which peripherals are fitted; the board-level complement to the SoC's TRM. C1
Bring-up plan
Simple words: a written list of what you will do, in which order, to make a new board run your code, and what you still need to find out. Analogy: the plan for the first week at a new school, written before the first day. Precise: a document that records the board's documentation set, the console and recovery procedure, the reference boot, the devicetree reading, the bring-up order of curriculum 11.5, the acceptance test for each step and the open questions. C1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

If you want to learn how a machine works by taking it apart, pick one that comes with a good instruction book, and a way to put it back together if you make a mistake. A machine with no book, or one that locks itself if you open it, is a bad first machine, even if it is the fastest. Engineers choose their first board in the same way: by its books and its way back, not by its speed.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Every SoC vendor documents its chips differently. Some publish a full technical reference manual (TRM) with every register; some publish it after a free registration; some circulate partial manuals; some publish nothing, and the community reverse-engineers the hardware. The boot chain varies as much: some families boot entirely from open source (boot ROM excepted), some need a vendor binary for DRAM training, and some only run vendor-signed code. Curriculum 11.4 summarises ten families on exactly these points. C1

For a first bring-up three things are not negotiable. You need a debug console, a UART you can wire to, because before your console driver works nothing else tells you what happened. You need a recovery path: a way to boot a known image when yours fails, such as a removable SD card or a USB recovery mode in the boot ROM. And the boot chain must run your code: either the whole chain is open, or the vendor's chain hands over to a payload you choose. Without any one of these you cannot make progress, however good the rest is. C1

Among boards that pass those three, documentation decides. A full TRM and an errata document turn "the device does nothing" into a question you can answer by reading; mainline Linux and U-Boot support give you working drivers to compare with; open DRAM initialisation means nothing in the chain is a sealed envelope. The lab's tool encodes this as gates followed by a weighted score, prints a ranking and the first steps of a bring-up plan. R1

The forensic lab shows the most dangerous bug such a tool can have: a gate that rejects only the bad values it knows about. A record that spelled "locked" differently passed the gate, and the team bought a board whose boot ROM will run only vendor-signed code.

Three patterns stand out. First, documentation and openness are not the same axis: Raspberry Pi has excellent community documentation and a closed first stage; NXP i.MX has full reference manuals and a DDR PHY blob; TI AM62x has very detailed TRMs and a binary device-management firmware. Second, the families where you cannot replace the boot chain (Qualcomm, and Apple except through the owner's permissive mode) are still usable for OS work, but only as a payload of the vendor's chain, which removes the boot-chain chapters of this course from what you can practise. Third, recovery paths differ more than people expect, from "rewrite the SD card" to USB protocols in the ROM to "none without the vendor's tool". C1

Reading a documentation set

For a candidate board, collect the seven layers of Figure 1 and ask of each one a question you can answer yes or no.

  • SoC TRM. Does it give the full address map, the clock tree with every gate and divider, the reset controller's lines, the pin multiplexer's function tables, and a register chapter for each peripheral you need first (UART, timer, interrupt controller, GPIO, SD/eMMC)? A TRM that describes registers without the clock and reset chapters leaves you with exactly the "device reads all zeros" problem of F4-35.
  • Errata. Is there a published errata document for the SoC and for its cores? Curriculum 11.5 says to read it before chasing "impossible" bugs; a vendor that publishes none has not necessarily fixed everything. C1
  • Board schematic. Does it show the debug UART connector, its signal voltage, the boot-mode straps and which peripherals are fitted? Without it you are guessing which pins to probe.
  • Devicetree source. Is there one in mainline Linux or U-Boot for this exact board, not only for the SoC's evaluation kit? It is the plan your kernel will read (F4-31) and the most compact summary of the board.
  • Mainline drivers. For each block you will write a driver for, is there a mainline driver to read? Its quirks list is often the only record of hardware bugs.
  • Boot chain. Are the boot ROM's image format and recovery mode documented (by the vendor or U-Boot's tools)? Is DRAM initialisation open? Is the secure-boot state of retail boards known (fused or open)?

Gates before scores: and why gates must allow, not deny

A weighted score lets strengths cancel weaknesses. That is right for TRM quality versus mainline support, and wrong for the three gates: no amount of documentation compensates for a boot chain that will not run your code. So the tool evaluates gates first and ranks every board that fails a gate below every board that passes, printing the reason. R1

How a gate is written matters as much as having one. A gate can list the values that pass ("allow list") or the values that fail ("deny list"). Board records are written by people, from documents, in words: "locked", "signed", "fused", "locked-signed", "vendor-only", "secure boot enabled on retail units". A deny list rejects only the spellings its author thought of; every new spelling passes. An allow list passes only "open" and "payload", values the team has defined, and sends everything else, including typos and "unknown", to the "fail" side where a human looks at it. The forensic lab is this difference, in one line of code. R3 R4

The weights themselves are a judgement, and the code says so. This course weights the TRM most (30 for a full manual, 15 for a partial one), then the boot chain (20 open, 10 payload), then mainline Linux (15, or 8 if partial), mainline U-Boot (10), the recovery path (10 for USB, 8 for SD), and smaller weights for errata, open DRAM initialisation and the UART. The mini-project asks you to argue for different weights for a different purpose (for example, a robot that must use a camera from day one). R1

The bring-up plan, step by step

Curriculum 11.5 extends the platform checklist of section 9.1 with eight items, which the tool prints in condensed form for the winning board and which exam P asks you to write out for a board you have not seen. C1

  1. Serial console first. Find the debug UART pins (schematic), use a 3.3 V USB-to-serial adapter, never 5 V, and confirm the boot ROM's or U-Boot's output before anything else.
  2. Recovery path. Know how to recover from a bad image (SD card swap, FEL, mask-ROM mode, uuu) and practise it once before writing anything to on-board storage.
  3. A known-good stack. Boot the vendor's or mainline U-Boot and Linux; save its devicetree and kernel log. This is your reference for every later question ("what clock rate does Linux set for this UART?").
  4. Read the devicetree top to bottom: memory, CPUs, interrupt controller, timer, UART, clocks, resets, pinctrl, regulators, then devices.
  5. Bring up in order: memory map and MMU, interrupt controller, timer, UART driver, then GPIO with a heartbeat LED (trusting firmware-enabled clocks at first), then storage, USB, Ethernet, display last.
  6. Clocks and resets only when a device needs one the firmware left off, each recorded in the design log.
  7. DMA: check every device for coherency (dma-coherent) and handle caches accordingly.
  8. Errata: read them before chasing "impossible" bugs.

A good plan adds what the checklist cannot know: the acceptance test for each step (for the console: "U-Boot's banner at 115200 baud", for the timer: "a 10 ms busy wait measured against the counter", as F4-35's kernel prints), the documents each step depends on, and the open questions the documentation did not answer, each with how you will answer it.

How the hardware actually does it

A TRM is the vendor's integration document. The CPU cores, the interconnect and many peripherals are licensed IP whose own manuals the SoC vendor may or may not pass on; the TRM describes how they were wired: base addresses, which clock and reset line feeds each block, which pins each function can use, which interrupt number each raises. That integration information is what the devicetree encodes for your kernel, and it is exactly what reverse engineering must recover when no TRM exists. C1

Electrically, three board facts decide whether your first hour goes well: the debug UART's signal level (connecting a 5 V adapter to a 3.3 V or 1.8 V SoC pin can damage it), the boot-mode straps (which medium the ROM tries first, and how to force recovery), and the power supply the board needs. All three are in the schematic and the board maker's documentation, not in the SoC's TRM.

Safety

Use a 3.3 V USB-to-serial adapter for the debug UART (never 5 V), and check the schematic for boards whose UART runs at 1.8 V, which need a matching adapter. Connect ground first, and only TX, RX and ground: never connect the adapter's power pin to a board that has its own supply. Use the power supply the board maker specifies. Practise the recovery path before writing to on-board storage. See the university safety rules.

Worked example

Question. Score the four fictional boards of the lab's input by hand, decide which pass the gates, and check against the tool.

Board P. Gates: UART yes, recovery USB, boot chain open: pass. Score: TRM full 30 + errata 5 + boot chain open 20 + DRAM init "blob" 0 + mainline Linux 15 + U-Boot 10 + recovery USB 10 + UART 5 = 95.

Board Q. Gates pass (UART yes, SD, open). Score: TRM partial 15 + errata "no" 0 + open 20 + DRAM open 5 + Linux partial 8 + U-Boot 10 + SD 8 + UART 5 = 71.

Board R. Boot chain "locked": not in the allow list, gate fails. Score: 30 + 5 + 0 + 0 (no DRAM field) + 15 + 0 (no U-Boot field) + 10 + 5 = 65. It has the second-best documentation of the four and is out.

Board S. UART "no": gate fails. Score: 0 + 0 + payload 10 + 0 + partial 8 + 0 + SD 8 + 0 = 26.

Ranking. P, Q (pass); then R, S (fail), each with its reason. The tool agrees line for line and prints P's plan with its UART note. R1



What the numbers do not say. Q's lower score comes mostly from a partial TRM and partial mainline Linux; if your project needs only the UART, timer and SD card, and Q's TRM covers those chapters, Q may be the better choice. The score orders candidates for a closer look; the plan you write for the top two is the decision.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A school's paperwork is about one building. An SoC's TRM serves many boards, and the board maker's schematic and devicetree decide what you can use; two boards with the same SoC can differ in UART pins, boot media and recovery buttons.
    • 

  • Ben could ask a teacher when the handbook was unclear. Many SoC vendors offer no public support; the "teachers" are the mainline driver code and the community, and their answers are code, not explanations.
    • 

  • A school's "known problems" page is written by the school. Errata are written by the vendor, about its own silicon, and list only what it has found and chosen to publish.
    • 

  • Choosing a school is a one-time decision. A board choice keeps costing or paying for months: every driver you write either has a TRM chapter behind it or a week of experiments.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Choosing by performance. The fastest SoC is rarely the best documented. For a first bring-up the bottleneck is how fast you learn, not how fast the board runs.
    • 

  • Trusting the SoC's documentation for a board question. UART pins, boot straps and fitted parts are in the schematic. Ask for it before buying.
    • 

  • Deny-list gates. A gate that rejects "locked" lets "locked-signed" through. Write gates as allow lists and send everything else to review (forensic lab).
    • 

  • Treating "unknown" as "probably fine". The tool scores unknown fields as zero and fails unknown gate values. If a fact matters, find it out before buying.
    • 

  • Skipping the recovery drill. The day you need recovery is the day your image broke the board's boot; practise while it still works.
    • 

  • Planning without a reference boot. Without a known-good devicetree and kernel log, every "does it work?" question has no comparison point.
    •
