Story hook

The school keeps one attendance sheet. Whoever is writing on it holds it; anyone else who needs it waits by the desk. Mostly this is fine: writing a name takes seconds. To be fair, the office hands out numbered tickets, and the sheet goes to ticket 41, then 42, then 43.

One afternoon Dara is filling in the sheet when the bell rings. The rule says: when the bell rings, whoever is nearest must stop what they are doing and record the bell on the attendance sheet. Dara is nearest. Dara stops, turns to the desk to record the bell, and waits for the sheet. The sheet is in Dara's other hand, and that hand will not move until the bell has been recorded. Nobody else can help; Dara will wait until the caretaker comes at night.

That is the forensic case of this chapter, found in a real kernel log from this build. The chapter builds the locks that prevent it: spinlocks that know about interrupts, sleeping mutexes, semaphores, condition variables, and two detectives that catch mistakes early: a lock-order checker and a lockup detector.

What you will be able to do

  • Build a ticket spinlock on C++ atomics with documented memory orders, and say exactly when it must disable interrupts.
  • Build a mutex, a semaphore and a condition variable that sleep on F3-26's wait queues without losing wake-ups.
  • Detect lock-order inversions before they deadlock, and mutexes taken in interrupt handlers, with a small debug checker.
  • Detect a real deadlock with a time-based lockup detector and print who holds and who waits for what on every CPU.
  • Prove the B10 acceptance tests in QEMU: 10 million items through a bounded buffer with no loss or duplicate, an inverted pair reported, a mutex in an interrupt handler reported.
  • Diagnose a single-CPU lockup from a lockup report and addr2line.
Jargon box
Spinlock
Simple words: a lock you wait for by checking again and again. Analogy: standing by the desk until the sheet is free. Precise: a lock whose waiters busy-wait on a memory location; correct only for short critical sections that never sleep; in a kernel, combined with disabling preemption and, where needed, interrupts. D3
Ticket lock
Simple words: a spinlock that serves waiters in arrival order. Analogy: numbered tickets at the office desk. Precise: a spinlock with two counters: next (atomically incremented to take a ticket) and serving (incremented on release); a CPU enters when serving equals its ticket, so the lock is first-come, first-served. D3 R2
IRQ-safe lock
Simple words: a lock taken with interrupts switched off on this CPU, because an interrupt handler also takes it. Analogy: the bell may not ring while you hold the sheet. Precise: lock_irqsave() saves RFLAGS and executes cli before taking the lock; unlock_irqrestore() releases it and restores the saved interrupt flag. Any lock that an interrupt handler takes must be taken this way everywhere else. D3 R3
Preemption count
Simple words: a counter of spinlocks held by this CPU; while it is above zero the scheduler must not switch threads. Analogy: "do not move the student while they hold the sheet". Precise: Cpu::preempt_count, incremented by Spinlock::lock and decremented by unlock; trap_dispatch preempts only when it is zero (F3-26). R2
Mutex
Simple words: a lock you wait for by going to sleep. Analogy: take a ticket, sit down, and be called. Precise: a mutual-exclusion lock with an owner; a thread that finds it held sleeps on a wait queue; may be held across blocking operations; never taken in interrupt context. D1
Adaptive spinning
Simple words: waiting a moment before going to sleep, if the holder is busy on another CPU. Analogy: if the person with the sheet is writing right now, waiting a few seconds is cheaper than sitting down. Precise: before sleeping, Mutex::lock spins up to 2000 iterations while the owner thread is in state Running; sleeping and waking cost a switch each. R2
Semaphore
Simple words: a counter of available "permits"; taking one waits if none are left. Analogy: a box with N hall passes. Precise: an integer with down (P: wait until positive, then decrement) and up (V: increment and wake one waiter), after Dijkstra. D1
Condition variable
Simple words: a place to sleep until another thread says "something changed". Analogy: the "call me when the book comes back" list at the library desk. Precise: an object with wait(m) (atomically release mutex m and sleep; re-acquire before returning), signal and broadcast; the waiter re-checks its condition in a loop. D1 D4
Lost wake-up
Simple words: a "wake up" that arrives just before the sleeper goes to sleep, so it sleeps forever. Analogy: being called while you were still walking to your seat. Precise: a race between checking a condition and sleeping on it; prevented here by enqueuing the thread on the wait queue before releasing the lock that protects the condition, with interrupts off. D1
Lock-order inversion
Simple words: one code path takes lock A then B, another takes B then A. Analogy: two students each holding a book the other needs. Precise: a cycle in the graph whose edges are "lock X was held while lock Y was acquired"; a necessary condition for an ABBA deadlock, detectable even when no deadlock happened. D3
Lock-order checker
Simple words: debug code that remembers which locks were taken while others were held and complains about the reverse order. Analogy: a hall monitor who writes down who held which book first. Precise: here lockdep_check_order: per thread, the lock classes held; globally, a table before[a][b]; the first acquisition of a while holding b after before[a][b] was set is reported. Linux's "lockdep" is a far larger version of the same idea. D5
Lockup detector
Simple words: a timer inside the spin loop that gives up after too long and prints who holds what. Analogy: the caretaker at night. Precise: Spinlock::lock measures its wait with the TSC and calls lockup() after 2000 ms; the first CPU to report prints every CPU's thread, interrupt depth, awaited lock and held locks, then panics. R3

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Some things in school exist only once, like the attendance sheet. Only one person may write on it at a time. Sometimes you wait standing up because it will be quick (a spinlock); sometimes you take a ticket and sit down until you are called (a mutex). A box of hall passes lets five students out at once, not more (a semaphore). And two students who each hold a book the other one needs will wait for ever unless everyone agrees to pick up books in alphabetical order.

When two threads change the same thing at the same time, the result can be garbage. A lock makes them take turns. The hard parts are choosing how to wait, and making sure nobody ends up waiting for ever.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

SP203 built these tools in user space with std::mutex and std::condition_variable (F2-36, F2-37). The kernel cannot use them: they are implemented on top of system calls into a kernel, and this is the kernel. It builds its own, from two ingredients: atomic instructions and F3-26's ability to make a thread sleep and wake.

Spinlock. The lab's spinlock is a ticket lock: lock() takes a ticket with an atomic fetch_add on next_ and spins until serving_ shows its number; unlock() adds one to serving_. It never sleeps, so it is the only lock an interrupt handler may take, and it must only protect a few instructions. While a CPU holds a spinlock, preempt_count is above zero, so F3-26's scheduler will not switch the holder away.

Interrupts. If an interrupt handler on CPU 0 takes a spinlock that the thread it interrupted already holds, CPU 0 spins on a lock that only CPU 0 can release: Figure 2. So the rule is: a lock that any interrupt handler takes is always taken elsewhere with lock_irqsave(), which disables interrupts on this CPU first and restores the previous state afterwards. Inside a handler interrupts are already off (the CPU cleared IF when it entered through an interrupt gate), so the handler itself can use plain lock().

Mutex. A mutex records its owner thread. If it is taken, the caller sleeps on the mutex's wait queue; unlock() clears the owner and wakes one waiter, who loops back and tries again. A mutex may be held across long operations, but because it can sleep it must never be taken in an interrupt handler; the lab's Mutex::lock checks irq_depth and reports such a call as a bug (a B10 acceptance test) instead of executing it.

Semaphore and condition variable. A semaphore is a counter with down (wait until positive, then decrement) and up (increment, wake one). A condition variable lets a thread wait for a condition protected by a mutex: wait(m) releases m and sleeps in one step, then re-acquires m. The waiter always re-checks its condition in a while loop, because another thread may have changed it again before the waiter ran.

The bounded buffer. The B10 test passes 10 million numbers from producers to consumers through a 64-slot ring protected by one mutex, with two condition variables (not_full, not_empty). Consumers mark every number in a 10-million-bit bitmap; a bit set twice is a duplicate, a bit never set is a lost item. On one CPU the run passed with no loss and no duplicate R2; F3-28 runs the same code on 8 CPUs.

Detectives. Two kinds of bugs are hard to find by testing: deadlocks that need unlucky timing, and lock misuse that happens to work. The lock-order checker catches the first kind from a single, harmless run: it records "B was taken while A was held" and reports the first time "A is taken while B is held". The lockup detector catches actual deadlocks: a spinner that has waited 2 seconds prints a report of every CPU and panics.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Memory orders in the ticket lock

Taking a ticket is a fetch_add with relaxed order: it only has to be atomic. The load that lets a CPU in (serving_ == my) uses acquire order, and the store that lets the next CPU in uses release order (sync.cc lines 60, 63, 81). Together they make every write inside the previous holder's critical section visible to the next holder before it reads anything protected by the lock D2. On x86 ordinary loads and stores already have these properties at the hardware level, so the orders mainly stop the compiler from moving protected accesses out of the critical section; on the weakly ordered CPUs of DR402 they also produce fences. The C++ orders are what the code promises, independent of the CPU.

The release uses serving_.load(relaxed) + 1 followed by a store, not a fetch_add: only the holder ever writes serving_, so no other CPU can race with it. A ticket lock with 32-bit counters wraps after 232 acquisitions; the comparison is equality, so wrapping is harmless as long as fewer than 232 CPUs wait at once.

Why tickets, and what they cost

A simple test-and-set lock lets whichever CPU wins the race in; under contention one CPU can lose for a long time. Tickets make the order fair. The price is that every waiter spins on the same cache line, which every release invalidates; McKenney discusses queued locks (MCS and the Linux qspinlock) that give each waiter its own line D3. Fairness also has a sharp edge in a virtual machine: if the vCPU holding ticket n+1 is descheduled by the host, everyone behind it waits even after the lock is free. That is one reason the lab's runs under TCG are slow under contention.

Sleeping without losing a wake-up

The dangerous moment of a sleeping lock is between "I see the lock is taken" and "I am asleep". If the owner unlocks in between and wakes nobody (because the sleeper is not yet on the queue), the sleeper sleeps forever. The lab closes the gap in WaitQueue::sleep (thread.cc lines 340–355): the caller holds the guard spinlock of the mutex with interrupts off; sleep takes the scheduler lock, puts the thread on the queue and marks it Blocked, and only then releases the guard. A waker must take the guard to see the state and then the scheduler lock to wake; by then the sleeper is on the queue. CondVar::wait applies the same rule one level up: it takes its own guard before releasing the mutex, so a signal cannot slip in between (sync.cc lines 146–153).

Adaptive spinning, and why it was needed

The first version of the mutex went to sleep immediately. On one CPU that is ideal. On 8 emulated CPUs (F3-28) the 10-million-item run became so slow that it looked hung: every handover of the buffer mutex cost a sleep, a wake-up IPI and a switch on both sides. The lab therefore spins up to 2000 iterations first, but only while the owner is running on some CPU (sync.cc lines 99–107); if the owner is asleep or preempted, spinning is pointless and the caller sleeps at once. Linux and Solaris mutexes use the same heuristic D3. The 2000 is a guess tuned on this emulator, not a measured optimum.

The lock-order checker in detail

Locks are grouped into classes by name: every lock named "account:alice" is one class. Each thread keeps the classes it holds (Thread::held, at most 8). Before waiting for a lock of class b, lockdep_check_order looks at every held class a: if the table says a was earlier taken while b was held (before[b][a]), it prints both call sites; then it records before[a][b]. The check runs before spinning, so an inversion is reported even in the run that is about to deadlock (you will see this in F3-28). The checker finds only two-lock cycles, and it ignores a lock taken while a lock of the same class is held (a == b) — which is exactly the shape of this chapter's forensic case, so that case is caught by the lockup detector instead. The scheduler lock is not checked at all, because it is released by a different thread (F3-26).

The lockup detector

Each spin loop reads the TSC; after 2000 ms worth of cycles (calibrated against the PIT at boot) it calls lockup(). Several CPUs may time out at once; an atomic flag lets only the first report, while the others halt, so the log is readable. The report switches the console to panic mode (it must not wait for the console lock, which a stuck CPU might hold) and prints, for every online CPU, the current thread, its interrupt depth, the lock it is spinning on and the classes it holds. Those four columns are usually enough to name the bug.

What was and was not reproduced

The single-CPU B10 test ran at the curriculum's full size, 10 million items, in a little over 2000 ticks of guest time R2. The re-run "after B11 on 4 and 8 CPUs" uses smaller sizes by default in F3-28 and is explained there. Read-copy-update (RCU) is named in the curriculum as a topic C1; this chapter does not implement it.

How the hardware actually does it

An atomic fetch_add on x86-64 compiles to a lock xadd: the CPU performs the read, the addition and the write as one indivisible operation with respect to every other CPU, using the cache-coherence protocol to own the cache line for the duration D6. The spin loop calls cpu_relax(), the pause instruction, which tells the core it is in a spin-wait loop (saving power and, on hyper-threaded cores, giving the sibling thread resources). cli and sti clear and set RFLAGS.IF on this CPU only: disabling interrupts never protects against another CPU, which is why kernel code needs both the lock and, where an interrupt handler is involved, cli. The lockup detector reads the time-stamp counter with rdtsc.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Written from memory, not checked in this build: that std::atomic::fetch_add compiles to lock xadd with g++ 13 for x86-64 (check with objdump -d on kernel.elf, which this build did not record); x86-TSO ordering ("loads are not reordered with older loads, stores not with older stores", the store-buffer exception) as described in the Intel SDM Vol. 3 "Memory Ordering" section; the effect of pause; RFLAGS.IF being bit 9 and the pushfq-based save and the conditional sti restore in kbase.h; the TSC being constant-rate on the emulated CPU. The QEMU runs show only that the code works on QEMU 8.2.2's TCG model.

Worked example

Ticket arithmetic. A lock starts with next = serving = 0. CPU 1 calls lock(): fetch_add returns 0 and sets next = 1; serving == 0, so CPU 1 enters at once. CPU 2 arrives: ticket 1, next = 2, spins. CPU 3 arrives: ticket 2, next = 3, spins. CPU 1 unlocks: serving = 1; CPU 2 enters. CPU 2 unlocks: serving = 2; CPU 3 enters. At any moment, next − serving is the number of CPUs inside or waiting: here at most 3, then 2, then 1, then 0 after CPU 3 unlocks.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nobody waits politely. A spinning CPU does not stand still: it reads the lock's memory as fast as it can, and that traffic slows the holder down. A waiting student costs nothing.
    • 

  • The bell is not in the same person. In school, the person who rings the bell is somebody else. On a CPU the interrupt handler runs on the same CPU, in the middle of the interrupted thread; that is why a single CPU can deadlock with itself.
    • 

  • Ticket order is not "fair" in time. First-come, first-served at the lock says nothing about which thread gets the CPU; a ticket holder that is preempted or whose virtual CPU is descheduled blocks everyone behind it.
    • 

  • "Alphabetical order" must be global and enforced. In school a rule on the wall is enough; in a kernel the order must hold across all code paths, written by different people, which is why a checker is needed.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Plain lock() on a lock shared with an interrupt handler. Works almost always and deadlocks one CPU when the interrupt hits inside the critical section. Rule: if any handler takes it, every other user takes it with lock_irqsave().
    • 

  • Restoring "interrupts on" instead of the saved state. unlock_irqrestore restores what was there; code that blindly executes sti enables interrupts inside a caller that had them off.
    • 

  • Sleeping with a spinlock held. Calling Mutex::lock, a semaphore down or thread_sleep_ticks inside a spinlock section switches away with preempt_count > 0. Other CPUs spin until a lockup report.
    • 

  • if instead of while around a condition wait. After waking, the condition may be false again (another consumer took the item). Always loop.
    • 

  • Stop markers at the wrong time. The first version of the test let each producer put a stop marker when it finished; consumers then stopped while other producers still had items, and items were "lost". The bitmap found it.
    • 

  • Debugging method. Read a lockup report column by column: which lock is each CPU waiting for, which locks does it hold; draw the arrows "waits for → held by"; a cycle is a deadlock, an arrow from a CPU to itself is an interrupt or recursion problem. Then addr2line the two addresses in the report.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F3-26 gave the kernel threads, wait queues and a preemptive scheduler on one CPU. What carries over: WaitQueue, preempt_count, the scheduler lock and the timer hook; SP203's memory model and condition-variable discipline. What changes: shared kernel data is now protected by named locks of the right kind; misuse is reported (inversions, mutexes in handlers) and real deadlocks end in a readable lockup report rather than a silent hang. What comes next: F3-28 starts the other CPUs. The same locks now face true parallelism: the producer/consumer test runs on 8 CPUs, the TLB must be shot down on every CPU, and an ABBA bug that never fires on one CPU deadlocks on eight.

Mini-project


Interrupt-safety tracking. Extend the checker so it learns, per lock class, two facts: "taken in an interrupt handler" (irq_depth > 0) and "taken with interrupts enabled". As soon as a class has both, print a report naming both call sites, without any deadlock having to happen. (Linux's lockdep tracks this as "IRQ-safe" and "IRQ-unsafe" usage D5; design yours from the description above, not from its source.)


Rubric (10 points). Both facts recorded per class with call sites (3); the forensic kernel reports the problem within the first second, before the lockup (3); the correct sync run reports nothing (2); a short note on false positives, for example a lock that is only taken with interrupts enabled before any handler is installed (2).



Summary


    

  • A ticket spinlock takes a ticket with an atomic add and enters when serving reaches it: fair, short, never sleeps. Acquire on entry, release on exit.
    • 

  • Any lock an interrupt handler takes must be taken elsewhere with interrupts disabled (lock_irqsave), or one CPU deadlocks with itself.
    • 

  • Mutexes, semaphores and condition variables sleep on wait queues; the thread is queued before the protecting lock is released, so no wake-up is lost. Waiters re-check in a loop.
    • 

  • Adaptive spinning (spin while the owner runs) made the mutex usable under emulated SMP.
    • 

  • A lock-order checker reports A→B / B→A from a single harmless run; a lockup detector turns real deadlocks into a per-CPU report.
    • 

  • All three B10 acceptance tests passed on one emulated CPU, at full size; the forensic deadlock was reproduced and located. Nothing was tested on hardware.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. next − serving = 3: one CPU inside (ticket 14) and two waiting (tickets 15 and 16).
    2. 

  2. Taking a ticket only needs atomicity: no data is protected yet, so no ordering is needed. The load of serving_ that lets a CPU in must be acquire so that its reads of protected data cannot be moved before it (by the compiler or, on weak CPUs, the hardware); it pairs with the release store in unlock.
    3. 

  3. Good answers say: the interrupt handler runs on the same CPU as the code it interrupted; if the interrupted code holds the lock, the handler spins, and the holder cannot run until the handler returns. Rule: a lock that a handler takes is taken everywhere else with interrupts disabled.
    4. 

  4. A spinlock never sleeps. A mutex may put the caller to sleep, and an interrupt handler has no thread of its own to put to sleep: it borrowed the interrupted thread's stack, so sleeping would block an unrelated thread inside a half-finished handler. The lab reports such a call as a bug.
    5. 

  5. A waker could take the guard in the gap, see the mutex free or the waiter missing, and wake nobody; the sleeper then queues itself and sleeps although the event already happened: a lost wake-up.
    6. 

  6. If the owner is not running (asleep, preempted, or waiting), it cannot release the mutex soon; spinning only wastes this CPU. Spinning pays only while the owner is making progress on another CPU.
    7. 

  7. Legitimate: many objects of one class (two accounts, two inodes) may be locked together in a well-defined order the checker cannot see from names alone, so reporting every same-class nesting would flood the log. Hidden bug: taking the same lock twice (recursion, or an interrupt on the same CPU, as in the forensic case), and ABBA between two objects of one class.
    8. 

  8. The handler uses rxq.lock() (interrupts are already off); the thread uses rxq.lock_irqsave()/unlock_irqrestore(). With plain lock() in the thread, a report like Evidence 1: one CPU running the driver thread, irq depth 1, waiting for 'rxq', holding 'rxq'.
    9. 

  9. A producer that finished early put its stop markers while other producers were still putting items; a consumer that took a marker stopped, and if all consumers stopped, the remaining items were never consumed (and producers blocked on a full buffer). The last producer to finish knows that every item is already in the buffer before any marker, and FIFO order delivers the markers after them.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. One CPU (cpu0). It runs thread 'stats_worker' at interrupt depth 1, i.e. inside an interrupt handler that interrupted that thread. It has spun 2000 ms on 'stats'; the last recorded owner is cpu0 itself. addr2line: the spinning call site is in stats_hook(), test_sync.cc:241 (the line after stats_lock.lock(): the address is the call's return address); the owner's call site is stats_worker, line 248.


Hypotheses. Another CPU holding the lock: excluded, there is one CPU and the owner is cpu0. A missing unlock in the worker: would show depth 0 and the worker's own call site as the spinner. A same-CPU interrupt re-entry: fits depth 1, owner = spinner's CPU, two different call sites (thread and hook).


Root cause. stats_worker takes stats_lock with plain lock(), so interrupts stay enabled inside the critical section. The timer interrupt arrives on the same CPU, the timer hook stats_hook calls stats_lock.lock() and spins. The owner is the interrupted thread, which can only continue after the handler returns: the CPU waits for itself. The "holds: 'stats'" column is the interrupted thread's held list, shown because the handler runs on that thread's stack.


Why the quick test passed. The bug needs a timer interrupt to land inside the worker's critical section. The worker spends most of its loop inside it (2000 increments under the lock, a few instructions outside), so here it fired within the first second (the log has no "still alive" line); with a truly tiny critical section it fires rarely and randomly, which is worse.


Why the checker stayed silent. Both acquisitions are of the same class 'stats'; the checker skips same-class pairs (Layer 3), and nothing about the order of two different locks is wrong.


Fix. Take stats_lock with lock_irqsave() in the worker, as the correct counter test does (lines 218–220); or make the hook not take a lock at all (a per-CPU counter or an atomic increment).


Prevention. The mini-project's interrupt-safety tracking reports "taken in a handler and taken with interrupts enabled" at the first occurrence, without needing the unlucky timing; the lab step 4 "same lock already held by this CPU" check reports the deadlock immediately instead of after 2 s.
