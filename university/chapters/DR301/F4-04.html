Story hook

The school has an old clock in the entrance hall. It keeps running at night on its own small battery, so in the morning it already shows the right time. But it is a strange clock. Its numbers are written in a code the caretaker chose years ago, the year is split across two cards with the century hidden in a drawer, and once a second all its cards flip at the same time. If you copy the time card by card while they flip, you can write down 23:00:00 at one minute to midnight, half of the old time and half of the new.

The principal does not want to walk to the hall every time someone asks the time. So once, carefully, she reads the clock at the exact moment the seconds card flips, writes "at this tick of my own stopwatch it was exactly 08:15:00", and from then on answers every question from her stopwatch. This chapter is that careful reading. You will write a driver for the PC's CMOS real-time clock, decode its formats, find the century through ACPI, read it without tearing, and join it to the kernel's monotonic clock. Then you will compare the result with the host's clock and with a date you choose.

What you will be able to do

  • Read the CMOS RTC through its index and data ports and decode its status registers.
  • Decode BCD and binary values and 12-hour and 24-hour modes from the format bits, and find the century register through the ACPI FADT.
  • Read a consistent snapshot of registers that the hardware updates on its own, using the update-in-progress flag and a double read.
  • Convert a civil date to Unix time with an algorithm you can prove correct, and test it against C++20's <chrono> over three centuries.
  • Join wall-clock time to a monotonic clock at a second boundary, and explain why the kernel should not read the RTC for every timestamp.
  • Diagnose a date that is right on some days and absurd on others.
Jargon box
Real-time clock (RTC)
Simple words: a small clock chip that keeps the date and time even when the computer is off. Analogy: the battery clock in the entrance hall. Precise: a battery-backed counter of seconds, minutes, hours, day, month and year, on PCs compatible with the Motorola MC146818 and reached through CMOS index port 0x70 and data port 0x71. D1
Wall-clock time
Simple words: the time people read on a clock: date and time of day. Analogy: what the hall clock shows. Precise: a time scale tied to civil time (here UTC), which can be set and can jump; contrasted with a monotonic clock, which only moves forward at a steady rate from an arbitrary start. D4
Monotonic clock
Simple words: a counter that only goes up, used to measure intervals. Analogy: the principal's stopwatch. Precise: a clock that never goes backwards and is not affected by setting the date; in the lab kernel, the count of PIT interrupts since boot (F4-03). D4
BCD (binary-coded decimal)
Simple words: writing each decimal digit in its own 4 bits. Analogy: the clock's private code: one card per digit. Precise: an encoding in which a byte holds two decimal digits, the tens in bits 7–4 and the units in bits 3–0, so 45 is stored as 0x45; value = (byte >> 4) × 10 + (byte & 0xF). D1
Update-in-progress (UIP)
Simple words: a flag that warns the clock is about to change its numbers. Analogy: a light that comes on just before the cards flip. Precise: bit 7 of RTC status register A; while it is set, an update cycle is imminent or running and the time registers may be inconsistent. D1
Unix time
Simple words: the number of seconds since the start of 1970 (UTC), ignoring leap seconds. Analogy: the school counting every second since its founding day, instead of using a calendar. Precise: seconds since 1970-01-01T00:00:00Z computed as days × 86400 + seconds of the day, where every day has exactly 86400 seconds. D4
FADT century field
Simple words: a number in an ACPI table saying which CMOS byte holds the century. Analogy: the note saying which drawer holds the century card. Precise: a one-byte field of the Fixed ACPI Description Table (signature "FACP") giving the CMOS RAM index of the century register; 0 means not supported. D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A computer has a tiny clock with its own battery, so it knows the date when you switch it on. The clock writes its numbers in a slightly odd way, and it changes all its numbers at once every second. If you read it right at that moment, you can get a mix of two times. So the computer waits for a calm moment, reads twice, and keeps the answer only if both reads agree. After that it uses its own fast stopwatch and only looks at the clock again now and then.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The PC's RTC lives in a small block of battery-backed memory, the CMOS RAM, reached through two I/O ports: write a register number to port 0x70, then read or write its value at port 0x71. Registers 0x00, 0x02 and 0x04 hold seconds, minutes and hours; 0x07, 0x08 and 0x09 hold day of month, month and the last two digits of the year. Status register A (0x0A) contains the update-in-progress flag; status register B (0x0B) says how the time is stored: bit 2 set means binary, clear means BCD; bit 1 set means 24-hour, clear means 12-hour with bit 7 of the hours register meaning PM. D1 D3

The year register has two digits only. Where the century is kept is not fixed by the old chip: the ACPI FADT names a CMOS register for it, and the kernel reads that register too. If the FADT says 0, there is no century register and the kernel has to assume one. D2

Because the chip updates its registers once a second, a driver that reads six registers one after another can be interrupted by the update half-way. The safe method is to wait until the update flag is clear, read everything, and repeat until two complete readings are identical. D1

Finally, the decoded date is converted to Unix time, which is what the rest of the kernel stores and compares. The RTC is slow to read (each register is two port accesses) and has whole seconds only, so the kernel reads it once at boot, ties it to its monotonic clock, and computes the wall time from ticks afterwards. Milestone C3 asks for exactly that: an RTC driver, Unix-time conversion, and the link to the monotonic clock of B8.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Registers, formats and the century

The format bits in register B are set by firmware and the kernel should honour them rather than change them; changing the format of a running clock that the firmware or another operating system also uses invites confusion. The decoder therefore handles all four combinations (Listing 1). Two details deserve care. In 12-hour mode the hours register holds 1–12 with bit 7 as PM; midnight is 12 AM (hour 0) and noon is 12 PM (hour 12), so the conversion is "12 becomes 0, then add 12 if PM". And bit 7 must be masked off before BCD conversion, otherwise 8 PM in BCD (0x88) would decode as 88. The host test runs the same instant through all four encodings and checks they agree (R1). D1 R1

The century register is a convention: it is not part of the original chip's clock registers but a byte of CMOS RAM that firmware maintains. Its index comes from the FADT; on QEMU's pc machine the lab read 0x32 (R3). The FADT field's offset is 108 in the lab's code; that number, like the table's signature "FACP", is written from memory of the ACPI Specification and is confirmed only by QEMU producing a plausible value and a correct date. D2 R3

Reading without tearing

The MC146818 sets UIP shortly before it starts the once-a-second update, so a read that begins while UIP is clear has a guaranteed window to finish before the registers change. The lab's driver goes further: it reads two full snapshots, each after waiting for UIP to clear, and accepts only if they are equal byte for byte, counting the retries. This handles slow readers (an interrupt between two register reads can take longer than the guaranteed window) and does not depend on the exact length of the window. An alternative is the update-ended interrupt: set bit 4 of register B and read the registers in the interrupt handler on line 8, which comes right after an update. D1 D3

Watch out

Port 0x70 is shared with another function: on PC hardware its bit 7 controls whether non-maskable interrupts are delivered. A driver that writes a register index with bit 7 set disables NMIs as a side effect. The lab always writes indices below 0x80. Also, every access to the RTC is an index write followed by a data access; if an interrupt handler also uses CMOS between the two, the index is lost. The lab kernel does not touch CMOS in interrupts; a real kernel takes a lock (with interrupts off) around the pair. D3

Not verified — check before relying on this

From memory and confirmed only by QEMU 8.2.2's mc146818rtc model and the host test: the register numbers 0x00–0x09, 0x0A and 0x0B, the meaning of register B bits 1, 2 and 4, PM in hours bit 7, UIP in register A bit 7 and the update-ended interrupt on line 8 (check the MC146818 datasheet, D1, and the OSDev wiki "CMOS" page, D3); the FADT signature "FACP" and the century field at offset 108 (ACPI Specification, D2); NMI gating by port 0x70 bit 7 (chipset documentation; check your platform's datasheet). The guaranteed time between UIP rising and the update is deliberately not quoted: read it in D1.

Civil date to Unix time

The conversion must be exact for every date the kernel will ever see, which rules out loops over months with special cases written in a hurry. The lab uses a closed-form algorithm (Listing 1, lines 41–51): shift the year so that it starts on 1 March, which puts the leap day at the end of the year where it does not disturb the month lengths; count whole 400-year eras (each exactly 146097 days); within an era, the day of the year from March follows the formula (153 × m' + 2) / 5 + d − 1 with m' = 0 for March, and the day of the era is yoe × 365 + yoe/4 − yoe/100 + doy; finally subtract 719468, the number of days from 0000-03-01 to 1970-01-01 in this calendar. Instead of trusting the derivation, the host test compares it with C++20's <chrono> for every day from 1900 to 2199, 109573 days (R1), and two static_asserts pin known values at compile time. D5 D6 R1

Time zones, leap seconds, and what the RTC holds

The lab runs QEMU with -rtc base=utc, so the RTC holds UTC. Real PCs that also boot Windows often keep local time in the RTC, which the kernel cannot detect from the chip; Linux records the choice in user space and Windows in its registry. Your kernel must pick a convention and document it; this course uses UTC. Unix time ignores leap seconds: a day always has 86400 seconds. D4

Joining the clocks

The kernel waits until the seconds register changes; at that moment the RTC's second has just begun, so the pair (Unix time of the new second, current tick) has an error of at most one tick plus the read time instead of up to a second. From then on, wall = U + (ticks − T) / 1000. Over hours the PIT tick and the RTC drift apart because they come from different oscillators; a kernel re-reads the RTC now and then and slews the offset slowly rather than jumping (or uses network time). In the lab, the PIT milliseconds counted between RTC second changes come out a little below 1000 and vary from second to second (R3, R4); that describes QEMU's emulation on a shared build machine, not any hardware. R3 R4

How the hardware actually does it

The original MC146818 was a separate chip with a 32.768 kHz crystal input, an oscillator divider chain and 64 bytes of battery-backed RAM, of which 14 hold the clock and status. On modern PCs, the RTC is a block inside the chipset (the platform controller hub or the SoC's fusion controller hub) that keeps the same programming interface; the coin cell on the motherboard powers it while the machine is off. UEFI firmware offers the runtime services GetTime and SetTime as an alternative interface that hides the chip; the curriculum names it as an option. In QEMU the RTC model computes the time from the host clock and the -rtc option, so "drift" in the lab is a property of emulation. D1 D7

Not verified — check before relying on this

The crystal frequency, the 64-byte RAM size and the location of the RTC in modern chipsets are from memory; check the MC146818 datasheet (D1) and your platform's chipset datasheet. The UEFI service names are from memory of the UEFI Specification (D7).

Worked example

Decoding one snapshot. R4's raw line: sec=58 min=59 hour=23 day=31 mon=12 year=26 century=20 B=02. Register B = 0x02: bit 2 clear, so BCD; bit 1 set, so 24-hour. BCD: 0x58 → 5 × 10 + 8 = 58; 0x59 → 59; 0x23 → 23; 0x31 → 31; 0x12 → 12; 0x26 → 26; century 0x20 → 20, so the year is 20 × 100 + 26 = 2026. Date: 2026-12-31 23:59:58, as configured with -rtc base=. R4

Unix time by hand. For 2026-12-31: m = 12 > 2, so y stays 2026. era = 2026 / 400 = 5; yoe = 2026 − 2000 = 26. m' = 12 − 3 = 9: doy = (153 × 9 + 2) / 5 + 31 − 1 = 1379 / 5 + 30 = 275 + 30 = 305 (integer division). doe = 26 × 365 + 26/4 − 26/100 + 305 = 9490 + 6 − 0 + 305 = 9801. days = 5 × 146097 + 9801 − 719468 = 730485 + 9801 − 719468 = 20818. Seconds: 20818 × 86400 = 1798675200; plus 23 × 3600 + 59 × 60 + 58 = 86398 gives 1798761598, the number printed in R4. R4

The fixed date. With -rtc base=2026-12-31T23:59:58 (R4), the kernel reads 23:59:58, waits for the edge to :59, and then sees 00:00:00, 00:00:01, 00:00:02 on 2027-01-01: day, month, year and the century register all rolled over consistently, and the snapshot never tore (0 retries). R4

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The hall clock's cards flip all at once; the registers are read one by one. The tearing problem exists only because the reader takes several separate looks; a person glancing at a clock sees it whole.
    • 

  • The principal's stopwatch is perfect; the PIT is not. Two oscillators drift apart, and the lab's own numbers show the emulated tick and the RTC disagreeing by several milliseconds per second. The analogy hides the need to re-synchronise.
    • 

  • The century drawer is not part of the clock. The century register is firmware's convention in CMOS RAM, found through ACPI; a real clock has no hidden drawer that some other office decides about.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Ignoring register B. Symptom: correct dates on some days and nonsense on others (forensic lab). Always print the raw registers with B.
    • 

  • PM bit not masked before BCD. Symptom: hours like 88 in the evening on 12-hour machines.
    • 

  • One pass of reads. Symptom: once in a long while, timestamps jump by a minute or an hour around boot. Snapshot twice.
    • 

  • Hard-coding the century. Symptom: fine until the clock or a test sets a year outside your assumption. Use the FADT field.
    • 

  • Reading the RTC for every timestamp. Symptom: slow logging and timestamps with one-second resolution. Join to the monotonic clock.
    • 

  • Writing the RTC in local time without saying so. Symptom: the clock is off by the zone offset after booting another OS. Pick UTC and document it.
    •

Summary


    

  • The PC's RTC is reached through ports 0x70/0x71; its format (BCD or binary, 12- or 24-hour) is in register B and must be honoured.
    • 

  • The century register's index comes from the ACPI FADT.
    • 

  • Registers that change on their own need a consistent snapshot: wait for UIP to clear and compare two complete readings.
    • 

  • Civil date to Unix time uses a closed-form algorithm, tested against an independent implementation over three centuries.
    • 

  • The kernel reads the RTC once at a second edge and computes wall time from its monotonic clock afterwards, re-synchronising slowly.
    • 

  • Ignoring the format bit produces dates that are right only when every field is below ten.
