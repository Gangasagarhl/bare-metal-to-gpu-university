Story hook

Over a year, many theatre companies visit the school. A few leave a full printed copy of their scripts and stage plans in the library, and anyone may read them. Some leave nothing, but they let the school's drama club watch their rehearsals, and after years of watching the club has written its own notes, good enough to put on the same plays. Some bring stage managers who work only from sealed, signed scripts; the interpreter can hand those scripts over but never read them. And one company keeps everything secret; the only knowledge of it comes from a group of students who watched its shows very, very carefully.

If the drama club wants to stage a play on its own, which company should it learn from first? This chapter answers that question for GPUs. It maps the GPU families outside AMD and NVIDIA by where their knowledge comes from and how much signed firmware they need, and it ends with a scored list of targets a single learner can realistically drive.

What you will be able to do

  • Classify a GPU family by its documentation (vendor manual, partial guide, driver source only, none) and its firmware dependence (none, some engines, everything).
  • Explain why a GPU's display half and its rendering half are separate problems, and why display is the realistic first goal.
  • Survey the open Linux GPU drivers from their UAPI headers and say what such a survey can and cannot prove.
  • Rank candidate targets with an explicit, criticisable scoring rule.
  • Find the right first documents for a chosen target (PRM, TRM, architecture guide, driver source, reverse-engineering notes).
  • Diagnose a wrong conclusion drawn from a header survey.
Jargon box
PRM (Programmer's Reference Manual)
Simple words: the vendor's own book about how to program its chip. Analogy: the full printed scripts a company leaves in the library. Precise: Intel's Graphics Programmer's Reference Manuals, published per generation, in volumes covering the display engine, render and media engines, memory interface and command formats; coverage differs by generation (curriculum 16.1). D2 D1
TRM (technical reference manual)
Simple words: the big manual for a whole chip, with every block's registers. Analogy: the school building's complete plans, including the hall's projector room. Precise: a SoC vendor's document describing each on-chip block and its registers; for display controllers it is often the only documentation needed (NXP and TI publish them fully, according to curriculum 16.1). D1
Reverse engineering (GPU)
Simple words: working out how a chip is programmed by watching what its official driver does. Analogy: the drama club's notes from years of watching rehearsals. Precise: lawful observation of a vendor driver's interaction with the hardware (traced memory-mapped I/O, captured command streams, hypervisor-based observation as in Asahi Linux's m1n1), followed by experiments that confirm each hypothesis; the method of DR401 at scale. D1 D5
Firmware dependence
Simple words: how much of the GPU only works after a vendor's secret program is loaded into it. Analogy: stage managers who work only from sealed, signed scripts. Precise: the set of signed firmware binaries a driver must load before an engine works (command processors, power management, security, a command-stream front end); "everything" means no engine runs without it, which puts the hardware interface behind an interface defined by the vendor's firmware. D1 R1
Display controller
Simple words: the part of a chip that sends a picture to the screen. Analogy: the projector and its operator, separate from the actors. Precise: the hardware block that reads framebuffers from memory, composes planes and generates the timing for one or more outputs (called a pipe, CRTC, VOP, DSS or HVS depending on the vendor); it needs no shader compiler and on SoCs usually no firmware. D1
Mesa
Simple words: the open-source collection of user-space graphics drivers on Linux. Analogy: the shared workshop where every company's stage crew builds sets. Precise: the project that contains the OpenGL and Vulkan drivers for most open GPU kernel drivers, with a shared compiler infrastructure (NIR) and the Gallium driver framework; for a hobby kernel it is the realistic source of a shader compiler. D3 D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every graphics chip is made by some company. Some companies write a book that explains exactly how to talk to their chip. Others write nothing, and people had to figure it out by watching. Some chips also need a secret program, written by the company, loaded into them before they work at all; you can load it, but you cannot read it or change it. If you want to make a chip show a picture with your own program, pick one with a book and no secret program. And start with the part of the chip that sends pictures to the screen: it is much simpler than the part that draws them.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Curriculum table 16.1 lists the GPU families found in PCs and SoCs and, for each, five things: where you find it, what official documentation exists, which open Linux kernel driver drives it, which Mesa driver drives it from user space, how the knowledge was obtained, and what firmware it needs. Read the table as a map of where to look; each entry must be confirmed in the documents it names. Summarised by kind of knowledge: D1

  • Vendor manuals. Intel publishes PRMs for many generations of its integrated and discrete graphics. Broadcom released a "VideoCore IV 3D Architecture Reference Guide" in 2014 for the 3D core of the Raspberry Pi 0–3. SoC vendors such as NXP and TI publish TRMs that include their display controllers. D1 D2 D4
  • Vendor-written open drivers without a manual. Imagination wrote and upstreamed the powervr kernel driver and a Vulkan driver in Mesa; there is no public programming manual. Broadcom- and Raspberry Pi-funded developers wrote v3d for VideoCore VI and VII. D1
  • Reverse engineering. Mali (lima, Panfrost, Panthor), Adreno (freedreno, turnip), Vivante (etnaviv), Apple (asahi) and, historically, NVIDIA (nouveau with envytools). In several cases vendors later contributed, which the table records. D1 D5

Firmware cuts across these groups. Mali Utgard, Midgard and Bifrost, Vivante GC and VideoCore IV's 3D core need none. Mali Valhall with the command-stream front end (CSF), current Adreno, PowerVR, Apple AGX and recent NVIDIA need firmware for the GPU's own microcontrollers before any job runs. Intel sits in between: display and older-generation command submission work without the GuC firmware, newer generations increasingly depend on it. D1

The build container offers one small, real piece of evidence for this. It carries no GPU, but it has two NVIDIA GSP firmware images under /lib/firmware/nvidia/580.178.04/, of 30,483,544 and 75,028,464 bytes (R1): tens of megabytes of signed code that the open kernel modules load into the GPU. Compare that with the 39,424-byte VGA BIOS of F4-44. R1 R3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What a header survey can prove

The open kernel drivers all expose their interfaces as Linux UAPI headers under /usr/include/drm/, and the build container has those of Linux 6.8 (package linux-libc-dev 6.8.0-146.146, R3). Listing 1 counts each header's ioctl definitions and picks out the ones that submit work. R1 shows that every family in table 16.1 has an interface in this kernel except two: panthor_drm.h and asahi_drm.h are absent. The submission ioctls have different names but one shape: PANFROST_SUBMIT, LIMA_GEM_SUBMIT, ETNAVIV_GEM_SUBMIT, MSM_GEM_SUBMIT, VC4_SUBMIT_CL, PVR_SUBMIT_JOBS, I915_GEM_EXECBUFFER2, XE_EXEC, NOUVEAU_EXEC. v3d alone has four, one per engine kind (SUBMIT_CL, SUBMIT_TFU, SUBMIT_CSD, SUBMIT_CPU). Every one of these is the kernel-mediated model of F4-42: an ioctl per job. R1 D3

What the survey does not prove matters as much. A header's absence proves only that this kernel version does not export that interface. It says nothing about a newer kernel, about a driver maintained outside the mainline tree, or about hardware support. A header's presence proves an interface exists, not that it works on your chip or generation. And the ioctl count measures interface size, not driver quality or documentation. The forensic lab is about exactly this mistake. R1

From the author's memory, not checked: the Panthor driver was merged into mainline Linux after 6.8, and the Apple GPU kernel driver was maintained in the Asahi Linux tree rather than in mainline. Check the kernel's drivers/gpu/drm/ directory at the version you use (D3) and the Asahi Linux documentation (D5).

Display first

A complete GPU driver has two halves (Figure 2). The display half reads framebuffers and generates timing; it needs mode setting, EDID, a few hundred registers, and on most SoCs no firmware. The rendering half needs memory management with its own MMU, job submission, synchronisation, recovery and, above all, a shader compiler. Curriculum 16 states the conclusion: getting pixels on a screen natively is the realistic first goal on almost every platform. In F4-46 you do this on an Intel integrated GPU. D1

The shader compiler deserves its own warning. Every rendering target needs one, and writing one for an undocumented instruction set is a research project. Curriculum 16.3's advice is to reuse Mesa's compilers through a port of the user-space driver once your kernel offers a DRM-like interface, which is how the open drivers on other operating systems have been brought up. D1 D3

Scoring targets

Listing 2 turns the two axes into a rule you can argue with. Each target gets five ratings: documentation (0–3), firmware (0–2), an open driver to read (0/1), a test machine you own (0/1) and whether it is display-only (0/1). The score is 3·doc − 3·fw + 2·open + 2·owned + display; any target that needs firmware for everything is out of reach regardless of score, any target without documentation likewise, and otherwise 12 or more is a realistic first target and 5 or more a study or porting project. The weights are this chapter's teaching choice, not a standard, and the ratings in target_score.in are the author's reading of table 16.1 and of 16.3; the "owned" column assumes a learner with an Intel laptop and a Raspberry Pi 3. R2 D1

R2's verdicts reproduce curriculum 16.3: virtio-gpu, Intel display and Intel render on older generations and an NXP display controller are realistic; VideoCore IV 3D, Panfrost and etnaviv are study or porting projects; the newest Intel render engine, current AMD and NVIDIA, CSF Mali, current Adreno and Apple AGX are out of reach. That agreement is by construction (the thresholds were set so that it holds) and is not evidence that either is right. Its value is that each verdict now has a reason you can check: change a rating after you open a document, and the verdict follows. R2

How the hardware actually does it

Where firmware sits explains why it dominates the ranking. A GPU that needs no firmware exposes its engines' registers and command formats directly: the driver writes a control list or command buffer into memory and points the engine at it, as in G6 for VideoCore IV. A GPU with a firmware-run front end exposes a different interface: the driver writes requests into queues that the firmware reads, and the firmware programs the engines. The documentation you need is then the firmware's interface, which only the vendor defines and can change between firmware versions; and on most such GPUs the firmware is signed, so you cannot replace it. NVIDIA's GSP goes furthest: a large part of what a kernel driver does runs inside it (F4-42). D1

Worked example

Scoring two targets by hand.

  1. Intel display engine, older generation. doc 3, fw 0, open 1, owned 1, display 1: 3·3 − 0 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 14. Firmware is not 2 and doc is not 0, and 14 ≥ 12: realistic first target. R2 prints 14.
  2. Mali Valhall CSF (Panthor). doc 1, fw 2, open 1, owned 0, display 0: 3 − 6 + 2 + 0 + 0 = −1. fw = 2, so the verdict is out of reach, whatever the score. R2 prints −1.
  3. Sensitivity. If a learner owns an RK3588 board, owned becomes 1 and the score rises to 1; the verdict does not change, because firmware gates it. If a learner without a Raspberry Pi rates VideoCore IV with owned 0, its score drops from 10 to 8: still a study project. A rule whose verdicts change only when the facts change is the point of writing it down.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Watching rehearsals has rules. Lawful observation of a vendor driver is a method with legal and licence limits (DR401's "observe lawfully"); the drama club's watching has none.
    • 

  • Scripts left in the library can be wrong or incomplete. Vendor manuals have gaps and errata, and coverage changes between generations; a printed script is complete by definition.
    • 

  • Sealed scripts can change between visits. Firmware interfaces can change between firmware versions, so a driver written against one may fail on the next; a company's stage managers do not change their language between seasons.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Choosing by performance instead of documentation. Symptom: months spent on a GPU whose firmware interface is undocumented. Fix: rate documentation and firmware first.
    • 

  • Starting with rendering. Symptom: no pixels on screen after a long time. Fix: display half first (Figure 2).
    • 

  • Treating table 16.1 as a source. Symptom: a dossier citing "the curriculum" for a hardware fact. Fix: open and cite the document the table names.
    • 

  • Reading absence as non-existence. Symptom: "Linux has no driver for X" because a header is missing (forensic lab). Fix: state the version the survey covers.
    • 

  • Planning a shader compiler. Symptom: the project's critical path is a compiler for an undocumented ISA. Fix: port Mesa's (16.3).
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F4-42's map of one GPU driver and F4-44's read-only look at one GPU. What carries over: the kernel-mediated submission model, now seen in every open driver's UAPI R1; the habit of separating what a piece of evidence proves from what it suggests. What changes: the question is no longer "how does this driver work" but "can one person write it", and the answer depends more on documents and firmware than on technique. What comes next: F4-46 takes the top-ranked real hardware target, the display engine of an Intel integrated GPU, and works through EDID, modes and tear-free page flips.

Summary


    

  • GPU families differ most in two things: documentation (vendor manual to none) and firmware dependence (none to everything).
    • 

  • Vendor documents exist for Intel (PRMs), Broadcom VideoCore IV 3D (architecture guide) and many SoC display controllers (TRMs); much of the rest was learned by reverse engineering.
    • 

  • Display and rendering are separate halves; display is the realistic first goal.
    • 

  • All open drivers in the surveyed UAPI submit work with an ioctl per job.
    • 

  • A header survey proves what one kernel version exports, nothing more.
    • 

  • A written scoring rule makes a target choice checkable; firmware for everything is a gate, not a weight.
