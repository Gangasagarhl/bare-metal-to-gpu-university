Story hook

The projector in the school hall does not show a slide all at once. It paints it, one line at a time, from the top of the screen to the bottom, and then it goes dark for a moment before it starts again at the top. It does this 75 times a second, so fast that the audience sees a still picture. The operator's rule is simple: change slides only in the dark moment. One evening a new operator decided that waiting was a waste and swapped the slide the instant the next one was ready. Most of the time nobody noticed. But in a slow pan across a picture of the school, the audience saw the top half of one slide and the bottom half of the next, with a sharp edge between them, jumping up and down the screen. That edge is called a tear.

This chapter takes over the display of an Intel integrated GPU from the firmware, the most realistic real-hardware display target in curriculum 16.3. It parses the monitor's EDID, turns it into a mode, and explains the one rule that matters most for flipping between buffers: change the scanout address only so that it takes effect in vertical blank.

What you will be able to do

  • Parse an EDID base block and turn its preferred timing into a display mode (Linux's drm_mode_modeinfo).
  • Compute line time, frame time and refresh rate from a mode.
  • Explain why a page flip must take effect in vertical blank, and what an asynchronous flip shows.
  • Plan milestone G5 on an Intel GPU: read-only state dump, EDID over GMBUS, scanout address change, mode set, with the documents to read for each step.
  • Diagnose tearing from a frame-timing log (the course forensic).
Jargon box
Vertical blank (vblank)
Simple words: the short pause after the screen has been drawn to the bottom and before it starts again at the top. Analogy: the dark moment between two showings of a slide. Precise: the lines of a video frame after the last active line (in the 640 × 480 mode of R1, lines 480–495 of 496) during which no visible pixels are scanned out; display engines latch double-buffered registers such as the surface address at its start. D2 R1
Page flip
Simple words: telling the display to show a different picture buffer. Analogy: changing slides. Precise: changing a plane's scanout (surface) address to another framebuffer; a synchronised flip takes effect at the next vertical blank and its completion is reported by an event; Linux's DRM_IOCTL_MODE_PAGE_FLIP schedules one. D2
Tearing
Simple words: a visible line where the top of the screen shows one picture and the bottom another. Analogy: swapping the slide while it is being projected. Precise: the artefact of changing the scanout address while active lines are being scanned out, so that one refresh shows parts of two buffers; the DRM header warns that DRM_MODE_PAGE_FLIP_ASYNC "may cause tearing on the screen". D2 R2
Display mode (timing)
Simple words: the screen size together with how fast the pixels are sent. Analogy: the projector's settings: slide size, painting speed, length of the dark moment. Precise: a pixel clock and horizontal and vertical timings (active, sync start, sync end, total) with sync polarities; struct drm_mode_modeinfo holds exactly these fields. D2 R1
Detailed timing descriptor (DTD)
Simple words: an 18-byte entry in the EDID that describes one mode exactly. Analogy: one line of the projector's label giving all settings for one slide size. Precise: an EDID descriptor with the pixel clock in units of 10 kHz and 12-bit (horizontal) and 10/6-bit (vertical) active, blanking, sync offset and width fields split across bytes; the first descriptor is the preferred timing. D3 R1
GMBUS
Simple words: the small controller in an Intel GPU that talks to the monitor to read its EDID. Analogy: the intercom to the projector room. Precise: Intel's I²C controller in the display engine, used for DDC to read EDID from a connected monitor (named in curriculum G5's reading list). D1 D4

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From EDID to a mode

The monitor describes itself in its EDID. The lab records five EDID blocks from QEMU's virtio-gpu (with the F4-43 driver, at five requested resolutions) and parses them with edid.h: the 8-byte header, the checksum, the manufacturer letters packed as three 5-bit values, the product code, the year, the version, the number of extension blocks and the four 18-byte descriptors, of which the first is the preferred timing and one may be the monitor's name. R1 shows all five pass: header and checksum valid, manufacturer "RHT", product 0x1234, EDID 1.4, one extension, name "QEMU Monitor", and a preferred timing whose size equals the requested one. Each timing is then converted to Linux's drm_mode_modeinfo, the structure that KMS uses for a mode. R1 D2

Not verified — check before relying on this

The EDID byte layout in edid.h (offsets of manufacturer, product, year, version, extension count, the 18-byte descriptors and the bit fields inside a DTD) is from the author's memory of the VESA E-EDID standard (D3, not opened). The five tests prove it is consistent with QEMU's EDID generator for the fields checked (sizes, header, checksum); they do not prove the sync-offset and sync-width bit splits, which are not compared with an independent decoder. G5's acceptance test asks for exactly that comparison: your parser's output against Linux's parsed EDID for a real monitor.

From a mode to time

A mode says how long everything takes. For QEMU's 640 × 480 mode in R1: pixel clock 32,140 kHz, horizontal total 864 pixels, vertical total 496 lines. One line takes 864 / 32.14 MHz = 26.882 µs; one frame takes 496 lines = 13,333.7 µs; the refresh rate is 75.00 Hz. Of the 496 lines, 480 are visible, and the remaining 16 (about 430 µs) are the vertical blank. Listing 3 prints the same numbers (R2). R1 R2

Flips

A display engine reads the framebuffer at its surface address continuously, one line at a time. Double buffering means: draw the next frame into a second buffer, then flip, i.e. change the surface address. If the change takes effect in the middle of the visible lines, the current refresh shows the old buffer above that line and the new one below: a tear. Display hardware solves this with double-buffered registers: the driver writes the new address at any time, and the hardware copies it into the register it actually uses at the start of vertical blank. The KMS page-flip contract in the UAPI header says the same in software terms: the CRTC "will be reprogrammed to display that fb after the next vertical refresh", the ioctl returns at once, an event reports completion, and a second flip while one is pending is refused with EBUSY; DRM_MODE_PAGE_FLIP_ASYNC skips the wait and "may cause tearing". D2

Listing 3 models both policies with QEMU's mode and a renderer that finishes a frame every 11 ms, faster than one 13.3 ms refresh. R2: synchronised flips latch at line 480, every refresh shows exactly one buffer, and two buffers (1 and 6) are never shown because a newer one replaced them before the next blank. Asynchronous flips latch at the line where they are requested, and six of eight refreshes are torn. Neither policy is free: synchronised flips drop frames when rendering outruns the display, asynchronous ones tear. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Taking over from the firmware's GOP

When the machine boots with UEFI, the firmware's graphics driver (GOP) has already set a mode, chosen a pipe, a plane and a port, and allocated a framebuffer; your loader can hand its address and size to your kernel (DR301's console did this). Milestone G5 starts from that state: first dump it read-only (which pipe drives which port with what timing), and compare the dump with what Linux reports on the same machine; then read the EDID yourself; then move the plane's surface address to your own buffer and flip it at vertical blank; only at the end perform a full mode set. Each step is reversible on its own except the last, and the order follows the F4-44 rule: read before you write. D4 D1

Untested on hardware: this build had no Intel GPU, and the Intel PRMs (D1) and the i915/xe display code (D5) were not opened. The following are from the author's memory and must be confirmed in the PRM display-engine volume for your exact generation before any register is read or written: that the display registers are in the MMIO range of BAR0 (GTTMMADR) and that reading them is safe while the firmware's mode is active; register names such as PIPECONF/TRANS_CONF, HTOTAL_A, VTOTAL_A, PLANE_CTL, PLANE_SURF (older DSPSURF), PLANE_STRIDE, PIPE_FRMCOUNT, GMBUS0–GMBUS5; that a write to the surface register arms a flip which the hardware latches at the start of vertical blank; that newer generations need display power wells enabled and DMC firmware for low-power states (curriculum 16.1 names DMC). Register offsets differ between generations; do not copy them from another generation's code.

EDID over GMBUS

On real hardware, EDID is read from the monitor over DDC, an I²C bus in the display cable, at the conventional 7-bit address 0x50. On Intel GPUs the GMBUS controller performs the I²C transfer and hands the bytes to the driver; i915's and xe's display code are the open references. A robust reader checks the header and checksum of every 128-byte block, retries a failed block, and reads extension blocks as the base block's byte 126 announces. Your parser is the same as in this lab; only the source of the bytes changes. D1 D3 D5

Not verified — check before relying on this

The DDC address 0x50 and the retry behaviour are from the author's memory of the VESA DDC/E-DDC standards (not in the source list; title only) and of the i915 code (D5); neither was opened in this build.

Flipping without tearing, and proving it

G5 asks that "a double-buffered animation flips without tearing at the monitor's refresh rate". Three things must hold: the flip is armed by writing the surface address (not by copying pixels into the scanned-out buffer); it takes effect at vertical blank; and the driver does not write into a buffer until the flip away from it has completed, i.e. it waits for the flip-done or vertical-blank interrupt (DR302) before reusing the old buffer. The evidence is a frame-timing log like the one in the forensic lab: for every flip, the time it was requested, the scanout line at that time (Intel display engines expose a scanline counter, unverified), the time the flip completed and the line at completion. In a correct log every completion is in vertical blank and completions are a whole number of frame periods apart. D2 R3

Safety

Spare machine only, data you do not need, boot from removable media (curriculum safety box). A wrong mode set can leave the panel dark until reboot; a laptop's built-in panel (eDP) has power-sequencing requirements that, if violated, may stress the panel: work on an external monitor first and read the PRM's panel power-sequencing section before touching eDP. Keep a serial console or network log, because the screen may be your only output and may go dark.

How the hardware actually does it

A display engine is a fixed-rhythm machine. The transcoder counts pixels and lines at the pixel clock, generates sync pulses, and asks the planes for one line of pixels at a time; each plane fetches its line from memory at its surface address plus line × stride, the pipe blends the planes, and the port encodes the result for HDMI, DisplayPort or the laptop's eDP panel. Registers that change what is shown (surface address, plane size, some pipe settings) are double-buffered: the driver writes an "armed" value, and the hardware transfers it to the "live" register at a defined point, the start of vertical blank. Interrupts at vertical blank or at flip completion tell the driver that the old buffer is free. D1 D2

Worked example

Decode QEMU's 640 × 480 preferred timing by hand. From R1: clock 32,140 kHz; h 640 800 819 864; v 480 482 484 496.

  1. Horizontal: active 640, sync from 800 to 819 (offset 160, width 19), total 864, so blanking = 864 − 640 = 224 pixels.
  2. Vertical: active 480, sync from 482 to 484, total 496, blanking 16 lines.
  3. Line time = 864 / 32,140,000 s = 26.882 µs. Frame time = 496 × 26.882 = 13,333.7 µs. Refresh = 1 / 0.0133337 s = 75.00 Hz; edid_test's rounded integer arithmetic gives 75 (R1), Listing 3 gives 75.00 (R2).
  4. Vertical blank lasts 16 × 26.882 = 430.1 µs. A flip that must take effect in blank has that window, every 13.3 ms.
  5. A flip requested at 46,500 µs (R2, buffer 4): 46,500 / 13,333.7 = 3.487 frames, so refresh 3 began at 40,001.0 µs; (46,500 − 40,001.0) / 26.882 = 241.8, line 241. Synchronised: latched at 40,001.0 + 480 × 26.882 = 52,904.5 µs. Asynchronous: refresh 3 shows buffer 3 in lines 0–240 and buffer 4 in lines 241–479, as R2 prints.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The dark moment is not empty. During vertical blank the link still carries sync signals and, on HDMI and DisplayPort, other data; it is "blank" only for the picture.
    • 

  • The operator does not swap slides by hand. The driver arms a register; the hardware swaps at the right line. A write that arrives after the blank has started waits for the next blank.
    • 

  • The audience may not see a tear at all. A tear is visible only when consecutive frames differ in the region around the tear line; a static picture tears invisibly.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Drawing into the buffer on screen. Symptom: flicker and half-drawn frames even with synchronised flips. Fix: draw into the back buffer only; reuse a buffer only after the flip away from it completed.
    • 

  • Asynchronous flips for latency. Symptom: tears at random lines (forensic lab). Fix: synchronised flips; reduce latency by rendering later, not by flipping earlier.
    • 

  • Refresh computed with the active size. Symptom: 104 Hz for a 75 Hz mode. Cause: clock / (640 × 480). Fix: divide by the totals.
    • 

  • Wrong DTD bit split. Symptom: a 1280-wide mode decoded as 256 or 1024. Fix: the upper 4 bits of size fields come from the shared byte (Listing 1 lines 31–34).
    • 

  • Skipping the read-only dump. Symptom: a dark screen and no idea which pipe drives the monitor. Fix: G5's order, dump first.
    •
