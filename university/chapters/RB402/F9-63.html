Story hook

Analogy

Nadia's bicycle has no speedometer, and nothing in her body measures how fast her lean is growing. Yet she reacts to both. Tomás asks her how. She thinks about it: "I know what I just did with the handlebar, so I know roughly what the bike should be doing now. Then I feel where it actually is. If it is leaning more than I expected, I must have been wrong about how fast it was tipping, so I adjust my guess."

That is an observer: a model in the head, driven by the same commands as the body, corrected by the senses whenever they disagree with the model's prediction. The controllers of F9-61 and F9-62 needed all four states of the cart-pole; the robot measures only two of them, and only in whole encoder counts. This chapter builds the part of the controller that fills in the rest, and shows what happens when it trusts its senses too much.

What you will be able to do

  • Build an observer x̂[k+1] = Adx̂[k] + Bdu[k] + L(y[k] − Cx̂[k]) and explain each term.
  • Design the observer gain L by pole placement or by the dual Riccati equation (the steady-state Kalman gain), using the code of F9-61 and F9-62.
  • Prove the separation principle for the combined controller and observer, and check it numerically.
  • Compare an observer with finite differences of encoder counts on smoothness and accuracy.
  • Diagnose an observer that is too fast for its sensors from a log of counts, estimates and forces.
Jargon box
State observer (Luenberger observer)
Simple words: a program that runs a copy of the robot's model alongside the robot, and nudges the copy towards the sensor readings, so that the copy's state can stand in for states nobody measures. Analogy: Nadia's guess of her speed and lean rate, kept up to date by what she feels. Precise: x̂[k+1] = Adx̂[k] + Bdu[k] + L(y[k] − Cx̂[k]); the estimation error e = x − x̂ obeys e[k+1] = (Ad − LC)e[k]. B1D1
Observer gain
Simple words: how strongly each sensor surprise corrects each estimated state. Analogy: how much Nadia changes her guess when the bike leans more than she expected. Precise: the matrix L ∈ ℝn×p; its choice places the eigenvalues of Ad − LC, which is possible for any set exactly when (Ad, C) is observable. B1
Innovation (measurement surprise)
Simple words: the difference between what the sensors read and what the model expected them to read. Analogy: "it is leaning more than I thought". Precise: ν[k] = y[k] − Cx̂[k]; for a well-tuned observer it looks like the sensor noise, with no pattern left in it. B1D1
Separation principle
Simple words: you can design the controller as if you had the true state, and the observer as if there were no controller; together they behave as each promised. Analogy: Nadia's riding rule and her guessing skill can be learned separately. Precise: for linear models, the closed loop of u = −Kx̂ with the observer above has the eigenvalues of Ad − BdK together with those of Ad − LC. B1R3
Output feedback
Simple words: control that uses only what the sensors measure, not the full state. Analogy: riding with your real senses, not with a magic display of every number. Precise: u is a function of y and past inputs only; here u = −Kx̂ with x̂ from an observer driven by y and u. B1
Steady-state Kalman gain (observer design)
Simple words: the observer gain that best balances how much the robot is pushed around against how noisy the sensors are. Analogy: trusting your feel more on a smooth road and your expectations more on a bumpy one. Precise: for process noise covariance W and sensor noise covariance V, L = Kᵀ of the LQR problem for (Adᵀ, Cᵀ) with weights W and V (the dual Riccati equation); this form of the observer is a steady-state Kalman predictor. The full, time-varying Kalman filter is treated in RB302. B1B2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The robot can feel two things: where its cart is and how far its pole leans. Its balancing rule also needs to know how fast both are changing. One way is to look at the numbers twice and subtract. But the sensors only report whole steps, like a ruler with marks every millimetre, so the subtraction jumps around: nothing, nothing, a big step, nothing.

A better way is to keep a pretend robot in the computer. Every time the real robot gets a push, the pretend one gets the same push and moves the same way. The computer compares where the pretend robot thinks the cart is with where the real sensor says it is, and gently pulls the pretend robot towards the real one. After a short while, the pretend robot's speeds are a good guess of the real robot's speeds, and they do not jump.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why not just differentiate?

The course cart-pole measures x with an encoder of 1 mm per count and θ with an encoder of 2048 counts per turn (exercise values; 0.00307 rad per count). The velocities v and ω are needed by u = −Kx. Finite differences (y[k] − y[k−1])/T turn one count of rounding into a velocity jump of 0.1 m/s or 0.307 rad/s at 100 Hz. MA301 called this the noise amplification of differentiation. Multiplied by the LQR's gains (Kω = −34.4 alone turns 0.307 rad/s into 10.5 N), every count change becomes a kick of several newtons. Run R2 measures it: with finite differences, the force changes by 8.85 N rms from one sample to the next and hits the 10 N limit. U1R2D1

The observer equation

Run a copy of the sampled model: x̂[k+1] = Adx̂[k] + Bdu[k]. If the copy started exactly right, it would stay right, because it gets the same input. It never starts right, and the cart-pole is unstable, so an uncorrected copy drifts away from the real robot exponentially. So add a correction proportional to the innovation, the sensor surprise:

x̂[k+1] = Adx̂[k] + Bdu[k] + L (y[k] − C x̂[k])

Subtract it from the true x[k+1] = Adx[k] + Bdu[k] with y = Cx: the error e = x − x̂ obeys e[k+1] = (Ad − LC)e[k]. The input has dropped out. The error decays at the rates set by the eigenvalues of Ad − LC, whatever the controller does. B1D1

Designing L by duality

The eigenvalues of Ad − LC are those of its transpose Adᵀ − CᵀLᵀ. That is a state-feedback problem for the pair (Adᵀ, Cᵀ) with gain Lᵀ, so the pole-placement code of F9-61 designs observers unchanged: L = tr(acker(tr(Ad), tr(C), poles)). It works exactly when (Adᵀ, Cᵀ) is controllable, which by F9-60's duality means (Ad, C) observable. B1D1

Listing 1 makes the point as strongly as possible: an observer that sees only the cart position (F9-60 showed this is observable). With observer poles at s = −8, −9, −10, −11 (time constants 0.125 s to 0.091 s, faster than the LQR's slowest poles at −1.73 ± 1.27j), run R1 gives L = [0.3636, 4.9991, −19.0012, −93.7958]ᵀ and observer eigenvalues 0.9231, 0.9139, 0.9048, 0.8958, which are esT for the chosen s. The LQR of F9-62 then acts on x̂. The pole starts at 0.05 rad while the observer believes it is at 0; the first force is 0 (the observer sees nothing wrong yet), the pole falls further to −0.067 rad, and by 1.2 s the estimate of θ agrees to 0.0006 rad. At 3 s the whole estimation error is 1.1×10−8 (Figure 2). R1R3

Controller and observer together

With u = −Kx̂ = −K(x − e), the plant is x[k+1] = (Ad − BdK)x[k] + BdKe[k], and the error is e[k+1] = (Ad − LC)e[k]. In the combined state (x, e) the system matrix is block upper triangular, so its eigenvalues are those of the two diagonal blocks: the controller's and the observer's, unchanged. This is the separation principle. Run R3 checks it by comparing the characteristic polynomial of the 8×8 matrix with the product of the two 4×4 ones: the largest coefficient difference is 2.8×10−14. B1R3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

How fast should the observer be?

A fast observer forgets its errors quickly but believes every sensor reading strongly; a slow one smooths noise but lags behind real changes. A common starting point, used in this chapter, is observer poles a few times faster than the controller's, then adjusting by simulation with realistic noise. The forensic lab shows the failure on the fast side. On the slow side, a lagging estimate acts like a delay in the loop, and F9-62 showed what delay does to an LQR. B1D1

Choosing L from the noise: the dual Riccati equation

Instead of naming observer poles, say how big the disturbances and the sensor errors are, and let the Riccati equation find L. If the process noise w[k] (entering as x[k+1] = Adx + Bdu + w) has covariance W and the sensor noise has covariance V, the steady-state Kalman gain is L = (Kdual)ᵀ, where Kdual is the LQR gain for the pair (Adᵀ, Cᵀ) with weights Q = W, R = V. That is one line with Listing 1 of F9-62 (Listing 2, line 28). B1B2

Listing 2 models the random push as W = σF²BdBdᵀ (a force of standard deviation σF = 0.2 N entering through Bd; a tiny 10−10 is added on every state to keep the problem well posed) and the encoder rounding as V = diag(qx²/12, qθ²/12). The 1/12 is the variance of an error spread evenly over one count of width q: ∫−q/2q/2 e² de / q = q²/12. Run R2 gives observer eigenvalues 0.9605 ± 0.0051j and 0.7983 ± 0.1634j: slower than the poles placed by hand in Listing 1, because the design knows the sensors are coarse. D1R2U1

This observer is optimal (least mean-square error) only if the noise really is white, Gaussian and of the stated size. Encoder rounding is none of those exactly: it is bounded, and it is correlated from sample to sample when the robot moves slowly. Treat W and V as tuning knobs with a physical meaning, as Bryson's rule treats Q and R. B2D1

Predictor and current estimator

The observer in this chapter is a predictor: it uses y[k] to produce x̂[k+1], so the controller at sample k uses an estimate built from measurements up to k − 1. A current estimator first corrects x̂[k] with y[k], then computes u[k] from the corrected estimate. It reacts one sample sooner, at the price of doing the correction between reading the sensor and sending the command, which lengthens the computational delay inside the sample. With 100 Hz and four states, either is easy; at higher rates or with large models, the choice matters for the timing budget. B1D1

What separation does not promise

The separation principle is about eigenvalues of the nominal linear model. It does not say the combination is robust. An LQR acting on the true state has the margins measured in F9-62; the same LQR acting on an observer's estimate can have much smaller margins, because the observer's model is now inside the loop and its errors are not checked by anything. Measure the margins of the combined controller (gain scaling and delay, as in F9-62's checks.cpp) instead of assuming them. B1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The statements that an LQR's margins are not guaranteed once an observer is added, that the steady-state Kalman gain is mean-square optimal under white Gaussian noise, and the predictor and current-estimator forms are tagged to B1 and B2, which were not opened in this build. The specific margins of this chapter's combined controller were not computed; the lab's step 4 asks you to compute them.

Other observer shapes

A full-order observer estimates every state, including those already measured; a reduced-order observer estimates only the unmeasured ones and passes measured states straight through, which saves computation but passes their noise straight to the controller. Observers with the nonlinear model inside (and, for noisy systems, the extended Kalman filter of RB302) handle large angles that the linear copy here gets wrong. The structure, predict with the model and correct with the innovation, is the same in every case. B1B2

How the hardware actually does it

The observer runs in the same loop as the controller, at the same rate, with the same model. Three hardware details decide whether it works. First, it must be driven by the force actually applied, after the driver's limit and any delay; Listings 1 and 2 feed it the clipped F, not the demanded one. If the driver clips or delays commands the observer does not know about, the model copy and the robot drift apart. Second, the sensor readings must be taken at a known time in the sample: an encoder read late in the sample is a slightly different measurement than the model assumes. Third, the encoder resolution sets V; take it from the encoder's datasheet and the gearing, not from a guess. D1U1

The computation per sample is a 4×4 matrix-vector product, a 4×1 input term and a 4×2 correction: a few dozen multiply-adds. A small microcontroller does this with time to spare at 100 Hz; on processors without floating-point hardware, fixed-point arithmetic needs care with the scaling of L, whose entries in Listing 1 range from 0.36 to 94 (MA302 covers number formats). R1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. The encoder resolutions (1 mm per count, 2048 counts per turn) and the 0.2 N random push are exercise values, not measurements of any kit. No rig was used. A real rig's encoder resolution, sample timing and disturbance level must be measured or taken from the datasheets of the parts in your kit (recorded in the dossier), and W and V set from them.

Safety

An observer that has diverged (for example after a sensor cable was unplugged, or with a wrong sign in C) reports a confident but wrong state, and the controller acts on it at full force. Check the innovation at run time: if |y − Cx̂| stays larger than a few counts for more than a few samples, stop the motor. Keep the emergency stop and the driver's current limit in place as in F9-61 and F9-62 (safety, BR-08).

Worked example

Task. The cart of MA301 F0-67, sampled at T = 0.1 s, measures only its position. Design an observer with eigenvalues 0.5 and 0.6.

  1. From F9-59: Ad = [[1, a12], [0, a22]] with a22 = e−0.4·0.1 = 0.960789 and a12 = (1 − e−0.04)/0.4 = 0.098026. C = [1, 0].
  2. With L = [l1, l2]ᵀ: Ad − LC = [[1 − l1, a12], [−l2, a22]].
  3. Characteristic polynomial: z² − (1 − l1 + a22)z + ((1 − l1)a22 + l2a12).
  4. Desired: (z − 0.5)(z − 0.6) = z² − 1.1z + 0.3.
  5. Match the z term: 1 − l1 + 0.960789 = 1.1, so l1 = 0.860789.
  6. Match the constant: (0.139211)(0.960789) + 0.098026 l2 = 0.3, so l2 = (0.3 − 0.133752)/0.098026 = 1.69595.
  7. Meaning: a 1 cm surprise in position moves the position estimate by 0.86 cm and the speed estimate by 1.7 cm/s.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nadia's inner model is learned and fuzzy; the observer's model is exactly Ad, Bd, C, and it trusts them completely. If the real mass or length differs, the observer is confidently wrong in a consistent way, and nothing in the innovation may show it at once.
    • 

  • Nadia can notice that a sense is broken ("my foot is numb") and ignore it. The observer has no such judgement unless you program a check on the innovation (Safety box).
    • 

  • People combine many senses with different delays. The observer here assumes every measurement belongs to the same instant as the sample.

Expected observations


As the observer gets faster, rms force change grows steeply (run R4 shows a factor of about 28 between the two designs there), while rms θ changes little; as it gets slower, rms x and θ grow because the estimate lags. A useful design sits where the force change is a small fraction of the force limit and the lag does not cost margin. The margins with an observer are expected to be smaller than with the true state; how much smaller is what step 4 measures.


Verification


Observer eigenvalues inside the unit circle and equal to the requested ones (pole placement) or reported (Kalman design); the separation check of checks.cpp holds for your K and L; the program builds with no warnings and no sanitizer report.


Troubleshooting


    

  • Results differ from run to run → the generator was not seeded with a constant; keep Noise rng(2026).
    • 

  • Kalman design fails to converge → W has no weight on a direction that is unstable and unobservable from y; add the small 10−10 term.
    • 

  • Every method looks equally good → the push is too small to matter or the encoders too fine; return to the listed values.

Summary


    

  • An observer runs the model alongside the robot and corrects it with L times the innovation y − Cx̂.
    • 

  • The error obeys e[k+1] = (Ad − LC)e[k]; L places those poles whenever (Ad, C) is observable, by duality with state feedback.
    • 

  • The separation principle: controller and observer poles appear unchanged in the combined loop (checked to 2.8×10−14).
    • 

  • Finite differences of encoder counts make the force buzz (8.85 N rms change per sample); a Kalman-designed observer gives 0.10 N.
    • 

  • The steady-state Kalman gain comes from the dual Riccati equation with process and sensor noise covariances W and V.
    • 

  • An observer that is too fast for its sensors passes every count step to the motor; separation does not guarantee robustness.
    •
