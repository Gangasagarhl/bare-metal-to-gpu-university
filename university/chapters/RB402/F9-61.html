Story hook

Analogy

Nadia's first rides go wrong in two opposite ways. On Monday she reacts to every lean very gently, and the bike sinks slowly onto its side before her steering catches it. On Tuesday Tomás tells her to react hard, and she does: every small lean gets a big jerk of the handlebar, the bike swings from one side to the other, and after the third swing she runs out of handlebar and tips over. On Wednesday he gives her a rule: "steer into the lean this much for every bit of lean, and this much more if the lean is growing; and when you drift off the path, first let the bike lean back towards it." She rides straight.

Tomás's rule is a list of numbers, one per thing Nadia can feel, saying how much muscle to use for each. Choose the numbers and you choose how the ride behaves: how quickly a lean dies away, whether it swings, how much effort it takes. This chapter shows how to compute those numbers so that the robot's motion has exactly the speeds you ask for, and what happens when you ask for more than its muscles can give.

What you will be able to do

  • Design a state-feedback gain u = −Kx that puts every closed-loop pole where you choose, for any controllable single-input system.
  • Derive the gains by matching characteristic polynomials by hand for small systems, and with Ackermann's formula in code.
  • Convert a continuous pole choice into a sampled design with z = esT, and predict what changes if you skip that step.
  • Predict how gains and forces grow when you ask for faster poles, and check the design against an actuator limit.
  • Track a setpoint with state feedback and explain the wrong-way start of a balancing robot.
Jargon box
State feedback
Simple words: the controller looks at the whole state and sets the input as a weighted sum of all of it. Analogy: Nadia's rule: so much steering for the lean, so much for the lean rate, so much for her drift and speed. Precise: u = −Kx (or u = −K(x − r) with a reference r), K ∈ ℝm×n; the closed loop is x′ = (A − BK)x. B1D1
Pole placement (eigenvalue assignment)
Simple words: choosing the gains so that the controlled machine's natural motions have exactly the speeds you asked for. Analogy: deciding how fast a lean should die away, then finding the reaction strengths that make it so. Precise: given (A, B) and a set Λ of n numbers closed under complex conjugation, find K with eig(A − BK) = Λ; possible for every Λ exactly when (A, B) is controllable. B1
Characteristic polynomial (closed loop)
Simple words: the polynomial whose roots are the closed-loop poles; its coefficients are what the gains change. Analogy: the recipe that sets every speed of the ride at once. Precise: det(sI − (A − BK)) = sn + a1sn−1 + … + an; pole placement matches it to the desired Π(s − λi). D1
Ackermann's formula
Simple words: a formula that gives the gain row directly from the model and the desired poles, for one input. Analogy: a ready-made rule-maker: give it the bike and the ride you want, it gives the reaction strengths. Precise: K = [0 … 0 1] Wc−1 φ(A), where Wc is the controllability matrix and φ is the desired characteristic polynomial. B1
Controllable canonical form
Simple words: a way to write a single-input model so that the characteristic polynomial's coefficients appear directly in A. Analogy: rearranging the bike's controls so each knob turns exactly one coefficient. Precise: a change of coordinates z = Tx giving Ac with ones on the superdiagonal and −an, …, −a1 in the last row, and Bc = [0, …, 0, 1]ᵀ; it exists exactly when (A, B) is controllable. B1
Wrong-way response (non-minimum-phase behaviour)
Simple words: to go one way, the system must first move the other way. Analogy: to turn a bicycle left, you first steer slightly right so the bike leans left. Precise: a step response that starts in the opposite direction to its final value, caused by a transfer-function zero in the right half-plane (for the cart-pole, between F and x). B1R3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the loop this chapter builds: the computer reads the state, multiplies it by a gain row K and sends the force to the cart, once every sample period. Figure 2 shows what the gain does on the pole map: it moves the four open-loop poles, one of them unstable, to four places you choose. Figure 3 shows the price: faster poles need larger forces, and an actuator limit decides what "faster" can mean.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A balancing robot needs a rule: "if the pole leans this way, push the cart this much". Make the rule out of four numbers, one for each thing the robot knows: where the cart is, how fast it moves, how far the pole leans, how fast the lean grows. Multiply each by its number, add them up: that is the push.

Different numbers give different behaviours. Small numbers give a lazy robot that catches the pole slowly. Big numbers give a nervous robot that catches it fast but pushes very hard. Engineers do not guess the numbers. They first say how fast each kind of wobble should die away, then compute the four numbers that make it happen. That is pole placement. The catch: if you ask for a very fast robot, the numbers say "push with 260 newtons", and a small motor simply cannot.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Closing the loop

With the model x′ = Ax + Bu of F9-59 and the controller u = −Kx, the closed loop is x′ = (A − BK)x. Its poles are the eigenvalues of A − BK, and they decide everything F9-59 taught you to read from poles: stable if all have negative real part, how fast each motion decays (1/|Re λ|), whether it oscillates (Im λ ≠ 0). For one input, K is a row of n numbers, and the characteristic polynomial det(sI − A + BK) has n coefficients, each depending on the gains. If the system is controllable (F9-60), the map from gains to coefficients is one-to-one: you can get any polynomial, so any set of poles (complex ones in conjugate pairs, so that K is real). B1D1

For two states you can match coefficients by hand (worked example). For four, use Ackermann's formula (Layer 3 and Listing 1). Run R3 checks the method for the cart-pole: the desired polynomial for poles −2 ± 1j, −5, −6 is s4 + 15s3 + 79s2 + 175s + 150, and the characteristic polynomial of A − BK computed with Listing 1's K agrees to four decimals in every coefficient. R3

Choosing the poles

Placement turns the design question into "where should the poles go?". Useful rules for a first design, all to be checked by simulation:

  • Faster than the instability. The pole falls with time constant 1/4.84 ≈ 0.21 s (F9-59). Closed-loop poles much slower than that leave the pole to fall a long way before the cart catches it; the design here uses rates 2 to 6 1/s, around the same speed as the fall.
  • Damped. A complex pair −σ ± jω with ω small compared with σ gives little overshoot. −2 ± 1j has a damping ratio of 2/√5 ≈ 0.89.
  • Not faster than needed. Every doubling of speed multiplies the gains (and the forces) many times over (Layer 3).
  • Slow enough for the sampling. With T = 0.01 s, poles at a few per second are far below the sample rate; a pole at −100 would need a design that respects the sampling (MA302 F0-79 covers how sampling moves poles).

Listing 2's design uses −2 ± 1j, −5 and −6. It gives the gain row K = [−7.6453, −9.0195, −55.0946, −11.9597] for the continuous model, and run R1 confirms eig(A − BK) = −2 ± 1j, −5, −6. R1

Reading the signs

The force is F = −Kx = 7.65x + 9.02v + 55.1θ + 11.96ω. The θ and ω terms are what you would guess: if the pole leans towards +x, push the cart towards +x, under it, as you steer a bicycle into its lean. The x term is the surprise: if the cart is 0.1 m to the right of its target, the controller pushes it further right with 0.77 N. Why? To bring the cart back left, the pole must first lean left, so that the cart can then accelerate left under it without the pole falling. Leaning the pole left requires a short push right. This is the wrong-way response of the jargon box. Run R3 shows it for a setpoint: asked to move from 0 to 0.2 m, the cart first goes back to −0.0109 m, then arrives at 0.2000 m. D1R3

A computer runs the loop: design in z

The robot's computer measures the state every T = 0.01 s and holds the force for one period. F9-59 showed that the exact sampled model is x[k+1] = Adx[k] + Bdu[k]. The closed loop is then x[k+1] = (Ad − BdKd)x[k], and a continuous pole s corresponds to the discrete pole z = esT. So the clean way is: choose poles in s, map them with z = esT, and place them for (Ad, Bd). Ackermann's formula works unchanged for discrete models. B1U1

Run R1: Kd = [−7.0970, −8.4162, −52.4093, −11.3663], about 7 % smaller than the continuous K, and eig(Ad − BdKd) = 0.9801 ± 0.0098j, 0.9512, 0.9418, exactly esT for the chosen s. Running the continuous K at 100 Hz instead gives 0.9805 ± 0.0097j, 0.9517 and 0.9345: still stable, but the pole meant to be at −6 is now at ln(0.9345)/0.01 ≈ −6.8 1/s. At 100 Hz and these speeds the difference is small; at 20 Hz or with fast poles it is not. R1D1

Finally, the real test: the discrete design on the nonlinear cart-pole from F9-59, released at 0.1 rad. The pole is caught (θ = −0.0425 rad at 0.5 s, 0.0001 rad at 4 s), the cart runs out to about 0.125 m and comes back, and the peak force is 5.24 N, at the first sample. R1

Faster costs force

Listing 3 scales all four poles by a factor α and releases the pole at 0.15 rad with a 10 N force limit. Up to α = 2 the pole is caught, although from α = 1.5 the controller asks for more than 10 N and the limit clips it for part of the time. At α = 3 the demand is 260 N and the pole falls; at α = 4, 1,620 N, and it falls faster. The linear design knows nothing about the limit: it promised poles at −6 ± 3j, −15, −18, and that promise holds only while the force is not clipped. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why controllability is exactly the condition

If (A, B) is controllable with one input, the matrix T = Wc̃Wc−1 (with Wc̃ the controllability matrix of the canonical pair) transforms the system into controllable canonical form: Ac has ones on the superdiagonal and the last row −an, …, −a1 (the open-loop characteristic coefficients), Bc = [0, …, 0, 1]ᵀ. In those coordinates u = −Kcz only changes the last row: the coefficients become ai + kc,n+1−i. So the gain needed is simply desired minus open-loop coefficient, then K = KcT. If (A, B) is not controllable, the Kalman decomposition of F9-60 shows that the uncontrollable eigenvalues appear in A − BK whatever K is: they cannot be moved. If they are stable (stabilisable), the rest can still be placed. B1D1

Ackermann's formula

Collecting the canonical-form argument into one expression gives K = [0 … 0 1] Wc−1 φ(A), where φ(s) = sn + α1sn−1 + … + αn is the desired polynomial and φ(A) = An + α1An−1 + … + αnI. It is short and easy to check, which is why Listing 1 uses it. It is also numerically fragile: it inverts Wc, whose condition number grows quickly with n and with the spread of the eigenvalues (F9-60's singular values for the cart-pole already span a factor of about 60). For a handful of states in double precision it is fine; for larger models, numerical libraries use methods based on orthogonal transformations instead. B1U1

How the gains grow with speed

Scaling every pole by α scales the desired coefficients αi by about αi: the last coefficient, which mostly sets the position gain, by α4. Run R3: the discrete position gain is −7.1 at 1×, −105.4 at 2×, −495.9 at 3× (ratios 15 and 70 against α4 = 16 and 81; not exact, because the open-loop coefficients are subtracted first). The force for a given disturbance grows the same way. A pole-placement design therefore has a steep cost curve, and the curve is invisible unless you simulate with the actuator limit. R3D1

More than one input

With m > 1 inputs, K has m·n numbers but there are still only n poles: the same poles can be reached by many different gains. The extra freedom can shape the closed-loop eigenvectors (which states take part in which motion) or improve robustness, but pole placement alone does not say how to use it. That gap, together with the steep cost curve, is the motivation for the LQR of F9-62, which chooses K by weighing state error against effort instead of by naming poles. B1

What placement does not promise

Poles describe the nominal model with unlimited actuators and exact state. They say nothing about how much the model may be wrong before the loop goes unstable, how noise on the measured state reaches the force, or what a delay does (F9-62's forensic lab). A set of well-placed poles can have a small gain margin. Every pole-placement design must be checked with the nonlinear model, the actuator limit, the sample period and measurement noise, as Listings 2 and 3 do for the first two. B1D1

Setpoints and integral action

u = −K(x − r) moves the cart to a position r because any cart position with v = θ = ω = 0 is an equilibrium of the model needing zero force (the A matrix has a zero column for x). For outputs whose equilibrium needs a steady input, the reference must also set a feedforward ur, which depends on an accurate model. A constant disturbance (a slope in the track, a belt that drags) leaves a steady error under pure state feedback; adding the integral of the position error as an extra state and placing the poles of the enlarged system removes it. The RB202 integral term and this augmented state are the same idea. B1D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The canonical-form argument, Ackermann's formula, the statement on multi-input freedom and the statement that numerical libraries use orthogonal-transformation methods for placement are tagged to B1 or given from general knowledge; B1 was not opened in this build, and no library was consulted. The formula itself is verified for this chapter's examples by runs R1 and R3 (placed poles equal the requested ones).

How the hardware actually does it

On the robot, u = −Kx is four multiplications and three additions per sample, a tiny cost even for a small microcontroller. The difficulties are elsewhere. The state must be known: x and θ come from encoders, while v and ω must be estimated (F9-63; the forensic lab of F9-63 shows what crude differences do). The loop must run on time: the gains were designed for exactly T = 0.01 s, and a late or skipped sample changes Ad and Bd. The force is limited by the motor driver, and the sign of every sensor must match the model: F9-59's forensic lab showed one wrong sign in B. Finally, the design here assumes the force acts instantly; a delay between computing and applying it is F9-62's subject. D1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. All results come from the simulated cart-pole of F9-59 with exercise values (M = 1.0 kg, m = 0.2 kg, l = 0.5 m, b = 0.1 N·s/m) and an exercise force limit of 10 N. No rig was used. A real cart-pole's mass, length, friction and force limit must be measured or taken from the datasheets of the parts in your kit, recorded in the dossier, and the design repeated with those values.

Safety

A balancing rig moves fast and can hit its end stops hard; a mis-signed gain makes it run away at full force. Before the first closed-loop test: set the driver's current limit, fit end-stop switches that cut the motor, keep the emergency stop in your hand, and run the same code in the simulator first. Follow safety and BR-08.

Worked example

Task. The cart of MA301 F0-67 (A = [[0, 1], [0, −0.4]], B = [0, 0.5]ᵀ, state position and speed) should return to its target with poles at −2 ± 2j. Find K by matching coefficients.

  1. With K = [k1, k2]: BK = [[0, 0], [0.5k1, 0.5k2]], so A − BK = [[0, 1], [−0.5k1, −0.4 − 0.5k2]].
  2. Characteristic polynomial: det(sI − A + BK) = s(s + 0.4 + 0.5k2) + 0.5k1 = s² + (0.4 + 0.5k2)s + 0.5k1.
  3. Desired polynomial: (s + 2 − 2j)(s + 2 + 2j) = (s + 2)² + 4 = s² + 4s + 8.
  4. Match: 0.5k1 = 8 gives k1 = 16; 0.4 + 0.5k2 = 4 gives k2 = 7.2.
  5. Meaning: 16 N per metre of position error, 7.2 N per m/s of speed. The cart's own friction (0.4) already supplies some damping, so k2 only has to add the rest.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nadia learns her rule over weeks, by feel, and keeps adjusting it. A pole-placement gain is computed once from a model and does not adapt; if the model is wrong, the poles are not where you put them.
    • 

  • A rider uses different rules at different speeds and leans. Our K is one linear rule, valid near upright; far from it the nonlinear terms of F9-59 matter.
    • 

  • Nadia's muscles tire gradually. A motor driver has a hard limit: below it, the design works as computed; above it, the force simply stops growing.

Summary


    

  • State feedback u = −Kx gives the closed loop A − BK; if (A, B) is controllable, every pole set can be placed.
    • 

  • For one input the gain is unique: match coefficients by hand or use Ackermann's formula.
    • 

  • Design in z for a sampled controller: map the chosen s with z = esT, then place for (Ad, Bd).
    • 

  • The cart-pole's gains have a surprising sign on position: it must move away first (wrong-way response).
    • 

  • Faster poles cost force steeply (about α4 on the position gain here); a 10 N limit dropped the 3× design.
    • 

  • Poles promise nothing about saturation, noise, delay or model error: check every design by simulation.
    •
