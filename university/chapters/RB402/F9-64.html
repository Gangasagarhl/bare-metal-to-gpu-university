Story hook

Analogy

The last test of Nadia's summer: ride down the path and stop with her front wheel on a chalk line, one metre before the garden wall. Her brakes are weak. On her first try she rides as she always does, reacting to where she is: she pedals hard while the line is far away, brakes when she reaches it, and rolls into the hedge in front of the wall. On her second try she does something new. While riding she keeps asking: "If I keep going like this, where will I be in two seconds? Can my weak brakes still stop me before the wall?" The moment the answer starts to be "only just", she brakes, and she stops on the line.

Looking ahead with a model of your own body, planning within your limits, and re-planning every moment is model predictive control. The LQR of F9-62 is a reflex: a fixed rule from the present state. MPC is a plan, recomputed many times a second, that knows about the brakes' strength and the wall. This chapter explains the idea at concept level, with a small solver you can read, and shows the failure that comes from looking ahead too little.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the receding-horizon principle: predict, optimise over a horizon, apply the first move, measure, repeat.
  • Formulate a small MPC problem: prediction model, quadratic cost, input limits and a soft state limit, as a quadratic program in the future inputs.
  • Relate MPC to LQR: show that they agree when no limit is active and the terminal cost is the Riccati solution.
  • Judge the practical costs: computation per sample, solver accuracy, horizon length.
  • Diagnose a horizon that is too short for the system's stopping distance.
Jargon box
Model predictive control (MPC)
Simple words: at every sample, use a model to plan the best sequence of inputs for the next stretch of time, respecting the limits; apply only the first input; repeat. Analogy: Nadia asking "if I keep going like this, can I still stop?" every moment. Precise: a feedback law u[k] = u0*(x[k]) where u0* is the first element of the minimiser of a finite-horizon optimal-control problem with constraints, solved online from the current state. D1
Prediction horizon
Simple words: how far ahead the controller plans. Analogy: how far down the path Nadia looks. Precise: the number N of future samples (N·T seconds) over which the model predicts and the cost is summed. D1
Receding horizon
Simple words: the planning window slides forward one sample every time a new measurement arrives, so the plan is always for "the next N samples from now". Analogy: the stretch of path Nadia looks at moves with her. Precise: at sample k the problem covers k … k+N; only u[k] is applied; at k+1 a new problem over k+1 … k+N+1 is solved from the measured x[k+1]. D1
Constraint (hard and soft)
Simple words: a limit the plan must respect. A hard limit can never be crossed in the plan; a soft limit can be crossed at a high price. Analogy: the brakes' strength is hard (it is what it is); "don't touch the hedge" is soft (better not, but not impossible). Precise: hard: u[k] ∈ [−umax, umax] imposed exactly; soft: a penalty ρ·max(0, x − xmax)² added to the cost, which keeps the problem solvable even when the limit cannot be met. D1
Quadratic program (QP)
Simple words: an optimisation problem with a quadratic score and straight-line limits; the kind of problem a linear MPC must solve at every sample. Analogy: finding the lowest point of a smooth bowl, inside a fenced field. Precise: minimise UᵀHU + 2xᵀFᵀU subject to linear inequalities on U, with H symmetric positive definite. D1
Terminal cost
Simple words: a price on where the plan ends, standing in for everything after the horizon. Analogy: "and after those two seconds, how much trouble am I still in?" Precise: a term x[N]ᵀPx[N] in the cost; with P the solution of the LQR's Riccati equation, it is the exact unconstrained cost-to-go from x[N]. D1B1
Warm start
Simple words: starting the solver from the previous plan, shifted by one sample, instead of from zero. Analogy: Nadia keeps most of her plan from a moment ago and only adjusts it. Precise: initialising the iterative QP solver at (u1*, …, uN−1*, uN−1*) from the previous sample; it cuts the iterations needed when the state changes little between samples. D1R3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The controllers so far were like reflexes: look at where you are now, push by a fixed rule. A reflex does not know that the motor is weak or that there is a wall ahead. It pushes as hard as the rule says, the motor gives what it can, and by the time the rule says "stop!", it is too late to stop.

A planning controller is different. Every twentieth of a second it imagines the next two seconds: "if I push like this, then like this, where will I be?" It tries many plans in its head, keeps the best one that never asks the motor for more than it can give and never reaches the wall, and does only the first step of that plan. Then it looks again and plans again. Planning takes much more computing than a reflex, and it only looks as far ahead as you let it.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The task, and why a clipped LQR fails it

Take the simplest robot from F9-59: a cart of 1 kg with friction 0.1 N·s/m, state (x, v), sampled at T = 0.05 s. It must move from x = 0 to a parking spot at 1.0 m. A wall stands at 1.1 m, and the motor gives at most 2 N. Listing 2 first designs an LQR with Q = diag(100, 1) and R = 0.01 on the error from the parking spot (K = [65.30, 13.07]) and clips its output to ±2 N. R1

The LQR wants about 65 N at the start; the motor gives 2. The clipped controller pushes at +2 N until it is nearly at the spot (it switches between 0.75 s and 1.0 s in the log), then brakes at −2 N. But the LQR's rule was designed for unlimited force: it planned to brake with 60 N at the last moment. With 2 N, braking from 1.4 m/s takes 0.7 s and about half a metre. The cart reaches 1.337 m, 24 cm into the wall, and parks only at 2.95 s. The total cost (the same Σ(eᵀQe + Ru²) the LQR minimises) is 1306.66. R1D1

Predictions as a function of the plan

From the current state x0 and a candidate plan U = (u0, …, uN−1), the sampled model predicts every future state: x1 = Adx0 + Bdu0, x2 = Ad²x0 + AdBdu0 + Bdu1, and so on. Stacked, X = Sxx0 + SuU: the predictions are linear in the plan. (Su is built from the same blocks AdjBd as the controllability matrix of F9-60.) The cost Σ(xkᵀQxk + Ruk²) + xNᵀPxN is therefore a quadratic function of U, and the limits are simple bounds on U and linear bounds on X. That is a quadratic program. D1

The MPC loop

At every sample: measure (or estimate, F9-63) the state; solve the QP for the best plan that respects |uk| ≤ 2 N and keeps the predicted x below the wall; apply u0; discard the rest (or keep it as a warm start); repeat. Listing 2 uses a horizon of 40 samples (2 s) and the LQR's Riccati matrix P as terminal cost. Run R1: the MPC is already braking at 0.75 s, while the clipped LQR is still accelerating, because its prediction shows that 2 N cannot stop it later. The cart peaks at 1.043 m, below the wall, parks at 1.90 s and pays a cost of 1161.32, less than the clipped LQR's 1306.66 even by the LQR's own measure. R1

The wall is a soft limit here: a large penalty (weight 1000) on any predicted position beyond 1.1 m. A hard state limit could make the QP impossible to solve (if the cart is already too fast to stop, no plan avoids the wall); a soft one always has an answer and makes the controller do its best. The force limit is hard, because it is physics. D1

MPC and LQR agree when nothing limits them

Without active limits and with terminal cost P from the Riccati equation, the MPC's first move is exactly the LQR's −Kx: the terminal cost already tells the plan the exact optimal cost of everything after the horizon. Run R3 checks it from an error of −0.01 m (far from any limit): LQR −Ke = 0.65303 N, MPC with no wall weight 0.65302 N. So MPC is a generalisation of LQR: identical in the middle of the operating range, and different where limits bite. R3D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The condensed QP

With X = Sxx0 + SuU and the block-diagonal weight Q̄ = diag(Q, …, Q, P), the cost is J(U) = (Sxx0 + SuU)ᵀQ̄(Sxx0 + SuU) + R UᵀU = UᵀHU + 2x0ᵀFᵀU + constant, with H = SuᵀQ̄Su + RI and F = SuᵀQ̄Sx. H depends only on the model and the weights, so it is built once; each sample only needs Fx0. Without limits the minimiser is U* = −H−1Fx0, a linear gain, and its first row is the LQR gain (when P is the Riccati solution). With limits there is no formula; an iterative solver is needed. D1

This "condensed" form eliminates the states. The alternative keeps x1 … xN as variables with the model as equality constraints; the problem is larger but sparse, which suits long horizons and large models. D1

The teaching solver and its accuracy

Listing 1 solves the QP by projected gradient with momentum (the method known as FISTA): step downhill along the gradient 2(HU + Fx0) plus the soft-wall term, clip each uk to [−umax, umax] (the projection), add a momentum term, repeat a fixed number of times. The step size 1/(2λmax) comes from the largest eigenvalue of H plus the wall term, found by power iteration. Every step is simple and has a fixed cost, which suits a real-time loop. D1

The weakness is accuracy. Run R3 starts the same problem from zero (cold start) with the wall weight 1000: after 300 iterations the first move is 0.28231 N, less than half the true optimum (about 0.653 N); after 3000, 0.65037; after 30,000, 0.65288. The stiff soft-wall term makes λmax large and the steps small. In the closed loop of Listing 2 this matters much less, because the warm start begins each solve from the previous plan, which is already nearly right; the closed loop peaked 4.3 cm past the target and 5.7 cm before the wall. But a fixed iteration budget means the controller applies an approximate optimum, and you must check how approximate. R3R1

Computation

Each iteration of Listing 1 does at least one N × N matrix-vector product: 1600 multiply-adds for N = 40, and the soft-wall loop adds about as much again. At 300 iterations per sample that is at least 480,000 multiply-adds every 0.05 s, compared with 2 for the LQR's u = −Kx on this cart. Halving T or doubling N multiplies the work. This is why MPC first spread where systems are slow (process plants with time constants of minutes), and why fast robots use carefully engineered solvers, short horizons, or MPC laws computed offline. R3D1

Horizon length, terminal cost and stability

A short horizon is cheap but short-sighted: the plan cannot "see" a limit that only matters later. The terminal cost P tells the plan what happens after the horizon, but P was computed for the unconstrained LQR: it assumes unlimited braking. So if the stopping distance under the real force limit is longer than the horizon covers, the plan thinks it can stop when it cannot. The forensic lab shows exactly this. A rule from this example: the horizon should cover at least the time needed to stop from the highest speed under the force limit, with margin. For the cart: vmax/amax = 1.54 m/s ÷ 2 m/s² ≈ 0.77 s, so N·T should exceed about 0.8 s (N ≥ 16 at T = 0.05 s). The rule is conservative: it guarantees that a full stop fits inside every plan. Run R3 sweeps the horizon: N = 5 reaches 1.186 m (through the wall), N = 10 reaches 1.071 m, N = 15 1.044 m and N = 20 1.043 m. A 0.5 s horizon was enough here, because the plan saw the wall early enough to start braking, and the terminal cost covered the rest; but the margin shrank from 5.7 cm to 2.9 cm, and the rule tells you where you can stop worrying. D1R2R3

Unlike LQR, an MPC loop is not automatically stable, and the QP is not automatically solvable at the next sample. The theory adds two ingredients: a terminal cost that is a valid Lyapunov function near the target, and a terminal constraint (the plan must end in a region where the unconstrained LQR is known to respect all limits). With both, stability and "recursive feasibility" can be proved. This course treats them at concept level only. D1

Beyond this chapter

Real MPC applications add features this concept chapter leaves out: an observer or Kalman filter for the state (F9-63) and for slowly changing disturbances (to remove steady errors); nonlinear models solved by sequential QPs; separate control and prediction horizons; rate limits on Δu; and explicit MPC, where the QP's solution is precomputed for regions of the state space and stored as a lookup of affine laws. The structure of Figure 3 is the same in all of them. D1

How the hardware actually does it

On a robot, the optimiser must finish inside the sample period, every time, with time left for sensing, estimation and communication. Iterative solvers do not have a fixed run time unless you fix the iteration count, as Listing 1 does; then the answer's accuracy varies instead. A safe design measures the worst-case solve time on the target processor, runs the solver with a hard deadline, and has a fallback (the previous plan's next move, or an LQR) if the deadline is missed. The solve time of Listing 1 was not measured in this build; the lab asks you to measure it on your computer. D1U1

MPC's limits are only as good as the model. The wall in Listing 2 is a number in the cost, and the cart model has no mass error or delay; on a real cart a model error or an unmodelled delay (F9-62) moves the real trajectory away from the predicted one, and the prediction that "we stay 5.7 cm before the wall" is no guarantee. R1D1

Safety

A soft limit in an MPC cost is not a safety device. It can be crossed by design (it is soft), by model error, by a solver that ran out of iterations, or by a horizon that is too short (forensic lab). Physical end stops, limit switches that cut the motor, and a current limit in the driver must protect a real rig independently of the controller (safety, BR-08).

Worked example

Task. A scalar system xk+1 = xk + uk, cost Σ(xk+1² + uk²) over the horizon with terminal weight 1, from x0 = 3. Find the MPC's first move for N = 1 and N = 2, with no limit and with |u| ≤ 1.

  1. N = 1: minimise u0² + (3 + u0)². Derivative 2u0 + 2(3 + u0) = 0 gives u0 = −1.5.
  2. N = 2: minimise u0² + u1² + (3 + u0)² + (3 + u0 + u1)².
  3. Partial derivatives: u0: 2u0 + 2(3 + u0) + 2(3 + u0 + u1) = 0, i.e. 3u0 + u1 = −6; u1: 2u1 + 2(3 + u0 + u1) = 0, i.e. u0 + 2u1 = −3.
  4. Solve: from the second, u1 = −(3 + u0)/2; substitute: 3u0 − (3 + u0)/2 = −6, so 5u0 = −9, u0 = −1.8 and u1 = −0.6.
  5. The longer horizon acts harder now (−1.8 instead of −1.5), because it sees that the error left after one step will cost again.
  6. With |u| ≤ 1, both unconstrained answers violate the limit; the convex cost decreases towards them, so the constrained optimum sits on the bound: u0 = −1 (and u1 = −1 for N = 2). Clipping the unconstrained plan happens to give the same here; in general it does not, because one input's limit changes what the others should be.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nadia plans with a rough, flexible picture; the MPC plans with an exact linear model, and it is exactly as wrong as the model is.
    • 

  • Nadia's planning takes no visible time. The MPC's planning takes a measurable share of every sample, and if it does not finish, there is no plan.
    • 

  • Nadia looks as far ahead as she needs to. The MPC looks exactly N samples ahead, no further, unless the terminal cost tells it the truth about what lies beyond.

Forensic lab


Scenario: "The cart that hit the wall"


A team ran the parking MPC on a small embedded board. To save computing time, a team member shortened the horizon from 20 to 5 samples. In bench tests with small moves everything looked the same. On the first full 1 m move, the cart hit the soft wall's position. The team believes the solver has a bug, because "every plan it printed stayed under the wall". You have the log of both configurations; for the short horizon, every second sample shows the plan's predictions.

Summary


    

  • MPC plans an input sequence over a horizon with a model, applies the first move, and re-plans at the next sample.
    • 

  • For linear models and quadratic costs, each plan is a quadratic program in the future inputs.
    • 

  • With no active limits and the Riccati P as terminal cost, MPC reproduces the LQR exactly (0.65302 against 0.65303 N).
    • 

  • With a 2 N limit and a wall, the MPC parked below the wall (1.043 m) where the clipped LQR went through it (1.337 m).
    • 

  • The price: hundreds of thousands of multiply-adds per sample here, approximate solutions from fixed iteration counts, and a run-time deadline.
    • 

  • The horizon must cover the stopping time under the limits; 5 samples hit the wall, 20 did not.
