Story hook

Analogy

Tomás is teaching his younger cousin Nadia to ride a bicycle in the park. She asks him a good question: "How do you know which way to steer?" He thinks about it. Knowing where she is on the path is not enough. He also has to know how fast she is going, how far the bike is leaning, and whether the lean is getting bigger or smaller. With those four things he can say what the next second will bring, and what to do about it. Without any one of them he is guessing.

That short list, everything you must know now to predict the next moment, is what engineers call the state. This course builds a small robot that has the same problem as Nadia: a cart on a track that must keep a pole standing upright, the way a rider keeps a bicycle upright. In this chapter you write down its state and the rules that move it forward in time. Every later chapter of RB402 steers with those rules.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a state-space model x′ = Ax + Bu, y = Cx + Du from equations of motion, choosing and ordering the state variables.
  • Linearise a nonlinear model about an equilibrium and say over what range the linear model can be trusted, with evidence from a simulation.
  • Compute the open-loop poles as eigenvalues of A and explain each one physically.
  • Discretise a model for a controller that runs every T seconds (zero-order hold) and check that z = esT.
  • Convert a state-space model into a transfer function and back to the ideas of MA301.
  • Diagnose a sign error in a model from a push-test log.
Jargon box
State vector
Simple words: the short list of numbers that tells you everything about a machine right now that matters for what it does next. Analogy: where the rider is, how fast she goes, how far the bike leans, and how fast the lean grows. Precise: a vector x(t) ∈ ℝn such that x(t0) and the input u on [t0, t] determine x(t) and the output for all t ≥ t0; MA301 F0-67 introduced it as the state of a dynamic system. B1U1
State-space model (A, B, C, D)
Simple words: four tables of numbers that say how the state changes and what the sensors see. Analogy: the rider's inner rule book: "if I lean this much and push the pedal this hard, next moment I will lean that much". Precise: the linear time-invariant model x′ = Ax + Bu, y = Cx + Du with A ∈ ℝn×n (dynamics), B ∈ ℝn×m (input), C ∈ ℝp×n (output), D ∈ ℝp×m (direct feed-through). B1
Equilibrium (operating point)
Simple words: a state where the machine would stay forever if nothing disturbed it. Analogy: the bicycle perfectly upright; a breath of wind tips it over, so it is an unstable equilibrium. Precise: a pair (xe, ue) with f(xe, ue) = 0 for x′ = f(x, u). B1
Linearisation (about an equilibrium)
Simple words: replacing a curved rule by a straight-line rule that is good near one point. Analogy: the rider's rule "a little more lean needs a little more correction", which is only true for small leans. Precise: for small deviations δx = x − xe, δu = u − ue, δx′ ≈ A δx + B δu with A = ∂f/∂x and B = ∂f/∂u evaluated at (xe, ue) (Jacobian matrices). B1D1
Eigenvalue and eigenvector
Simple words: special directions in which the system just grows or shrinks (or spins) at its own rate, without turning into another direction. Analogy: the "falling over" motion of the bike, which grows on its own, at its own speed. Precise: λ ∈ ℂ and v ≠ 0 with Av = λv; the eigenvalues are the roots of det(λI − A) = 0. For x′ = Ax they are the poles of the system (MA301 F0-69). B1D1
Matrix exponential
Simple words: the rule that carries the state forward by a chosen time when nobody pushes. Analogy: "if I do nothing for one second, this is where I will be". Precise: eAt = I + At + (At)²/2! + (At)³/3! + …; the solution of x′ = Ax is x(t) = eAtx(0). B1D1
Discrete-time state-space model (Ad, Bd)
Simple words: the same rule book, written for a computer that looks and acts only every T seconds. Analogy: a rider who blinks: she sees only snapshots and holds her steering still between them. Precise: x[k+1] = Adx[k] + Bdu[k] with Ad = eAT and Bd = (∫0T eAτdτ) B, exact when u is held constant over each period (zero-order hold, MA302 F0-79). B1D1
Cart-pole (inverted pendulum on a cart)
Simple words: a cart that must keep a stick standing upright by moving under it. Analogy: balancing a bicycle by steering under its lean. Precise: a cart of mass M on a horizontal track, driven by a horizontal force F, carrying a pole hinged at the cart; in this course a massless rod of length l with a point mass m at its end; four states (x, v, θ, ω), one input F. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A rider does not need to remember her whole trip to know what happens next. Four numbers are enough: where she is, how fast she goes, how far she leans and how fast the lean changes. Engineers write such numbers as one list, the state. They then write a rule book: "from this state, with this push, the next state will be that". With the rule book a computer can look ahead, and that is what it needs to steer.

The real rule book of a falling pole is curved: the further it leans, the faster it falls, and not in a straight line. Near upright the curve is almost straight, and a straight rule is much easier to work with. This chapter writes the straight rule, checks how far it can be trusted, and turns it into the form a computer that looks only every hundredth of a second can use.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From equations of motion to state space

Any lumped mechanical or electrical model can be written as a set of first-order differential equations x′ = f(x, u) with an output y = h(x, u). The recipe is: pick the variables whose derivatives appear (positions and velocities for mechanics, capacitor voltages and inductor currents for circuits), make each second derivative the derivative of a velocity, and stack everything into one vector. MA301 F0-67 did this for a cart with friction, m·v′ = F − c·v: with x = (position, velocity) it is x′ = [[0, 1], [0, −c/m]] x + [0, 1/m] F. When f and h are linear the model is x′ = Ax + Bu, y = Cx + Du. B1U1

A transfer function (MA301 F0-70) describes only the path from input to output. A state-space model describes the inside: every variable that stores energy. That is why it is the natural form for robots with many sensors and actuators, for simulation, and for the designs of the rest of this course: controllability (F9-60), pole placement (F9-61), LQR (F9-62) and observers (F9-63) are all statements about A, B and C. B1

The cart-pole equations

Put the cart at position x and the pole's point mass at (x + l sin θ, l cos θ), with θ measured from straight up. The kinetic energy is T = ½(M + m)v² + m l cos θ · v ω + ½ m l² ω² and the potential energy is V = m g l cos θ. Lagrange's equations, with the force F and the friction −b v acting on the cart coordinate, give two equations: D1B2

(M + m) x″ + m l cos θ · θ″ − m l sin θ · ω² = F − b v
cos θ · x″ + l θ″ = g sin θ

The first is Newton's law for the whole machine in the x direction; the second says the pole's mass accelerates sideways according to gravity's pull, less the acceleration of the hinge under it. Solving the two for x″ and θ″ gives the nonlinear model x′ = f(x, F) coded in deriv() of Listing 2. Because the derivation can hide sign errors, check it: with b = 0 and F = 0 no energy enters or leaves, so T + V must stay constant. Run R3 simulates 2 s of a large swing and finds a largest relative change of 1.8×10−14, rounding-level. D1R3

Linearising about upright

Upright and at rest, x = (x0, 0, 0, 0) with F = 0 is an equilibrium for any position x0. For small θ and ω: sin θ ≈ θ, cos θ ≈ 1, and the term m l sin θ ω² is a product of small quantities, so it is dropped. The two equations become (M + m)x″ + m l θ″ = F − b v and x″ + l θ″ = g θ. Solving them:

x″ = (F − b v − m g θ) / M
θ″ = ((M + m) g θ − F + b v) / (M l)

so with the state (x, v, θ, ω) and input F:

A = [[0, 1, 0, 0], [0, −b/M, −m g/M, 0], [0, 0, 0, 1], [0, b/(M l), (M+m) g/(M l), 0]], B = [0, 1/M, 0, −1/(M l)]ᵀ

With the course's values this is the matrix printed by Listing 3: a23 = −1.962, a43 = 23.544, b2 = 1, b4 = −2. The signs carry physics: pushing the cart forward (F > 0) makes the pole rotate backwards (b4 < 0), and a forward lean (θ > 0) pulls the cart backwards (a23 < 0). If you balance a stick on your palm, you feel both. D1R1

How far can the straight-line model be trusted? Listing 4 releases the pole with no force and compares the two models (Figure 3). From 0.05 rad the linear prediction of θ is within 1.5 % of the nonlinear one for 0.6 s, by which time the pole has passed 0.45 rad. From 0.30 rad the error is 3.6 % after 0.3 s and 27 % after 0.6 s. The rule of thumb this gives for this machine: the linear model is good while |θ| stays below a few tenths of a radian; a controller designed with it must keep the pole there. R2

Open-loop poles: what the eigenvalues say

Maths you need here

MA201 taught matrices, products and inverses but not eigenvalues. Here is the minimum. A number λ is an eigenvalue of a square matrix A if Av = λv for some non-zero vector v (an eigenvector). Rearranged, (λI − A)v = 0 has a non-zero solution only if det(λI − A) = 0. For an n×n matrix this determinant is a polynomial of degree n in λ, the characteristic polynomial; its n roots (some may be complex, in conjugate pairs) are the eigenvalues. For x′ = Ax, an initial state along an eigenvector v stays along v and is multiplied by eλt: it decays if Re λ < 0 and grows if Re λ > 0. These are exactly the poles of MA301 F0-69 and F0-71. B1D1

Listing 3 computes det(sI − A) = s⁴ + 0.1 s³ − 23.544 s² − 1.962 s and its roots 4.844, 0, −0.083 and −4.861 (1/s). Each has a meaning: R1

  • +4.844: the pole falling over. A lean grows like e4.844t, doubling every ln 2 / 4.844 = 0.143 s. This is why balancing is hard: anything slower than about a tenth of a second loses. R1
  • −4.861: its mirror image, a lean that is already moving back towards upright at just the right rate dies away.
  • 0: the cart's position. Nothing pulls the cart back to any particular place, so every position is an equilibrium; there is no constant term in the polynomial (Listing 3 prints it as −0.0000, a rounding residue of exactly zero).
  • −0.083: a slow motion in which friction bleeds off the cart's speed.

Without friction (Listing 5) the poles become exactly ±√((M + m)g/(M l)) = ±4.852 and a double pole at 0. Friction changes them only a little; gravity and geometry dominate. R3D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The solution of a linear model

For x′ = Ax + Bu the solution is x(t) = eAtx(0) + ∫0t eA(t−τ)B u(τ) dτ. The first term is the free motion, the second the forced motion; they add because the system is linear (superposition). If A has n independent eigenvectors V = [v1 … vn], then A = V Λ V−1 with Λ = diag(λi), and eAt = V eΛt V−1: the free motion is a mix of the modes eλitvi. This is the precise version of "each pole contributes ept" from MA301. When eigenvectors are missing (repeated eigenvalues, as for the double pole at 0 without friction) terms such as t·eλt appear; the frictionless cart's position then grows linearly with t at constant speed. B1D1

Discretisation for a sampled controller

The controllers of this course run every T = 0.01 s (100 Hz, an exercise choice). Between samples a digital-to-analogue output or a PWM register holds the force constant: a zero-order hold (MA302 F0-79). Integrating the solution above over one period with u constant gives the exact discrete model

x[k+1] = Ad x[k] + Bd u[k], Ad = e^(AT), Bd = (∫₀ᵀ e^(Aτ) dτ) B

Both blocks can be read off one larger matrix exponential: exp([[A, B], [0, 0]]·T) = [[Ad, Bd], [0, I]]. Listing 1's c2d does exactly this, and expm evaluates the exponential by scaling the matrix down by a power of two until it is small, summing a Taylor series, and squaring back up. B1D1U1

If λ is an eigenvalue of A, then eλT is an eigenvalue of Ad with the same eigenvector. Run R1 confirms it: the discrete poles 1.0496, 1.0000, 0.9992 and 0.9526 equal esT of the continuous ones to six digits. Stability moves from "Re s < 0" to "|z| < 1" (MA302 F0-79). Note how close every z is to 1: at 100 Hz the state changes little per sample; the unstable mode grows by 4.96 % per sample. R1D1

Watch out

Forward Euler, Ad ≈ I + AT, Bd ≈ BT, is tempting and is wrong by a term of order T². For a stable but fast pole it can even produce |z| > 1 (MA301 F0-67 showed the step-size limit). Use the exact zero-order-hold model for design; use Euler only when you have checked the error is small for your T. U1

From state space to a transfer function, and back

Taking Laplace transforms with x(0) = 0 gives sX = AX + BU, so X = (sI − A)−1BU and

G(s) = C (sI − A)^(−1) B + D

Every entry of (sI − A)−1 has det(sI − A) as denominator, so the poles of G are among the eigenvalues of A. "Among": some eigenvalues may cancel and not appear in G at all. F9-60 shows that this happens exactly when part of the state cannot be moved by the input or seen at the output. Going the other way, one transfer function has infinitely many state-space realisations: for any invertible T, the coordinates z = T−1x give A′ = T−1AT, B′ = T−1B, C′ = CT with the same G(s) and the same eigenvalues. Choose physical coordinates when you can; they make the numbers mean something and make sign errors visible. B1D1

For the frictionless cart-pole the angle row gives θ(s)/F(s) = −(1/(M l)) / (s² − (M + m)g/(M l)) = −2/(s² − 23.544): a pair of real poles at ±4.852, one in the right half-plane, and a negative gain. No proportional controller on θ alone can balance it and also bring the cart back, because θ alone does not tell you where the cart is; F9-60 makes that precise. D1R3

Computing eigenvalues honestly

Listing 1 finds eigenvalues by forming the characteristic polynomial (the Faddeev–LeVerrier recursion) and finding its roots (the Durand–Kerner iteration that MA301 F0-71 used). That is short and fine for the four- to eight-state models of this course, but polynomial roots are very sensitive to rounding when roots cluster or n grows (MA302 F0-78 on conditioning). F9-63 meets this limit and uses a coefficient comparison instead. For larger models use a numerical library routine designed for eigenvalue problems, and test it against known cases. U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The general facts of Layer 3 (the solution formula, diagonalisation of eAt, the block-matrix exponential for zero-order hold, non-uniqueness of realisations) are tagged to B1, which was not opened in this build; they are standard linear-systems results and the numerical cases used here are recomputed by runs R1 and R3. Which eigenvalue algorithm a particular numerical library uses, and its accuracy guarantees, must be taken from that library's own documentation for the version you install; none was consulted here.

How the hardware actually does it

In a robot the state vector is four numbers in the controller's memory, and the model is a few small arrays. Every period the controller reads its sensors, builds or estimates the state, computes the force and writes it to the motor driver, which holds it until the next period: the zero-order hold made physical. The arithmetic for a four-state model is tiny. The hard parts are elsewhere. Only some states are measured (a cart encoder and a pole-angle encoder give x and θ, not v and ω; F9-63 builds the rest). The numbers in A and B come from masses, lengths and friction that must be measured on the real rig, and from the motor's conversion of current to force (F1-69, F1-70). And the "hold for exactly T" assumption is only as good as the software's timing (RB304, F9-45 to F9-48). U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. No cart-pole rig was used in this build; every number in this chapter belongs to the simulated cart-pole with exercise values. A real rig's masses, pole inertia (a real rod is not massless), friction (often not viscous) and motor constants must be measured and the model rebuilt; check its predictions against a logged push test as in the forensic lab before designing with it.

Safety

A real cart-pole has a cart that can accelerate hard and a pole that falls. Work in the simulator first. On a real rig: end stops on the track, a physical emergency stop within reach that cuts motor power, hands and hair clear of the track, a force or current limit set in the motor driver, and nobody leaning over the pole. Follow the faculty safety rules (safety) and the sim-to-real bridge BR-08.

Worked example

Task. The cart of MA301 F0-67 has m = 2 kg and viscous friction c = 0.8 N·s/m. (a) Write its state-space model with position as the output. (b) Find its transfer function from state space. (c) Discretise it for a controller with T = 0.1 s, by hand.

  1. State (x, v): x′ = v and v′ = (F − c v)/m = −0.4 v + 0.5 F. So A = [[0, 1], [0, −0.4]], B = [0, 0.5]ᵀ, C = [1, 0], D = 0.
  2. sI − A = [[s, −1], [0, s + 0.4]], with determinant s(s + 0.4). Its inverse is [[s + 0.4, 1], [0, s]] / (s(s + 0.4)). Then C(sI − A)−1B = [1, 0] · [0.5, 0.5 s]ᵀ / (s(s + 0.4)) = 0.5 / (s(s + 0.4)). Poles 0 (position) and −0.4 (friction). Run R3 checks it at s = 1 and s = 2: 0.357143 and 0.104167 both ways.
  3. Over one period with F held, the velocity obeys a first-order equation with time constant 2.5 s: v(T) = e−0.4Tv + 0.5(1 − e−0.4T)/0.4 · F. With e−0.04 = 0.960789: Ad,22 = 0.960789 and Bd,2 = 1.25 × 0.039211 = 0.049013.
  4. Integrating the velocity: x(T) = x + (1 − e−0.4T)/0.4 · v + 0.5(T/0.4 − (1 − e−0.4T)/0.16) F. So Ad,12 = 0.098026 and Bd,1 = 0.5 × (0.25 − 0.245066) = 0.002467.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A rider feels her lean all the time and through several senses at once. The controller sees only what its sensors measure, only every T seconds; velocities in particular are not measured at all on most rigs (F9-63).
    • 

  • A rider's inner rule book is learned, curved and adapts when she carries a heavy bag. The state-space model is linear, fixed and only valid near upright (Figure 3); if the load changes, someone must change the numbers.
    • 

  • Four numbers are enough for this cart-pole only because the model leaves things out: a flexible pole, a stretching belt, the motor's own electrical state. A real bicycle and rider have many more states. Choosing the state is a modelling decision, and leaving out a fast state can bite (the forensic lab of F9-62 is one example).

Troubleshooting


    

  • Energy drifts by more than 10−6 → a sign or factor error in deriv, or too large an RK4 step; halve the step and see whether the drift changes (integration error) or not (equation error).
    • 

  • eig prints a pair like 0.0000+0.0003j where you expected a double zero → the root finder's accuracy near repeated roots (Layer 3); compare with the characteristic polynomial instead.
    • 

  • Linear and nonlinear disagree from the very first sample → the state order or a sign differs between cartA and deriv (forensic lab).

Summary


    

  • A state-space model x′ = Ax + Bu, y = Cx + Du describes every internal variable; the state is what you must know now to predict the future.
    • 

  • Nonlinear models are linearised about an equilibrium with Jacobians; check the range of validity against a nonlinear simulation (here: good while |θ| stays below a few tenths of a radian).
    • 

  • Eigenvalues of A are the poles: the cart-pole has +4.844 (falling, doubling every 0.143 s), −4.861, 0 (position) and −0.083 (friction) per second.
    • 

  • A sampled controller sees x[k+1] = Adx[k] + Bdu[k] with Ad = eAT; poles map by z = esT.
    • 

  • G(s) = C(sI − A)−1B + D; its poles are among the eigenvalues of A.
    • 

  • Signs and the order of state variables are the most frequent model errors; a push test against a simulation catches them.
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. State (y, y′): A = [[0, 1], [−k/m, −c/m]], B = [0, 1/m]ᵀ, C = [1, 0], D = 0. The spring constant sets a21 (restoring force), the damping sets a22, the mass divides both and sets b2.
    2. 

  2. (b). Every cart position with the pole upright and everything at rest is an equilibrium, so position neither returns nor runs away. (a) is wrong: the +4.844 pole says the pole falls. (c) a zero eigenvalue is a correct feature of this model. (d) B is non-zero.
    3. 

  3. Your words may differ. The linear model replaces sin θ by θ and cos θ by 1 and drops the ω² term. From 0.05 rad the pole stays in the region where these are close for most of the 0.6 s, so errors stay below 2 %. From 0.30 rad it is already where sin θ < θ noticeably and quickly passes 1 rad, where the approximations fail; the linear model over-predicts the fall because sin θ grows more slowly than θ.
    4. 

  4. z = e4.844 × 0.005 = 1.0245; at T = 0.02 s, z = e0.0969 = 1.1017.
    5. 

  5. a43 = (M + m)g/(M l) halves to 11.772; b4 = −1/(M l) halves to −1; the frictionless unstable pole becomes √11.772 = 3.43 1/s (doubling time 0.20 s instead of 0.143 s). The longer pole falls more slowly and is easier to balance, as anyone who has balanced a broom and a pencil knows.
    6. 

  6. (b). Poles of G are eigenvalues of A unless they cancel (F9-60). (a) misses cancellations; (c) is false; (d) confuses D with the dynamics.
    7. 

  7. b4 = −1/(M l) < 0. The force accelerates the hinge forward; the pole's mass, by inertia, stays behind, so relative to the hinge the pole rotates backwards. (The second Lagrange equation says the same: x″ appears with the opposite sign to θ″.)
    8. 

  8. In between, closer to the small case early on: run it to see. The error grows with how far into large angles the motion goes during the window; the third start passes 1 rad within 0.6 s, so the final error is several percent, clearly above the 0.05 rad case.

Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. The cart positions agree to 0.1 mm for the first 0.1 s (0.0081 m at 0.09 s in both). The angles have the same size and opposite sign from the first logged sample (−0.0018 robot, +0.0018 model at 0.03 s), and the difference grows to 0.25 rad by 0.3 s.
    2. 

  2. Equal size and opposite sign right after the push points to the input column of the θ″ row: b4 has the wrong sign (+2 instead of −2). An error in a43 would change the growth rate, not flip the first response, and an error in the cart row would show in x first; x agrees early on.
    3. 

  3. Eigenvalues depend only on A. B's sign does not change them, so "one unstable pole at +4.844" was true of both the correct and the faulty model.
    4. 

  4. Backwards: the hinge accelerates forward under the pole's mass. The robot column (θ negative) is right; the model column is wrong.
    5. 

  5. An automated push test: simulate the nonlinear and the linear model from the same small state with the same small force for a few samples and require agreement within a few percent (Listing 4 is most of it). Run it in continuous integration whenever a model file changes.
    6. 



How the evidence was produced: push_log.cpp builds the correct A and the faulty B with B(3, 0) = +1/(M l), steps the model exactly with c2d, and steps the nonlinear cart-pole with RK4, both with F = 2 N for the first 0.1 s (run R4). Wrong answers and why: "the nonlinear simulation is wrong" — the energy check (run R3) and physical reasoning support it; "the time step is too large" — the disagreement is in sign from the first sample, which no step-size error produces; "a43 is wrong" — the size of the growth agrees in both columns.
