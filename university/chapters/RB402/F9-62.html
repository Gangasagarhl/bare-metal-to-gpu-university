Story hook

Analogy

Nadia now rides well, and Tomás stops giving her rules. Instead he tells her what matters. "On the narrow bridge, staying on the line matters a lot; don't worry about jerking the handlebar. On the gravel path, keep your arms smooth, even if you wander a little." She works out the rest herself: on the bridge she reacts hard to every drift; on gravel she lets small drifts go and steers gently.

That is a different way of designing a controller. In F9-61 you named the speeds of the motion and computed the gains. Here you name the price of each kind of error and the price of effort, and let mathematics find the gains that make the total price as small as possible. The result, the linear-quadratic regulator, is the controller this course uses to balance the cart-pole. The chapter ends with the course's forensic case: a design that balances perfectly in simulation and shakes on the robot, because the robot is late.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a quadratic cost for a control task and choose its weights with Bryson's rule.
  • Compute the LQR gain by iterating the discrete Riccati equation, and explain each term of the equation from the principle of optimality.
  • Predict the cost of a run from x0ᵀPx0, and prove closed-loop stability with the Lyapunov identity.
  • Trade speed against force by scaling R, and check the design against force, track and angle limits.
  • Diagnose an unmodelled loop delay from a log, compute the delay margin, and redesign with the delay in the model.
Jargon box
Quadratic cost (weights Q and R)
Simple words: a score that adds up, at every sample, how far the state is from where you want it and how hard the controller pushes, each squared and weighted. Analogy: Tomás's "staying on the line matters a lot, jerking the bars matters little". Precise: J = Σk≥0 (x[k]ᵀQx[k] + u[k]ᵀRu[k]) with Q symmetric positive semidefinite and R symmetric positive definite. B1
Linear-quadratic regulator (LQR)
Simple words: the state-feedback gain that makes the quadratic cost as small as possible for a linear model. Analogy: the riding style Nadia settles into once she knows what matters. Precise: for x[k+1] = Ax[k] + Bu[k], the minimiser of J over all input sequences is u[k] = −Kx[k] with K = (R + BᵀPB)−1BᵀPA, where P solves the discrete algebraic Riccati equation. B1D1
Discrete algebraic Riccati equation (DARE)
Simple words: the matrix equation whose solution gives the best gain. Analogy: the bookkeeping that tells Nadia what every lean will cost her from now on. Precise: P = Q + AᵀPA − AᵀPB(R + BᵀPB)−1BᵀPA; under stabilisability of (A, B) and detectability of (A, Q1/2) it has exactly one solution P ⪰ 0 that makes A − BK stable. B1
Cost-to-go matrix (value function)
Simple words: a matrix that tells you, for any starting state, the total cost the best controller will still pay. Analogy: how much trouble a given lean is going to be, before you have done anything about it. Precise: J*(x0) = x0ᵀPx0, with P the stabilising DARE solution. D1R1
Bryson's rule
Simple words: a first guess for the weights: one over the square of the largest value you can accept. Analogy: "on this bridge I can drift 20 cm at most" becomes the weight on drift. Precise: Qii = 1/(xi,max)², Rjj = 1/(uj,max)², off-diagonal entries zero; a starting point to be tuned, not an optimum. B1
Lyapunov function (quadratic)
Simple words: an "energy" that is positive everywhere except at rest and always goes down along the motion, which proves the motion settles. Analogy: a measure of trouble that only ever shrinks while Nadia rides. Precise: V(x) = xᵀPx with P ≻ 0 and V(x[k+1]) − V(x[k]) = −x[k]ᵀ(Q + KᵀRK)x[k] < 0 for x ≠ 0 proves asymptotic stability of the closed loop. B1D1
Delay margin
Simple words: how late the controller's commands may arrive before the loop becomes unstable. Analogy: how slow Nadia's reactions can get before she starts to wobble. Precise: the largest delay d (here in samples between computing u[k] and applying it) for which the closed loop with that delay is still stable; for sampled loops, the largest d with spectral radius < 1. B1R3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Instead of telling the robot exactly how fast to react, give it a score to keep low. Every hundredth of a second, the score goes up by a little if the pole leans, a little if the cart is off the middle, and a little if the motor pushes hard. A computer program then finds the reaction rule with the lowest possible total score.

If you say "pushing hard is cheap", the rule reacts fast and pushes hard. If you say "pushing hard is expensive", the rule reacts gently and lets the pole wobble a bit longer. You choose the prices; the maths chooses the rule. One thing the score cannot know about: if the robot's commands arrive late, a rule that was perfect on paper can make the robot shake.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Writing down what matters

For the sampled cart-pole, the cost is J = Σ (x[k]ᵀQx[k] + R u[k]²) summed over all future samples. With a diagonal Q, xᵀQx = qxx² + qvv² + qθθ² + qωω². Bryson's rule gives a first choice: if 0.2 m of cart error, 1 m/s, 0.1 rad and 1 rad/s are each "about as bad as you can accept", and 10 N is the most force you want to use, then Q = diag(1/0.2², 1/1², 1/0.1², 1/1²) = diag(25, 1, 100, 1) and R = 1/10² = 0.01. Each term of the cost is then about 1 when its variable reaches its limit, so the weights compare like with like across units. B1D1

The gain, and what it promises

Listing 2 computes the LQR for these weights. Run R1 gives K = [−42.086, −38.0996, −171.8088, −34.3595] after 608 Riccati iterations, and the closed-loop poles z = 0.9828 ± 0.0125j, 0.9034, 0.8110, which are s = ln(z)/T ≈ −1.73 ± 1.27j, −10.16 and −20.95. Nobody chose those poles; they are where the cost put them. The signs follow the pattern of F9-61: push under the lean, and move away first to go back. R1

The LQR also tells you the price in advance. The matrix P from the Riccati equation gives the minimum cost from any start: x0ᵀPx0. From θ = 0.05 rad this is P33·0.05² = 4084.63 × 0.0025 = 10.2116. Running the nonlinear cart-pole with the 10 N limit from that start, Listing 2 adds up the actual cost: 10.2460, 0.3 % more than the prediction. The force stayed below 10 N (peak 8.59 N), so the only difference is the nonlinearity, which is small at 0.05 rad. R1D1

Turning the knob

Keep Q and multiply R by a factor (Listing 3). Cheap force (R × 0.01) gives large gains (Kθ = −638.6), a peak force of 31.9 N and settling in 1.26 s. Expensive force (R × 100) gives small gains (Kθ = −43.8), 2.19 N and 2.22 s. Figure 2 shows the shape: going from R × 1 to R × 0.01 almost quadruples the force (8.59 N to 31.9 N) to gain 0.12 s. The slowest closed-loop pole hardly moves (−1.735 to −1.343): it belongs to bringing the cart back to the middle, which the cost weights only lightly. R2

Unlike F9-61, every one of these designs is stable, and none needed you to guess pole positions. That is the main practical advantage of LQR: one or two knobs with a physical meaning, and a guaranteed stable result for any positive weights (Layer 3 gives the condition). B1

How far can it lean?

The course lab (Listing 4, in the Lab section) asks a practical question: from what starting lean can each design still recover, with a 10 N motor and a track of ±0.5 m? Run R5 finds 0.342 rad (19.6°) for R × 0.1, 0.353 rad (20.2°) for R × 1, 0.343 rad for R × 10 and 0.306 rad for R × 100. The middle setting is best: harder designs saturate the motor and overshoot; softer designs let the pole lean too far before they act. Bryson's rule landed near the best by matching R to the real limit. R5

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Where the Riccati equation comes from

Suppose the best cost from any state is quadratic, J*(x) = xᵀPx (true for the finite-horizon problem by induction, and in the limit). The principle of optimality says the best first move minimises "cost now plus best cost from where you land":

xᵀPx = min over u of [ xᵀQx + uᵀRu + (Ax + Bu)ᵀ P (Ax + Bu) ]

The bracket is quadratic in u. Setting its gradient to zero gives 2Ru + 2BᵀP(Ax + Bu) = 0, so u = −(R + BᵀPB)−1BᵀPA x = −Kx: the best move is linear in the state. Putting it back and requiring equality for every x gives P = Q + AᵀPA − AᵀPB(R + BᵀPB)−1BᵀPA, the DARE. Listing 1 solves it by doing exactly what the derivation describes: start with P = Q (the cost of zero steps to go), apply the equation to get the cost of one more step, and repeat until P stops changing. B1D1

Why the result is stable

Rearranging the DARE with Acl = A − BK gives the Lyapunov identity AclᵀPAcl − P = −(Q + KᵀRK). Read it along the closed-loop motion: V(x) = xᵀPx falls at each step by xᵀ(Q + KᵀRK)x, which is positive unless x = 0 (given detectability). So V keeps falling and the state goes to zero: a proof of stability without computing a single eigenvalue. Run R3 checks the identity for the cart-pole design: the largest entry of the residual is 3.8×10−7, or 9.3×10−11 relative to the largest entry of P. Such an identity, checked numerically, is the core of the "written stability argument" the course project asks for. B1R3

The conditions matter. If (A, B) is not stabilisable, no gain can stabilise the loop (F9-60). If a mode is unstable and invisible in the cost (Q puts no weight on anything that reveals it), the optimiser sees no reason to stabilise it, because it costs nothing. Weighting every unstable state, or every state, avoids this. B1

Robustness: what is guaranteed, and what is not

Continuous-time LQR with full state feedback is known for strong guaranteed margins (B1 states them; see the box below). Sampled LQR does not carry the same guarantee, so measure. Run R3 scales the gain by a factor κ, applying κK instead of K: the sampled cart-pole loop stays stable for 0.180 < κ < 6.534. So the motor can be almost five times weaker or six times stronger than modelled. That is a comfortable gain margin. R3

Delay is a different story. Figure 3 and run R3 show the spectral radius with a delay of d samples between computing a command and applying it. For this chapter's design (R × 1) the loop is unchanged up to d = 3 (0.9829 to 0.9830) and unstable at d = 4 (1.0176): one extra sample tips it over. The aggressive design R × 0.1 fails at d = 2; R × 10 survives up to d = 6 (0.9989), and R × 100 is barely affected. High gains buy speed and spend delay margin. R3

Not verified — check before relying on this

The guaranteed margins of continuous-time LQR with full state feedback are stated in the literature as a gain margin from one half to infinity and a phase margin of at least 60 degrees. These values are given from memory, were not checked against B1 in this build, and apply to the continuous-time problem, not to this chapter's sampled design. The Source Researcher must confirm them and their conditions in B1 before the sentence above is expanded.

Putting the delay in the model

A known delay of d samples can be modelled exactly: add the d commands still "in flight" to the state. The augmented state (x, u[k−d], …, u[k−1]) has 4 + d entries; the oldest command drives the plant, the newest is the one being computed. Run LQR on this larger model and the gain uses the queued commands too: the controller knows what it has already sent. Run R3 does this for d = 4: the closed-loop spectral radius is 0.9829 (back to the undelayed value), and on the nonlinear robot with the queue and the 10 N limit the pole stays within 0.00138 rad between 1 s and 3 s. The price is a larger model and a controller that must remember its last d commands. R3D1

Solving the Riccati equation in practice

Plain iteration (Listing 1) is easy to check but can be slow: 608 iterations here, more for slow unstable modes or near-singular R. Library solvers use matrix decompositions instead of iteration; the result must be the same stabilising P, which you can confirm with the Lyapunov identity and the closed-loop eigenvalues. Keep P exactly symmetric after each step (Listing 1, line 22), because rounding otherwise accumulates an antisymmetric part. Scale the states as in F9-60 so that the entries of Q and P are not spread over many orders of magnitude. D1U1

How the hardware actually does it

On the robot, LQR at run time is the same four multiply-adds as F9-61; all the work is done at the desk. What the robot adds is time. Between the moment a sensor reading is taken and the moment the motor current changes there are several stages: sensor sampling and filtering, transfer to the controller (a bus or a network message), the computation, transfer to the motor driver, the driver's own update cycle, and the current settling in the motor winding. Each adds a fraction of a sample or more, and some (queues in a communication stack, a driver that buffers commands) add whole samples. None of this is in Ad, Bd unless you put it there. D1U1

The way to find a delay is to measure it: put a sequence number in every command and have the driver report the number of the command it is executing (as the forensic log does), or toggle a test output when the command is computed and another when the motor current changes, and measure the gap with an oscilloscope. RB304 covers loop timing and jitter on real-time systems. U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. No rig was used in this build. The 4-sample delay of the forensic lab is injected in simulation; the list of delay sources above is a checklist, not a measurement. The delay of a real rig must be measured as described, and the values recorded in the dossier. All force limits, masses and lengths are exercise values.

Safety

A limit cycle like the one in the forensic lab drives the motor back and forth at full current several times a second: it heats the motor and driver and stresses the belt and end stops. If a rig starts to shake, stop it with the emergency stop; do not try to steady the pole by hand. Set the driver's current limit and end-stop cut-outs before any closed-loop test, and test every design with the measured delay in the simulator first (safety, BR-08).

Worked example

Task. A scalar sampled system x[k+1] = 1.1x[k] + u[k] (unstable: it grows 10 % per sample) with cost Σ(x² + u²). Find P, K and the closed-loop pole.

  1. Scalar DARE with a = 1.1, b = 1, q = r = 1: p = q + a²p − a²b²p²/(r + b²p) = 1 + 1.21p − 1.21p²/(1 + p).
  2. Multiply by (1 + p): p + p² = 1 + p + 1.21p(1 + p) − 1.21p² = 1 + p + 1.21p. So p² − 1.21p − 1 = 0.
  3. The positive root: p = (1.21 + √(1.21² + 4))/2 = (1.21 + √5.4641)/2 = (1.21 + 2.33754)/2 = 1.77377.
  4. Gain: K = abp/(r + b²p) = 1.1 × 1.77377 / 2.77377 = 0.70343.
  5. Closed loop: a − bK = 1.1 − 0.70343 = 0.39657. Stable, and the cost from x0 = 1 is p = 1.77.
  6. Sanity check: with r very large the gain shrinks towards the smallest gain that still stabilises (pole near 1/1.1); with r very small, K → a and the pole → 0 (dead-beat).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nadia trades off as she rides and changes her mind with the terrain. The LQR gain is computed once for fixed weights; changing the trade-off means recomputing K (or keeping several and switching).
    • 

  • A rider's cost is not quadratic: a small wobble costs nothing, falling off costs everything. Quadratic costs make the maths easy, but they punish large errors smoothly, not like a cliff. Limits that must never be crossed are F9-64's subject.
    • 

  • Nadia's reactions are slow and she knows it; she anticipates. The LQR assumes its commands act instantly unless you put the delay into the model.

  • LQR minimises Σ(xᵀQx + uᵀRu); the answer is u = −Kx with K from the discrete Riccati equation.
