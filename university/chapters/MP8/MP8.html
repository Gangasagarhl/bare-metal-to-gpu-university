Story hook

Ines and Tomas have each built a good part of a house before. Ines installed the heating of one house; Tomas wired the electrics of another; both passed their inspections. For their last job of the apprenticeship they must build one house in which the heating thermostat switches through Tomas's wiring, and in which the house stays safe when the thermostat stops answering. Neither of their earlier inspection certificates says anything about that. The new risk lives where their two trades touch.

This handbook is the job sheet for that last house. It does not teach a new technology. It asks you to join two systems you already built and tested, to prove that the joined system does something neither part can do alone, to break it on purpose in front of witnesses, and to defend what you built and what you would do differently. The SE501 chapters (F12-28, F12-29, F12-30) teach how to plan, review and defend a mega project; this handbook is the project itself.

What you will be able to do

  • Propose an integrated system that combines at least two goal areas, names the new behaviour that only integration creates, and passes R0 D1 §11.6.
  • Write the interface contracts that cross technology boundaries (GPU and host, perception and control, service and client), with a contract test for every sentence, before you integrate.
  • Build a walking skeleton of your system with a stand-in behind each contract, and grow it into the real system milestone by milestone without breaking its end-to-end test.
  • Derive a timing budget across components (frame period, delay, maximum age, braking) and turn it into acceptance tests.
  • Run a fault matrix and a game-day on the integrated system, and write the post-mortem from evidence.
  • Lead the project through gates R0–R4, defend it in public, and write a retrospective built from records.
Jargon box
Goal area
Simple words: one of the big things this university prepares you to build, such as an operating system, GPU kernels, a robot or a drone. Analogy: one trade on a building site: heating, electrics, plumbing. Precise: in the MP8 card, the areas a proposal must combine (at least two); in this handbook, an area counts when the learner brings a finished mega project of it (MP1–MP7) whose acceptance tests still pass from a clean checkout D1 §11.6; the counting rule is this handbook's proposal (NOTES.md).
Stand-in backend
Simple words: a slower, simpler implementation that keeps exactly the same promise as the real one, so the rest of the system can be built and tested before the real one runs. Analogy: a temporary electric heater in the finished room, wired to the same thermostat line the real boiler will use. Precise: an implementation of a component behind an interface contract that produces contract-conforming outputs with identical semantics but without the target hardware (here the CPU loop running the same per-cell rule as the GPU kernel); it is the stub of the walking skeleton and remains as the test reference after the real backend arrives; course definition.
Fault matrix
Simple words: a table of the things you will break on purpose, and what the system must do each time. Analogy: the list of drills: power cut, burst pipe, smoke in the kitchen, each with the right reaction. Precise: a list of injected faults (rows) at each interface or component, each with the expected observable behaviour, the acceptance test that checks it, and its result; the generalisation of the failover matrix of F12-28 and the failsafe matrix of MP7 to an integrated system; course definition.
Risk register (project)
Simple words: the list of what could still go wrong with the project, who watches each risk, and which milestone removes it. Analogy: the site manager's list: "the roof tiles may arrive late; the ground may be soft". Precise: a table kept in the repository with, per risk, a description, likelihood and impact (judgements), the owner, the milestone or spike that retires it and its current state; reviewed at every gate; introduced in F12-28 Layer 2, named here.
Cut list
Simple words: the features you agree in advance to drop first if time runs out. Analogy: "if we are late, the garden path waits; the roof does not". Precise: an ordered list in the R0 proposal of features (never tests) that may be removed with the reviewers' agreement at a gate; scope is the only variable a late project may cut, because acceptance tests are never skipped or weakened without approval and a log entry D2 §0, §19.2.

Terms from earlier chapters used here: mega project, integration milestone, interface contract, contract test, walking skeleton (F12-28); design-review gate, entry criteria, review condition (F12-29); claims ledger, retrospective, final defence (F12-30); message age (SE403), data age and safe state (RB403); inflation (F9-54); threat model (SS401); game day (DS402); post-mortem (SE402); test oracle (SE302).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Mapping (F12; proposed in NOTES.md): the last house of the apprenticeship = MP8; the trades you already mastered = the finished mega projects you combine; the thermostat line between heating and electrics = an interface contract; the temporary heater on the same line = the stand-in backend; the list of drills = the fault matrix; the fire drill with witnesses = the game-day; the handover walk-through = the public defence (F12-30).

You have built big things before, one at a time. Now you join two of them so that together they do something new, like a robot that sees with code you wrote for a graphics card. You agree in writing on exactly what passes between the parts. You make the thinnest working version first. Then you break it on purpose, in front of people, and show it stays safe. Finally you explain what you built, what went wrong, and what you would do differently.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What the card fixes, and what you decide

The MP8 card fixes five milestones: a proposal combining at least two goal areas; a design document and threat model, reviewed; integration milestones that you define and that are approved at R1; an incident game-day and post-mortem; and a public defence. The deliverables are all of those plus a retrospective on what you would do differently. The rubric weights technical depth 25 %, integration quality 20 %, evidence and testing 20 %, leadership and communication 20 % and reflection 15 %, and the project passes all five gates R0–R4 D1 §11.6. A gate is passed only with written reviewer approval, by peer learners and, where available, a human mentor; agent reviewers may assist, and the owner decides who signs D1 §11.6.

Everything between those fixed points is yours: the combination, the contracts, the integration milestones and their tests. F12-28 shows how to plan it (milestones with tests, estimates, the critical path, the weekly re-forecast), F12-29 how each gate is held, and F12-30 how the defence is prepared. This handbook does not repeat them; it gives the project's own milestones, gate checklists, rubric and a starter lab, and points to those chapters at each step.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Contracts across technology boundaries

A contract between two components of one technology hides few surprises: both sides share a language, a clock and a build. A contract between goal areas crosses more than a function call. In the example, C-grid crosses from device memory to host memory, from a GPU work queue to a control loop, and from the timing of a sensor to the timing of a motor. Each crossing adds sentences that are easy to forget. Listing 1 writes them as code: the version and what happens with a newer one; the geometry, with the cell size as an integer in millimetres so that no reader can guess the unit; the allowed cell values (exactly three, so a graded cost from a different planner is refused instead of misread); a sequence number that never repeats; and a time stamp that means the time the sensor data was taken, not the time the kernel finished. That last choice decides the safety argument: the consumer can only bound the age of what it knows about the world if the stamp is the world's time.

The producer side has its own sentence that is easy to miss: what happens at the border of the map. The learner's F9-54 inflation writes only inside the map, so cells outside count as free; a GPU gather written naively with the same occ() helper, which reports outside cells as occupied, would mark every cell near the border lethal. Contract test P2 exists for that sentence R1. In general: for every sentence of a contract, name the test that would fail if one side broke it, and write both the producer's and the consumer's test.

Same rule, three compilers

The stand-in backend is useful only if it computes the same thing as the real backend. The example makes that true by construction: the per-cell rule lives in one header (Listing 2) that g++ compiles for the CPU and nvcc and hipcc compile inside the kernels. The test then has two parts. On the CPU, the gather form is compared with the learner's scatter reference from F9-54 (P1 and P2). On the device, the kernel's output is compared with the same reference (Listing 4); in this build that comparison could not run, because the container has no GPU R6. The two parts are not equivalent: compiler differences, memory errors and launch errors only show on the device. M3 exists to close that gap, and until it does, every claim about the GPU path is "compiled, untested on hardware".

The timing budget

The consumer may use a frame only while its age, now minus the sensor stamp, is at most the maximum age A. With a frame period T and a delay D from sensor sample to published frame, the newest frame is D old when it arrives and almost T + D old just before the next one arrives. With the example's choices T = 100 ms, D = 30 ms and A = 250 ms, a healthy stream never exceeds 130 ms; one lost frame raises the worst age to 230 ms, still usable; two lost frames reach 330 ms and the controller goes to its safe state. That is a design decision, written in the contract: the system tolerates one dropped frame and stops on the second.

The same arithmetic bounds how far the robot travels on old information. At speed v with deceleration a, the robot may drive for up to A plus one control step after the last sample before it starts braking, then needs v²/(2a) to stop. With v = 0.4 m/s, a = 2 m/s² and a 10 ms control step: 0.4 × 0.26 + 0.16 / 4 = 0.104 + 0.04 = 0.144 m, and the time from the last sample to standstill is at most 250 + 10 + 200 = 460 ms. Acceptance test A2 checks exactly that budget in simulated time: the last frame before the injected fault was stamped at 1,900 ms, the budget ends at 2,360 ms and the robot stood still at 2,350 ms R3. The numbers are the example's choices, not properties of any real robot; M5 replaces D and T by measured distributions on the target, and if they do not fit, the contract changes at a gate.

What the budget does not cover must be stated as a limitation in the claims ledger: an obstacle that appears closer than the stopping distance after the last frame cannot be avoided by any perception rate; and the clearance check of the simulation uses a perfect sensor. Those limits belong in the defence (F12-30, "the scope of a claim").

Failure modes that only exist after integration

Each finished MP has its own failure tests. MP8's fault matrix adds the failures of the joints: a producer that stops (a hung kernel, a crashed process), a producer that is slow (a busy GPU, a slow copy), a producer that is wrong in a contract-detectable way (version, geometry, values), a producer that is wrong in a way the contract cannot detect (a plausible but incorrect map, caught only by the reference comparison of M3 and by the ground-truth oracle in simulation), a consumer restart, and the safety path that bypasses both (e-stop, watchdog). For the cluster example the rows are node loss, network partition, a GPU error during a job and a scheduler restart; for the ground station they are replica crashes and partitions (F12-30). Write each row with its expected observable behaviour before running it.

The threat model of the joined system

Joining systems creates new trust boundaries. In the example, whoever can write C-grid frames can stop the robot (denial of service) or, worse, make it drive into an obstacle by publishing a free map with a fresh stamp (tampering and spoofing). The STRIDE method of F11-14 applied per element and per interaction finds these; the robot threat model of F11-15 and the safety mechanisms of F11-21 give the mitigations to consider (an authenticated local channel, a process boundary the network cannot reach, a safety layer independent of perception). The R1 reviewer asks: which threat is most dangerous, and which test shows the mitigation works D1 §11.6?

Evidence that reviewers can rerun

Every claim in an MP8 report is one of four kinds: a test result (rerunnable from a clean checkout), a measurement (labelled with machine, versions, date and the number of runs), a source (a document section, opened), or a stated limitation. The claims ledger of F12-30 checks that mix. Real-hardware results cannot be rerun by a reviewer, so the curriculum keeps them as extra evidence, never as a replacement for the emulated test D2 §0. That is why the skeleton test, not the real run, is the test that must stay green at every gate.

How the hardware actually does it

MP8 uses hardware from the earlier mega projects; this section says what the integration adds.

  • A GPU on or next to the robot. The perception kernel needs the GPU of MP3 or MP4 reachable from the robot's control computer, either on board or over a link. A link adds its own delay and loss to D and needs its own contract and threat-model entry. Which GPUs, boards and operating systems the vendor runtimes support is a question for the vendor documents, not for this handbook (unverified box in Layer 2).
  • Time across devices. The age check compares a stamp taken at the sensor with a clock read at the controller. On one computer that is one clock; across two computers the clocks must be related, or the stamp must be converted at a boundary the contract names. Clock synchronisation is covered in F5-10; the example avoids the problem by running perception and control on one computer.
  • Access is a dependency. Booked GPU time, the kit robot and a supervisor's availability are dated dependencies in the plan, as F12-28 requires. The example's R0 condition C1 is exactly that: a date for GPU access (Worked example).
  • Emulator first, hardware as a separate milestone. Each component's acceptance tests run in QEMU or a simulator; a real-hardware run is recorded separately and never replaces the emulated test D2 §0, §19.2.
Safety

Read the university's safety rules before any step with real hardware. Robots and drones can hurt people: every physical step starts in a simulator and runs on hardware only after the simulator milestone passes; a physical kill switch or e-stop, a clear zone and supervision are required; LiPo batteries are charged and stored only as their maker documents, never unattended; no mains-voltage work at any level. For MP8 the R3 checks of MP6 (e-stop tested, speed limits set, clear zone, supervision arranged, battery handling per the maker's instructions) and of MP7 (current national aviation authority rules recorded, location permission, weather, supervision, failsafe configuration, battery condition, propeller-off bench tests) apply before any real run D1 §11.6, Honesty first. The owner sets who may supervise and signs the safety items in person; safety items are never deferred as conditions (F12-29). Firmware and kernels go to real hardware only with a recovery path; GPU and network stress tests run only on machines the owner designated D2 §19.2.

Watch out

Untested on hardware in this build: the container has no GPU, robot, board or drone. The kernels of Listings 4 and 5 were compiled for real and their runs show the runtimes' own "no device" errors R6; the robot is simulated. Every hardware step of M3, M5 and M7 is untested here by construction.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A house's trades do not run at different speeds. A thermostat and a boiler agree on "on" and "off"; perception and control also have to agree on time. In software the age of information is part of the contract, and a contract that is correct in content can still be dangerous when late. The house has nothing like that.
    • 

  • A temporary heater is visibly not a boiler. A stand-in backend can be indistinguishable from the real one at the contract and still differ on the device (memory errors, compiler differences). The analogy suggests that a working stand-in proves the real one; only M3's device comparison does.
    • 

  • Fire drills in a house do not change the house. A game-day on software is run against a build, and the build changes daily; a passed game-day is evidence about one commit, which is why the post-mortem and the claims name commits and logs.
    •
