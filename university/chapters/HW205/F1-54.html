Story hook

On a hot July evening the restaurant's kitchen gets so hot that the head chef, Ana, does something the guests never notice: she slows the line. Two burners go down to a simmer, the grill runs at half power, and orders take a little longer. When the kitchen cools, the burners go back up. Nobody has to tell her; the thermometer on the wall and her rule — "above 38 degrees, slow down; below 35, speed up" — are enough. The restaurant serves fewer plates per hour, but the cooks do not collapse and nothing catches fire.

Meanwhile, in the pantry, the clerk Tomás keeps the stock ledger. Every line has a few extra check digits, so that if one digit fades, he can tell exactly which one and fix it; if two fade, he can at least tell the line is wrong. One week the same line has a faded digit every day, then twice a day, then five times. Tomás fixes it each time and writes it in his log. On Sunday night, two digits on that line fade together; the ledger cannot be trusted, and the restaurant closes for the night. Ana's rule is thermal throttling, Tomás's check digits are ECC memory, and his log is this chapter's forensic lab.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain where a chip's power goes (dynamic power ∝ C·V²·f, plus static leakage) and why lowering voltage and frequency together saves so much.
  • Describe how temperature limits, throttling and trip points protect hardware, and what an operating system's part is.
  • Build and test a SECDED code: correct every single-bit error, detect every double-bit error, and show what happens with three.
  • Read a memory-error log: corrected (CE) and uncorrected (UE) errors, and their location fields.
  • Diagnose a failing memory location from a growing CE pattern before it becomes a UE.
Jargon box
Dynamic and static power
Simple words: dynamic power is used each time transistors switch; static power leaks away even when nothing switches. Analogy: gas burned while cooking, and the pilot flames that burn all night. Precise: dynamic power of CMOS logic is about α·C·V²·f (activity factor, switched capacitance, supply voltage, clock frequency); static power comes from leakage currents and grows with temperature. D1 D2
DVFS (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling)
Simple words: running a chip slower and at lower voltage when full speed is not needed or is too hot. Analogy: turning burners down to a simmer. Precise: changing a chip's clock frequency together with the lowest supply voltage that is safe at that frequency; on PCs exposed as performance states (P-states) controlled by the OS or the hardware. D2 D7
Thermal throttling
Simple words: slowing a chip down automatically when it gets too hot. Analogy: Ana slowing the line on a hot evening. Precise: reduction of frequency, voltage or duty cycle triggered when a temperature sensor passes a limit, by hardware, firmware or the OS, to keep the chip below its maximum junction temperature. D2 D7
Thermal zone and trip point
Simple words: an area with a temperature sensor, and temperatures at which something must happen. Analogy: the kitchen's thermometer and the numbers on Ana's rule. Precise: ACPI describes thermal zones with methods for the current temperature (_TMP) and trip points for passive cooling (_PSV), critical shutdown (_CRT) and hibernate (_HOT), as listed in the curriculum's catalog. D4 D7
ECC (error-correcting code) memory
Simple words: memory that stores extra check bits so that it can fix small errors. Analogy: Tomás's check digits. Precise: a memory system in which the memory controller stores check bits computed from each data word and, on every read, uses them to correct some errors and detect others; reported to software as corrected or uncorrected errors. D1 D3
SECDED (single-error correction, double-error detection)
Simple words: a code that fixes any one wrong bit in a word and notices any two. Analogy: check digits that fix one faded digit and flag two. Precise: a Hamming code extended by one overall parity bit; for 64 data bits it needs 8 check bits, a (72,64) code, as Listing 1 builds and tests. D1 R1
Syndrome
Simple words: the number the decoder computes that says where the error is. Analogy: the line-and-column pointer Tomás gets from the check digits. Precise: for a Hamming code, the XOR of the positions of all 1 bits in the received word (equivalently, the recomputed check bits XOR the stored ones); zero means no detected error, otherwise it names the flipped position if there was exactly one flip. D1 R1
CE and UE (corrected and uncorrected errors)
Simple words: errors the memory fixed by itself, and errors it could only report. Analogy: a fixed faded digit, and a line that cannot be trusted. Precise: error classes reported by memory controllers through the machine-check architecture and operating-system drivers (Linux EDAC); a CE is logged and counted, a UE in data being used is fatal to that data and commonly to the process or the system. D5 D6 D7
Machine check
Simple words: the processor's alarm for a hardware error it cannot hide. Analogy: closing the restaurant for the night. Precise: an exception (#MC on x86, prepared for in Track B's IDT set-up) raised by the machine-check architecture when hardware detects an error, with details in machine-check bank registers. D5 D7

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A computer chip turns electricity into work, and almost all of that electricity ends up as heat. The faster it works, the more heat it makes. If it gets too hot it can break, so every chip has a thermometer inside, and when it gets too hot the chip slows down until it cools off. That is why a laptop can get slower on a hot day or when its fan is blocked.

Memory has a different problem. It stores billions of tiny ones and zeros, and once in a while one of them flips by itself. Good memory for servers stores a few extra "check bits" with every group of bits. When the computer reads the group, it checks it. If one bit flipped, the check bits tell exactly which one, and the computer flips it back. If two flipped, it cannot fix them, but it knows something is wrong and stops before it uses bad data.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Where the power goes

Each time a CMOS gate switches, it charges or discharges a small capacitance; the energy for that is proportional to C·V², and it happens up to f times per second, so dynamic power is about α·C·V²·f, with α the fraction of the circuit that switches. D1 D2 On top of that, transistors leak current even when they do not switch (static power). D2 A chip can run at a lower clock frequency with a lower supply voltage; because the voltage enters squared, lowering both saves much more power than lowering the frequency alone. Listing 2's table shows it in toy units: at 0.8 of the frequency and 0.9 of the voltage the dynamic power is 0.9² × 0.8 = 0.648 of full; at half frequency and 0.75 voltage it is 0.281. R2 The same work then takes longer, which is why "race to idle" versus "run slowly" is a real design question (curriculum milestone C15). D7

Heat and throttling

The heat must flow from the chip through the package, heat spreader and cooler to the air; the temperature rises until the heat flowing away equals the power put in. D3 Listing 2 models this with one line (line 35): the temperature moves 5 % of the way per step towards "ambient + 80 × power". At full speed with leakage the toy chip would settle near 25 + 80 × 1.1 = 113 toy degrees, above its limit of 90. With the throttling rule — one level slower above 90, one level faster below 85 — it stays between about 84 and 90 and does 0.82 of the work it would do at full speed. R2 The gap between 85 and 90 is hysteresis: without it the controller would switch level on every step around a single limit.

On real computers several layers do this. The processor itself has temperature sensors and slows down by hardware when it reaches its limit; firmware describes thermal zones and trip points to the OS through ACPI; and the OS can choose lower performance states earlier or run fans faster. The curriculum's catalog lists ACPI thermal zones (_TMP, _PSV, _CRT, _HOT, _TZP), the Intel digital thermal sensor registers and AMD's per-family documents as the sources, and warns: "Firmware or the EC usually protects the hardware even if your OS ignores thermal events; do not override fan control until you understand the zone's trip points." D7

Not verified — check before relying on this

The catalog lists only the names _TMP, _PSV, _CRT, _HOT and _TZP. Their meanings used in this chapter (current temperature; passive-cooling trip point; critical shutdown trip point; "hot" trip point at which the OS should put the system to sleep; polling period) were written from memory. Check the thermal-management chapter of the ACPI Specification.

Safety

Do not disable thermal protection, change fan curves or raise voltage or power limits on a computer to "test" this chapter. Overheating can permanently damage a processor, its voltage regulators or a battery, and hot laptop batteries can catch fire. The lab only reads sensors.

ECC: fixing one bit, noticing two

A Hamming code places check bits at the positions that are powers of two (1, 2, 4, 8, …); the check bit at position p is the parity of all positions whose number has bit p set. If one bit flips, recomputing the checks gives the position number of the flipped bit in binary — the syndrome. D1 Adding one overall parity bit separates one flip (parity wrong) from two flips (parity right, syndrome non-zero). Listing 1 builds this code for 64 data bits — 7 Hamming bits plus 1 parity bit, 72 bits in all — and tests it exhaustively on the data word 0x0123456789ABCDEF: all 72 single-bit errors corrected; all 2556 pairs of flips detected and none "corrected"; and of the 59640 triples, 45304 were "corrected" into wrong data and 14336 detected (those whose syndrome named a position that does not exist). R1 SECDED protects against one flip and warns about two; three or more can pass as a wrong "correction". That is why a growing number of corrected errors at one place is a warning, not a comfort.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Facts about real ECC memory written from memory: that ECC DIMMs for DDR4 widen each 64-bit word with 8 check bits (a 72-bit bus); that DDR5 modules have two 32-bit subchannels, each with its own check bits on ECC modules, plus on-die ECC inside the DRAM chips that is separate from the system's ECC; that server memory controllers use stronger codes than SECDED ("chipkill" or symbol codes that survive a whole failed DRAM chip); and that controllers "scrub" memory in the background to correct errors before a second one accumulates. Check the JEDEC DDR4 (JESD79-4) and DDR5 (JESD79-5) standards and your memory controller's documentation.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

How memory errors reach software

Analogy first: Tomás fixes a faded digit on the spot but also writes it in his log, so that someone can see a pattern. When the memory controller corrects an error, the program reading the data gets the right value; the controller also records the event — which channel, DIMM, rank, bank, row and column, and the syndrome — in its error registers. D5 D7 On x86 these reports go through the machine-check architecture: corrected errors are logged in machine-check bank registers and signalled with a low-priority interrupt or found by polling; an uncorrected error in data being consumed raises the machine-check exception (#MC), whose handler must decide whether the system can continue. D5 Operating systems turn these registers into logs: on Linux the EDAC drivers named in the catalog (for example ie31200_edac, skx_edac, i10nm_edac, amd64_edac) report counts per memory location; FreeBSD has mca(4) and Windows has WHEA. D6 D7 Track B's kernel prepares for this from its first IDT: the TSS gets a separate stack for machine checks (curriculum B2). D7

Not verified — check before relying on this

Not checked in this build: the x86 vector number of #MC (18); the names of the machine-check registers (IA32_MCG_CAP, IA32_MCi_STATUS, IA32_MCi_ADDR, IA32_MCi_MISC), the corrected-machine-check interrupt (CMCI), the "recoverable" error classes that let an OS kill only one process; the Linux EDAC sysfs layout (/sys/devices/system/edac/mc/mc0/…/ce_count and ue_count) and the tools rasdaemon and edac-util. Check the Intel SDM Volume 3 machine-check chapter, the AMD APM, and the kernel's EDAC documentation for your kernel version.

Why one location fails more and more

Analogy first: a faded ink line fades again after each rewrite. A DRAM cell stores charge in a tiny capacitor that leaks and must be refreshed; a weak cell — from manufacturing variation, wear or damage — loses its charge faster, so it flips more often as temperature rises or as it degrades. D3 D8 SECDED hides each single flip, but when that weak bit and a second bit of the same 72-bit word are wrong at the same moment, the word becomes uncorrectable. This is the reason for "predictive failure" policies: replace the module, or ask the OS to stop using the page (page offlining), when corrected errors at one location rise. D6

Not verified — check before relying on this

The Linux feature names for retiring a page with repeated corrected errors (soft page offlining, the memory_failure path, the rasdaemon page-ce threshold) and the field studies on DRAM error rates were not opened. Check the kernel documentation and the cited study by its title before quoting rates.

Power states as a driver's job

Analogy first: dimming the dining room at night requires every station to save its work before the lights go down. Performance states (P-states, DVFS), idle states (C-states: how deeply an idle core sleeps) and system sleep states (S3 suspend to RAM, S4 hibernate, S5 off) are described to the OS by ACPI and, on Arm platforms, requested through PSCI and SCMI firmware interfaces; Linux's cpufreq chooses frequencies and cpuidle chooses idle depth. D4 D7 For drivers the hard part is system sleep: "Suspend to RAM (S3) and Modern Standby (S0 low-power idle) need every driver to save and restore state: plan for it late" (curriculum catalog). The NVMe and NIC drivers of this course would each need to save their queue and ring addresses and rebuild them after resume. D7

How the hardware actually does it

Power: on-board voltage regulators deliver one or more supply rails to the processor; the processor requests voltage changes from them and switches its clock generators, while a power-management controller inside the chip watches currents, temperatures and limits many times per second. D2 D7 Temperature: on-die sensors (diodes or ring oscillators) are read by that controller; a hardware trip forces throttling or shutdown independently of the OS. D7 ECC: the encoder and decoder sit in the memory controller on the processor, in the path of every read and write; correction adds a little logic on the read path, and the error registers are written in parallel. D3 D5 On the build machine none of this is visible: it is a virtual machine whose /sys/class/thermal folder is empty and which has no /sys/class/hwmon or /sys/devices/system/edac folder (R4). R4

Worked example

Task 1: decode a single flip by hand. Data bit 0 sits at codeword position 3 (the first position that is not a power of 2; secded.cpp lines 17–21). Suppose position 3 flips in a clean word.

  1. The syndrome is the XOR of the positions of all ones. In a clean word it is 0 (the check bits were set to make it so). Flipping position 3 changes the XOR by 3: syndrome = 0 XOR 3 = 3 = binary 0000011.
  2. The number of ones changed by one: parity is now odd.
  3. Rule: parity odd and syndrome < 72 → flip position 3 back. Corrected.

Task 2: two flips. Positions 3 and 5 flip. Syndrome = 3 XOR 5 = 0b011 XOR 0b101 = 0b110 = 6; parity even (two changes). Rule: parity even, syndrome ≠ 0 → detected. Without the parity bit the decoder would have "corrected" position 6 — a third wrong bit. Listing 1 confirms: 2556 of 2556 pairs detected. R1

Task 3: three flips. Positions 3, 5 and 9 flip: syndrome = 3 XOR 5 XOR 9 = 6 XOR 9 = 0b0110 XOR 0b1001 = 0b1111 = 15; parity odd. Rule: flip position 15 — a data bit that was right. Now four bits are wrong and the decoder reports "corrected": silent data corruption, one of the 45304 cases Listing 1 counted. R1

Task 4: the power table. Level 2 runs at 0.6 frequency and 0.8 voltage: 0.8² × 0.6 = 0.64 × 0.6 = 0.384 of full dynamic power, for 0.6 of the speed — energy per unit of work 0.384 / 0.6 = 0.64 of full speed's (the same work takes 1/0.6 as long). R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Ana decides; a chip's limit is enforced in hardware. Software can choose to slow down earlier, but the last line of protection does not ask the OS. D7
    • 

  • A kitchen cools in minutes; a chip in milliseconds. Chip temperatures change much faster than room temperatures, so controllers act many times per second. D2
    • 

  • Check digits on paper are visible; ECC check bits are not. Software never sees the 8 extra bits, only the corrected data and the error reports. D3
    • 

  • Tomás knows which line; a log may not. Mapping an error to a physical module needs the platform's address-decoding information, which is why EDAC drivers are platform-specific. D6
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: your Hamming decoder "corrects" two-bit errors. Cause: no overall parity bit, or the rule ignores it. How to find it: test all pairs, as Listing 1 does. Fix: decide by parity first.
    • 

  • Symptom: decoder crashes on some errors. Cause: syndrome larger than the word (possible with three or more flips). Fix: treat it as detected (line 61's syndrome < 72). This build's first draft had exactly that crash.
    • 

  • Symptom: a benchmark gets slower over time on a laptop. Cause: thermal throttling. How to find it: log frequency and temperature with the benchmark. Fix: report it; do not compare throttled and unthrottled runs.
    • 

  • Symptom: throttling oscillates rapidly. Cause: one threshold without hysteresis. Fix: two thresholds.
    • 

  • Symptom: corrected errors ignored for weeks, then a crash. Cause: no alert on rising CE counts. Fix: the forensic lab's prevention.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Of the 35 CE lines, 33 have the same location — channel 1, DIMM 0, rank 0, bank 3, row 0x1a2b, bit 17, syndrome 0x4b — with varying columns; per day: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. Two other CEs (day 2 and day 5, channel 0, DIMM 1) have different locations each. The UE at 23:58 on day 7 is at the same row and bank, with bits 17 and 40 wrong, followed by a machine check and reset.


Root cause. A weak bit 17 in row 0x1a2b of DIMM 0 on channel 1 flipped more and more often (the counts roughly double every day or two). SECDED corrected each single flip. At 23:58 a word in that row had bit 17 and another bit (40) wrong at the same time: a double error, detected but not correctable, so the machine-check handler reset the system.


Ruling out. Random flips: random events do not repeat at one row and bit 33 times; the two other CEs are what random flips look like. A failing channel: the other DIMM on another channel is not involved, and the errors are confined to one row. Overheating: no temperature evidence, and heat would spread errors over many locations.


Fix. Replace DIMM 0 on channel 1 (after confirming the mapping of "channel 1, DIMM 0" to a physical slot in the board's documentation); until then, retire the affected page or row if the OS supports it.


Prevention. Alert when one location has repeated CEs. A sliding-window rule "2 or more CEs at the same row within 24 hours" fires on day 2 at 05:06, 23 h 52 min after the first one and almost six days before the crash; a rule counted per calendar day fires on day 3 (13:56 and 21:48).


How the evidence was made. ecc_log.cpp prints 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 CEs for the weak cell at pseudo-random times, adds two unrelated CEs, sorts each day, and ends with the UE line.
