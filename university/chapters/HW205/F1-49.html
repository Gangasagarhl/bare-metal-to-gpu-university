Story hook

The restaurant keeps its everyday food in the pantry down the corridor, but the big supplies live in a warehouse across town. Amira, who runs the warehouse, shows Tomás two halls. In the old hall, every box sits on a huge turntable of shelves. One picker stands on a moving ladder. To fetch a box he slides the ladder to the right ring of shelves and then waits until the turntable brings the box round to him. "Fetching two boxes that sit side by side is quick," Amira says. "Fetching two boxes from opposite sides of the hall is slow: the ladder moves, then we wait for the turntable."

The new hall has no moving parts at all: thousands of small lockers in drawers, all reachable at once. "Faster," Tomás says. "But there is a catch," Amira replies. "A locker that already holds something cannot be refilled. To make lockers usable again, we must empty a whole drawer at once. So I never refill in place. I put the new version in an empty locker and cross out the old one in my ledger. At night I tidy up: I move the still-useful boxes out of a drawer, then empty that drawer." Tomás thinks for a moment. "So you sometimes move boxes nobody asked you to move." "Exactly," says Amira. "That extra work is the price of the fast hall." This chapter is about both halls: the hard disk drive and the solid-state drive.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how a hard disk drive (HDD) finds a sector and why its access time has a seek part and a rotation part.
  • Explain why NAND flash is programmed in pages but erased in blocks, and what the SSD controller's flash translation layer does about it.
  • Compute a byte offset from a logical block address, and write amplification from a simulator's counters.
  • Run the university's FTL simulator and predict how the fill level and TRIM change its write amplification.
  • Inspect the disks of a Linux machine with lsblk and say what its numbers do and do not prove.
  • Diagnose an "SSD that got slow when full" from simulator logs.
Jargon box
Block device
Simple words: a storage device that you read and write in fixed-size pieces, each with a number. Analogy: the warehouse, where every box space has a number in the ledger. Precise: a device presenting storage as an array of equal-sized logical blocks addressed by number; reads and writes transfer whole blocks. D3 §37.1
Sector and LBA (logical block address)
Simple words: a sector is one numbered piece of a disk; the LBA is its number. Analogy: the number of a box space in the ledger. Precise: the host addresses a drive by LBA, a block number starting at 0; byte offset = LBA × logical block size. The drive maps LBAs to physical locations itself. D3 §37.1 D4 §44.5 R2
HDD (hard disk drive)
Simple words: a disk that stores data magnetically on spinning plates. Analogy: the old hall with the turntable and the ladder. Precise: a drive with one or more rotating platters, read/write heads on an actuator arm, data in concentric tracks divided into sectors. D3 §37.2
Seek time and rotational latency
Simple words: the time to move the head to the right ring, then the time to wait for the data to spin under it. Analogy: sliding the ladder, then waiting for the turntable. Precise: HDD access time = seek time + rotational latency + transfer time; average rotational latency is half a revolution. D3 §37.3 D3 §37.4
NAND flash
Simple words: chips that keep data without power and without moving parts. Analogy: the drawers of lockers in the new hall. Precise: non-volatile memory built from transistor cells that trap charge, organised into pages (the unit of read and program) grouped into blocks (the unit of erase). D4 §44.1 D4 §44.2
Erase-before-write
Simple words: flash cannot be overwritten; it must be erased first, and only whole blocks can be erased. Analogy: a full locker cannot be refilled; you empty whole drawers. Precise: programming can only move cells from the erased state toward the programmed state; returning them requires a block erase. D4 §44.3
SSD (solid-state drive) and its controller
Simple words: a drive made of flash chips plus a small computer that manages them. Analogy: the new hall and Amira with her ledger. Precise: a drive whose controller runs firmware that maps logical blocks to flash pages, cleans blocks, spreads wear, corrects bit errors and talks to the host over an interface such as SATA or NVMe. D4 §44.5 D6
FTL (flash translation layer)
Simple words: the part of the SSD's firmware that hides the erase rules from the computer. Analogy: Amira's ledger and her nightly tidying. Precise: the mapping from logical pages to physical flash pages with out-of-place updates, garbage collection and wear levelling. D4 §44.5 D4 §44.7
Garbage collection (flash)
Simple words: cleaning: copy the still-useful pages out of a block, then erase the block. Analogy: the nightly tidy-up of a drawer. Precise: FTL process that selects a victim block, relocates its valid pages and erases it to create free pages. Not the same as garbage collection of memory in programming languages. D4 §44.8
Write amplification
Simple words: how many pages the flash really wrote for each page you asked to write. Analogy: boxes moved by the warehouse divided by boxes the restaurant delivered. Precise: WA = flash page writes ÷ host page writes; extra writes come from garbage-collection copies. D4 §44.5 D4 §44.8 R1
Wear levelling
Simple words: spreading erases evenly so no block wears out early. Analogy: using every drawer in turn instead of always the one by the door. Precise: FTL policy that balances program/erase cycles across blocks, because each block survives a limited number of erase cycles. D4 §44.4 D4 §44.10
TRIM
Simple words: a message from the computer: "these blocks no longer hold anything I need". Analogy: the restaurant phoning to say "throw away the boxes on these shelves". Precise: a host command (named differently per interface) that tells the drive which logical blocks are unused, so the FTL can treat their pages as invalid. D4 §44.8

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A computer forgets everything in its memory when the power goes off. So it needs a place that remembers: a disk. To the computer, every disk looks the same. It is a long list of numbered boxes, all the same size. The computer says "give me box 5" or "put this in box 5". That number is called the logical block address.

A hard disk drive keeps the boxes on spinning plates covered with a magnetic layer. A tiny head on an arm reads and writes them. To reach a box, the arm moves to the right ring, then waits until the box spins round under it. Boxes that sit next to each other are quick to read one after the other. Boxes far apart are slow, because of all that moving and waiting.

A solid-state drive has no moving parts. It uses flash chips, which keep their data without power. Flash has a strange rule: you cannot write over something already written. First you must erase, and erasing only works on a big group of boxes at once. So the drive's own little computer cheats politely. It writes every new version to an empty place and remembers in a table where the newest version is. Later it tidies up. That tidying sometimes means copying data you did not ask it to copy, and that extra copying is the reason a nearly full SSD can get slower.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

One interface: an array of logical blocks

Operating systems see a drive as a block device: an array of logical blocks numbered from 0, each of the same size, read and written whole. D3 §37.1 The byte offset of a block is its LBA multiplied by the logical block size. Listing 2 does exactly this with a disk image file: LBA 5 with 512-byte sectors starts at byte 5 × 512 = 2560, and reading 512 bytes from there returns what was written. R2 The drive, not the host, decides where an LBA physically lives. On an HDD the controller maps LBAs to tracks and sectors (and keeps spare sectors for defects); on an SSD the FTL maps them to flash pages. D3 §37.1 D4 §44.5

Some drives report a logical block size smaller than the physical unit they really store. The lab machine reports exactly that: its virtual disks say LOG-SEC 512 and PHY-SEC 4096. R3 With those two sizes, 4096 ÷ 512 = 8 logical sectors share one physical sector, so LBA 5 is slot 5 of physical sector 0, and LBA 8 starts physical sector 1 (arithmetic, printed by Listing 2). R2 Because a sector is the smallest unit the device writes, a write that covers only part of a physical sector means the device has to read, modify and write the whole physical sector (this follows from the block rule; the opened sources do not spell it out), which is why partitions and file systems try to align their structures to the physical size. D3 §37.1

The hard disk drive

An HDD stores bits as magnetised regions on one or more platters that spin on a common spindle. Each surface has a head on an arm; all arms move together on one actuator. Data lies in concentric tracks, and each track is divided into sectors. D3 §37.2 To serve a request the drive must (1) move the arm to the right track, the seek; (2) wait for the right sector to rotate under the head, the rotational latency; (3) transfer the bits as they pass under the head. D3 §37.3 D3 §37.4 The first two are mechanical and dominate a small random request, which is why sequential access is hundreds of times cheaper than random access on an HDD ("use disks sequentially"). D3 §37.4 The arithmetic of rotation is in the worked example; real seek times and rotation speeds come only from a named drive's datasheet.

NAND flash: pages, blocks and the erase rule

A flash cell is a transistor that can trap charge; the trapped charge shifts the voltage at which the transistor turns on, and reading measures that shift. D4 §44.1 A cell that distinguishes 2 levels stores 1 bit; 4 levels store 2 bits, 8 levels store 3 bits (2n levels for n bits, the same counting as F0-20). These are called SLC, MLC and TLC cells. D4 §44.1 More bits per cell make the cell slower to read and program and less durable (OSTEP quotes manufacturers rating MLC blocks at about 10,000 program/erase cycles and SLC at about 100,000); the resulting bit errors are the controller's problem, and F1-54 explains the codes that fix them. D4 §44.4

Cells are organised into pages, the unit of reading and programming, and pages into blocks, the unit of erasing. Programming can only move a cell one way; only an erase moves it back, and only for a whole block at a time. A block also survives only a limited number of program/erase cycles before it becomes unreliable. D4 §44.2 D4 §44.3 D4 §44.4 Listing 1's first two lines show the simulator enforcing the rule: programming page 5 works once, the second attempt is refused. R1

The flash translation layer

Because a page cannot be rewritten in place, the FTL writes each new version of a logical page to a free physical page and updates its mapping table; the old physical page becomes invalid. D4 §44.7 In Listing 1, logical page 7 moves from physical page 0 to 1 to 2 over three writes (Figure 2). R1 Free pages run out, so the FTL performs garbage collection: it picks a victim block, copies its still-valid pages elsewhere, erases it and returns it to the free list. D4 §44.8 Those copies are writes the host never asked for, so the flash writes more pages than the host: the ratio is the write amplification. In the simulator, overwriting all 24 logical pages in order ten times needed 240 flash writes for 240 host writes (WA 1.00), because whole blocks became invalid together and could be erased without copying; the same number of random overwrites needed 520 flash writes and 280 copies (WA 2.17). R1

Two more FTL duties follow from the wear limit. Wear levelling spreads erases across blocks; the simulator does not level wear at all, and its random run left erase counts from 12 to 21 per block, the kind of imbalance wear levelling exists to prevent. R1 D4 §44.10 Blocks that reach their cycle limit fail, so a controller must stop using them; how it records them is a vendor detail not covered by the opened sources. D4 §44.4

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Over-provisioning and fill level. An SSD has more physical flash than it offers as logical space (our toy: 32 physical pages, 24 logical). The spare space is where garbage collection finds partly invalid blocks. The fuller the logical space, the more valid pages each victim holds and the more copying each erase costs: the forensic run shows flash writes per host write rising from 1.12 at 50 % full to 3.30 at 95 % full. D4 §44.8 R4
  • Why TRIM matters. A file system that deletes a file only updates its own metadata; without TRIM the drive still believes those pages are valid and keeps copying them. In the forensic run, deleting files without TRIM changed nothing (3.42), while sending TRIM for the same pages brought the ratio to 1.00. D4 §44.8 R4 Each interface has its own form of the command; the names are in the unverified box below.
  • Garbage-collection policy. The toy uses a greedy policy (fewest valid pages) and cleans lazily, only when it needs a new block and one erased block is left. The Lab Engineer's first draft of the toy kept two erased blocks and cleaned eagerly, and its sequential run printed WA 1.90 instead of 1.00, because it cleaned blocks before they were fully invalid (that draft's log was not kept; reproduce it by setting kReserve to 2 on line 87 of ftl.h and cleaning before every write). Policy matters as much as hardware (OSTEP: the ideal victim block contains only dead pages). D4 §44.8
  • Disturbs and error correction. Reading or programming a page can flip bits in neighbouring pages (read disturb, program disturb), and wear makes cells harder to read; FTLs program the pages of a block in order to reduce program disturb. D4 §44.4 D4 §44.7 A real controller stores error-correcting codes with the data and corrects such flips on every read; the code principle is F1-54's, and the controller's own scheme is a vendor detail not covered by the opened sources.
  • Interfaces. The same block abstraction is carried by different command sets and host interfaces: ATA commands over SATA through an AHCI host controller (curriculum milestone C5), SCSI commands over SAS, and NVMe commands over PCI Express (C6, next chapter); eMMC, SD and UFS do the same job in phones and boards. D6 The curriculum's hardware catalog (section 6) lists the standard and the reference drivers for each.

How the hardware actually does it

Both drives contain a computer. An HDD's controller keeps a cache (the track buffer), schedules the queued requests itself because it knows the head position and the track layout, and drives the spindle motor and the arm. D3 §37.3 D3 §37.5 An SSD's controller runs the FTL as firmware, keeps the mapping table in memory (page-level tables can reach a gigabyte for a terabyte drive), and drives several flash chips, each with its own planes, in parallel. D4 §44.2 D4 §44.5 D4 §44.9 The warehouse analogy registered for this faculty already warns about this: "SSDs have internal processors and queues". Towards the host, the drive is a bus device (F1-45): the host's driver writes commands into memory, the drive fetches them and moves data by DMA (F1-44), then signals completion with an interrupt (F1-42). D7 §36.3–36.5 F1-50 follows exactly that path for NVMe, with a real trace.

What a computer reports about its disks is what the device (or the virtual machine) claims, not a fact about the physical medium. The lab machine is a virtual machine: lspci shows its disks as "Virtio 1.0 block device" functions, and lsblk reports ROTA 1 (rotational) for them. R3 Whether the storage behind them spins is unknown from inside; the flag only repeats what the virtual device advertises.

Untested on hardware

No physical HDD or SSD was measured in this build. The FTL behaviour (write amplification, garbage collection, wear levelling, the effect of TRIM) comes from the university's toy simulator, whose block and page counts are made up; real drives hide their FTL inside firmware and differ between models. The only real-device observation is the virtual machine's own description of its virtual disks (R3). Seek times, rotation speeds and flash endurance figures for real products must come from the product's datasheet or from your own measurement. To test the FTL claims on hardware you would need an SSD you are allowed to erase (the reference kit's x86-64 test PC, build/KIT.md), its datasheet, and a benchmark run at known fill levels with and without TRIM.

Worked example

Task A — rotation arithmetic. A made-up disk spins at 6,000 revolutions per minute (a round number chosen for arithmetic, not a product's specification). How long is one revolution, and what is the average rotational latency?

  1. Revolutions per second: 6,000 ÷ 60 = 100.
  2. One revolution: 1 ÷ 100 s = 0.01 s = 10 ms.
  3. On average the wanted sector is half a revolution away: 10 ms ÷ 2 = 5 ms. D3 §37.3
  4. If, in this made-up drive, an average seek took 4 ms and the transfer 0.1 ms, one random 4 KiB read would cost about 4 + 5 + 0.1 = 9.1 ms, so about 1 ÷ 0.0091 ≈ 110 such reads per second. A second read of the very next sector costs almost only the transfer time.

Task B — write amplification from counters. Listing 1's random run printed host writes 240 flash writes 520 gc copies 280. R1

  1. Check the bookkeeping: every flash write is either a host write or a copy: 240 + 280 = 520. ✓
  2. WA = 520 ÷ 240 = 2.1666…, printed as 2.17.
  3. Meaning: for every page the host wrote, the flash programmed 2.17 pages, and the extra 1.17 pages per host page also cost erase cycles (123 erases in this run).

Task C — LBA to byte offset. With 512-byte logical sectors, LBA 16 starts at 16 × 512 = 8192 bytes; with 4096-byte physical sectors it is slot 16 mod 8 = 0 of physical sector 16 ÷ 8 = 2, exactly the last line of Listing 2's output. R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A warehouse picker carries one box at a time; drives do not. An SSD works on many flash chips in parallel and an HDD reorders queued requests to shorten the arm's travel; both keep many requests in flight (F1-50). D3 §37.5 D4 §44.2
    • 

  • Emptying a drawer is free; erasing a block is not. Each erase wears the block, and after enough cycles it must be retired. Lockers in a drawer do not wear out from being emptied. D4 §44.4
    • 

  • Amira knows which boxes are rubbish; the SSD does not. Without TRIM the drive cannot know that a deleted file's pages are useless, and keeps copying them (forensic lab). R4
    • 

  • "Across town" suggests distance; the cost is time. The slowness of storage comes from mechanics (HDD) or from flash program and erase operations and controller work (SSD), not from metres of cable.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a benchmark of an SSD is fast when new and much slower after filling it. Cause: garbage collection copies more valid pages when free space is scarce. How to find it: compare runs at different fill levels (the forensic lab). Fix: leave free space; make sure TRIM reaches the drive.
    • 

  • Symptom: small writes are slow on a drive with 4096-byte physical sectors. Cause: writes not aligned to physical sectors force read-modify-write. How to find it: lsblk -o LOG-SEC,PHY-SEC and the partition start sector. Fix: align partitions to the physical sector size (Task C).
    • 

  • Symptom: "my disk is rotational" (or not) according to the OS. Cause: the flag is what the device or hypervisor reports. How to find it: look at the bus and device ID (lspci -nn): the lab machine's "rotational" disks are virtio devices. Fix: trust the vendor documentation of the real device, not one flag.
    • 

  • Symptom: confusion between flash garbage collection and garbage collection in languages such as Java. Cause: same words. Fix: say "FTL garbage collection" or "block cleaning".
    •

Summary


    

  • Drives present a numbered array of logical blocks; byte offset = LBA × block size.
    • 

  • An HDD's access time is seek + rotational latency + transfer; the first two are mechanical, so random access is expensive.
    • 

  • NAND flash is programmed per page and erased per block, and blocks wear out after many erases.
    • 

  • The SSD's FTL writes out of place, maps logical to physical pages, cleans blocks by garbage collection and levels wear.
    • 

  • Write amplification = flash writes ÷ host writes; the simulator measured 1.00 sequential and 2.17 random.
    • 

  • Fuller drives copy more during cleaning; TRIM lets the drive forget deleted data.
    • 

  • What the OS reports about a disk is what the device claims: the lab machine's virtio disks say "rotational".

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Flash writes per host write: 1.12 (day 1), 3.30 (day 9), 3.42 (day 10), 1.00 (day 11). On every line flash = host + gc_copies (for example day 9: 800 + 1838 = 2638), so all extra writes are garbage-collection copies, not retries or errors.


Root cause. With the logical space 95 % full, every block chosen for cleaning still holds many valid pages, so each erase requires many copies: write amplification rose from 1.12 to 3.30 and the drive spent most of its time copying. Deleting files on day 10 changed only the file system's metadata; with no TRIM the drive still treated those pages as valid (ratio 3.42). On day 11 TRIM was sent for the same pages (in the story, the OS update enabled it), the drive marked them invalid, and cleaning became free again (gc_copies 0, ratio 1.00).


Why "wearing out" is wrong. Wear would show as errors or retired blocks, not as a ratio that returns to 1.00 the moment TRIM arrives. Nothing in the counters is an error.


Fix and verification. Make sure TRIM reaches the drive (file-system mount option or periodic trim job, per the OS documentation; the exact commands depend on the OS and must be checked there), keep free space, then re-measure write speed at the same fill level.


How the evidence was made. forensic.cpp runs the toy FTL (16 blocks of 8 pages, 112 logical pages) through four phases of 800 random writes: half full, 95 % full, writes restricted to 30 pages without TRIM, and the same after TRIM of pages 30–105.
