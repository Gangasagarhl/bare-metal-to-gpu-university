Story hook

The restaurant has started sending meals to a second restaurant across the river. Two people make it work. In the dispatch office, Rosa packs each meal: she writes the destination and the sender on the box, puts the meal inside, adds padding if the box is too empty to travel, and seals it with a tamper sticker whose pattern depends on everything inside. At the loading dock, Kwame takes each sealed box and turns it into something the road can carry: he loads the van, drives in the agreed rhythm, and talks to the dock on the other side to agree how fast both sides can go.

One Monday the vans arrive, the dock lights on both sides are green, and yet every box arrives with a broken tamper sticker and is thrown away. "The road is up," says Kwame, "my light is green." "Then why do we receive nothing?" asks Rosa. The answer turns out to be in the handover between the office and the dock: each side thought it had to wait a moment before handing over a box, so together they waited twice as long, and every box was grabbed at the wrong instant. Rosa is an Ethernet MAC, Kwame is a PHY, and "link up, no packets" is one of the classic bugs of real network hardware.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the jobs of the Ethernet MAC and PHY and the interfaces between them (MII family for data, MDIO for management).
  • Build an Ethernet frame byte by byte, compute its frame check sequence (CRC-32), and have tshark confirm it.
  • Read MAC addresses, EtherTypes and the FCS in a real capture, and say what a capture point can and cannot see.
  • Capture your own machine's traffic with tshark or Wireshark, and frames from an emulated NIC in QEMU.
  • Diagnose "link up, no packets" from logs and a PHY register dump pointing to a clock-delay mismatch.
Jargon box
Ethernet frame
Simple words: one packet as it travels on an Ethernet link: addresses, type, data and a check value. Analogy: the sealed box with destination, sender, meal and tamper sticker. Precise: the IEEE 802.3 unit of transmission: destination address, source address, a type/length field, the payload (padded to a minimum size), and a 32-bit frame check sequence; preceded on the wire by a preamble and a start delimiter. D1 D3
MAC address
Simple words: the 6-byte name of a network interface. Analogy: the address label on the box. Precise: a 48-bit address; one bit marks group (multicast/broadcast) versus individual addresses, another marks locally administered versus globally unique ones; all-ones is the broadcast address. D1 R2
EtherType
Simple words: a number in the frame that says what kind of data is inside. Analogy: "meal" or "cutlery" written on the box. Precise: the 2-byte type field after the addresses; tshark decoded 0x0806 as ARP and 0x0800 as IPv4 in this build's captures. D1 R2
FCS (frame check sequence)
Simple words: a 4-byte number computed from the whole frame, used to detect damage. Analogy: the tamper sticker whose pattern depends on the contents. Precise: a CRC-32 over the frame from the destination address to the end of the padding, appended by the transmitting MAC and checked by the receiving MAC, which drops frames whose FCS does not match. D1 R2
MAC (media access controller)
Simple words: the part of the network hardware that builds and checks frames. Analogy: Rosa in the dispatch office. Precise: the data-link-layer block that frames data, generates and checks the FCS, filters by destination address and moves frames to and from memory (with the DMA engine of the NIC, F1-52). D1 D5
PHY (physical-layer device)
Simple words: the chip that turns bits into signals on the cable and back. Analogy: Kwame at the loading dock. Precise: the physical-layer chip or block that encodes and decodes the line signal, negotiates speed and duplex with the link partner (auto-negotiation) and reports link state in management registers. D1 D5
MII family (MII, RMII, RGMII, SGMII)
Simple words: the sets of wires that carry frame data between a MAC and a PHY. Analogy: the handover hatch between office and dock. Precise: media-independent interfaces between MAC and PHY; RGMII forwards a clock with the data and uses both clock edges; SGMII is a serial variant (curriculum catalog). D1 D2 D5
MDIO (management data input/output)
Simple words: a slow two-wire bus the MAC's driver uses to read and set the PHY's registers. Analogy: the office's telephone to the dock. Precise: the management interface of IEEE 802.3 (register sets of clause 22 and clause 45) used to read link status and configure the PHY. D1 D5
Link up
Simple words: the PHYs at both ends agree they are connected. Analogy: both dock lights green. Precise: the PHY's link-status indication, reached after auto-negotiation; it says nothing about whether the MAC–PHY data path delivers good frames. D1 D5

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When your computer sends a message on a network cable, two helpers do the work. The first one, the MAC, packs the message into a frame. It writes who the frame is for and who sent it. It adds a check number made from every byte, like a seal. If a single bit changes on the way, the seal will not match and the receiver throws the frame away.

The second helper, the PHY, deals with the cable. It turns ones and zeros into electrical signals and back. It also talks to the PHY at the other end to agree on a speed. When they agree, a little "link" light comes on.

The MAC and the PHY are joined by a small bundle of wires that carries the frames, and by a separate, slow wire pair that lets the computer ask the PHY questions such as "is the link up?". The link light only tells you that the two PHYs agree. It does not prove that frames get through.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The frame, field by field

Listing 1 builds a frame the way a MAC sends it. The first 6 bytes are the destination address, here the broadcast address ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff; the next 6 are the source address 52:54:00:12:34:56; then the 2-byte EtherType 0x0806, which says "ARP inside". D1 R1 The payload is an ARP request (a message asking which MAC address owns an IP address; ARP itself belongs to milestone C9). D3 The header plus payload is 42 bytes; the program pads it with zeros to 60 bytes and appends the FCS, giving 64 bytes. R1 Multi-byte fields such as the EtherType are sent high byte first ("network byte order", the put16 function). D3

tshark, the command-line version of Wireshark, read the frame back and decoded every field: "Destination: Broadcast", "Type: ARP (0x0806)", "Padding: 000000…", "Frame check sequence: 0x8b1fe63c [correct]", "[FCS Status: Good]", and the ARP request from 10.0.2.15 for 10.0.2.2. R2 tshark also explained two bits of each address: in 52:54:00:12:34:56 the "LG bit" says "Locally administered address (this is NOT the factory default)" and the "IG bit" says "Individual address (unicast)"; for ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff the IG bit says "Group address (multicast/broadcast)". R2 QEMU's default NIC addresses start with 52:54:00, a locally administered prefix, which is why no manufacturer owns them. R4

The FCS is a CRC-32

The FCS is a cyclic redundancy check: the frame's bits are treated as a long binary number, divided (in arithmetic without carries) by a fixed 33-bit generator polynomial, and the 32-bit remainder is sent. D1 D4 Listing 1's crc32 does that bit by bit with the reflected form of the polynomial, 0xEDB88320, starting from 0xFFFFFFFF and inverting the result. Three independent checks show it is the right CRC: it gives 0xCBF43926 for the text "123456789", exactly the value Python's zlib.crc32 printed for the same text; tshark accepted the FCS of frame 1; and for frame 2, where one bit of the sender's IP address was flipped after the FCS was computed, tshark reported "Frame check sequence: 0x8b1fe63c incorrect, should be 0x83fc8600" — and 0x83fc8600 is exactly our program's CRC of the damaged frame, 0x0086FC83, written in wire byte order. R1 R2 R3 A receiving MAC does the same comparison in hardware and drops the frame; the operating system never sees it, which is why a bad FCS shows up only as an error counter. D1 D5

MAC, PHY and the wires between them

The MAC is digital logic: it frames, checks, filters and moves frames to memory. The PHY handles the analog world: line coding for the medium, recovery of the clock from the received signal, and auto-negotiation, in which the two PHYs on a link exchange their abilities and pick a common speed and duplex. D1 The PHY publishes the result in management registers that the driver reads over MDIO; the standard defines a basic register set (IEEE 802.3 clause 22) and an extended one (clause 45), and each PHY adds vendor-specific registers described in its datasheet. D1 D5

Frame data travels between MAC and PHY over an interface of the MII family. On PCs the MAC and PHY are often inside one NIC chip; on SoC boards the MAC is part of the SoC and the PHY is a separate chip joined by RGMII-type wiring. RGMII sends a clock together with the data and transfers data on both clock edges, and the receiver can only sample the data correctly if the clock is delayed relative to the data; that delay may be added by the PHY, by the MAC or by a longer circuit-board trace, and it must be added exactly once (curriculum catalog: "a classic 'link up but no packets' bug"). D2 D5

Not verified — check before relying on this

Numbers and names written from memory, to be checked in IEEE 802.3 (frame format clause; clause 22 management registers) and the RGMII specification: the minimum frame size of 64 bytes including the FCS (Listing 1 used it, but tshark would accept other sizes too, so the run does not prove the rule); the maximum standard payload of 1500 bytes; the preamble and start-frame-delimiter lengths; the clause 22 register numbers (basic control register 0, basic status register 1 with the link-status bit, identifier registers 2 and 3); the size of the RGMII clock delay in nanoseconds. Use a PHY's datasheet for its own delay settings.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What a capture can see

Analogy first: a photograph of the box taken in the office shows the label and the meal, but not the van or the road. A packet capture is software looking at frames at one point in the stack; it never sees the line signals and normally not the preamble. D4 This build captured at three points:

  • Our own pcap (Listing 1): the full 64-byte frame including padding and FCS, because we wrote them ourselves; tshark needed to be told the FCS is present (-o eth.fcs:Always -o eth.check_fcs:TRUE). R2
  • QEMU's network back end (the lab's qemu_capture): frames sent and received by a real driver, the iPXE boot ROM on QEMU's emulated Intel e1000, recorded by QEMU's filter-dump. The ROM's DHCP, IPv6 router solicitation and an ARP announcement are all there; the ARP frame is 42 bytes: no padding and no FCS at this capture point. R4
  • The Linux loopback interface of the build machine (the lab's loopback): three UDP datagrams to 127.0.0.1. tshark shows an Ethernet header with source and destination 00:00:00:00:00:00: loopback frames never reach any MAC or PHY, the kernel only gives them a header-shaped wrapper. R5
Not verified — check before relying on this

The general rule "captures taken by the operating system on transmit see the frame before the MAC adds padding and FCS, and receive captures usually have the FCS removed by the NIC" was not tested on a physical NIC in this build. Check the Wireshark User's Guide (sections on capture setup and on Ethernet FCS) and your NIC driver's documentation; some NICs can be configured to keep the FCS.

Why a clock delay must be added exactly once

Analogy first: Rosa and Kwame each waited "one moment" before the handover; together they waited two moments and grabbed every box at the wrong instant. In a source-synchronous, double-data-rate interface such as RGMII, the sender changes the data at the clock edges. If the receiver also samples at the clock edges, it samples exactly while the data is changing, and reads garbage. A delay of about a quarter of the clock period moves the sampling point into the middle of the stable window between changes. D2 The university's toy model (rgmii_model.h) shows the shape of this without real numbers: data changes every 4 ticks, samples within 1 tick of a change are unreliable, and one "delay switch" adds 2 ticks. Its sweep in the forensic answer key shows 100 of 100 frames good at a total delay of 2 ticks and 0 of 100 at 0 or 4 ticks. R7 Nothing in the link state changes: auto-negotiation and MDIO do not use this data path, so the PHY reports link up while every received frame fails its FCS check. D5 R6

On Linux, the driver of an SoC MAC learns from the board's devicetree which side adds the delays, and the PHY driver configures the PHY to match; reading these settings is part of the bring-up plan of curriculum milestone C12 and of the SoC chapters (BR-07). D5 The exact devicetree property names and values are in the unverified box of the forensic answer key.

How the hardware actually does it

In a NIC the MAC is a block of digital logic with transmit and receive FIFOs, an FCS generator and checker that work as the bytes stream through, address filter tables, statistics counters and a DMA engine that fetches and stores frames through descriptor rings (F1-52). The PHY contains analog front ends, clock recovery, encoders and decoders, the auto-negotiation state machine and the management registers. D1 D5 Named examples with public datasheets appear in the curriculum catalog (for instance two PHYs whose datasheets are public); every register or timing number you use must come from such a datasheet for your exact part (AH-21). The lab's devices are emulated: QEMU's e1000 model receives and sends whole frames to a software network, so there is no PHY signal, no FCS on the wire and no clock delay to get wrong. R4

Worked example

Task. Decode the first 16 bytes that Listing 1 printed, ff ff ff ff ff ff 52 54 00 12 34 56 08 06 00 01, by hand, and decide the IG and LG bits of the source address. R1

  1. Bytes 0–5: ff ff ff ff ff ff, the destination: all ones, broadcast.
  2. Bytes 6–11: 52 54 00 12 34 56, the source.
  3. Bytes 12–13: 08 06, high byte first: 0x0806, ARP (as tshark decoded). R2
  4. Bytes 14–15: 00 01: the first ARP field, hardware type 1 (Ethernet in tshark's decode).
  5. First byte of the source: 0x52 = 0101 0010 in binary. Its lowest bit (bit 0) is 0 and bit 1 is 1. tshark reported IG = individual and LG = locally administered for this address, and IG = group for ff (1111 1111, bit 0 = 1). So the IG bit is bit 0 of the first byte, and the LG bit is bit 1. R2
  6. Check with the loopback capture: 00 → both bits 0 → tshark: "Individual address" and "Globally unique address (factory default)". R5

Second task: frame size arithmetic. A frame carries a 28-byte payload: 14 + 28 = 42 bytes before padding. Listing 1 pads to 60, so 18 padding bytes, and the FCS makes 64. A 100-byte payload would need no padding: 14 + 100 + 4 = 118 bytes.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Boxes are packed one after another; bits stream. The MAC computes the FCS while the bytes flow and the receiving MAC checks it as they arrive; nothing waits for a whole "box" to be packed before the van starts. D1
    • 

  • Kwame drives the van; a PHY only encodes. The cable is passive. The "road rhythm" of the story is the PHY's line coding and clock recovery, and the receiving PHY reconstructs the clock from the signal itself. D1
    • 

  • A torn sticker can be seen; a bad FCS is invisible to software. The receiving MAC silently drops the frame and only increments a counter; captures on the receiver normally never show it (forensic lab). D5
    • 

  • "Waiting a moment" sounds harmless. In RGMII the delay is a fraction of a clock period set by hardware strapping, registers or board layout; it is not something software chooses at run time per frame. D2
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: tshark shows no FCS or reports a wrong one on your own frames. Cause: the capture format does not say whether an FCS is present. How to find it: the frame length (60 versus 64 for a minimum frame). Fix: tell the dissector (-o eth.fcs:Always), as the lab does.
    • 

  • Symptom: your hand-made CRC differs from tshark's. Cause: wrong bit order, missing initial value or final inversion, or bytes appended high byte first. How to find it: test with "123456789" against an independent implementation. Fix: follow Listing 1 lines 16–26 and 67–69.
    • 

  • Symptom: link light on, no traffic, receive CRC error counter climbing. Cause: MAC–PHY data path timing (for RGMII, clock delay added twice or never) or a damaged cable. How to find it: error counters plus the PHY's delay registers (forensic lab). Fix: add the delay exactly once.
    • 

  • Symptom: a capture on lo shows zero MAC addresses and you think something is broken. Cause: loopback has no NIC. Fix: capture on the real interface to see real MAC addresses.
    • 

  • Symptom: EtherType or ARP fields look byte-swapped. Cause: writing little-endian. Fix: network byte order (high byte first), put16.
    •

Summary


    

  • An Ethernet frame is destination, source, EtherType, payload (padded), FCS; multi-byte fields go high byte first.
    • 

  • The FCS is a CRC-32; Listing 1's CRC matched Python's zlib and tshark, and a one-bit error was caught.
    • 

  • Bit 0 of the first address byte means group/individual; bit 1 means locally administered (tshark's decode).
    • 

  • The MAC frames, checks, filters and moves frames to memory; the PHY handles the medium and auto-negotiation.
    • 

  • Data goes over an MII-family interface; management goes over MDIO; "link up" comes from the management side only.
    • 

  • RGMII needs the receive clock delayed exactly once; twice or never gives link up with every frame failing its FCS.
    • 

  • Captures see frames at one software point: our pcap, QEMU's back end and loopback each saw something different.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. tx_frames=20 with tx_errors=0; rx_frames_ok=0 with rx_crc_errors=20; 20 ARP requests unanswered. The PHY's status register has LINK = 1 and AUTONEG_DONE = 1. The delay control register shows RX_CLK_DELAY = 1 (and the strap status says the PHY's receive delay is strapped on), while the MAC configuration line says mac_rx_clock_delay=on; the board note says the trace adds no delay.


Root cause. The receive clock is delayed twice: once by the PHY (strap) and once by the MAC (new configuration). In the toy model each switch adds 2 ticks; total 4 ticks puts the sampling point exactly on the data changes, so every received frame is corrupted and fails its FCS. The answer-key sweep (R7) shows 0 good frames at 4 ticks and 100 of 100 at 2 ticks:




Ruling out the alternatives. Cable or far end down: the link is up with auto-negotiation complete, and the same desk works with revision A. Transmit broken: plausible from "no replies" alone, but every reply did arrive as a frame (20 CRC errors for 20 requests), so the far end received our requests; transmit delay is set once (PHY TX delay on, MAC TX off). Receive path: every frame fails the FCS, the signature of a sampling problem.


Fix and verification. Turn one receive delay off: set the MAC's receive clock delay off (as on revision A), or clear the PHY's delay and keep the MAC's; then confirm rx_crc_errors stays at 0 and DHCP succeeds.


Not verified — check before relying on this
On Linux, the devicetree phy-mode values for RGMII ("rgmii", "rgmii-id", "rgmii-rxid", "rgmii-txid") and the convention for which side applies the delay were written from memory. Check the Linux kernel's Ethernet devicetree binding documentation and the phylib documentation of the kernel version you use, and the PHY's datasheet for its delay registers and strap pins.


Prevention. The bring-up checklist records, for each direction, who adds the clock delay (PHY strap or register, MAC setting, board trace) and requires that exactly one is on; the first network test checks the receive CRC error counter, not only the link light.


How the evidence was made. rgmii_evidence.cpp runs the toy link with PHY receive delay on, MAC receive delay on and no board delay, 20 frames; the register dump and log lines are printed by the program in its own format.
