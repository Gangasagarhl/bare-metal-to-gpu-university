Story hook

Parcels arrive at the restaurant's back door all day, often while every cook is busy. Lena, who runs the stores, solves this with a round rack of eight numbered shelves by the door, each with an empty crate on it. The courier has one rule: put each parcel into the crate on the next shelf, flip the shelf's little flag to "full", and ring the kitchen bell — but never touch a shelf past the red marker. When Lena has a free minute she empties the full crates, puts fresh empty ones on the shelves, and moves the red marker forward. Nobody waits for anyone: the courier and Lena work at their own speed, and the marker tells each of them where their part of the rack ends.

One week Lena's new assistant forgets the last step. He empties the crates perfectly — but never moves the red marker. On Monday morning seven parcels arrive and are stored. Then the courier reaches the marker. From then on every parcel is sent back, although the rack is full of empty crates. The rack is a NIC descriptor ring, the courier is the NIC's DMA engine, the red marker is the tail register, and "the network dies after a few seconds" is this chapter's forensic lab.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how a NIC moves frames to and from memory without the CPU copying them: descriptor rings, DMA, head and tail, interrupts.
  • Trace who owns each ring slot (driver or NIC) through a sequence of receives and refills, and predict when packets are dropped.
  • Read a real QEMU e1000e trace: ring set-up writes, tail-register writes, descriptor fetches and interrupts, and correlate it with tshark's view of the same frames.
  • Compare the e1000-family ring design with NVMe queues (F1-50) and name what a virtio-net device does differently.
  • Diagnose a receive stall from counters and a register dump.
Jargon box
NIC (network interface controller)
Simple words: the network card or chip of a computer. Analogy: the back door with its courier and parcel rack. Precise: a device, on PCs attached over PCIe, containing at least a MAC (often also a PHY), packet buffers, DMA engines, descriptor rings and interrupt logic; the driver controls it through registers in a BAR. D1 R2
Descriptor (NIC ring)
Simple words: a small record in memory that tells the NIC where a packet buffer is and, after use, what happened to it. Analogy: one shelf with its crate and flag. Precise: a fixed-size structure (16 bytes for the e1000e descriptors in this chapter's trace) holding a buffer's physical address, a length and command/status bits; the NIC reads it by DMA and writes status back. D1 R3
Descriptor ring
Simple words: a circular list of descriptors shared by the driver and the NIC. Analogy: the round rack of shelves. Precise: a contiguous array of descriptors in host memory whose base address and length are written to NIC registers; the NIC and driver advance around it, wrapping at the end; separate rings exist for transmit and receive. D1 D4
Head and tail (of a NIC ring)
Simple words: two slot numbers that split the ring into "NIC's part" and "driver's part". Analogy: the courier's position and the red marker. Precise: the head is advanced by the NIC as it consumes descriptors; the tail is written by the driver to hand descriptors to the NIC; the NIC uses the slots from head up to, not including, tail. D1 R1
DMA (direct memory access)
Simple words: a device reading or writing the computer's memory by itself, without the CPU copying each byte. Analogy: the courier walking to the shelf himself. Precise: bus-master transactions (on PCIe: memory read and write requests from the device) addressed with physical or I/O-virtual addresses that the driver provided. D4 D6
Interrupt (from a NIC)
Simple words: the NIC's signal to the CPU that something happened, for example a packet arrived. Analogy: the kitchen bell. Precise: a legacy pin interrupt, MSI or MSI-X message raised when an enabled cause bit is set in the NIC's interrupt cause register; the driver reads the causes and handles the rings. D1 D4 R3
Missed packet (no descriptor)
Simple words: a packet the NIC had to throw away because the driver had given it no free buffer. Analogy: a parcel sent back at the red marker. Precise: a receive drop counted by the NIC when head reaches tail; the frame never reaches memory or the operating system. D1 R1
virtqueue
Simple words: the queue format of virtio devices, the "paravirtual" devices made for virtual machines. Analogy: a rack designed for couriers who are part of the same company. Precise: a ring structure defined by the VIRTIO specification, shared between a guest driver and a host device, with buffers described in a descriptor table and an "available" and a "used" ring; QEMU's virtio-net NIC uses it. D3 R2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A network card receives messages all the time, even when the computer's processor is busy with something else. So the card and the computer agree on a set of empty boxes in the computer's memory, arranged in a circle. When a message arrives, the card puts it into the next empty box by itself, marks the box as full and rings a bell.

When the processor has time, it takes the messages out of the full boxes, puts empty boxes back and tells the card "you may use the boxes up to here". The card never goes past that point. If the processor is too slow and the card runs out of empty boxes, new messages are lost. Sending works the same way the other way round: the processor fills boxes with messages and the card picks them up.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why rings?

Frames arrive whenever the network delivers them, at rates set by the other machines, while the CPU runs other code. The NIC therefore needs memory it can write without asking the CPU each time, and the driver needs a way to find the new frames later. A ring of descriptors in host memory does both: the driver prepares many buffers in advance, the NIC fills them by DMA as frames arrive, and the driver collects them in batches. D1 D4 This is the same producer–consumer idea as NVMe's queues in F1-50; the NIC's version is older and its details differ (see Layer 3).

Who owns which slot

Listing 1 is the university's toy ring with 8 slots. A descriptor in the toy has a buffer number, a length and a "done" flag; a real one has a 64-bit buffer address and status bits defined by the NIC's datasheet. R1 D1 The rule is in ring_model.h: the NIC may write slots from head up to, but not including, tail; when head equals tail it owns nothing and must drop the frame (line 33). At start-up the driver attaches a buffer to every slot but writes tail = 7, not 8, because with 8 slots a tail equal to head would mean "nothing for the NIC", the same value as "everything for the NIC"; keeping one slot back removes the ambiguity. R1 NVMe solved the same ambiguity on its completion queue with a phase bit (F1-50); here the driver owns the gap.

The run (R1) shows the cycle. Five frames arrive: head moves to 5, slots 0–4 are done. The driver's interrupt handler finds them, attaches fresh buffers and writes tail = 4 — the slot before the next one it will check — so the NIC's part now wraps from 5 round to 3. Nine more frames arrive before the driver runs again: the NIC fills slots 5, 6, 7, 0, 1, 2, 3, reaches the tail at 4 and drops the last two: missed=2. The driver then handles 7 frames and writes tail = 3. R1 Missed frames never reach memory; only the counter knows about them.

Transmit is the mirror image

For sending, the driver is the producer: it writes a frame into a buffer, fills the next transmit descriptor (address, length, command bits), and writes the transmit tail register; the NIC fetches the descriptor and the frame by DMA, sends the frame, and reports completion in the descriptor or by advancing its head. D1 In the lab's real trace each transmit tail write (register 0x3818, values 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) is followed by QEMU's e1000e_tx_descr event, and the low 16 bits of the descriptor word it prints are 0x1ad = 429, 0x46 = 70, 0x1ad = 429, 0x1b9 = 441 and 0x2a = 42 — the lengths tshark reports for the DHCP Discover, the IPv6 router solicitation, the second Discover, the DHCP Request and the ARP announcement in the same run. Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Reading the real trace

Analogy first: Lena sets up the rack by telling the courier where it stands, how long it is and where the red marker starts. The QEMU trace (R3) shows a real driver, the iPXE boot ROM, doing exactly that with QEMU's e1000e model of the Intel 82574L (the name pci.ids gives ID 8086:10d3, R2). R2 R3 In order:

  • Register 0x3800 ← 0x7f5da00, 0x3804 ← 0, 0x3808 ← 0x100, 0x3818 ← 0: a transmit ring at physical address 0x7f5da00, 0x100 = 256 bytes long, tail 0. R3 D7
  • Register 0x2800 ← 0x7f5db00, 0x2808 ← 0x100, 0x2818 ← 0: the receive ring right after it, 256 bytes. QEMU itself names the write to 0x2818: "Setting RDT[0] = 0" — RDT is the receive descriptor tail. R3 D7
  • Registers 0x5400 and 0x5404 ← 0x12005452 and 0x80005634: read as little-endian bytes these are 52 54 00 12 and 34 56 — the MAC address 52:54:00:12:34:56 given on the QEMU command line. R3
  • Register 0x2818 ← 8: "Setting RDT[0] = 8": the ROM hands 8 descriptors to the NIC. R3
  • Then five rounds: a transmit tail write, a transmitted descriptor, a receive descriptor fetched at 0x7f5db00, 0x7f5db10, 0x7f5db20, 0x7f5db30 (each 0x10 = 16 bytes after the previous one; QEMU prints "length: 16"), "Received packet written to guest", an interrupt cause bit set in register 0xc0, and the ROM moving RDT to 9, 10, 11, 12. R3

Two derived numbers: with 16-byte descriptors, each 256-byte ring holds 256 / 16 = 16 descriptors; and the ROM gives the NIC 8 of the 16 at first, then one more after each frame it takes — the refill pattern of Listing 1, on a real driver. The four received frames match tshark's list: the two DHCP Offers, the router advertisement and the DHCP ACK. R3

Not verified — check before relying on this

The ring register names used above — 0x2800 RDBAL, 0x2804 RDBAH, 0x2808 RDLEN, 0x2810 RDH, 0x2818 RDT and 0x3800–0x3818 TDBAL, TDBAH, TDLEN, TDH, TDT — are confirmed from QEMU's own register header for the device the lab ran (D7, tier 4: "what this implementation does"), not from the Intel datasheets. Still not checked against any document: that 0x5400/0x5404 are the receive address registers RAL0/RAH0 with an "address valid" bit at the top of RAH (which would explain the 0x8000 in 0x80005634); that 0xc0 is the interrupt cause register ICR and the meaning of its bits; the meaning of the 0xb in the transmit descriptor words (end-of-packet and report-status bits); the 82574L's receive descriptor format. Check the Intel 82574 family datasheet (and the 8254x developer's manual for the e1000), or QEMU's hw/net/e1000x_regs.h, which holds those definitions.

Interrupts, batching and polling

Analogy first: if the bell rang for every parcel, Lena would do nothing but walk to the door. A driver handles all done descriptors per interrupt, as Listing 1's service loop does, and NICs can delay or merge interrupts (interrupt moderation); under heavy load Linux drivers switch to polling the ring for a while (NAPI). D4 D5 In the trace the e1000e sets a cause bit for each received frame: register 0xc0 goes from 0x0 to 0x80, then to 0x80000083 (R3). Which bits mean what, and how the ROM acknowledges them, is in the unverified box above. Multi-queue NICs have several receive rings, steer flows between them by hashing packet headers (receive-side scaling) and give each ring its own MSI-X vector, so that several CPU cores receive in parallel. D2 D5 These are named here as a map; none was tested in this build.

Memory rules: addresses, ordering, coherence

Analogy first: the courier only knows street addresses, not the names Lena uses inside the building. The NIC is given physical (or IOMMU-translated) addresses; the 0x7f5db00 in the trace is a physical address the ROM chose, not a pointer the CPU uses in a program. R3 D4 A driver must also order its writes: the descriptor and buffer contents must be visible in memory before the tail write tells the NIC to look, and a driver must read a descriptor's "done" status before it reads the length and data the NIC wrote. Linux provides DMA-mapping functions and memory barriers for exactly these rules. D5 Listing 1 runs in one thread, so it cannot show reordering; it only shows the protocol.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Named from memory, not checked: the Linux functions dma_alloc_coherent, dma_map_single, wmb()/dma_wmb() and rmb()/dma_rmb(), the NAPI functions, and the claim that the Linux e1000e driver writes its tail register only after a write barrier. Check the kernel's DMA API documentation, the memory-barriers document and drivers/net/ethernet/intel/e1000e in the kernel version you use.

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a NIC, the receive path is a pipeline: the MAC (F1-51) checks the FCS and the address filter, the frame goes into an on-chip packet buffer, a descriptor-fetch engine keeps a few descriptors cached ahead of head, and the DMA engine writes the frame into host memory with PCIe memory-write transactions, then writes the descriptor status back and raises the interrupt. D1 D2 If the descriptors run out, the on-chip buffer fills and further frames are dropped and counted. The lab's devices are QEMU's software models; they behave like this at the level of registers and memory but have no on-chip buffer, timing or PCIe link. R3 The real QEMU NICs (R2) also show the hardware differences in their BARs: the e1000 has a memory BAR and an I/O BAR; the e1000e has three memory BARs and one I/O BAR; the virtio-net device has an I/O BAR, a small memory BAR and a 64-bit prefetchable memory BAR. R2

Worked example

Task. In Listing 1's second round (head = 5, tail = 4), nine frames arrive. Which slots do they go to, how many are missed, and where are head and tail afterwards? Then, if the driver handles them and writes tail = (next − 1) mod 8, what tail does it write?

  1. The NIC owns slots 5, 6, 7, 0, 1, 2, 3 — seven slots (from head 5 up to tail 4, wrapping).
  2. Frames 1–7 go to slots 5, 6, 7, 0, 1, 2, 3; head becomes 4 = tail.
  3. Frames 8 and 9 find head = tail: missed = 2.
  4. The driver starts at slot 5 (its "next" after round 1) and handles 7 frames: 5, 6, 7, 0, 1, 2, 3; its next slot is 4.
  5. Tail = (4 + 8 − 1) mod 8 = 3.

The run printed "missed=2", the seven frames in that order and "wrote tail = 3". R1

Second task: from the real trace. The receive ring starts at 0x7f5db00 with 16-byte descriptors. Descriptor number k is at 0x7f5db00 + 16·k. The fourth frame (k = 3) is at 0x7f5db00 + 0x30 = 0x7f5db30 — the address QEMU printed for the fourth received frame. R3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lena moves the marker when she likes; a driver's tail write is a precise register write. It goes across PCIe to the NIC, and its order relative to the descriptor writes matters (Layer 3). D5
    • 

  • Parcels sent back go back to the sender; missed frames vanish. Ethernet has no automatic resend; a higher protocol (for example TCP) may resend later. D1
    • 

  • The rack has one courier; a modern NIC has many rings. Multiple queues let several CPU cores work at once (Layer 3). D2
    • 

  • The courier reads the shelf number; the NIC reads a physical address. The driver must translate its buffers into addresses the device can use, possibly through an IOMMU. D4
    • 

  • The F1-50 "doorbell" and this chapter's "bell" differ. In NVMe the doorbell is the host telling the device; here the bell is the device's interrupt telling the host. The NIC's equivalent of an NVMe doorbell is the tail register write. (Ruling A3: the two images stay distinct.)
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: receive works for a few seconds, then nothing; a "missed" or "no buffer" counter rises. Cause: tail never advanced, or buffers not refilled. How to find it: compare head and tail registers (forensic lab). Fix: write the tail after refilling.
    • 

  • Symptom: the NIC receives nothing at all from the start. Cause: tail written as 0 (equal to head) or never written, ring length 0, or receive not enabled. How to find it: trace or read back the ring registers. Fix: set base, length, then tail, then enable.
    • 

  • Symptom: corrupted or stale data, only sometimes. Cause: missing barrier or DMA synchronisation between the CPU and the device. Fix: use the kernel's DMA API and barriers (unverified box).
    • 

  • Symptom: off-by-one, ring "full" while empty. Cause: giving all N slots (tail = head). Fix: keep one slot back.
    • 

  • Symptom: frame lengths 4 bytes longer than tshark shows. Cause: the NIC was configured to keep the FCS (F1-51). Fix: check the receive control settings.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F1-51 (frames, FCS, MAC and PHY) and F1-50 (NVMe queues, doorbells, phase). What carries over: rings in host memory, a producer and a consumer, a register write that hands over work, DMA by the device, and QEMU traces as evidence. R3 What changes: work arrives unasked from the network, so the driver must prepare receive buffers in advance and frames are lost when it falls behind; status is written back into the same descriptor; interrupts must be batched to keep up. D1 D4 What comes next: F1-53 asks what happens when the NIC writes not only into buffers the receiving driver prepared, but directly into an application's memory on another machine: RDMA.

Summary


    

  • A NIC moves frames by DMA through descriptor rings in host memory; separate rings for receive and transmit.
    • 

  • The NIC owns slots from head up to tail−1; the driver hands slots over by writing the tail register.
    • 

  • If head reaches tail, the NIC drops frames and counts them; nothing reaches memory.
    • 

  • One slot stays with the driver so that full and empty differ.
    • 

  • The real e1000e trace showed ring set-up, RDT 8 → 12, 16-byte descriptors and transmit lengths equal to tshark's frame lengths.
    • 

  • NVMe and virtio solve the same handover with different completion and full/empty rules.
    • 

  • Barriers and DMA mapping keep the CPU's and the NIC's views of memory consistent.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. 3 frames delivered per second for 2 seconds (3, 6), then only 1 more (7) and never again; missed rises 2, 5, 8, 11 — every frame after the seventh is dropped by the NIC.


Root cause. The NIC was given 7 slots at start-up (tail = 7). Head reached 7 = tail after 7 frames; the tail is still 7 at every sample. The driver did refill every slot ("buffers attached in all 8 slots") but never wrote the tail register again, so the NIC never learned the slots were free. Line 31 of rx_stall.cpp marks the missing write:

25–32service loopTakes each done frame and attaches a buffer.Correct, except for the missing tail write at line 31.


33–36printingCounters per second, register dump, driver state.The evidence pack.




Ruling out the alternatives. Link failure: the missed counter grows, so the NIC still receives frames from the wire. Interrupts stopped: the driver's next slot (7) equals head; there is nothing new for it to find. Buffers not refilled: the dump says all slots have buffers.


Fix. After the service loop, write the tail: nic.writeTail((driverNext + slots - 1) % slots); as ring.cpp line 37 does. Verify that delivered keeps rising by 3 per second and missed stays 0.


Prevention. A test that receives more frames than the ring size (here, more than 7) must be part of the driver's test suite; and a debug check that warns when the missed counter rises while the driver holds refilled slots.
