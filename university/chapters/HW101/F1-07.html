Story hook

Analogy

Mr Okafor, the plumber, carries two tools into the restaurant's basement. The first is a pressure gauge with two little hoses. He touches one hose to a joint in the pipe and the other to the drain, and the needle shows the difference in pressure. Nothing has to be taken apart, and almost no water leaves the pipe.

The second tool is a flow meter. To use it, he must close the valve, cut the pipe open and fit the meter into the gap, so that all the water passes through it. "Never," he warns his apprentice Ines, "fit the flow meter straight across the tank's outlet and the drain. It has almost no resistance. The whole tank would empty through it in one rush, and the meter would burst."

A multimeter is both tools in one box, plus a third: a tester for how narrow a pipe is. Used the right way, it tells you the truth about a circuit. Used the wrong way, it can lie to you or even get damaged. This chapter is about using it the right way.

What you will be able to do

  • Measure voltage with the meter in parallel with the part, and read the sign correctly.
  • Measure current with the meter in series, and explain why it must never go across a source.
  • Measure resistance with the power off and the part isolated.
  • Estimate how much a meter disturbs the circuit it measures (loading and burden), with a C++ model.
  • Diagnose a meter whose current fuse has opened, from a notebook of readings.
Jargon box
Multimeter
Simple words: one meter that can measure voltage, current and resistance. Analogy: the plumber's pressure gauge, flow meter and pipe tester in one box. Precise: a test instrument with selectable modes and ranges (at least voltage, current and resistance), connected through two probes to sockets chosen for the mode; the exact sockets, ranges and limits are given in its user manual. D5 §10.4 D21 D4
Voltmeter (in parallel)
Simple words: the voltage mode: probes touch the two ends of a part. Analogy: the pressure gauge's two hoses on two joints. Precise: a voltage measurement is made with the meter connected in parallel with the part; an ideal voltmeter draws no current; a real one has a large but finite input resistance. D5 §10.4
Ammeter (in series)
Simple words: the current mode: you open the loop and let the current pass through the meter. Analogy: the flow meter fitted into the cut pipe. Precise: a current measurement is made with the meter in series, so the circuit's current flows through the meter's small internal (shunt) resistance; an ideal ammeter has zero resistance. D5 §10.4 D13
Ohmmeter
Simple words: the resistance mode: the meter sends its own small current through a part. Analogy: testing how narrow a pipe is after taking it out of the building. Precise: a resistance measurement in which the meter applies a known small current or voltage from its own battery; it is valid only with the circuit unpowered, and the part should be isolated from parallel paths. D5 §10.4 D21
Input resistance
Simple words: how much the voltmeter itself resists current; bigger is better. Analogy: how little water the gauge's hoses let through. Precise: the resistance a voltmeter presents between its probes; it appears in parallel with the measured part and causes loading (F1-04); its value is given in the meter's manual. D5 §10.4 D21 D4
Burden (shunt) resistance
Simple words: the small resistance inside the meter in current mode. Analogy: the slight narrowing the flow meter adds to the pipe. Precise: the series resistance an ammeter adds to the circuit; the voltage across it (the burden voltage) slightly reduces the current being measured. D13
Fuse
Simple words: a weak link that breaks on purpose when the current is too high. Analogy: a pipe section designed to burst first, safely, before anything else does. Precise: a protective device that opens the circuit when its current exceeds its rating for long enough; a meter's current input is protected by a fuse whose rating is in its manual. D13 D21 D4

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the three ways of connecting a meter. Voltage: across the part, with the circuit working. Current: in series, with the loop opened and the meter fitted into the gap. Resistance: the part out of the circuit, with no power at all.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A multimeter is a box with a dial, a display and two probes, one red and one black. The dial chooses what you measure.D21

To measure voltage, you touch the probes to the two ends of a part, while the circuit is on. The meter sits beside the part. It is like Mr Okafor's pressure gauge: it compares two points and takes almost nothing from the circuit.D5 §10.4

To measure current, you must open the loop and put the meter into the gap. All the current then flows through the meter. It is like fitting the flow meter into a cut pipe. On many meters, you also move the red probe to a different socket for current. The manual says which.D13 D4

Here is the golden rule. In current mode, the meter has almost no resistance. Never touch its probes straight across a battery. A huge current would flow through it, and its fuse would blow, or worse.D21

To measure resistance, switch the circuit off and take the part out. The meter uses its own tiny battery to test the part. If the circuit is still on, the reading is wrong, and the meter can be harmed.D21 D5 §10.4

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Voltage: parallel, and the loading error

A voltmeter shows V(red) − V(black), as in F1-01. It is connected in parallel with the part, so it adds a path for current: its input resistance Rm. For an ideal meter Rm would be infinite. For a real one it is large, and it is listed in the manual.D5 §10.4 D21 D4

When the part's own resistance is small compared with Rm, the effect is negligible. When it is comparable, the meter loads the circuit exactly as a sensor loaded the divider in F1-04, and the reading is too low. Listing 1 shows this: on a divider of two 1 MΩ resistors, a meter with Rm = 1 MΩ (pretend) reads 2.000 V instead of 3.000 V.R1

Current: series, burden, and the fuse

An ammeter goes in series and adds its small burden resistance Rs to the loop. That lowers the current a little: I = V ÷ (R + Rs). The error matters only when the circuit's own resistance is small.D13 D5 §10.4 In Listing 1, a 10 Ω burden in a 100 Ω loop reduces the reading by 9.1 %; a 0.1 Ω burden by 0.1 %.

Because Rs is small, putting an ammeter across a source (in parallel) makes a near short circuit (F1-03). The current is limited only by the source's internal resistance and Rs. That is why current inputs are fused: the fuse opens to protect the meter.D21 D5 §10.1 A meter with an open fuse reads 0 in current mode, whatever the circuit does: a classic trap, and the forensic lab.

Resistance: power off, part isolated

An ohmmeter drives its own small current through the part and measures the voltage. Any other source in the circuit would add to or fight with that current, so the circuit must be unpowered.D21 D5 §10.4 Other parts in parallel with the one you test give you the parallel combination (F1-04), not the part's own value; lift one leg of the part, or take it out. Capacitors should be discharged first (F1-05).

Ranges, resolution and accuracy

A meter shows a limited number of digits. The smallest step it can show on a range is its resolution. How close the reading is to the truth is its accuracy, which the manual states.D4 When you do not know the size of a voltage or current, start on the highest range and work down, unless the meter selects ranges by itself (the manual says).D4 How your meter shows an over-range reading (some show "OL") is in its manual.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Inside, a digital multimeter uses an analog-to-digital converter (F1-08, HW302). For current it uses its shunt, whose small voltage is the burden voltage (D13). How the chosen meter turns resistance and voltage into a reading is in its documentation, not in this chapter.D5 §10.4 Every measurement disturbs the system a little. An engineer's habit is to ask, for each reading, "how much did my instrument change what I measured?", and to estimate it, as Listing 1 does. Changing signals add a further question: is the meter fast enough? That leads to the oscilloscope and the logic analyser in later courses (HW204).

How the hardware actually does it

The current shunt is a resistor of very small, precisely known value. The meter reads the voltage across it and divides by its resistance: Ohm's law used backwards.D13 D5 §9.4 The fuse is a short piece of conductor that melts when too much current heats it, opening the path (F1-03's I² × R heating used on purpose).D5 §9.5 The values of the shunts and the fuses, and how to replace a fuse safely, are in the meter's manual (D4); a replaced fuse must match the manual's rating exactly.

Worked example

Exercise values.

  1. Voltmeter loading. Divider: 6 V, R = 100 kΩ top and bottom; meter Rm = 1 MΩ across the bottom. Bottom becomes 100 kΩ ∥ 1 MΩ = 100,000 × 1,000,000 ÷ 1,100,000 ≈ 90.9 kΩ. Reading = 6 × 90.9 ÷ (100 + 90.9) ≈ 2.857 V, about 4.8 % low.
  2. Same divider with Rm = 10 MΩ: bottom ≈ 99.0 kΩ; reading ≈ 2.985 V, 0.5 % low.
  3. Ammeter burden. 1 V across 100 Ω: true current 10 mA. With Rs = 1 Ω: 1 ÷ 101 ≈ 9.901 mA, 1 % low.
  4. Ammeter across a source (do not do this). 6 V battery with pretend internal resistance 0.5 Ω, meter Rs = 0.1 Ω: I = 6 ÷ 0.6 = 10 A. Power in the meter's shunt: 10² × 0.1 = 10 W. Any small fuse opens.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. A pressure gauge on a water pipe takes no water at all; a voltmeter does take a little current. That small current is the loading error of Listing 1, and it can be large in high-resistance circuits.D5 §10.4
    2. 

  2. Cutting a water pipe is a big job; opening a circuit loop is easy, and so is forgetting to close it. After a current measurement the loop must be restored; with the meter removed and the gap left open, nothing works.
    3. 

  3. A plumber's pipe tester does not need the water off; an ohmmeter needs the power off. The ohmmeter injects its own current, and any live source corrupts the reading.D5 §10.4 D21

Summary


    

  • Voltage: meter in parallel, circuit powered; reading = V(red) − V(black).
    • 

  • Current: meter in series, loop opened; never across a source.
    • 

  • Resistance: power off, part isolated; the meter supplies its own current.
    • 

  • A voltmeter's input resistance loads high-resistance circuits; an ammeter's burden lowers low-resistance currents.
    • 

  • Current inputs are fused; an open fuse reads 0 in current mode.
    • 

  • Every real reading needs mode, range, date, instrument and accuracy.
    • 

  • No mains, ever; supervised, low-voltage kits only.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: steps 1–3 are as expected: battery 6.00 V; resistor 4.00 V (6 − 2 V LED); current 4.00 mA (4 V ÷ 1 kΩ, minus a negligible burden). In step 4 Ines put the meter, in current mode, with its probes on the battery terminals: "OL" means the current was above the range. With the model's pretend 0.5 Ω battery resistance and 0.1 Ω shunt, about 10 A flowed, far above the pretend 500 mA fuse, which opened. Steps 5–7: every current reading is 0.00 mA, but step 6 shows 4.00 V across the resistor, which means 4 mA is flowing when the loop is closed without the meter. Root cause: the meter's current fuse opened at step 4; the meter, not the circuit, is faulty. "LED broken" is ruled out by step 6 (the loop conducts and the LED takes its 2 V share). Action: stop using current mode; the supervisor replaces the fuse with exactly the rating in the manual (D4) and checks the meter on a known circuit. Prevention: current only in series; before connecting in current mode, say aloud "where does the current come from and where does it go?". How the evidence was made: meter_log.cpp with meter_log.in (steps 1–7), ideal model with pretend values (R1).
