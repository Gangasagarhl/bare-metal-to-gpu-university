Story hook

Analogy

In the restaurant kitchen there are two ways news reaches Aiko. The oven has a thermometer with a needle. It can point anywhere: a bit warmer, a bit cooler, any value in between. The pass has a bell. When a dish is ready, a cook rings it. The bell is either ringing or not.

The thermometer tells Aiko much more, but she must look closely, and a shaky needle is hard to read. The bell tells her only one thing, yet she hears it clearly even when the kitchen is noisy. Kemal, the new waiter, once rang the bell three times by accident because his hand trembled on the rope. The cooks thought three dishes were ready.

Electronics uses both kinds of signal. Analog signals are like the thermometer. Digital signals are like the bell. This chapter shows how a circuit turns one into the other, why a trembling hand fools a simple bell, and how a capacitor and two thresholds make a light blink on its own: the HW101 course project.

What you will be able to do

  • Tell an analog signal from a digital one, and give an example of each.
  • Convert an analog voltage into a 1-bit and a 3-bit digital value with a threshold and with equal steps.
  • Explain why digital signals tolerate noise, and how a single threshold can still be fooled.
  • Use hysteresis (two thresholds) to clean a noisy signal.
  • Predict the blink period of an RC relaxation oscillator and check it against a simulation.
Jargon box
Signal
Simple words: a voltage that changes over time to carry information. Analogy: any message reaching Aiko: the needle's position or the bell's ringing. Precise: a quantity, here a voltage, whose variation in time represents information. D18
Analog signal
Simple words: a signal that can take any value in a range. Analogy: the oven thermometer's needle. Precise: a signal whose value varies continuously and whose exact value carries the information. D18
Digital signal
Simple words: a signal that is read as one of a few values, usually just HIGH or LOW. Analogy: the order bell: ringing or not. Precise: a signal interpreted as one of a finite set of levels, in binary logic two (1 and 0), each corresponding to a range of voltages. D16
Threshold
Simple words: the line between "counts as LOW" and "counts as HIGH". Analogy: how hard the rope must be pulled before the bell rings. Precise: the input voltage at which a digital input's interpretation changes; real logic families specify ranges guaranteed to be read as LOW or HIGH in their datasheets. D16 D4
Noise
Simple words: unwanted wiggles added to a signal. Analogy: Kemal's trembling hand on the bell rope. Precise: unwanted random or interfering variations superimposed on a signal. D14
Hysteresis
Simple words: using two thresholds: a higher one to switch on, a lower one to switch off. Analogy: the bell only rings after a firm pull, and only resets after the rope is fully let go. Precise: a property of a circuit whose switching threshold depends on its present state; a Schmitt trigger has an upper threshold for rising inputs and a lower one for falling inputs. D14
Sampling and quantisation
Simple words: reading an analog voltage at moments in time and rounding it to one of a fixed set of steps. Analogy: Aiko writing down the thermometer every few minutes, to the nearest mark. Precise: sampling takes values at discrete times; quantisation maps each value to one of 2n codes for an n-bit converter (an analog-to-digital converter, ADC). D18 D19
Oscillator
Simple words: a circuit that keeps switching back and forth on its own. Analogy: a kitchen timer that rings, resets itself and rings again. Precise: a circuit that produces a periodic output without a periodic input; a relaxation oscillator does it by charging and discharging a capacitor between two thresholds. D15

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A signal is a voltage that changes to carry a message. Some signals can be any value. A light sensor's voltage creeps up slowly as the room gets brighter. That is an analog signal, like the oven thermometer.D18

Other signals are read as just two values: HIGH or LOW, 1 or 0. That is a digital signal, like the order bell. Computers use digital signals everywhere. A bit, which you met in KID101, travels on a wire as HIGH or LOW.D16

How does a circuit decide? It uses a threshold. Above the line, it reads HIGH. Below, it reads LOW. A little wobble on the wire does not matter, as long as the voltage stays clearly on one side. That is why digital signals are so reliable.D16 D17

But a slow, shaky signal near the line can cross it several times. Then one real event looks like many, just as Kemal's trembling hand rang the bell three times. The cure is to use two lines: one to switch on and a lower one to switch off. That trick is called hysteresis.D14

The same two-line trick, with a capacitor, makes a light blink all by itself. You will build that, first in the simulator.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From analog to digital

Turning an analog voltage into numbers takes two steps. Sampling takes its value at chosen moments. Quantisation rounds each value to one of a fixed number of steps. With n bits there are 2n steps: 1 bit gives 2 (a single threshold), 3 bits give 8.R1 Listing 1 does both for a smooth wave: for a 0–5 V range and 3 bits, each step is 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625 V wide, and a sample of 3.147 V becomes code 5 (101 in binary, MA102), because 3.147 ÷ 0.625 = 5.03.R1

Something is always lost. Between samples you do not know what happened, and within a step all voltages look the same. More bits and faster sampling lose less. HW302 (F1-64) studies analog-to-digital converters properly.

Logic levels and noise margin

Real digital inputs do not use one sharp line. Their datasheets guarantee that voltages below one level are read as LOW and voltages above a higher level as HIGH; in between, the result is not guaranteed. Outputs are guaranteed to be lower than the LOW limit or higher than the HIGH limit. The gap between what an output guarantees and what an input needs is the noise margin: noise smaller than that cannot flip a bit.D16 D17

Hysteresis

A slow input near a single threshold, with a little noise on it, can cross the threshold several times. Each crossing is a new edge. A counter connected to it counts too many events: the forensic lab. A Schmitt trigger input uses two thresholds. A rising input must pass the upper one to become HIGH; a falling input must pass the lower one to become LOW. Noise smaller than the gap between them cannot cause extra edges.D14

The RC blinker: a relaxation oscillator

Now combine F1-05 and hysteresis. Take a Schmitt-trigger inverter: its output is HIGH when its input is LOW, and LOW when its input is HIGH. Connect a resistor from its output back to its input, and a capacitor from its input to ground.D15

  1. Start: the capacitor is empty, the input LOW, so the output is HIGH. The capacitor charges towards Vdd through R.
  2. When Vc passes the upper threshold VT+, the output switches LOW. Now the capacitor discharges towards 0 V through R.
  3. When Vc falls below the lower threshold VT−, the output switches HIGH again. Repeat forever.

Each half is a piece of the RC curve.D5 §10.5 Using F1-05's "time to reach a voltage" formula, the HIGH time (charging from VT− to VT+) is tH = R × C × ln((Vdd − VT−) ÷ (Vdd − VT+)), and the LOW time (discharging from VT+ to VT−) is tL = R × C × ln(VT+ ÷ VT−). With exercise values Vdd = 5 V, VT+ = 3 V, VT− = 2 V and R × C = 1 s, both are ln 1.5 ≈ 0.4055 s, a period of 0.811 s. An LED with its own series resistor (F1-06) on the output blinks at that rate. Listing 2 simulates it.R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. The blink period depends on R, C and the two thresholds. Real Schmitt thresholds vary from part to part and with supply voltage and temperature, within limits the datasheet states; so do R and C within their tolerances. An engineer therefore predicts a range of periods, not one number, and checks the measured period against that range.D15 D14 D4 The first cycle is longer, because the capacitor starts from 0 V instead of from VT−: Listing 2 shows 0.9163 s, which is τ × ln(5 ÷ 2). The same "a capacitor charging to a threshold" idea, at nanosecond scale inside chips, sets gate delays (F1-15) and limits clock speeds (F1-18).

How the hardware actually does it

A digital input is built from transistors (F1-09). A Schmitt-trigger input switches at a higher point on a rising input and at a lower point on a falling one; how the transistors inside achieve that is HW102's topic.D14 An analog-to-digital converter turns each sample into a code; how a particular converter does it, and its resolution and speed, are in its datasheet. HW102 builds gates from transistors, and HW302 studies converters.

Worked example

Exercise values.

  1. 3-bit code. Range 0–5 V, 8 steps of 0.625 V. A sample of 4.268 V: 4.268 ÷ 0.625 = 6.83, so code 6 (110). A sample of 0.732 V: 1.17, so code 1 (001). A sample of 4.915 V: 7.86, so code 7 (111).
  2. 1-bit. Threshold 2.5 V: 1.853 V gives 0; 3.147 V gives 1.
  3. Blinker HIGH time. tH = 1 s × ln((5 − 2) ÷ (5 − 3)) = ln 1.5 = 0.4055 s.
  4. Blinker LOW time. tL = 1 s × ln(3 ÷ 2) = 0.4055 s. Period 0.811 s, about 1.23 blinks per second.
  5. First HIGH period (from 0 V to 3 V): 1 s × ln(5 ÷ 2) = 0.9163 s.
  6. Slower blink. Double C: every time doubles: period 1.622 s.

Listings 1 and 2 print these values.R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. A bell is either ringing or silent; a digital wire is always at some voltage. "HIGH" and "LOW" are interpretations of ranges of an analog voltage. Between the guaranteed ranges, a real input's reading is undefined, which a bell has no equivalent for.D16
    2. 

  2. Aiko can glance at the thermometer whenever she likes; a sampled system sees only its samples. If the signal changes faster than the sampling, the samples can tell a completely wrong story (HW302 explains aliasing).D18
    3. 

  3. A trembling hand is one kind of noise; electrical noise has many sources (other wires, motors, the circuit's own parts), and hysteresis helps only with noise smaller than the gap between the thresholds.
    4.

Summary


    

  • Analog signals take any value; digital signals are read as one of a few levels, usually two.
    • 

  • Sampling and quantisation turn analog into numbers; n bits give 2n codes.
    • 

  • Thresholds and noise margins let digital signals ignore small noise.
    • 

  • Slow, noisy inputs near one threshold create extra edges; hysteresis cures this.
    • 

  • A Schmitt inverter, R and C make a relaxation oscillator: tH = RC ln((Vdd − VT−) ÷ (Vdd − VT+)), tL = RC ln(VT+ ÷ VT−).
    • 

  • Real thresholds and parts vary: predict a range, then measure.
