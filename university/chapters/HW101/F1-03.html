Story hook

Analogy

In the restaurant's back room there is a hand mill for grinding spices. Kwame turns the handle slowly and the mill grinds a little. When the dinner rush starts, he turns it fast and hard. Soon his arms ache and the mill's iron body is warm to the touch.

"It is not only how hard you push," says Aiko. "It is how hard you push and how fast the handle goes round. Both together decide how much work you do each minute." And the faster the work, the more of it ends up as warmth in the mill.

Electric parts are the same. Push (voltage) times flow (current) tells you how fast energy is delivered. In a resistor, almost all of that energy becomes heat. Too much, too fast, and a part can scorch the board it sits on. This chapter shows you how to calculate it before anything gets hot.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain power as the rate of energy transfer, in watts, with the mill picture and precisely.
  • Calculate power with P = V × I, P = I² × R and P = V² ÷ R, and choose the convenient form.
  • Compare the power in a part with its power rating, using exercise numbers labelled as such.
  • Calculate energy from power and time, E = P × t.
  • Diagnose "the hot resistor" from a schematic and meter readings.
Jargon box
Energy
Simple words: the ability to do work, such as making light, movement or heat. Analogy: the total effort Kwame spends at the mill during a shift. Precise: a quantity measured in joules (J); in a circuit, each coulomb moved through a voltage V transfers V joules. D5 §7.2 D3 §2.3.4
Power
Simple words: how fast energy is delivered or used. Analogy: how hard and how fast Kwame turns the mill at this moment. Precise: the rate of energy transfer, P = E ÷ t; in a part with voltage V across it and current I through it, P = V × I; measured in watts (W), one watt being one joule per second. D5 §9.5 D3 §2.3.4
Dissipation
Simple words: electrical energy turning into heat inside a part. Analogy: the mill's iron body getting warm. Precise: the conversion of electrical power into heat in a resistive part; for a resistor all of P = I² × R is dissipated. D5 §9.5
Power rating
Simple words: the most power a part may take without damage, as its maker says. Analogy: the mill's sign "do not turn faster than this". Precise: the maximum continuous power dissipation stated in a part's datasheet, under the conditions the datasheet states. D4 D9
Short circuit
Simple words: a path with almost no resistance straight across a source. Analogy: a burst pipe that lets the tank empty at once. Precise: an unintended low-resistance connection between two points at different potentials, which lets a large current flow; the current is limited only by the source's internal resistance and the wiring. D5 §10.1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows a power budget for the LED loop of F0-40 (exercise numbers: 6 V battery, LED taking 2 V, 200 Ω resistor, 20 mA). The battery delivers power; the resistor and the LED take it. The two shares add up to the battery's total.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A battery stores energy. When current flows, the battery hands that energy to the parts in the loop. A lamp turns it into light. A motor turns it into movement. A resistor turns it into heat.D5 §9.5

Power tells you how fast the energy is handed over. Think of Kwame at the spice mill. A strong push turned slowly is not much work per minute. A gentle push turned fast is not much either. A strong push turned fast is a lot. In a circuit, the push is the voltage and the speed of flow is the current. Multiply them and you get the power.D5 §9.5

Power is measured in watts (W). The exercise LED loop of Figure 1 uses 0.12 W, about a tenth of a watt. That is why we often use milliwatts (mW): one thousandth of a watt.

Here is the important safety idea. Every part has a limit, written by its maker. If a resistor must turn too much power into heat, it gets hot. It can scorch the board or burn a finger. So engineers calculate the power first, and pick a part that can take it.D4 D9

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why P = V × I

Voltage is energy per coulomb (F1-01). Current is coulombs per second. Multiply them: (joules per coulomb) × (coulombs per second) = joules per second. That is power, in watts.D5 §9.5 D3 §2.3.4 So for any part, the power is the voltage across it times the current through it.

Three forms for a resistor

For a resistor, Ohm's law lets you replace V or I:

  • P = V × I (works for any part);
  • P = I × R × I = I² × R (handy when you know the current);
  • P = V × (V ÷ R) = V² ÷ R (handy when you know the voltage).

All three give the same number for a resistor. Listing 1 computes all three, as a check. Note the squares: doubling the current through a resistor gives four times the power.D5 §9.5

Energy over time

Energy is power multiplied by time: E = P × t. A resistor taking 0.08 W for 60 s turns 0.08 × 60 = 4.8 J into heat.D5 §9.5 A battery's maker states how much it can deliver in its datasheet; that is what decides how long a circuit can run (D4).

Power budget

Energy is not created or lost in a circuit: the power the source delivers equals the total power taken by the parts.D5 §9.5 D5 §10.3 In Figure 1, 120 mW = 80 mW + 40 mW. Writing such a budget is a quick check on any calculation.

Power rating and margin

Each resistor's datasheet states a power rating. Running a part near its rating makes it hot. A rating holds only under the conditions the datasheet states. This course's rule is to keep a margin: choose a part whose rating is comfortably above the calculated power.D9 How much margin a project uses is a design rule that the team writes down. In this course's exercises, the "pretend rating" is an exercise number; the real rating comes from the datasheet (D4).

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. A part's temperature rises until the heat it loses to its surroundings equals the power it dissipates. How much it rises per watt depends on its size, its leads, the board and the air around it; datasheets describe this with a thermal resistance or with a derating curve that lowers the allowed power at higher temperatures.D9 D4 The same balance between power and heat loss comes back at a much larger scale in the processor and GPU courses (HW301).

How the hardware actually does it

In a resistor, the moving charges lose energy in collisions with the atoms of the material (F1-02). That energy becomes thermal energy: the part gets warmer.D5 §9.2 The heat then leaves through the part's body and leads into the board and the air. In the example datasheet D9, the sizes with higher ratings have larger bodies.D9 The exact sizes and ratings of the kit's resistors are in their maker's datasheet (D4).

Worked example

Exercise numbers, not real-part values. Each resistor in Listing 1's input has a pretend rating of 0.25 W.

  1. Kiri: 4 V across 200 Ω. I = 4 ÷ 200 = 0.02 A. P = 4 × 0.02 = 0.08 W. Check: I² × R = 0.0004 × 200 = 0.08 W. 0.08 ÷ 0.25 = 32 % of the pretend rating.
  2. Lars: 4 V across 1,000 Ω. P = V² ÷ R = 16 ÷ 1,000 = 0.016 W, 6.4 % of the rating.
  3. Musa: 7 V across 100 Ω. I = 0.07 A. P = 7 × 0.07 = 0.49 W, almost twice the pretend rating. This part would overheat.
  4. Nadia: 12 V across 1,000 Ω. P = 144 ÷ 1,000 = 0.144 W, 57.6 % of the rating.
  5. Energy for Kiri over one minute: E = 0.08 × 60 = 4.8 J of heat.

Notice Musa's case: the resistor is only ten times smaller than Lars's, with a bit more voltage, but the power is thirty times larger. P = V² ÷ R explains it: a ten times smaller resistance alone gives ten times the power, and 7 V instead of 4 V multiplies it again by (7 ÷ 4)² ≈ 3.R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. A tired person slows down; a resistor does not. Kwame stops when his arms ache. A resistor keeps taking the same power as long as the voltage stays, until it fails. Nothing in the part protects itself.D5 §9.5
    2. 

  2. Narrow water pipes do not warm up noticeably. In the registered pipe picture (guide 8.1), a narrow pipe only slows the flow. In a resistor, all the held-back energy becomes heat. The pipe picture alone would never warn you about a hot part.
    3. 

  3. An LED is not a resistor. P = V × I works for the LED, but I² × R and V² ÷ R do not, because the LED does not obey Ohm's law (F1-06).D5 §9.4
    4.

Summary


    

  • Power is the rate of energy transfer, in watts: 1 W = 1 J/s.
    • 

  • For any part, P = V × I, with V the voltage across that part.
    • 

  • For a resistor, also P = I² × R = V² ÷ R; all its power becomes heat.
    • 

  • Doubling current or voltage on a resistor gives four times the power.
    • 

  • Energy is power times time: E = P × t.
    • 

  • Source power equals the sum of the parts' powers (the power budget).
    • 

  • Check every part's power against its datasheet rating, with margin, before building.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: all three channels show 7.00 V across the resistor and 2.00 V across the LED; only the current differs: 7.00 mA in channels 1 and 3, 70.00 mA in channel 2. (In this ideal model the voltages are fixed by the supply and the LED, so they cannot reveal the fault; the current does.) Prediction: 7 V ÷ 1 kΩ = 7 mA. Fitted value: 7 V ÷ 0.070 A = 100 Ω, ten times smaller than the schematic. Power: channel 2: 7 × 0.070 = 0.49 W, about twice the pretend 0.25 W rating, hence the heat and the brown mark; correct channels: 7 × 0.007 = 0.049 W, about a fifth of the rating. The LED also carries ten times its designed current, which explains its brightness and puts it at risk. Ruled out: "faulty LED" — its voltage matches the others and the extra current is exactly explained by a 100 Ω resistor. Fix: power off, replace the resistor with the 1 kΩ part, inspect the LED and the board. Prevention: measure each resistor before fitting, or measure each channel's current at first power-on with a current-limited supply. How the evidence was made: hot_resistor.cpp with hot_resistor.in giving the fitted values 1000, 100, 1000 Ω (R1).
