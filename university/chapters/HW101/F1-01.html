Story hook

Analogy

The restaurant on the corner has a water tank on its roof. Every morning, Rosa, the manager, checks the plumbing before the cooks arrive. A pipe runs from the tank down to the kitchen taps, and a drain pipe carries the water back to a pump that lifts it up again.

One day the kitchen tap on the ground floor gushes, but the tap on the first floor only dribbles. Tomás, the new cook, asks why. "The water on the ground floor has fallen further," says Rosa. "It has more pressure behind it. What matters is the difference in height between the tank and the tap."

Tomás then asks a second question: "How much water goes through the pipe each minute?" That is a different thing. Pressure is the push. Flow is the amount moving. Electricity has both too, and engineers keep them carefully apart. This chapter is about that push and that flow.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain charge, current and voltage with the plumbing picture and with their precise definitions and units.
  • Calculate the charge moved by a steady current, Q = I × t, and check it with a small C++ program.
  • Explain why a voltage is always measured between two points, and what "ground" means.
  • Predict the sign of a voltmeter reading from where the red and black probes are placed.
  • Diagnose a set of readings in which the probes were swapped.
Jargon box
Charge
Simple words: the "stuff" of electricity: tiny particles carry it, and it can be positive or negative. Analogy: the water itself in the restaurant's pipes. Precise: electric charge is a property of particles such as electrons (negative) and protons (positive); it is measured in coulombs (C). D5 §9.1 D3 §2.3.1
Current
Simple words: how much charge flows past a point each second. Analogy: how much water flows through a pipe each second. Precise: the rate of flow of charge, I = Q ÷ t for a steady flow; measured in amperes (A); one ampere is one coulomb per second. D5 §9.1 D3 §2.3.4
Voltage
Simple words: the push that makes charge move, measured between two points. Analogy: the difference in water pressure between two places in the plumbing. Precise: the potential difference between two points: the energy given to (or taken from) each coulomb of charge that moves between them; measured in volts (V), where one volt is one joule per coulomb. D5 §7.2 D3 §2.3.4
Potential
Simple words: the "height" of one point, compared with a chosen zero point. Analogy: how high a tap is above the basement floor. Precise: the voltage of a point measured from a chosen reference point; only differences of potential have physical meaning. D5 §7.2
Ground (reference)
Simple words: the point we agree to call 0 V. Analogy: the basement floor from which every height in the building is measured. Precise: the node of a circuit chosen as the 0 V reference for all other potentials; in this course's battery circuits the designer chooses the battery's − terminal. D5 §7.2
Conventional current
Simple words: the direction engineers draw current: from + to − outside the battery. Analogy: drawing the water's direction on the building plan. Precise: the direction positive charge would move; in metal wires the electrons actually move the opposite way, and circuit diagrams use the conventional direction. D5 §9.1
Voltmeter
Simple words: a tool that shows the voltage between its two probes. Analogy: the plumber's pressure gauge with two hoses. Precise: an instrument (often one mode of a multimeter, F1-07) that displays the potential of its red probe minus the potential of its black probe. D21 D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Everything around you is made of tiny particles. Some of them carry charge. In a metal wire, very many tiny charged particles called electrons can move. When they move together in one direction, we have an electric current.D5 §9.1

Think of the restaurant's plumbing. The water is the charge. How much water passes a point each second is the current. A thin trickle is a small current. A gushing tap is a big current.

Water does not move by itself. Something must push it. In the restaurant, the height of the roof tank makes the pressure. In a circuit, a battery makes the push. That push is called voltage.D5 §10.1

Here is the important part. Pressure only makes sense between two places. Rosa compares the tank with the tap. A voltage is the same: it is always measured between two points. "The voltage at point B" really means "the voltage between point B and a point we agreed to call zero". We call that zero point ground.D5 §7.2

Last, the water must go round. The pump lifts it, it falls through the taps, and the drain brings it back. Charge also needs a closed loop. You met that rule in F0-39. Without a loop, there is no current.D5 §9.1

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Charge and current

Charge is measured in coulombs (C). Current is the rate at which charge passes a point. For a steady current, the charge moved in a time t is Q = I × t. One ampere means one coulomb passing each second.D3 §2.3.4 D5 §9.1 So 20 mA (0.020 A) flowing for 60 s moves 0.020 × 60 = 1.2 C. Listing 1 does this sum for four cases.

In a single loop with no branches, the same current flows through every part. Charge is not used up by a lamp or a resistor; it goes all the way round. What the parts use is energy, not charge.D5 §10.2 F1-04 turns this into the junction rule for circuits with branches.

Voltage, potential and ground

Voltage is energy per charge. If one coulomb moves from one point to another and gains or loses one joule, the voltage between the points is one volt.D5 §7.2 A battery gives energy to the charge that passes through it. The other parts take that energy back out, as light or heat.D5 §10.1 D5 §9.5

Because voltage is a difference, we pick one point as the reference and call it ground (0 V). The voltage of any point measured from ground is its potential. The voltage between two points is then the difference of their potentials: VBC = VB − VC. In Figure 1, VB = 4.5 V and VC = 1.5 V, so VBC = 3 V.D5 §7.2

The order matters. VCB = 1.5 − 4.5 = −3 V. A minus sign is not an error. It says that the first point is lower than the second.

Which way does current go?

On circuit diagrams, current is drawn leaving the battery's + terminal and returning to its − terminal. This is conventional current. In metal wires, the electrons really move the other way. Both descriptions give the same answers for every calculation in this course, so engineers keep the conventional direction.D5 §9.1

Reading a voltmeter

A voltmeter shows the potential of its red probe minus the potential of its black probe.D21 D4 Put black on ground and red on a point, and you read that point's potential. Swap the probes and the reading changes sign. F1-07 covers the multimeter in full, including how to measure current safely.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. The rule "voltages around any closed loop add up to zero" follows from voltage being a difference of potentials: walk around the loop, add each difference, and you are back at the start. In Figure 1: (6 − 4.5) + (4.5 − 1.5) + (1.5 − 0) = 6 V of drops, matching the battery's 6 V rise. This is Kirchhoff's voltage law; F1-04 uses it.D5 §10.3 The idea that a potential exists at every point is a model. It holds very well for the slow, small circuits of this course. It needs care for fast-changing signals and long wires, which HW204 and HW302 meet later.

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a battery, a chemical reaction moves charge from one terminal to the other. This builds up a potential difference between the terminals. When a closed loop is connected, charge flows through the outside circuit and the reaction keeps the difference going.D5 §10.1 A real battery's terminal voltage depends on its chemistry, on the current it delivers and on its internal resistance, which grows as the battery runs down.D5 §10.1 The number printed on it is a nominal label. Its real behaviour is given in the maker's datasheet (D4). This chapter does not quote any real battery number.

Worked example

Exercise numbers, not measurements. Ana's LED loop draws a steady 20 mA.

  1. Convert to amperes: 20 mA = 20 ÷ 1000 = 0.020 A.
  2. In 1 s it moves Q = 0.020 × 1 = 0.020 C.
  3. In one minute (Ben's case) it moves Q = 0.020 × 60 = 1.200 C.
  4. Chidi's loop draws a quarter of that current, 5 mA. In one minute: 0.005 × 60 = 0.300 C, a quarter of the charge.
  5. Dara's motor draws 1000 mA = 1 A. In 1 s it moves 1 C: one ampere is one coulomb per second.

Now the potentials of Figure 1. Black probe on GND: A reads 6 V, B reads 4.5 V, C reads 1.5 V. Across each part (red on the higher point): part 1: 6 − 4.5 = 1.5 V; part 2: 4.5 − 1.5 = 3 V; part 3: 1.5 − 0 = 1.5 V. The three drops add to 6 V. Listing 1 and the forensic generator check both sets of numbers.R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. An open pipe spills; an open wire does not. Cut a water pipe and the water pours out. Cut a wire and the current simply stops: charge does not leak out of the end into the room.D5 §9.1
    2. 

  2. Water moves at the speed you see; electrical effects do not. When you close a switch, the lamp lights at once, even though each electron in the wire drifts slowly. The push travels through the whole loop far faster than any single charge moves. The plumbing picture hides this.D5 §9.2
    3. 

  3. Real pressure comes from height; voltage comes from energy per charge. The roof tank is a helpful picture, but no part of a circuit is "higher" in space. The 4.5 V at B is about energy, not about where B sits on the table.
    4.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: all three of Omar's readings are negative, and in each one his red probe is on the lower point (GND below A, A above B, B above C). Expected values: from Mei's potentials, Omar's lines should read 6.00, 1.50 and 3.00 V: exactly his numbers without the minus sign. Conclusion: the circuit is fine; Omar placed the probes the other way round. Hypothesis "circuit faulty" is ruled out because the sizes match Mei's and Priya's readings. Priya: 1.50 + 3.00 + 1.50 = 6.00 V, equal to Mei's A-to-GND reading (Kirchhoff's voltage law). Prevention: "black on the lower point or on GND, red on the point you are asking about; a minus sign means the probes are reversed, not that the circuit is broken". How the evidence was made: probes.cpp with fixed exercise potentials A = 6, B = 4.5, C = 1.5, GND = 0, and probes.in listing the probe placements (R1).
