Story hook

Analogy

The restaurant's kitchen has two sinks. One is fed by a wide pipe. The other, added later by a hurried builder, is fed by a pipe as thin as a pencil. With the same roof tank behind both, the wide pipe fills a pot in seconds. The thin one takes ages.

Aiko, who runs the kitchen, makes a test. She opens each tap and counts how many cups fill in one minute. Then she asks Rosa to move the tank higher, so there is more pressure, and counts again. For each pipe, double the pressure gives about double the cups.

"So each pipe has its own number," Aiko says. "Pressure divided by flow. The thin pipe's number is big. The wide pipe's number is small." She has just discovered, with water, what this chapter calls resistance and Ohm's law.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain resistance with the narrow-pipe picture and with its precise definition and unit, the ohm.
  • Use Ohm's law, V = I × R, in all three forms, with correct unit prefixes (k, M, m).
  • Find a resistance from a table of voltage and current readings, by hand and with a C++ program.
  • Tell an ohmic part (straight-line V–I graph) from a non-ohmic one.
  • Diagnose a wrong resistor in a batch from test-bench readings.
Jargon box
Resistance
Simple words: how strongly a part holds back the current. Analogy: how narrow a pipe in the restaurant's plumbing is. Precise: the ratio of the voltage across a part to the current through it, R = V ÷ I; measured in ohms (Ω). D5 §9.4 D3 §2.3.4
Ohm (Ω)
Simple words: the unit of resistance. Analogy: a "narrowness number" for a pipe. Precise: one ohm is the resistance through which one volt drives one ampere (1 Ω = 1 V/A). D3 §2.3.4
Ohm's law
Simple words: for many parts, the current is proportional to the voltage. Analogy: twice the pressure through the same pipe gives twice the flow. Precise: V = I × R with R constant; parts that obey it over their working range are called ohmic. D5 §9.4
Resistor
Simple words: a part made to have a known resistance. Analogy: a pipe section deliberately made narrow. Precise: a two-terminal component manufactured with a stated nominal resistance, a tolerance and a power rating (values from its datasheet). D9 D4
Tolerance
Simple words: how far a real part may be from the number written on it. Analogy: pipes from the same shop are never exactly the same width. Precise: the maximum allowed difference between the actual and nominal value, given as a percentage in the datasheet (for example ±1 % or ±5 %). D5 §9.3 D9 D4
V–I graph
Simple words: a drawing of voltage against current for a part. Analogy: Aiko's table of pressure and cups per minute, drawn as dots. Precise: a plot of the voltage across a part against the current through it; for an ohmic part the points lie on a straight line through the origin whose slope is R. D5 §9.4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every part of a circuit holds back the current a little. Some parts hold it back a lot. How strongly a part holds back the current is its resistance.D5 §9.4

In the restaurant, a narrow pipe holds back the water. A wide pipe lets it rush through. A thick copper wire is like the wide pipe. A resistor is like the pencil-thin pipe: it is made on purpose to hold back the current by a known amount.D5 §9.3

Aiko found a rule. Double the pressure on the same pipe, and you get double the flow. For a resistor, the rule is the same. Double the voltage, and you get double the current. That rule is called Ohm's law.D5 §9.4

Resistance is measured in ohms, written with the Greek letter Ω. A resistor of 1,000 Ω holds back the current more than a resistor of 100 Ω.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Ohm's law in three forms

For a resistor, the voltage across it and the current through it are proportional: V = I × R. You can rearrange it to I = V ÷ R or R = V ÷ I. You met the second form in F0-39; now you use all three.D5 §9.4

The units fit together: one volt across one ohm drives one ampere.D3 §2.3.4 In practice you meet prefixes. 1 kΩ (kilo-ohm) is 1,000 Ω. 1 MΩ (mega-ohm) is 1,000,000 Ω. 1 mA is 0.001 A.D3 Table 7 A useful shortcut: volts divided by kilo-ohms gives milliamperes. For example, 5 V ÷ 2.2 kΩ ≈ 2.27 mA.

Reading resistance from a graph

Plot the voltage against the current. For an ohmic part, the points lie on a straight line through the origin. The slope of that line, rise in volts over run in amperes, is the resistance (Figure 1).D5 §9.4

Real readings are never perfect. Each pair gives a slightly different V ÷ I. A better estimate uses all the points at once. The least-squares slope through the origin is R = Σ(V × I) ÷ Σ(I × I): multiply each pair, add them up, and divide by the sum of the squared currents. Listing 1 computes it. This is the same "fit a line" idea you met in MA101's graphs.

Not everything obeys Ohm's law

Ohm's law is a property of some parts, not a law of nature for all of them. A diode (F1-06) has a curved V–I graph: almost no current at low voltage, then a steep rise. A lamp filament's resistance changes as it heats up.D5 §9.4 D5 §9.3 For those parts, V ÷ I still gives a number at one point, but that number changes from point to point.

Nominal value and tolerance

A resistor sold as 2.2 kΩ is not exactly 2,200 Ω. The maker states a tolerance: how far the real value may be from the nominal one. The tolerance, the power rating and how the value is marked on the part all come from the maker's datasheet.D4 D9

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. The resistance of a wire grows with its length and shrinks with its cross-section area; the material sets a constant called its resistivity. That is why long, thin wires resist more than short, thick ones, just like pipes.D5 §9.3 Resistance also changes with temperature, by an amount that depends on the material; a resistor's datasheet states how much, as a temperature coefficient (D4).D5 §9.3 D9 Fitting a line through the origin assumes the meter reads zero at zero current; a careful engineer also fits a line with an offset and checks that the offset is small.

How the hardware actually does it

In a metal, the moving electrons keep bumping into the atoms of the material and into each other. The bumps turn some of their energy into heat. That is resistance at the smallest scale.D5 §9.2 A resistor is made from a material and a shape chosen so that the bumping adds up to the wanted value. How a particular resistor is built (for example a carbon or metal film on a ceramic coreD5 §9.3) is described in its datasheet, together with its limits (D4). The heat side of the story is F1-03.

Worked example

Exercise numbers, not measurements. Yusuf applies five voltages to one resistor and reads the currents: (1 V, 0.46 mA), (2 V, 0.92 mA), (3 V, 1.35 mA), (4 V, 1.83 mA), (5 V, 2.26 mA).

  1. First pair: R = 1 V ÷ 0.00046 A ≈ 2,173.9 Ω.
  2. Third pair: R = 3 ÷ 0.00135 ≈ 2,222.2 Ω. The single-pair values scatter by a few tens of ohms.
  3. Least-squares slope: Σ(V × I) = 1 × 0.00046 + 2 × 0.00092 + 3 × 0.00135 + 4 × 0.00183 + 5 × 0.00226 = 0.02497 V·A.
  4. Σ(I × I) = 0.00046² + 0.00092² + 0.00135² + 0.00183² + 0.00226² ≈ 1.1337 × 10−5 A².
  5. R ≈ 0.02497 ÷ 0.000011337 ≈ 2,202 Ω, close to a 2.2 kΩ resistor.
  6. Check with the other form: at 5 V, I = 5 ÷ 2,202 ≈ 2.27 mA, close to the 2.26 mA read.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. Flow in a real pipe stays proportional to pressure only while it is smooth. At high flow the water becomes turbulent, the pipe's resistance to flow grows a lot, and then doubling the pressure gives less than double the flow. A resistor stays proportional over its working range. The pipe picture would make you expect a curve that a resistor does not have.D6 §14.7
    2. 

  2. A resistor heats up; a narrow pipe barely does. The energy a resistor holds back turns into heat. F1-03 shows that this heat can damage a part.D5 §9.5
    3. 

  3. Pipes leak; wires need a loop. As in F1-01, charge does not spill from an open end, so "a narrow pipe with nothing after it" has no electrical meaning.

Transition box

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: expected current 5 V ÷ 2.2 kΩ ≈ 2.273 mA; R1, R2, R4 and R6 match. R3 draws 22.727 mA and R5 draws 0.227 mA. Values: R3 = 5 ÷ 0.022727 ≈ 220 Ω; R5 = 5 ÷ 0.000227 ≈ 22 kΩ. Pattern: each is the label divided or multiplied by exactly 10: the same digits with the wrong multiplier, which points to parts from the neighbouring drawers (220 Ω and 22 kΩ) mixed into the 2.2 kΩ box. Tolerance ruled out: tolerance is a small percentage, not a factor of 10. Fix and prevention: remove R3 and R5; measure a sample from each box on arrival; keep neighbouring values in clearly separated stores. How the evidence was made: bench.cpp with bench.in listing the true values 2200, 2200, 220, 2200, 22000, 2200 Ω (R1).
