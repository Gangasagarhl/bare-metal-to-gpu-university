Story hook

Analogy

The restaurant is growing. Rosa wants water in two new places: a vegetable sink and a dishwasher. The plumber, Mr Okafor, offers two ways to connect them.

"Way one: one pipe goes through the vegetable sink's narrow tap, and then on through the dishwasher's narrow valve. All the water passes both, one after the other. Way two: the pipe splits in two. Some water goes to the sink, some to the dishwasher, and the two streams join again in the drain."

Rosa thinks. "In way one, if someone closes the sink tap, the dishwasher gets nothing. In way two, each one works on its own." Mr Okafor nods. "And in way two, the building draws more water in total." Rosa has just compared series and parallel connections. This chapter does the same with resistors, and gives you the rules to calculate both.

What you will be able to do

  • Recognise series and parallel connections on a circuit diagram.
  • Calculate the equivalent resistance of resistors in series and in parallel.
  • Apply the loop rule (voltages) and the junction rule (currents) to check every answer.
  • Design a voltage divider and predict its output.
  • Diagnose a divider whose output sags when a device is connected (loading).
Jargon box
Series
Simple words: parts connected one after another, so the same current passes through all of them. Analogy: Mr Okafor's way one: one pipe through the sink tap and then the dishwasher valve. Precise: components connected end to end with no branch between them, so they carry the same current; their voltages add. D5 §10.2
Parallel
Simple words: parts connected side by side between the same two points, so they share the same voltage. Analogy: way two: the pipe splits, feeds both, and joins again. Precise: components connected between the same two nodes, so they have the same voltage; their currents add. D5 §10.2
Node
Simple words: a meeting point of wires. Analogy: a pipe joint where several pipes meet. Precise: a point (or a set of points joined by wire) in a circuit, which has one potential. D8 §3.4
Equivalent resistance
Simple words: one resistor that would do the same job as a group. Analogy: one pipe that lets through the same flow as a whole set of pipes. Precise: the single resistance that draws the same current from a source as the network it replaces; series: R1 + R2; parallel: 1 ÷ (1/R1 + 1/R2). D5 §10.2
Kirchhoff's laws
Simple words: two bookkeeping rules: voltages round a loop add to zero; currents into a joint equal currents out. Analogy: water going round the building ends where it started; water into a pipe joint equals water out. Precise: the voltage law: the sum of potential differences around any closed loop is zero; the current law: the sum of currents into any node equals the sum out. D5 §10.3
Voltage divider
Simple words: two resistors in series that give a smaller voltage from their middle point. Analogy: a tap halfway down a pipe gives part of the full pressure. Precise: a series pair R1, R2 across Vin whose junction has Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) when nothing draws current from the junction. D8 §3.4
Loading
Simple words: when connecting something to a circuit changes the circuit's voltages. Analogy: opening an extra tap halfway down a pipe lowers the pressure there. Precise: the change in a circuit's behaviour caused by the current drawn by a connected load; a load across R2 of a divider forms R2 ∥ Rload and lowers Vout. D5 §10.2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

There are two simple ways to connect two parts. In series, they sit one after the other in a single path. All the current must pass through both. If one is removed, the path is broken and everything stops.D5 §10.2

In parallel, the path splits into branches. Each part sits on its own branch, between the same two meeting points. Each branch gets the full push of the battery. If one branch is broken, the others keep working.D5 §10.2

Think of Mr Okafor's pipes. Two narrow taps in series make the water's path harder: less water flows. Two narrow taps in parallel give the water two ways through: more water flows in total. Resistors behave the same way. In series, the total resistance goes up. In parallel, it goes down.D5 §10.2

Two bookkeeping rules help you check your sums. Round any loop, the battery's push is all used up by the parts. And at any meeting point, the current coming in equals the current going out.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Series

Resistors in series carry the same current I. By Ohm's law, V1 = I × R1 and V2 = I × R2. Kirchhoff's voltage law says these add up to the supply: V = V1 + V2 = I × (R1 + R2). So the pair behaves like one resistor Req = R1 + R2.D5 §10.2 The larger resistor takes the larger share of the voltage.

Parallel

Resistors in parallel have the same voltage V across them. Their currents are I1 = V ÷ R1 and I2 = V ÷ R2. Kirchhoff's current law at the node says the supply current is I = I1 + I2 = V × (1/R1 + 1/R2). So 1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2.D5 §10.2 For two resistors this can be written Req = R1 × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2). The result is always smaller than the smaller resistor. The smaller resistor takes the larger share of the current.

Engineers often write "∥" for "in parallel with": 1 kΩ ∥ 2 kΩ ≈ 667 Ω.

The voltage divider

Two resistors in series across a supply give, at their middle node, Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2), where R2 is the one between the middle and ground.D8 §3.4 Two equal resistors give half the supply. Dividers are everywhere: sensors (HW302) and the inputs of logic chips (HW102) meet them.

Loading

The divider formula assumes nothing draws current from the middle node. If you connect a device with resistance RL there, it sits in parallel with R2. The lower half becomes R2 ∥ RL, which is smaller, so Vout drops. If RL is much larger than R2, the drop is small. If RL is close to R2 or smaller, the drop is large.D5 §10.2 D8 §3.5 The forensic lab is about exactly this, and F1-07 shows that a meter itself is a load.

Power in combinations

In series, the larger resistor dissipates more (P = I² × R with the same I). In parallel, the smaller resistor dissipates more (P = V² ÷ R with the same V).D5 §10.2 D5 §9.5 Check each part against its rating (F1-03).

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Not every network reduces to series and parallel steps; a bridge of five resistors does not. For those, engineers write Kirchhoff's current law at every node with the unknown node potentials and solve the equations (nodal analysis, or node voltage analysis).D8 §3.4 Seen from its output, any network of resistors and sources behaves like one source in series with one resistance (the Thévenin equivalent); for a divider that resistance is R1 ∥ R2, which is why a load must be large compared with it.D8 §3.5

How the hardware actually does it

A wire joining parts has a very small resistance, so all points along it have almost the same potential: that is why a node can be treated as one point.D5 §9.3 D5 §9.4 A breadboard joins groups of holes underneath with metal strips, so parts plugged into one joined group are in the same node (to be confirmed for the kit's breadboard, D4). Which holes are joined on the kit's breadboard is shown in its documentation (D4); check it before wiring, because a part placed along a strip instead of across it is shorted out.

Worked example

Exercise numbers. A 9 V supply, R1 = 1 kΩ, R2 = 2 kΩ.

  1. Series. Req = 1 + 2 = 3 kΩ. I = 9 V ÷ 3 kΩ = 3 mA. V1 = 3 mA × 1 kΩ = 3 V. V2 = 3 mA × 2 kΩ = 6 V. Check: 3 + 6 = 9 V.
  2. Parallel. I1 = 9 ÷ 1 kΩ = 9 mA. I2 = 9 ÷ 2 kΩ = 4.5 mA. Total 13.5 mA. Req = 9 V ÷ 13.5 mA ≈ 0.667 kΩ. Check: 1 × 2 ÷ (1 + 2) = 0.667 kΩ, smaller than 1 kΩ.
  3. Divider. With R2 = 2 kΩ at the bottom, Vout = 9 × 2 ÷ 3 = 6 V: the series V2 again.
  4. Very unequal parallel. 1 kΩ ∥ 100 kΩ on 6 V: 6 mA + 0.06 mA = 6.06 mA, Req ≈ 990 Ω. The big resistor hardly changes anything.
  5. Loading. A divider of two 10 kΩ resistors on 6 V gives 3 V. Connect a 10 kΩ load to the middle: the bottom becomes 10 ∥ 10 = 5 kΩ, so Vout = 6 × 5 ÷ 15 = 2 V.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. Real pipes split unevenly for many reasons. In plumbing, bends, height and turbulence change how water splits. For ideal resistors in parallel, the split follows exactly I = V ÷ R for each branch. The pipe picture suggests more uncertainty than the rule has.D5 §10.2 D6 §14.7
    2. 

  2. An extra open tap in a building may not change the pressure much; a load on a divider can. A building's mains water often has a strong supply behind it. A divider made of large resistors is a "weak" source: a modest load can pull its voltage down a lot (loading). Do not trust the pipe intuition here; calculate.
    3. 

  3. Wires are not "pipes with a little resistance" in every case. We treat a node as one point. For fast signals and long wires that is no longer exact (HW204).
    4.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: nothing plugged: 3.00 V, 0.30 mA (design value 6 × 10 ÷ 20 = 3 V). A: 2.99 V. B: 2.00 V and 0.40 mA. C: 0.50 V and 0.55 mA. The supply current grows because each sensor adds a second path to ground. B: 2 = 6 × Rlow ÷ (10 kΩ + Rlow) gives Rlow = 5 kΩ; 10 kΩ ∥ RB = 5 kΩ gives RB = 10 kΩ. C: 0.5 = 6 × Rlow ÷ (10 kΩ + Rlow) gives Rlow ≈ 909 Ω; 10 kΩ ∥ RC ≈ 909 Ω gives RC ≈ 1 kΩ. A's input resistance is large compared with 10 kΩ (the model used 1 MΩ). Root cause: loading: sensors B and C have input resistances comparable with or smaller than the divider's resistors. The hypothesis "divider resistors changed" is ruled out because the unloaded reading is exactly the design value. Fixes: use much smaller divider resistors (more current and power wasted in the divider, check ratings), or put a buffer stage between divider and sensor (a later course topic), or choose sensors with high input resistance. How the evidence was made: divider_load.cpp with divider_load.in: open, 1 MΩ, 10 kΩ, 1 kΩ (R1).
