Story hook

Analogy

In the restaurant's basement there is an odd little tank. Across its middle is a stretchy rubber wall. Water can flow in on one side and push the rubber, and the same amount of water is pushed out on the other side. But no water ever crosses the wall.

When Rosa opens the supply, water rushes in at first. As the rubber stretches, it pushes back harder and harder. The flow slows down, and at last it stops: the rubber's push equals the supply's pressure. If the inlet pipe is narrow, the filling takes longer. If the tank is bigger, it takes longer too.

Hamid, the night cook, uses the tank as a timer: "When the gauge reaches the red mark, the bread is ready." The electric version of that tank is called a capacitor. With a resistor, it makes a timer, and a timer is the first step towards a blinking light and, much later, towards the clock of a processor.

Jargon box
Capacitor
Simple words: a part that stores charge on two plates that do not touch. Analogy (proposed): the basement tank with a stretchy rubber wall. Precise: a two-terminal component made of two conductors separated by an insulator; when a voltage V is applied, charges +Q and −Q collect on the two sides, with Q = C × V. D2
Capacitance
Simple words: how much charge a capacitor holds for each volt. Analogy (proposed): how big and how stretchy the tank's rubber wall is. Precise: C = Q ÷ V, measured in farads (F); one farad is one coulomb per volt; practical values use µF (10−6 F), nF (10−9 F) and pF (10−12 F). D2 D3
RC circuit
Simple words: a resistor and a capacitor together, used to make a delay. Analogy (proposed): the rubber-wall tank filled through a narrow pipe. Precise: a circuit in which a capacitor charges or discharges through a resistance, so its voltage changes gradually rather than at once. D2
Time constant (τ)
Simple words: the "speed setting" of an RC circuit: bigger means slower. Analogy (proposed): how long the tank takes to fill most of the way. Precise: τ = R × C, in seconds when R is in ohms and C in farads; after one τ a charging capacitor has covered 1 − e−1 ≈ 63.2 % of the way to the supply voltage. D2 R1
Exponential curve
Simple words: a curve that changes fast at first and then slower and slower. Analogy: the tank fills quickly at first, then creeps towards full. Precise: here V(t) = Vs × (1 − e−t/τ), where e ≈ 2.718 is the base of natural logarithms; in each further τ the remaining gap shrinks by the same factor e−1 ≈ 0.368. D2
Polarised capacitor
Simple words: a capacitor with a + leg and a − leg that must not be swapped. Analogy: a tank whose rubber wall may only be pushed from one side. Precise: a capacitor type (for example an electrolytic) that is designed to be used with one terminal always positive; its datasheet gives the polarity marking and the maximum voltage. D1 D4

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A capacitor is made of two metal plates with an insulator between them. Current cannot pass through the insulator. But when a battery is connected, charge piles up on one plate and leaves the other. The capacitor is "charged".D1 D2

Think of Hamid's tank with the rubber wall. Water flows in on one side and out on the other, but none crosses the wall. The more the wall stretches, the harder it pushes back. Charging slows down as the capacitor fills, and stops when its push equals the battery's.

Put a resistor in the way, and charging takes time. A bigger resistor means a narrower pipe: slower. A bigger capacitor means a bigger tank: slower too. Multiply the two and you get the circuit's time constant, its "speed setting".D2

That is how you can build a timer without a computer. Wait until the capacitor's voltage reaches a mark, then do something. In F1-08 you will use that idea to blink an LED.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Charge and voltage: Q = C × V

A capacitor's charge is proportional to its voltage: Q = C × V. The constant C is its capacitance, in farads. One farad is one coulomb per volt, which is very large; real parts are often in microfarads (µF) or nanofarads (nF).D2 D3 Example: a 100 µF capacitor at 5 V holds Q = 0.0001 × 5 = 0.0005 C.

Why the voltage cannot jump

To change the voltage, you must move charge, and moving charge takes current over time. Current is charge per second, so the capacitor's voltage changes at a rate of I ÷ C volts per second.D2 With a resistor limiting the current, the voltage cannot change instantly. That is the property every timing circuit uses.

Charging through a resistor

Connect supply Vs, resistor R and an empty capacitor in series. At the start, the capacitor has 0 V, so the whole supply is across R and the current is Vs ÷ R. As the capacitor charges, its voltage Vc rises, the resistor's share Vs − Vc shrinks, and so does the current. The result is the exponential curve of Figure 1:D2

Vc(t) = Vs × (1 − e−t/τ), with τ = R × C.

Ohms times farads gives seconds, so τ is a time.D3 After 1 τ the capacitor reaches about 63 % of Vs; after 3 τ about 95 %; after 5 τ about 99 %. These percentages are pure arithmetic of the formula, and Listing 1 prints them.R1

Discharging

A charged capacitor connected across a resistor alone discharges along the mirror curve: Vc(t) = V0 × e−t/τ. After 1 τ, about 37 % of the starting voltage is left.D2

Using the curve as a timer

To know when the capacitor reaches a voltage Vx, solve the formula for t: t = τ × ln(Vs ÷ (Vs − Vx)), where ln is the natural logarithm (a calculator key). For Vx = 63.2 % of Vs, this gives t = τ. F1-08 uses the same idea with two thresholds to make a blinker.

Note

Maths you need here. ex and ln(x) are inverse operations, just as squaring and square root are. MA101 covers powers and logarithms of 2; e is another base, and your calculator or std::exp and std::log in C++ compute them. You do not need to derive the curve at L1; you need to use it and check it.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. The exponential comes from a simple rule: at every instant, the rate of rise of Vc is proportional to the remaining gap, (Vs − Vc) ÷ (R × C). Listing 1 applies exactly this rule in tiny time steps (Euler's method, MA302) and lands on the formula's values within a few millivolts. Real capacitors are not ideal: they have a tolerance, a small leakage current through the insulator, a maximum voltage and, for some types, a capacitance that changes with voltage or temperature. All of these come from the datasheet (D4) and change how accurate an RC timer can be.D2

How the hardware actually does it

A capacitor's plates are often thin metal foils or films, rolled or stacked with an insulating layer between them. Larger plate area and a thinner insulator give more capacitance.D2 Inside chips, every wire and every transistor gate also has a little capacitance. Charging those tiny capacitances through the resistance of transistors is why signals take time to change, the propagation delay of F1-15, and why faster clocks need more power (HW301). Which capacitor types the kit contains, and their polarity marks and voltage ratings, are in the kit's datasheets (D4).

Worked example

Exercise values. Vs = 5 V, R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 µF.

  1. τ = R × C = 10,000 Ω × 0.0001 F = 1 s.
  2. Starting current: 5 V ÷ 10 kΩ = 0.5 mA.
  3. At t = 1 s: Vc = 5 × (1 − e−1) = 5 × (1 − 0.3679) ≈ 3.16 V.
  4. At t = 3 s: Vc = 5 × (1 − e−3) = 5 × (1 − 0.0498) ≈ 4.75 V.
  5. When does it reach 2.5 V (half)? t = 1 × ln(5 ÷ 2.5) = ln 2 ≈ 0.693 s.
  6. Charge stored when full: Q = 100 µF × 5 V = 500 µC = 0.0005 C.
  7. Replace C by 1,000 µF: τ becomes 10 s. Every time on the curve is ten times longer.

Listing 1 prints 3.162 V (step by step) and 3.161 V (formula) at 1 s, and 4.751 V at 3 s.R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. The guide's registry warns that capacitors have no simple pipe equivalent. The rubber-wall tank is a proposed picture. It gets "no flow through, push-back grows" right, but a real capacitor has no moving wall: the charges sit on plates and the "push-back" is the electric field between them.D2
    2. 

  2. A rubber wall stretches unevenly and can tear slowly; an ideal capacitor is exactly linear. Q = C × V is a straight line. Real parts depart from it in ways their datasheets describe, not in the way rubber does.
    3. 

  3. Current seems to "go through" a capacitor while it charges. In the tank, water clearly does not cross the wall. In a circuit, a meter in series with a charging capacitor shows a current, and charge leaves one plate as it arrives on the other. The analogy makes this look stranger than it is.D2
    4.

Summary


    

  • A capacitor stores charge: Q = C × V; capacitance is in farads (µF, nF in practice).
    • 

  • Its voltage cannot jump; it changes as charge flows in or out.
    • 

  • Charging through R: Vc = Vs × (1 − e−t/τ); discharging: V0 × e−t/τ.
    • 

  • τ = R × C; after 1 τ about 63 %, after 3 τ about 95 %, after 5 τ about 99 %.
    • 

  • Time to reach Vx: t = τ × ln(Vs ÷ (Vs − Vx)).
    • 

  • Step-by-step simulation and the formula agree when the step is small.
    • 

  • Polarised capacitors: correct polarity and voltage rating, from the datasheet.
