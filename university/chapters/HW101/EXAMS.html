Quizzes (Q)

Each chapter's "Check yourself" is its quiz: eight questions per chapter, with at least one "explain in your own words" and one "apply to a new case" (guide 11.3). Learners answer in writing after reading the chapter and before the lab, then mark themselves against the chapter's answers; a supervisor or mentor checks the "in your own words" answers against the generic rubric (guide 11.4). Each quiz is worth 2.5 % of the course (20 % in all).

Questions

  1. M1 (3 points, F1-01, Remember). Name the unit of charge, of current and of voltage, and write the relation between the ampere and the coulomb.
  2. M2 (4 points, F1-01, Apply). A motor draws a steady 250 mA for two minutes. How much charge passes through it? Show the unit conversion.
  3. M3 (4 points, F1-01, Understand). In a single loop, point A is at 12 V and point B at 7.5 V, both measured from ground (the battery's − terminal). (a) What does the voltmeter show with red on B and black on A? (b) With red on A and black on GND? (c) A learner says "the negative reading in (a) means the part between A and B is broken". Is that right? Say why in one sentence.
  4. M4 (4 points, F1-02, Apply). (a) A 3.3 kΩ resistor has 4.95 V across it: what current flows? (b) A 2.2 kΩ resistor carries 2.5 mA: what voltage is across it? Use the "volts over kilo-ohms gives milliamperes" shortcut and show it.
  5. M5 (5 points, F1-02, Analyse). Three readings on one part: (2 V, 0.60 mA), (4 V, 1.22 mA), (6 V, 1.79 mA). (a) Compute V ÷ I for each pair. (b) Compute the least-squares slope through the origin, R = Σ(V × I) ÷ Σ(I × I), as Listing 1 of F1-02 does. (c) Which resistor label (1 kΩ, 2.2 kΩ, 3.3 kΩ or 4.7 kΩ) is this part, and why is the fit better evidence than any single pair?
  6. M6 (5 points, F1-03, Apply). A 220 Ω resistor carries 40 mA. (a) What voltage is across it? (b) What power does it dissipate? Compute it with I² × R and check with V × I. (c) Its pretend rating is 0.25 W: is this design acceptable? (d) How much energy becomes heat in 30 s?
  7. M7 (4 points, F1-03, Understand). In a loop battery → resistor → LED, explain why the LED's power may be computed with P = V × I but not with P = V² ÷ R, and which voltage V must be used for each part.
  8. M8 (6 points, F1-04, Apply). A 9 V supply with R1 = 1.5 kΩ and R2 = 3 kΩ. (a) In series: the equivalent resistance, the current, and the voltage across each resistor; check with Kirchhoff's voltage law. (b) In parallel: each branch current, the total, and the equivalent resistance; check with Kirchhoff's current law.
  9. M9 (5 points, F1-04, Analyse). A divider of two 10 kΩ resistors on 9 V feeds a reference point. (a) What is the output with nothing connected? (b) A module with an input resistance of 20 kΩ is connected to the output: what is the output now? Show the parallel combination. (c) Explain in one or two sentences why the output fell.
  10. M10 (5 points, F1-04, Apply). (a) 35 mA flow into a node; one branch carries 12 mA out. What does the other branch carry, and which law says so? (b) A 9 V battery delivers 9 mA to a circuit of two parts in series. What is the battery's power, and what must the two parts' powers add up to?
  11. M11 (5 points, F1-01 to F1-04, Evaluate). A classmate wrote in a lab notebook: "The current after the resistor was smaller than before it, because the resistor used some of it up. Also my voltmeter read −3 V across the resistor, so the resistor is faulty." Evaluate both claims: say which is wrong, what the real explanation is, and what one measurement would settle each.

Part A — the parts and their laws (F1-01 to F1-04)

  1. F1 (3 points, F1-01, Remember). Define current and voltage in one sentence each, with their units, and state which way conventional current is drawn outside the battery.
  2. F2 (3 points, F1-01, Apply). A lamp draws 120 mA for 45 s. How much charge moves? Show the conversion to amperes.
  3. F3 (4 points, F1-01, Understand). In a loop of two parts on a 9 V battery, the junction B between them is at 5.5 V from ground. (a) What does a voltmeter show with red on GND and black on B? (b) With red on the + terminal (point A) and black on B? (c) Show that the two parts' voltages add up to the battery's.
  4. F4 (5 points, F1-02, Apply). Readings on one resistor: (2 V, 0.85 mA), (4 V, 1.72 mA), (6 V, 2.55 mA). (a) V ÷ I for each pair. (b) The least-squares slope through the origin. (c) Which label (1 kΩ, 2.2 kΩ, 3.3 kΩ) is it?
  5. F5 (3 points, F1-02, Analyse). Part X gives (1 V, 2 mA), (3 V, 6 mA), (5 V, 10 mA). Part Y gives (1 V, 0.1 mA), (2 V, 5 mA). Which part is ohmic? Give the evidence, and name a real part that behaves like Y.
  6. F6 (5 points, F1-03, Apply). A 330 Ω resistor has the full 9 V of a battery across it. (a) The current. (b) The power, by V² ÷ R. (c) Compare it with a pretend 0.25 W rating: is there margin? (d) The energy turned into heat in ten minutes. (e) Would a 680 Ω resistor in the same place be comfortable? Show the power.
  7. F7 (4 points, F1-03, Understand). Explain, with the formulas, why doubling the voltage across a resistor gives four times the power, and why a 1 Ω "no real resistor" case in F1-06 turns the whole battery into heat and damage.
  8. F8 (6 points, F1-04, Apply). A 12 V supply with R1 = 2.2 kΩ and R2 = 3.3 kΩ. (a) In series: Req, the current, the voltage across each resistor, and the loop check. (b) In parallel: the branch currents, the total, Req, and the node check.
  9. F9 (6 points, F1-04, Analyse). A divider of two 4.7 kΩ resistors on 5 V is meant to give 2.5 V. (a) A sensor with 4.7 kΩ input resistance is connected to the output: compute the output and the percentage change. (b) A sensor with 47 kΩ input resistance instead: output and percentage change. Is it within 5 % of the design? (c) State the divider's own current, and name the trade-off an engineer faces when choosing smaller divider resistors.

Part C — forensic question: "The board with two strangers" (18 points)

Scenario. Board B-7 has four identical LED channels. The schematic for every channel is: 9 V supply → resistor marked 1 kΩ → LED → ground. After the first hour of testing, Sofia reports a burnt smell and a brown mark next to channel 2, whose LED is also very bright; channel 3's LED is barely visible. She measured every channel with the supply on, without touching any part, with the meter in voltage mode across each part and in current mode in series with the channel.

  1. F19a (2 points, Analyse). Which readings are the same in all four channels, and which differ? Why can the voltage readings alone not tell the channels apart in this model?
  2. F19b (2 points, Analyse). What current does the schematic predict for each channel? Which channels agree with it?
  3. F19c (3 points, Analyse). From the readings, compute the resistance actually fitted in channel 2 and in channel 3.
  4. F19d (3 points, Analyse). Compute the resistor power in channel 2, in channel 3 and in a correct channel, and compare each with a pretend 0.25 W rating. Which channel explains the brown mark, and which the dim LED?
  5. F19e (4 points, Evaluate). State two hypotheses for each odd channel (for example "wrong resistor" and "faulty LED"), and show which the evidence supports and which it rules out. What do the two fitted values have in common, and what kind of mistake in the workshop does that point to?
  6. F19f (4 points, Evaluate). Give the fix for each channel, the risk channel 2's LED has already run, one check that would have caught both faults before the board was powered, and one habit that prevents the mix-up.

Method hints. Separate "what the supply and the LED fix" from "what the resistor decides". Use the current, not the voltages. Ask whether each difference is a few percent (tolerance) or a whole factor.

Practical (P) — predict, build and measure a small circuit

Weight 50 % (ruling B6). Time limit: 90 minutes. Allowed documentation: the HW101 chapters and their lab listings, the kit's datasheets and the meter's manual (when the kit exists), a calculator; nothing else, and no code written before the exam other than the starting files. The practical is the course card's "predict, build and measure a small circuit": the LED channel of F1-06, measured as in F1-07, plus the RC timer of F1-05.

The task

  1. Predict (on paper, about 20 minutes). Exercise values: a 6 V battery, an LED with pretend VF = 2 V and a pretend maximum of 20 mA, a target current of 10 mA, and a parts box of 220, 330, 390, 470, 560 and 680 Ω. (a) Compute the minimum resistor and choose the box value. (b) Predict the current, the voltage across the resistor, and the power in the resistor and in the LED. (c) A timer of R = 47 kΩ and C = 100 µF charges from the same 6 V: predict τ, the capacitor voltage at one τ, and the time to reach 3 V. (d) Predict what the model meter will change: the current with the meter (burden 1 Ω) in series, and the final capacitor voltage with the meter (input resistance 10 MΩ) across it.
  2. Build (in the simulator, about 40 minutes). Complete the five functions marked TODO in bench_exam_start.cpp (the minimum resistor; the choice from the box; the channel current with an extra series resistance; the time to reach a voltage; one time step of the capacitor with and without the voltmeter). Do not change anything else. Build with the course command printed in the file, run it with bench_exam_start.in, and make its self-check report 0 failures.
  3. Measure and compare (about 20 minutes). Copy the program's "measured" lines next to your predictions in a table, and write one short paragraph: where the measurements differ from the predictions, by how much, and why (burden, loading, step size). State the three rules for using the meter (voltage, current, resistance) as you would apply them on the real circuit.

Course project — an RC timer that blinks an LED without a microcontroller

Weight 30 % (ruling B6). Brief per guide 11.5. The project is built in three steps that the chapters' mini-projects already define: F1-05 (choose R and C for a timing target), F1-06 (the LED drive sheet) and F1-08 (the blinker: prediction, simulation, measurement, comparison).

Goal

Predict, simulate and (when the kit exists) build and measure a relaxation oscillator made of a Schmitt-trigger inverter, one resistor and one capacitor, driving an LED through its own series resistor, and show with evidence how well prediction, simulation and measurement agree.

Requirements

  • Functional: a period of a few seconds (easy to time by eye), chosen by you; a prediction sheet with the formulas tH = RC × ln((Vdd − VT−) ÷ (Vdd − VT+)) and tL = RC × ln(VT+ ÷ VT−), the predicted half-periods, the first (longer) HIGH, and a predicted range from the tolerances of R, C and the thresholds; an LED drive sheet (resistor with margin, powers); a simulation (a modified Listing 2 of F1-08) whose output is attached unedited.
  • Quality: every number labelled exercise, datasheet or measured; units at every step; the simulation checked against the formula (the half-periods after the first must match to the printed precision); the program builds with the course flags and no warnings.

Constraints

Hardware: none for the simulated version; for the built version only the kit's battery, breadboard, the chosen Schmitt-trigger part with its datasheet, R, C, LED and the course meter, under adult supervision, no mains. Time: about six hours in all, spread over F1-05 to F1-08. No microcontroller, no timer chip (its formula was not verified; F1-08 Part B box), no code other than the simulation.

Deliverables

  1. Prediction sheet (one page): formulas, values, half-periods, first HIGH, range.
  2. LED drive sheet (one page, F1-06 mini-project).
  3. Simulation source and its real output, with the build command.
  4. If the kit is available: the circuit built (photo or drawing), the period timed over ten blinks, three times, with date and instruments.
  5. A one-page comparison of prediction, simulation and measurement, and a short demo (show the blink, or the simulation's switch log, and explain one line of it).
