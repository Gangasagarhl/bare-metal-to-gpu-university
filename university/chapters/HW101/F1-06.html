Story hook

Analogy

The restaurant's drain pipe has a small flap valve inside. Water flowing out to the street pushes the flap open. If water ever tried to flow back in, it would push the flap shut. Mr Okafor, the plumber, says it is the most important part in the basement.

The flap is a little stiff. A trickle with almost no pressure does not open it. But once the pressure passes a certain level, it swings wide open, and then even a tiny extra push lets a lot more water through. "That is why there is a narrow section after it," says Mr Okafor. "Otherwise a big push would send a flood."

In F0-40 you met the LED as a one-way part that needs a resistor. Now you will see why, with the curve that describes the flap's stiffness, and you will calculate exactly how much current your LED gets.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a diode does, with the flap-valve picture and its precise V–I behaviour.
  • Identify anode and cathode on the symbol, and forward and reverse bias.
  • Calculate the series resistor for an LED with the simple fixed-voltage model, and judge when that model is good enough.
  • Solve an LED loop with a curve model in C++, and compare the two models.
  • Diagnose uneven brightness in LEDs that share one resistor.
Jargon box
Diode
Simple words: a part that lets current through one way and blocks it the other way. Analogy (proposed): the one-way flap valve in the restaurant's drain. Precise: a two-terminal semiconductor device that conducts readily when its anode is more positive than its cathode (forward bias) and passes only a tiny current in the opposite direction (reverse bias), up to its breakdown voltage. D2
Anode and cathode
Simple words: the diode's two ends: current goes in at the anode and out at the cathode. Analogy: the inlet and outlet side of the flap. Precise: in the forward direction, conventional current enters the anode and leaves the cathode; the symbol's triangle points from anode to cathode, towards the bar. D2
Forward voltage
Simple words: the voltage across a diode when it conducts. Analogy: the pressure needed to hold the flap open. Precise: the voltage across a forward-biased diode at a stated current; it changes only a little over a wide range of current, and its value for a given part comes from the datasheet. D2 D4
LED
Simple words: a diode that gives off light when current flows forward. Analogy: a flap valve that glows while water flows through it. Precise: a light-emitting diode; its colour and forward voltage depend on its semiconductor material, and its brightness rises with forward current up to the datasheet's maximum. D1 D2
I–V curve
Simple words: a graph of how much current flows at each voltage. Analogy: a chart of flow against pressure for the stiff flap. Precise: the plot of current against voltage for a part; a resistor's is a straight line (F1-02), a diode's is nearly flat and then rises very steeply. D2
Breakdown (reverse)
Simple words: what happens if you push a diode backwards too hard: it suddenly conducts and can be destroyed. Analogy: the flap torn off by too much back-pressure. Precise: the reverse voltage above which reverse current rises sharply; the datasheet gives the maximum reverse voltage, which for LEDs can be low. D2 D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A diode is a one-way part. Current can flow through it in one direction, from the anode to the cathode. In the other direction, it blocks.D2 On a circuit drawing, the diode is a triangle pointing at a bar. The triangle shows the way current may go.

An LED is a diode that glows. You met it in F0-40. Like Mr Okafor's flap, it needs a certain push before it opens. Below that push, almost no current flows and the LED is dark. Above it, the current rises very quickly.D1 D2

That fast rise is the danger. A little more voltage gives a lot more current, and too much current destroys the LED. So we never connect an LED straight to a battery. A resistor in series limits the current, just like the narrow section after the flap.D1

Turned the wrong way, an LED stays dark. Pushed backwards too hard, it can be damaged too. The datasheet says how much backward push it can take.D4

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The diode's I–V curve

For a resistor, current is proportional to voltage (F1-02). For a diode, the forward current grows exponentially with voltage. A common model is I = Is × (eV/Vn − 1), where Is is a tiny current and Vn a small voltage that depend on the device.D2 Because the curve is so steep, the voltage across a conducting diode changes only a little while the current changes a lot. That nearly constant voltage is the forward voltage.

In reverse, the model gives I ≈ −Is: practically nothing. Listing 1's last line shows −1.000e−18 A at −5 V. A real diode also has a reverse breakdown voltage, which this model does not include; its maximum reverse voltage is in the datasheet.D2 D4

The simple model and the series resistor

For design, engineers often replace the curve by a constant forward voltage VF from the datasheet. Then Kirchhoff's voltage law (F1-04) gives the resistor's share: VR = Vs − VF, and the current is I = (Vs − VF) ÷ R. To choose R for a wanted current: R = (Vs − VF) ÷ I.D2 This is F0-40's formula, now justified.

How good is it? Listing 1 solves the loop both ways. With 220 Ω on 5 V, the curve model gives 14.27 mA and the simple model (pretend VF = 2 V) gives 13.64 mA, about 5 % apart. With 1 Ω, the curve gives 2,875 mA and the simple model 3,000 mA: both say "destroyed", and that is the point. The simple model is good for choosing a resistor; it is useless for predicting what happens with no resistor.R1

Load line

The resistor gives a second relation: I = (Vs − V) ÷ R. Drawn on the LED's I–V graph, it is a straight line, the load line. The circuit must satisfy both, so it sits where they cross (Figure 1).D2 A bigger R makes the line lower and the current smaller.

Power and limits

The LED's power is P = VF × I (F1-03). The datasheet gives the maximum forward current and often a recommended operating current; stay below the maximum with margin.D4 Remember the resistor's power too: (Vs − VF) × I.

LEDs in parallel

Two LEDs in parallel have the same voltage. Because their curves are steep, a tiny difference between the two parts means a large difference in current. If they share one resistor, one LED can take most of the current.D2 The usual rule: give each LED its own resistor. The forensic lab shows this.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. A diode's forward voltage at a fixed current falls as it gets warmer; if two parallel LEDs share a resistor, the one taking more current heats more, its voltage drops, and it takes even more current. This positive feedback is why parallel LEDs without individual resistors are a known reliability problem.D2 Ordinary silicon diodes are used to let current through only one way, for example to protect a circuit if a battery is inserted backwards, or to turn alternating current into one-direction current (rectification).D2 Neither topic is needed for this course's labs.

How the hardware actually does it

A diode is a crystal of semiconductor with two regions joined together: one treated so it has spare electrons (n-type), one so it has missing electrons, "holes" (p-type). At the junction, a thin zone forms that blocks current. A forward voltage narrows that zone and lets charges cross; a reverse voltage widens it.D2 In an LED, electrons and holes meeting at the junction give off their energy as light; the material decides the colour and the forward voltage.D2 The same p–n junctions are the building blocks of the transistor in F1-09.

Worked example

Exercise numbers. A 5 V supply, an LED with pretend VF = 2 V and a pretend maximum current of 20 mA.

  1. Wanted current with margin: 14 mA. R = (5 − 2) ÷ 0.014 ≈ 214 Ω. Choose the next value you have above it, for example 220 Ω.
  2. Simple model check: I = 3 ÷ 220 ≈ 13.6 mA. Below 20 mA.
  3. Curve model (Listing 1): 14.27 mA at 1.860 V. Still below 20 mA; the simple model was about 5 % low.
  4. Resistor power: (5 − 1.86) × 0.01427 ≈ 0.045 W. LED power: 1.86 × 0.01427 ≈ 0.027 W.
  5. With 100 Ω instead: simple model 30 mA, curve model 31.0 mA. Both above the pretend maximum: not allowed.

These numbers come from the run below.R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. A flap valve opens at one pressure; a diode has no sharp opening point. The current grows smoothly and exponentially. "Forward voltage" is the voltage at a stated current, not a switch-on threshold.D2
    2. 

  2. A flap valve holds back any back-pressure until it breaks; a diode leaks a tiny reverse current and can break down at a voltage the datasheet states. For some LEDs that voltage is small; the flap picture would make you over-confident.D2 D4
    3. 

  3. A valve does not make light. The glow of an LED comes from the junction physics, not from flow "rubbing" anything. And unlike a valve, a conducting LED also turns part of its power into heat.
    4.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: resistor current 21.337 mA = LED A 4.267 mA + LED B 17.069 mA (current law holds). Both LEDs share 1.799 V; B takes four times A's current. If identical: each would take half, about 10.7 mA. Explanation: on a steep exponential curve, the same voltage gives very different currents if the curves are shifted even slightly; the model's Is for B is four times A's, a shift of only Vn × ln 4 ≈ 0.07 V along the voltage axis. Ruled out: "A is damaged" — A conducts normally and the currents add up exactly; a damaged LED would show an abnormal voltage or no current. Fix: one resistor per LED. With the simple model and pretend VF = 1.8 V: R = (5 − 1.8) ÷ 0.010 = 320 Ω each; small differences between the LEDs then change their currents only slightly, because most of the voltage is across the resistors. How the evidence was made: two_leds.cpp with two_leds.in = Is(A) 1e−18 A, Is(B) 4e−18 A, Vn 0.05 V; supply 5 V, 150 Ω (R1).
