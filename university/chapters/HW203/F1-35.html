Story hook

Analogy

Amina keeps the restaurant's stock book in the pantry. When a cook uses three spoons of sugar from a tray on the shelf, the stock book must change too. Ravi, the night cook, walks to the pantry and corrects the book after every spoon. He is never out of date, but he spends the evening in the corridor. Mei puts a small red sticker on the tray instead and keeps cooking; only when the tray goes back to the pantry does she correct the book, once, for all the changes.

Mei is far faster. But if the tray is put back without anyone reading the sticker, or the book is corrected on the wrong page, the restaurant's records are wrong, and nobody notices until a delivery is missing. Caches face the same choice for every write, and the bug in this chapter's forensic lab is exactly "the wrong page".

What you will be able to do

  • Explain write-through and write-back on a write hit, and write-allocate and no-write-allocate on a write miss.
  • Count the memory traffic of a trace under two policies, by hand and with the course simulator.
  • Explain the dirty bit, write-back of a victim and the write buffer.
  • Measure read, write and copy bandwidth on your machine and state which explanations your evidence can and cannot support.
  • Diagnose a write-back cache that loses data from its test output and source code.
Jargon box
Write-through
Simple words: every write goes to the cache and to the next level at once. Analogy: Ravi correcting the pantry book after every spoon. Precise: a write-hit policy in which a store updates the cached line and is also sent to the next level of the hierarchy, so the lower level always holds the current value. D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.4.1
Write-back
Simple words: writes stay in the cache; the next level is updated only when the line leaves. Analogy: Mei's red sticker, book corrected when the tray goes back. Precise: a write-hit policy in which a store updates only the cached line and marks it dirty; the whole line is written to the next level when it is evicted (or explicitly flushed). D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.4.1
Dirty bit
Simple words: a flag that says "this line was changed and memory does not know yet". Analogy: the red sticker on the tray. Precise: a status bit per cache line, set by a store in a write-back cache; a dirty victim must be written back before its slot is reused, a clean one may be dropped. D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.4, §11.4.1
Write-allocate
Simple words: on a write miss, first bring the line into the cache, then write it there. Analogy: fetching the tray before changing a jar on it. Precise: a write-miss policy that loads the missing line into the cache and then performs the write as a hit; usually paired with write-back. D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D9 ch. 5
No-write-allocate
Simple words: on a write miss, send the write onward and do not load the line. Analogy: correcting the pantry book directly without bringing the tray. Precise: a write-miss policy (also called write-around) that passes the write to the next level without allocating a line; usually paired with write-through. D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5
Write buffer
Simple words: a small queue where writes wait so the processor does not have to. Analogy: an outbox tray by the kitchen door that a runner empties. Precise: a buffer between a cache and the next level that holds pending writes (or written-back lines) so that the processor can continue while they drain; reads may need to check it. D2 ch. 5 D3 ch. 2, App. B

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Reading is easy for a cache: it hands out a copy. Writing is harder, because now two places, the cache and memory, could disagree.D1 ch. 6

One way is to write to both every time (write-through). Memory is always right, but every write costs a slow trip. The other way is to write only to the cache, put a sticker on the line (the dirty bit), and send the whole line back later (write-back). Much less traffic, but more to get right.D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.4.1

There is a second question: if you write to something that is not in the cache, do you fetch it first? Fetching it (write-allocate) helps if you will use it again soon. Not fetching it (no-write-allocate) saves a trip if you will not.D1 ch. 6

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Write hits

On a write hit a write-through cache updates the line and forwards the write to the next level; a write-back cache updates only the line and sets its dirty bit.D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.4.1 Write-through is simpler and keeps the lower level current, which helps when another agent (a device doing DMA, another processor) reads memory directly; its cost is traffic proportional to the number of stores. Write-back sends at most one line per eviction however many stores hit the line, at the price of the dirty bit, the write-back of dirty victims and more complex consistency with other agents.D1 ch. 6 D3 ch. 2, App. B

Write misses

On a write miss, write-allocate first loads the line and then treats the store as a hit; no-write-allocate sends the store onward and leaves the cache unchanged.D1 ch. 6 Write-allocate exploits locality of later accesses to the same line; no-write-allocate avoids loading a line that will only be overwritten. The usual pairings are write-back with write-allocate, and write-through with no-write-allocate.D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 CS:APP notes that caches further down the hierarchy tend to be write-back because of the larger cost of transfers there (chapter-level citation; Dive Into Systems says most modern caches use write-back because it amortises repeated writes to a line).D1 ch. 6 D11 §11.4.1

Evicting a dirty line

When a miss needs a slot whose line is dirty, the old line must be written back to the next level before (or while) the new one is installed; a clean victim can be dropped. The write-back goes to the victim's own address, reconstructed from its tag and its set index.D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.4.1 A write buffer lets the processor go on while those writes drain.D2 ch. 5 D3 ch. 2, App. B

Counting traffic

The worked example counts the memory transactions of one trace under both pairings with a 4-line cache. Two habits from that count generalise: under write-back, the cost of stores appears later, at eviction time, so a slow read can be "paying" for earlier writes; and under write-through, write traffic is the number of stores regardless of locality.D1 ch. 6

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Write-allocate and the read before the write. With write-allocate, a store that misses must bring the rest of the line in, because the store changes only some of its bytes. A loop that overwrites a large array therefore reads it from memory as well as writing it: up to twice the traffic of the bytes written.D1 ch. 6 D3 ch. 2, App. B In a multiprocessor this read also obtains permission to write the line, which is a coherence transaction (F1-36): in the Primer's terms a GetM request that "obtains the block in the Modified (read-write) state", or an Upg when the block is already held read-only.D4 §6.4.2 Processors offer ways around it, such as stores that bypass the cache for data that will not be reused soon; their names and exact behaviour belong to each architecture's manual.

Memory types. Not all memory should be cached the same way. Device registers (memory-mapped I/O, HW204) must not be cached at all, and frame buffers benefit from "write-combining", where writes are collected into larger bursts without being cached for reads. On x86 these memory types are selected by MTRRs and the page attribute table (PAT), as curriculum section 1.3 lists; the vendor manual that defines them (D7) could not be opened in the verification pass, so this paragraph names the mechanisms without describing them further.S1 D7 F4 and HW204 return to them with the specification in hand.

Persistence and devices. Because a write-back cache can hold the only current copy of data, anything that reads memory without going through the cache (a DMA device, a non-coherent accelerator) or anything that must survive power loss needs explicit cache maintenance or a coherent interconnect.D3 ch. 2, App. B The curriculum's DMA rows and F1-44 build on this.

How the hardware actually does it

Each line of a write-back cache stores a dirty bit next to its valid bit and tag. The cache controller (a state machine, F1-19) sets the dirty bit on a store hit; on a miss it checks the victim's dirty bit, and if set, reads the victim's data out of the data array into a write-back buffer, forms its address from the stored tag and the set index, and issues the write to the next level, then fills the slot with the new line.D2 ch. 5 D3 ch. 2, App. B Getting the victim's address wrong is the one-line hardware or simulator bug of the forensic lab.

Worked example

Cache: direct-mapped, 4 lines of 16 bytes. Trace: W 00, W 04, W 08, R 00, W 40, R 04, W 0c, W 00, R 80, W 84, W 88, R 00. Block = address ÷ 16, set = block mod 4. Blocks 0 (addresses 00–0f), 4 (40–4f) and 8 (80–8f) all map to set 0.

Write-back + write-allocate.

  1. W 00: miss → load block 0 (read 1); dirty. W 04, W 08, R 00: hits.
  2. W 40: miss; victim block 0 is dirty → write back (wb 1); load block 4 (read 2); dirty.
  3. R 04: miss; victim block 4 dirty → wb 2; load block 0 (read 3), clean. W 0c, W 00: hits; dirty.
  4. R 80: miss; victim block 0 dirty → wb 3; load block 8 (read 4). W 84, W 88: hits; dirty.
  5. R 00: miss; victim block 8 dirty → wb 4; load block 0 (read 5), clean. Final flush: nothing dirty.
  6. Total: 5 line reads, 4 line write-backs, 0 single writes.

Write-through + no-write-allocate. W 00, W 04, W 08: misses, not allocated: 3 single writes. R 00: miss → load block 0 (read 1). W 40: miss → single write 4. R 04: hit. W 0c, W 00: hits, written through → writes 5 and 6. R 80: miss → load block 8 (read 2). W 84, W 88: hits → writes 7 and 8. R 00: miss → read 3. Total: 3 line reads, 8 single writes.

Listing 1 prints both traces with the same totals.R1 Which is "cheaper" depends on the cost of a 16-byte line transfer versus a single write on the bus: count transactions and bytes for your own exercise numbers.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. The sticker is per line, not per jar. A dirty bit covers the whole line; writing back sends all 64 bytes even if one byte changed. Mei could correct a single entry in the book.D1 ch. 6
    2. 

  2. Nobody else reads the pantry book in the kitchen story. In a computer, devices and other cores may read memory while a dirty line sits in a cache; keeping them correct needs coherence protocols (F1-36) or explicit cache maintenance, which the restaurant has no equivalent for.D3 ch. 2, App. B D4 §6.4.2
    3. 

  3. Writing is not always cheaper later. In the story the late correction is pure gain. In a cache the deferred write-back lands on a later miss and can slow down an unrelated read at a bad moment.D2 ch. 5
    4.

Transition box

Where you came from: F1-33 and F1-34 treated every access as a read: hit or miss, then a copy. What carries over: lines, sets, ways, replacement, and the simulator's structure.D1 ch. 6 What changes: a write makes the cache hold the only current copy of data (write-back), so correctness now depends on the cache doing the right thing on eviction, and the cost of a store may be paid later by another access.D2 ch. 5 What comes next: F1-36 puts several cores, each with its own write-back cache, on one memory: how do they agree on the current value? That is cache coherence.

  • Write hit: write-through forwards every store; write-back marks the line dirty and writes it back on eviction.

  • Usual pairings: write-back + write-allocate, write-through + no-write-allocate.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: memory holds 14, 15, 10, 11, 12, 13 in blocks 0–5 instead of 10–15: each value sits two blocks after where it was written, wrapping around (R2). Dirty evictions: blocks 0 and 1 fill the two lines; writing block 2 evicts dirty block 0, block 3 evicts 1, block 4 evicts 2, block 5 evicts 3; reading block 0 evicts dirty 4, reading block 1 evicts dirty 5. Hypotheses: (H1) "dirty bit never set" would leave memory at 0 for blocks that were evicted, not filled with other blocks' values — ruled out; (H2) "victim written to the wrong address" predicts a victim's value appearing in another block — matches. Root cause: line 47 writes the victim to mem_.begin() + block * 16, the address of the incoming block, instead of line.block * 16. Then line 49 loads the incoming block from memory, which now contains the victim's data.
