Exam papers of HW203 The memory hierarchy (level L2–L3, 4 credits), written under owner ruling B4 by the Exam Writer and Lab Engineer pass. Everything here is answerable from chapters F1-32 to F1-39 and their lab listings. Cache geometries, latencies, bandwidth targets, miss penalties, page tables and byte costs that are not quoted from a run are exercise values (owner ruling A4), not the values of a real processor. Every measured number shown in a paper is copied from a recorded run on the course's build container (a shared cloud virtual machine), labelled as such, and is a measurement on that machine, not a specification (AH-23). Every model number comes from the course's own teaching models (the cache model cache.hpp of F1-33, the write-policy, MESI, TLB and DRAM models of F1-35, F1-36, F1-38 and F1-39), which stand in for real hardware and real tools such as Cachegrind and the CS:APP cache simulator (owner ruling A2).

Quizzes (Q)

Each chapter's "Check yourself" is its quiz, used in its graded variant (guide 11.4): eight questions per chapter, each set with an "explain in your own words", an "apply to a new case" and, as the L2 rule of guide 11.3 requires, a "predict, then run" question. Learners answer in writing after reading the chapter and before its lab; the "predict" half is handed in before the run. The chapter's answers section is the marking key; "in your own words" and "predict, then run" answers are marked with the generic rubric. Each quiz is worth 1.25 % of the course (10 % in all).

  • Quiz 1 — F1-32 Check yourself (latency and bandwidth with units; one block per latency; Little's law; why the chain is shuffled and padded; predict the stride-8 bandwidth; a tree walk; measurements on a virtual machine).
  • Quiz 2 — F1-33 Check yourself (capacity and bit widths; splitting 0x7A3C4; cache organisations; why the index uses middle bits; predict c0 in three geometries; four and sixteen 1 MiB arrays; a tag-only model; average memory access time).
  • Quiz 3 — F1-34 Check yourself (kinds of locality; cold miss rates by stride; classifying misses; the n = 16 column walk; predict a 4-set, 4-way cache; an image filter's stride; Cachegrind is not a counter; reading the stride table).
  • Quiz 4 — F1-35 Check yourself (write-through and write-back; usual pairings; a million stores; why write-allocate reads; predict W 40 appended; a logging buffer; a dirty victim's address).
  • Quiz 5 — F1-36 Check yourself (single writer or many readers; the silent E to M upgrade; a read of a Modified line; false sharing; predict a three-reader trace; per-worker statistics; directory versus snooping; counter++ on a coherent machine).
  • Quiz 6 — F1-37 Check yourself (sequential consistency; the TSO reordering; store buffers and 0/0; release/acquire and the SB test; predict the fence version; message passing with relaxed atomics; coherence versus consistency; tests are not proofs).
  • Quiz 7 — F1-38 Check yourself (pages; 9 index bits per level; the cost of a walk; first-touch faults; predict the split of 0x7A3C4; a 1 GiB hash table; TLB reach; minor and major faults).
  • Quiz 8 — F1-39 Check yourself (row buffers; the channel bit; 254 row hits of 256; one thread versus four; predict a padded stride; first touch on two sockets; refresh; a low channel bit).

Part C — forensic question F20: "The energy sum that read eight times more" (20 points)

Scenario. Every step, a particle simulator's diagnostics loop reads the mass of every particle twice (once for the total mass, once to weight the centre of mass). Kenji "tidied up" the code: the masses used to live in their own array of doubles (structure of arrays, SoA); now every particle is one struct Particle with eight double fields (position, velocity, mass, charge), an array of structures (AoS). The results are identical. Practical (P) — predict the cache behaviour of a loop nest, then measure

Weight 25 %. Time limit: 180 minutes. The practical is the course card's "predict cache behaviour of a loop nest, then measure": the loop nests of F1-34 (sum by rows, sum by columns) and the transpose of F1-34's extension, predicted by hand, computed with the course's cache model and then timed on a real machine.

Allowed documentation (engineers use documentation, guide 11.4): the HW203 chapters and their lab listings, a C++ reference and the C++ standard draft, the system's manual pages, and your own notes. Not allowed: communication with other people or with AI assistants, and any code written before the exam other than the starting files. Course project — a cache simulator in C++ validated against hand traces

Weight 20 %. Brief per guide 11.5. The project grows through the chapters' mini-projects: F1-33 (the simulator), F1-35 (writes), F1-36 (two cores with MESI), F1-37 (store buffers), F1-38 (address translation) and F1-39 (a DRAM back end). The F1-33 part and at least two of the later parts are required; the others are optional extensions for the top band.

Goal

Build a trace-driven cache simulator in C++ whose every count you can defend: each configuration is validated automatically against traces you traced by hand before running, and the simulator is then used to explain a real loop nest from your own code or from the course.

Requirements

  • Functional (required). Configurable sets S, ways E and line size B (powers of two where the address split needs them); the tag / set / offset split; LRU and FIFO replacement; a trace format with reads and writes and hexadecimal addresses; write-back with write-allocate and write-through with no-write-allocate, a dirty bit and a final flush (F1-35); counts of hits, misses, evictions, line reads, line write-backs and single writes.
  • Functional (at least two of). Two or more cores with private caches and MESI, counting bus transactions (F1-36); a TSO store buffer per core with forwarding (F1-37); a TLB and a 4-level page table built on demand, with walks and first-touch faults (F1-38); a DRAM back end with a configurable address mapping and row-buffer state (F1-39).
  • Quality. At least eight hand traces, each written down with its expected hit/miss sequence and totals before the first run, checked automatically by a test program that exits non-zero on any mismatch; at least one trace per extension; the traces of F1-33 (cachesim.in in its three geometries) and F1-35 (writepolicy.in) reproduced exactly; builds with the course flags (-std=c++20 -Wall -Wextra -Wpedantic -Werror, sanitizer build clean).
  • Evidence. One loop nest from your own code (or matrix multiplication in two loop orders) traced through the simulator with a geometry you can justify, and timed on your machine (median of at least five runs, machine named); a short comparison of the two.

Constraints

Hardware: any computer that builds C++20; no hardware counters are needed (none were available in this build, F1-34). Time: about 20 hours over F1-33 to F1-39. Language: C++20 with the standard library only. The simulator is a teaching model (ruling A2): it stands for tools such as the CS:APP cache lab's csim and Cachegrind, and the report must say what it does not model (prefetching, the real replacement policy, inclusion, timing).

Deliverables

  1. The source code and the hand-trace test file(s), with the build commands.
  2. The unedited output of the test program (all traces "ok") and of the evidence runs, each with toolchain, date and exit code.
  3. A README of one to two pages: the trace format, the configuration options, what is and is not modelled.
  4. A report of two to three pages: the loop-nest study (prediction, simulator counts, measured times) and one design decision explained.
  5. A ten-minute demonstration: run the tests, change one parameter live, and explain one line of a trace.
