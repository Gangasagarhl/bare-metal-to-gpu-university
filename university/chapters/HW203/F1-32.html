Story hook

Analogy

Amina is the head chef of a busy restaurant. Whatever is on her cutting board she can use at once. The shelves beside the stove hold a few dozen jars; the long shelf along the wall holds more; the back room holds more still. Everything else lives in the big pantry at the end of a long corridor.

One evening the new kitchen helper, Kofi, is sent to the pantry for a single jar of saffron. Amina waits, knife in hand, until he comes back. The dish cannot continue without it. Later the same evening, Kofi is sent to fetch forty sacks of rice for tomorrow. Nobody waits for any single sack; what matters is how many sacks per minute the corridor can carry, and whether several helpers can walk it at the same time.

Those are two different questions about the same corridor: how long until the first thing arrives? and how much arrives per minute once deliveries are flowing? Engineers call them latency and bandwidth, and a processor asks both of them billions of times a second.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain latency and bandwidth with their units, and why a program can be limited by either one.
  • Measure the load latency of your own machine for working sets of growing size with a pointer-chasing program, and read the plateaus of the curve.
  • Measure read bandwidth with independent loads and explain why it is far higher than "one block per latency".
  • Apply Little's law to compute how many bytes must be in flight to reach a given bandwidth.
  • Diagnose a latency benchmark that was fooled by a predictable access pattern.
Jargon box
Latency
Simple words: how long you wait from asking for something until it arrives. Analogy: the time of one round trip of the helper to the pantry. Precise: the time between issuing a memory request (for example a load) and the moment its data can be used; measured in seconds (ns) or in processor clock cycles. D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5
Bandwidth
Simple words: how much data arrives per second once it is flowing. Analogy: how many sacks per minute the corridor can carry. Precise: the rate of data transfer, in bytes per second (GB/s = 109 bytes per second in this course); also called throughput when it describes a measured rate. D2 ch. 5 D3 ch. 2
Memory hierarchy
Simple words: several layers of storage, from tiny and fast to huge and slow. Analogy: cutting board, shelves by the stove, shelf along the wall, back room, pantry, warehouse across town. Precise: the organisation of a computer's storage into levels (registers, caches, main memory, storage) in which each level is larger, slower and cheaper per byte than the one above it, and holds copies of data used by the level above. D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.1
Cache
Simple words: a small fast memory that keeps copies of data you used recently. Analogy: the shelves near the stove. Precise: a small, fast memory, managed by hardware, that holds copies of blocks of main memory so that later accesses to them are served quickly; F1-33 explains its organisation. D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.4
Working set
Simple words: the data a program keeps using during one phase of its work. Analogy: the jars a dish needs this evening. Precise: the set of memory locations (or its size in bytes) that a program accesses repeatedly during an interval of time; whether it fits in a cache level decides that level's hit rate. D1 ch. 6
Little's law
Simple words: to keep stuff arriving fast while each trip is slow, many trips must be under way at once. Analogy: forty sacks per minute with a five-minute walk means two hundred sacks must be on the way at any moment. Precise: in a steady system, the average number of items in the system equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends inside; for memory, bytes in flight = bandwidth × latency. D3 ch. 2
Memory-level parallelism
Simple words: several memory requests waiting at the same time instead of one after another. Analogy: several helpers walking the corridor together. Precise: the number of outstanding memory accesses a processor can have at once; it needs independent addresses, because a load whose address depends on another load cannot start until that load returns. D3 ch. 2
Prefetching
Simple words: fetching data before it is asked for, by guessing what comes next. Analogy: a helper who notices you take jars from left to right and brings the next one early. Precise: starting a memory transfer before the program's own access, either by instructions in the program (software prefetch) or by hardware that detects patterns such as fixed strides (hardware prefetch). D1 ch. 6 D3 ch. 2

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the memory hierarchy drawn as the restaurant. Each level up is smaller and faster; each level down is larger and slower. The figure has no numbers on purpose: sizes and times belong to a named product's documents, or to your own measurement in the lab.

Figure 2 shows why latency and bandwidth are different questions. On the left, each request waits for the one before it: the corridor carries one jar per round trip. On the right, several independent requests overlap: the trip still takes as long, but many jars are on their way at once.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A processor works on numbers that sit in its registers, its cutting board. When it needs a number that is not there, it must ask memory for it. If the number is on a nearby shelf (a cache), it comes back quickly. If it is only in the pantry (main memory), the processor waits for a long walk.D1 ch. 6 D11 §11.1

Why not make the whole pantry as fast as the shelf by the stove? Because fast storage is expensive and takes room, and big storage is slower to search and further away. So computers use a little fast memory and a lot of slow memory, and keep copies of the things you use most in the fast part.D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.1

The waiting time for one trip is the latency. The amount delivered per second once many trips are under way is the bandwidth. A program that needs one thing before it can know what to ask for next (like following clues on a treasure hunt) feels every trip's latency. A program that asks for many things at once (like a long shopping list) can use the bandwidth.D3 ch. 2

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why memory is slower than the processor

F1-20 showed the two cell types: an SRAM cell holds a bit in a small loop of transistors and can be read quickly; a DRAM cell holds a bit as charge on a capacitor, is much smaller, and must be read through a slower sense-and-restore process and refreshed.D2 ch. 5 D5 ch. 8 Because DRAM cells are small and cheap, main memory is built from DRAM, and because SRAM is fast, caches are built from SRAM.D2 ch. 5 Main memory also sits outside the processor chip, on a bus driven by a memory controller, which adds the time of crossing chips and of the controller's scheduling.D3 ch. 2

There is a second, more general reason. A larger memory needs more address decoding, longer wires and more cells per data line, so for the same technology a larger memory is slower than a smaller one.D2 ch. 5 This is why the hierarchy of Figure 1 exists: no single memory is both large and fast, so the designer builds several levels and relies on programs reusing the same data (F1-34 calls this locality).D1 ch. 6 D2 ch. 5 D11 §11.1, §11.3

Two different measures

Latency is the time from issuing a request until its data can be used. Bandwidth is the rate at which data is delivered, in bytes per second.D2 ch. 5 They are linked but not the same: a wide road with a long journey has high bandwidth and high latency. If only one request is outstanding at a time, the rate is at most one block per latency. For 64-byte blocks and a latency of 100 ns that is 64 bytes per 100 ns, which is 0.64 GB/s, whatever the road could carry (exercise numbers; Listing 3 prints the same sum).R3

Little's law

Little's law says that in a steady system the average number of items inside equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends inside.D3 ch. 2 For memory: bytes in flight = bandwidth × latency. To read 10 GB/s from a memory with 100 ns latency, 10 bytes per ns × 100 ns = 1,000 bytes must be on their way at every moment, about sixteen 64-byte blocks (exercise numbers, checked by Listing 3).R3 If the hardware can only keep a few requests outstanding, or the program cannot generate independent addresses, the full bandwidth is out of reach.D3 ch. 2

Dependent and independent loads

A load whose address is computed from the value of another load cannot start until that load has returned. Following a linked list is the standard example: p = p->next again and again. Such a chain exposes the full latency of every step, which is exactly why benchmarks use it to measure latency (Listing 1).D1 ch. 6 D3 ch. 2 Summing an array is the opposite: every address is known in advance (a + i), so the processor's out-of-order core (F1-29) and the hardware prefetchers can have many loads in flight, and the measured rate approaches the bandwidth (Listing 2).D3 ch. 2

Working sets and plateaus

If a program's working set fits in a cache level, almost all of its accesses are served by that level after the first pass, and its time per access stays flat as the working set grows, until the set no longer fits. Plotting time per access against working-set size therefore shows plateaus separated by steps, one plateau per level.D1 ch. 6 Listing 1 draws that curve for the machine it runs on. The positions of the steps are evidence about your machine's caches; the sizes the operating system reports are another (F1-33). Neither is a datasheet.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Outstanding misses. To overlap misses, a cache must keep accepting requests while earlier misses are pending (a "non-blocking" or "lockup-free" cache) and must track each pending miss until its data returns.D3 ch. 2 The number of misses a core can keep in flight is a design limit; by Little's law it caps the bandwidth one core can draw at roughly (outstanding misses × block size) ÷ latency. The measured multi-thread bandwidth in F1-39 rises with the number of threads on this build's machine, which is consistent with a per-core limit, but the build did not verify the limit's value for this CPU.

Not verified — check before relying on this

How many outstanding L1 data-cache misses one core of the build container's processor can track, and how many its hardware prefetchers add, were not checked. The documents to check are the processor vendor's optimisation reference manual for the exact model (the model string is in every .log of this chapter) and the Hennessy–Patterson chapter on memory hierarchy design (D3 ch. 2). To test it you need a physical machine with a documented processor model and its hardware performance counters (F1-34), which the build container does not expose.

Prefetching changes what you measure. Hardware prefetchers detect simple patterns, such as accesses to consecutive blocks, and fetch ahead.D3 ch. 2 A latency benchmark whose chain visits blocks in address order therefore measures something closer to bandwidth than latency. Listing 1 shuffles the visiting order to defeat this; the forensic lab shows what happens without the shuffle.

Virtual memory joins the curve. Each load also needs its virtual address translated. Once the working set spans more pages than the translation cache (the TLB, F1-38) covers, the random chain also pays for page-table walks, so the largest working sets in Listing 1 measure DRAM latency plus translation cost.D1 ch. 9 D11 §13.3.4 F1-38 separates the two.

Measurement discipline. Build with optimisation and without sanitizers (sanitizers add work to every access); warm up before timing; make the result observable (the checksum) so the compiler cannot delete the loop; use a monotonic clock (std::chrono::steady_clock); repeat and report the spread; and name the machine. On a shared virtual machine, other tenants and the hypervisor add noise, so one run is a sample, not a property of the processor.D6 §30.7.7 S1

How the hardware actually does it

A load first checks the L1 data cache. On a miss, the request goes to the next level, and so on; at the last cache level a miss becomes a request to the memory controller, which turns it into DRAM commands for the right channel, bank, row and column (F1-39).D2 ch. 5 D3 ch. 2 The data comes back as a whole block (a cache line, F1-33), is installed in the caches on the way, and the waiting load completes.D2 ch. 5 Meanwhile an out-of-order core keeps executing instructions that do not depend on the load; when nothing independent is left, the core stalls.D3 ch. 2 In F1-27 the "memory" stage of the five-stage pipeline took one cycle; that was a simplification valid only when the access hits in the first-level cache.

The build container reports an Intel Xeon processor (model string in the logs) running as a virtual machine with four CPUs. Its cache sizes appear in F1-33's cache_info.out as the kernel reports them. Nothing in this chapter is a specification of that processor: every time below was measured on the build container, once, and labelled so.

Worked example

Exercise numbers first. A memory system has 100 ns latency and the program wants 10 GB/s.

  1. Units: 10 GB/s = 10 × 109 bytes per 109 ns = 10 bytes per ns.
  2. Little's law: bytes in flight = 10 bytes/ns × 100 ns = 1,000 bytes.
  3. In 64-byte blocks: 1,000 ÷ 64 = 15.6, so about 16 block requests must be outstanding at all times.
  4. With only one request outstanding: 64 bytes per 100 ns = 0.64 GB/s.
  5. If the latency halves to 50 ns with the same 10 GB/s goal, only 500 bytes (7.8 blocks) need to be in flight (case C).

Listing 3 prints exactly these numbers from littles.in.R3 Then run.sh feeds it the measured numbers of this build's largest working set: the last line of Listing 1's random-chain output (latency) and of Listing 2's output (bandwidth). The result, in the second output block of Listing 3, says how many blocks the build container must have had in flight during Listing 2 if both measurements describe the same memory: about thirty. That is an inference from two measurements on one machine, not a hardware fact.R1 R3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. Distance is time, not metres. The pantry is "far" because of how DRAM is read, how many chips and controllers lie in between and how big it is, not mainly because of the length of the wires a signal travels.D2 ch. 5 D3 ch. 2 A kitchen helper's walk time grows with metres; memory latency does not follow a simple distance rule.
    2. 

  2. The chef does not choose what goes on the shelves. Caches fill automatically, block by block, and evict by a fixed policy (F1-33). Amina decides which jars to keep near the stove; a program can only influence the caches indirectly, through the order of its accesses.D1 ch. 6
    3. 

  3. Helpers fetch whole trays, not jars. Memory always moves a whole block (64 bytes on this build's machine) even when the program wanted 8 bytes; a helper bringing one jar has no such rule. This is why the order of accesses matters so much (F1-34).D1 ch. 6
    4.

Expected observations


On the build container (measured, one session, shown above): a flat region under 2 ns, a step after 32 KiB, a second plateau, a rise after 1 MiB and a long climb to a few hundred nanoseconds at 256 MiB; bandwidth highest for small blocks and lowest for the largest. Verification


(1) Your five runs agree within a spread you report. (2) Each step you marked lies within one doubling of a cache size reported by your operating system, or you wrote down why not. (3) Your Little's-law line gives a number of blocks in flight greater than one.

Summary


    

  • Latency is the wait for one request; bandwidth is the delivery rate once requests flow.
    • 

  • Memory is a hierarchy because no single memory is both large and fast; SRAM caches sit in front of DRAM main memory.
    • 

  • Dependent loads (pointer chasing) expose latency; independent loads can overlap and approach bandwidth.
    • 

  • Little's law: bytes in flight = bandwidth × latency.
    • 

  • A latency-versus-working-set curve has plateaus and steps that hint at cache sizes.
    • 

  • Predictable access orders let prefetchers hide latency; benchmarks must defeat them to measure it.
    • 

  • Every time in this chapter is a measurement on the build container, not a specification.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: up to 32 KiB both runs are under about 2 ns per load; from 1–2 MiB on, the random chain climbs to a few hundred ns while the sequential chain stays in the single or low double digits. Hypotheses: (H1) Rafael's machine has much faster DRAM — ruled out, it is the same machine and the same run session; (H2) the sequential chain lets the hardware prefetch the next blocks, so most loads hit in a cache — consistent: the only difference between the runs is the order of the links (lines 27–33 of Listing 1); (H3) the sequential chain also crosses pages in order, so translation is cheaper — possible as a contributor. Conclusion: Leila's random chain measures load latency; Rafael's measures how well prefetching hides it. Prevention: "Latency was measured with a randomly ordered pointer chain, each node in its own 64-byte block, on machine X with toolchain Y on date Z." How the evidence was made: chase.cc run with argument sequential (identity order) and random (fixed-seed shuffle) by run.sh (R1).
