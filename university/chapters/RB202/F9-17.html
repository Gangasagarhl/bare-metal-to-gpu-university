Story hook

Analogy

Tomás is learning to ride with a small trailer behind his bicycle. His aunt Ayşe gives him one rule for the speed: "The further below your speed you are, the harder you push." A little slow, a little push; very slow, a big push.

It works, mostly. He never quite reaches the speed he wants, though: when he is almost there, the rule says "push only a little", and a little is not enough to drag the trailer. So he settles a bit slow. "Push harder for every bit you are slow, then," says Ayşe. He tries. Now he gets closer, but when he glances at the speedometer only once in a while, he overshoots, eases off too much, overshoots again, and wobbles around his speed like a nervous rabbit.

Tomás has discovered the two faces of proportional control: a stronger reaction gets you closer to your goal, and a too-strong reaction, checked too rarely, makes you swing. This chapter measures both faces on the simulated motor and gives you the numbers that separate them.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a proportional controller with a clamped output, and state the units of its gain.
  • Predict the steady-state error of a proportional speed loop, with and without a load, and check it by simulation.
  • Explain why a larger gain makes the loop faster and more accurate, and why it cannot grow without limit.
  • Calculate the pole of the sampled loop and use it to predict smooth, ringing or unstable behaviour.
  • Diagnose a buzzing motor from a control log and choose a fix.
Jargon box
Proportional control (P)
Simple words: the further you are from the goal, the harder you correct. Analogy: "a little slow, a little push; very slow, a big push". Precise: the control law u = Kp·e with e = r − y; the P of PID. RB101 called it proportional correction. B1
Proportional gain (Kp)
Simple words: the number that says how hard to correct for each unit of error. Analogy: how much harder Tomás pushes for each km/h he is slow. Precise: the constant Kp in u = Kp·e, with units of command per unit of error (here volts per rad/s). B1
Steady-state error
Simple words: the difference between setpoint and output that is left once everything has settled. Analogy: Tomás settling a bit below his speed with the trailer. Precise: ess = r − y after all transients have died out; for the proportional speed loop of this chapter ess = (r + KloadTload)/(1 + L) with L = KpKdc. B1D1
Loop gain
Simple words: how strongly the whole loop reacts, going once round it. Analogy: how much speed Tomás gains, in the end, for each km/h he notices he is slow. Precise: the product of the gains round the loop at steady state, here L = Kp·Kdc (dimensionless); MA301 writes it L(0). B1D1
Saturation (actuator)
Simple words: the actuator cannot deliver more than its limit, however hard the controller asks. Analogy: Tomás pushing the pedals as hard as his legs can. Precise: a limit u = clamp(v, umin, umax) between controller and plant; while it is active, the loop is effectively open. B1
Overshoot
Simple words: how far the output goes past its final value, as a percentage. Analogy: Tomás going faster than his target before easing off. Precise: (ymax − y∞)/y∞ × 100 %, with y∞ the settled output. B1
Pole (of a sampled loop)
Simple words: the number that multiplies "how far from settled" every control period. Analogy: how much of his speed error Tomás still has at each glance at the speedometer, and whether it has flipped from too slow to too fast. Precise: for a first-order sampled loop y[k+1] − y∞ = p·(y[k] − y∞); |p| < 1 is stable, p < 0 alternates in sign. MA302 (F0-79) treats poles in the z-plane in general. B1D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A proportional controller is the simplest sensible rule: look at how far off you are and correct by an amount that matches. Far off, correct a lot. Nearly there, correct a little. RB101 used this rule to steer a robot back to a line.

The rule has one setting, the gain. A gentle gain is calm but lazy: it never quite gets to the goal, because near the goal it barely pushes. A strong gain gets closer and faster. But if you only check every now and then and react strongly each time, you overdo it, then overdo the correction, and you swing back and forth. Push it further and the swings grow until something hits its limit. Choosing the gain is choosing between "lazy" and "nervous".

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The law and its units

The proportional controller computes V = Kp·(r − y), then clamps V to the driver's range. With r and y in rad/s and V in volts, Kp has units of volts per rad/s. Writing the units down is the quickest check that a gain is sensible: Kp = 0.2 V per rad/s means "an error of 10 rad/s produces 2 V". In Figure 1 the gain is the slope; the flat parts at ±12 V are saturation. B1D1

Steady-state error

F9-16 derived the settled speed: y = (L·r − KloadTload)/(1 + L), with the loop gain L = KpKdc. Subtract it from r and simplify: D1

ess = r − y = (r + KloadTload) / (1 + L)

The error never reaches zero for any finite gain: proportional control needs an error to produce any command at all, and the motor needs a command (about 3 V for 300 rad/s) to keep turning. Doubling the gain roughly halves the error once L is large. Listing 1 checks the formula: its "error" and "predicted" columns agree to 0.1 rad/s for every gain that settles (run R1). B1R1

There is an important exception, which you will meet in position control (F9-19): if the plant itself integrates, so that a constant command makes the output grow without limit (a constant voltage makes a motor's angle grow), a proportional loop can settle with zero error for a constant setpoint. The speed loop of this chapter has no such integrator, so it keeps an error. B1

Speed of response

Put the controller into the model of F9-16. In each small step dt the motor closes the fraction dt/τ of the gap between its speed and KdcV. With V = Kp(r − y):

ynew = y + (dt/τ)·(L·(r − y) − y) = y + (dt/τ)·(1 + L)·(y∞ − y),  y∞ = L·r/(1 + L)

This is the same kind of update as before, with τ replaced by τ/(1 + L). So the closed loop is 1 + L times faster than the motor alone: with Kp = 0.05, L = 4.90 and the time constant falls from 196 ms to 33 ms. That is the good face of gain: more accuracy and more speed. D1U1

What sampling does

Note

Maths you need here. e−x is a number your calculator gives you; for small x it is close to 1 − x. Repeating "keep the fraction (1 − dt/τ)" many times over a period T keeps the fraction e−T/τ in total. MA301 explains why; here you only use the value.

The formula above assumes the controller reacts continuously. A digital controller reacts once per period T = 10 ms and holds its command in between. Over one period with V held constant, the motor keeps the fraction a = e−T/τ = 0.9503 of its old speed and gains b = Kdc(1 − a) = 4.875 rad/s for every volt (run R1 prints both): D1R1

y[k+1] = a·y[k] + b·V[k] = a·y[k] + b·Kp·(r − y[k]) = (a − b·Kp)·y[k] + b·Kp·r

The number p = a − bKp is the pole of the sampled loop: every period, the distance to the settled speed is multiplied by p. Figure 3 places it for each gain. For 0 < p < 1 the gap shrinks smoothly. For −1 < p < 0 the gap shrinks but flips sign every period: the speed overshoots, undershoots, overshoots, which is the ringing of Kp = 0.3 in Figure 2. For p < −1 the gap grows every period: the loop is unstable. Solving for the boundaries: D1B1

  • ringing starts at p = 0: Kp = a/b = 0.9503/4.875 = 0.195 V per rad/s;
  • instability starts at p = −1: Kp = (1 + a)/b = 1.9503/4.875 = 0.400 V per rad/s.

Listing 1 confirms both: Kp = 0.2 overshoots slightly (peak 286.0 against a settled 285.4), 0.3 and 0.38 ring and then settle, and 0.45 never settles (run R1). R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why the limit exists. The motor model alone would accept any gain if the controller reacted instantly; MA301 (F0-70) shows that. The limit comes from the loop's delay: a sampled controller sees the speed only every 10 ms and holds its command for 10 ms, which acts like a delay of roughly half a period. Real loops add more lag: the driver, the speed calculation from encoder counts, filters. MA301 (F0-71) adds a 10 ms driver lag and a 20 ms filter lag to the same motor and finds a critical gain of 0.3475 V per rad/s for a continuous loop. Every lag lowers the gain you can use. U1B1

Gain margin. Engineers rarely run near the boundary. The ratio between the critical gain and the gain actually used is the gain margin (MA301 F0-71 and F0-72). With Kp = 0.2 and the sampled-loop limit of 0.400, the margin is a factor 2. Åström and Murray discuss what margins are considered robust; this chapter does not quote a rule from memory. B1U1

Saturation changes the picture. The linear formulas assume the command stays inside ±12 V. For a 300 rad/s step, Kp = 0.1 asks for 30 V and Kp = 0.3 for 90 V at the first sample; both get 12 V, which is why their first four samples in Figure 2 are identical. During saturation the motor climbs at its open-loop full-voltage rate, and the gain only matters again once the error is small enough. For Kp = 0.45 the unstable loop grows until the command hits the limits and then oscillates between them: a limit cycle (forensic lab). R2R3

Noise gets amplified. Every wobble in the measured speed is multiplied by Kp before it reaches the driver. With speed computed from encoder counts (F9-22), each count of quantisation becomes a voltage step Kp times the speed-per-count. A high gain therefore also means a noisier command, which the motor hears as a hum. B1U1

How the hardware actually does it

In the microcontroller, the proportional law is one subtraction, one multiplication and a clamp, done once per timer interrupt. The result is written to the PWM unit as a duty cycle: the command in volts divided by the supply voltage. The PWM unit has a limited number of steps (its resolution, set by its counter width and clock; F1-70, HW204), so the command is rounded to the nearest step. The driver's limit in the simulation (±12 V) stands for the battery voltage of a real kit; its true value, and how it sags under load, come from the kit's documents and your measurements (F9-22). U1

The control period is set by the timer. The sampled-loop analysis says that the period is part of the design: change it and a, b and the safe gain range all change. That is why a gain copied from one robot to another, or from a fast loop to a slow one, can make a calm motor buzz. D1

Worked example

Problem. The specification for the motor says: settled error at most 5 % of a 300 rad/s setpoint with no load, and no ringing. Can a proportional controller with T = 10 ms meet it? With which gain?

  1. Error requirement. ess = 300/(1 + L) ≤ 15 rad/s, so 1 + L ≥ 20, L ≥ 19, and Kp ≥ 19/98.039 = 0.194 V per rad/s.
  2. No ringing. The pole must stay positive: Kp ≤ a/b = 0.195 V per rad/s.
  3. Answer. Only the narrow window 0.194–0.195 works. Kp = 0.195 gives L = 19.12 and ess = 300/20.12 = 14.9 rad/s, inside the limit, with a pole at 0.9503 − 4.875 × 0.195 = 0.000.
  4. Load. Now add a 2 mN·m brake: ess = (300 + 39.2)/20.12 = 16.9 rad/s, 5.6 %. The specification fails.
  5. Check by simulation. Listing 1's row for Kp = 0.2 (slightly outside the window) shows an error of 14.6 rad/s and a tiny overshoot to 286.0 rad/s (run R1).

Conclusion: proportional control alone is squeezed between accuracy and calmness, and any load pushes it out. F9-18 adds the term that removes the steady-state error without a high gain. D1R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Tomás's wobble comes partly from glancing at the speedometer only now and then, which is exactly the sampling effect of Layer 2. But a rider who notices wobbling instinctively softens his reaction; a controller keeps the same gain forever and will oscillate until someone changes the code. R3
    • 

  • A rider who pushes "a bit harder for each bit too slow" also learns from experience to push a steady amount more when towing a trailer. That learned extra push is integral action (F9-18); pure proportional control has no memory and cannot learn it. B1
    • 

  • Legs tire gradually; a driver's limit is a hard wall at ±12 V, and above it the loop is not "pushing very hard", it is open. R2

Lab


Goal


Map the useful range of the proportional gain for the simulated motor: measure the steady-state error, the overshoot and the stability boundary, and compare each with its formula.


Hardware and software needed


No hardware. A C++20 compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 through university/labs/run_lab.sh.


Safety


None beyond normal computer use. Note for later: on a real motor, an unstable gain drives the driver between its limits at the loop rate; F9-22 starts every real run with a low gain and a speed limit.

Expected observations


Overshoot appears exactly for the gains with a negative pole (0.2, 0.3, 0.38): 0.2 %, 4.5 % and 6.2 % from R1's columns. The boundary found by experiment lies between 0.39 and 0.41, close to the predicted 0.400; a gain very close to the boundary rings for a long time and may not have settled after 4 s. With a 5 ms period the boundary moves up to about 0.80 V per rad/s (Check yourself 6). These are predictions from the formulas and R1; your runs are the check.

The error column matches the formula to 0.1 rad/s for every stable gain, with and without load; the boundary from step 4 brackets (1 + a)/b.


Troubleshooting


    

  • A stable gain does not match the formula → the run is too short for a slowly ringing loop; lengthen it.
    • 

  • Changing the period changes nothing → the sub-step count was not changed with it, so the plant still advances 10 ms per sample.
    • 

  • Error larger than predicted with load → the load term was added with the wrong sign or units (mN·m versus N·m).

Summary


    

  • Proportional control: V = Kp(r − y), clamped to the driver's limits; Kp has units (here V per rad/s).
    • 

  • Steady-state error: ess = (r + KloadTload)/(1 + L) with L = KpKdc; never zero for this plant.
    • 

  • A continuous P loop is 1 + L times faster than the plant.
    • 

  • Sampled every T, the loop's pole is p = a − bKp; 0 < p < 1 smooth, −1 < p < 0 ringing, p < −1 unstable. For the pretend motor at 10 ms: ringing above 0.195, unstable above 0.400.
    • 

  • Saturation overrides the gain for large errors; an unstable loop with a clamped output becomes a limit cycle.
    • 

  • Higher gain also amplifies sensor noise; gains depend on the plant and the period and are not portable.
    •
