Story hook

Analogy

Amara is learning to stop her bicycle exactly at a chalk line in the playground. Her first attempts use one rule: "the further from the line, the harder I pedal or brake." She reaches the line fast, but she is still moving quickly when she gets there, rolls past, brakes back, overshoots the other way, and wobbles to and fro before she finally stands still.

Her friend Keanu watches and says: "Look at how fast you are coming, not only at how far you are. If you are rolling fast towards the line, start braking before you get there." Amara tries it. Now, as she approaches quickly, she eases off early; as she slows down, she eases the brake. She glides in and stops on the line.

The new rule looks at the rate at which the distance is changing. That is derivative action, the D of PID. This chapter shows how it calms a swinging system, and why it must be handled with care: it is very sensitive to jumps and to jitter in the measurement.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain derivative action as a reaction to the rate of change, and show that on a cart it acts like a damper.
  • Predict the damping ratio and the overshoot of a PD-controlled cart from its gains, and check them by simulation.
  • Compute the derivative from samples in C++, on the error or on the measurement, and explain the derivative kick.
  • Explain why differentiation amplifies measurement noise and how much, for a given sensor resolution and control period.
  • Diagnose a chattering actuator from a log and propose a fix.
Jargon box
Derivative action (D term)
Simple words: a part of the command that reacts to how fast things are changing. Analogy: Amara braking early because she is approaching the line fast. Precise: the term Kd·de/dt of the controller output; in discrete form Kd·(e[k] − e[k−1])/T, or −Kd·(y[k] − y[k−1])/T when taken from the measurement. The D of PID. B1D1
Derivative gain (Kd)
Simple words: how strongly to react to each unit of "how fast". Analogy: how firmly Amara brakes for each bit of extra speed. Precise: the constant Kd; units of command per (error unit per second), here N per (m/s) = N·s/m. B1
Damping
Simple words: anything that slows a moving thing in proportion to its speed, so swings die out. Analogy: the shock absorber on a bicycle fork. Precise: a force or effect opposing velocity, −c·v; it removes energy from an oscillation. MA301 (F0-68) measures it with the damping ratio ζ. B1U1
Damping ratio (ζ)
Simple words: a single number saying how strongly the swinging is slowed down. Analogy: soft fork (big swings) versus stiff fork (no swings). Precise: ζ = c/(2√(k·m)) for a mass–spring–damper; under-damped 0 < ζ < 1 (overshoots), critically damped ζ = 1, over-damped ζ > 1. U1B1
Settling time
Simple words: how long until the output stays close to its final value for good. Analogy: the moment Amara stands still on the line and stays there. Precise: the last time the response is outside a band (here ±2 %) around its final value. U1
Derivative kick
Simple words: a huge one-sample spike in the command when the setpoint jumps. Analogy: slamming the brakes because the chalk line was suddenly redrawn somewhere else. Precise: the impulse Kd·Δr/T produced by differentiating the error when the setpoint r changes by Δr in one sample. B1R3
Derivative on measurement
Simple words: taking the "how fast" from the measured output instead of from the error. Analogy: watching how fast the bicycle moves, not how fast the line moves. Precise: D = −Kd·dy/dt; equal to Kd·de/dt whenever the setpoint is constant, and free of derivative kick. B1D1
Noise amplification (of differentiation)
Simple words: small wobbles in readings become big wobbles in the speed you compute from them. Analogy: a shaky speedometer needle making Amara brake in jerks. Precise: a reading error of size ε produces an error up to 2ε/T in a two-point difference; the shorter T, the larger. U1D1

The idea in pictures

This chapter controls the position of a cart rather than the speed of a motor, because position control is where derivative action matters most. The cart is the one simulated in MA301 (F0-67): 2 kg, with weak friction of 0.8 N per (m/s), pushed by a force limited to ±10 N (exercise values). Figure 1 shows what a PD controller does to it: P acts like a spring pulling the cart to the setpoint, D like a damper resisting its motion.

Figure 2 shows the difference D makes after a 1 m step, and Figure 3 shows the derivative kick.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A spring pulls harder the further you stretch it. That is proportional control. Hang a weight on a spring and let go: it bounces up and down for a long time, because nothing stops it from gaining speed on the way to the middle. A proportional controller on a cart that slides easily does the same: it pulls the cart to the goal, the cart arrives fast, rolls past, and swings back and forth.

A shock absorber pushes against motion, harder the faster you move. Put one next to the spring and the bouncing dies away quickly. Derivative action is a shock absorber made of code: it looks at how fast the measured position is changing and pushes against that speed. Its weakness is that it believes every jump it sees. If the reading twitches, D thinks the cart moved very fast and pushes hard.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why P alone swings here

For the cart, the controller's output is a force F on a 2 kg mass. With P only, F = Kp(r − x). Compare this with a mass on a spring: the spring force is k·(rest position − x). They are the same equation with k = Kp: the controller is a spring of stiffness Kp = 8 N/m. The only damping is the friction, c = 0.8 N·s/m. MA301 (F0-68) gives the damping ratio of a mass–spring–damper: U1D1

ζ = c / (2·√(k·m)) = 0.8 / (2·√(8 × 2)) = 0.8 / 8 = 0.10

With ζ = 0.1 the standard second-order formula (MA301 F0-68) predicts an overshoot of exp(−πζ/√(1 − ζ²)) = 72.9 %. The simulation gives 74.1 % and takes 19.33 s to settle within ±2 % (run R1). Unlike the motor speed loop of F9-17, this plant integrates twice (force changes velocity, velocity changes position), and that is what lets it swing. U1R1B1

Adding D: a damper in code

Derivative action adds a force proportional to the rate of change. Taken from the measured position, D = −Kd·v, where v is the cart's velocity. That is exactly the force of a damper with coefficient Kd. The controller and the friction now act together like a spring of stiffness Kp and a damper of coefficient c + Kd (Figure 1), so D1B1

ζ = (c + Kd) / (2·√(Kp·m))

Listing 1 tries five values of Kd with Kp = 8 (run R1). The predicted and simulated overshoots agree to within about 1 percentage point: R1

  • Kd = 1.6 (ζ = 0.30): 37.2 % predicted, 37.7 % simulated; settles in 6.65 s.
  • Kd = 4.8 (ζ = 0.70): 4.6 % predicted, 4.5 % simulated; settles in 2.95 s.
  • Kd = 7.2 (ζ = 1.00): no overshoot; settles in 2.94 s.
  • Kd = 20 (ζ = 2.60): no overshoot, but sluggish: 9.90 s to settle.

Too little D leaves the swing; too much D makes the cart crawl, because the damper resists the motion that is needed to get there. The fastest settling lies between ζ ≈ 0.7 and 1. R1B1

The derivative from samples

A controller sees the position only every T = 10 ms, so it estimates the rate of change from two samples: v ≈ (x[k] − x[k−1])/T. This is the backward difference of MA301 (F0-66); it describes the average velocity over the last period, so it lags the true velocity by about half a period. Listing 1 computes D = −Kd·(x[k] − x[k−1])/T on line 42. U1D1

Error or measurement?

The textbook form of D differentiates the error, Kd·de/dt. Since e = r − x, de/dt = dr/dt − dx/dt. While the setpoint is constant, dr/dt = 0 and the two forms are identical. When the setpoint jumps, the difference sees the whole jump in one sample: Δe/T = 1 m/0.01 s = 100 m/s, times Kd = 4.8 gives 480 N. Listing 3 shows the controller asking for 488 N at the step (480 from D, 8 from P), which the driver clips to 10 N (Figure 3, run R3). That is the derivative kick. Differentiating the measurement instead gives no spike at all, because the cart's position does not jump. This course uses derivative on measurement from now on. B1R3

Noise amplification

Suppose the position sensor reports whole millimetres. While the cart moves slowly, the reading stays the same for a few samples and then steps by 1 mm. The difference quotient sees "0, 0, 0, 1 mm in one period": 0 m/s, then 0.001/0.01 = 0.1 m/s. With Kd = 4.8 that is a force step of 0.48 N. Make the loop ten times faster (T = 1 ms) and the same 1 mm step becomes 1 m/s and 4.8 N. The faster loop sees the cart no better, but its derivative is ten times jumpier. That is the forensic lab of this chapter. D1R4U1

The standard cure is to filter the derivative: pass it through a first-order low-pass filter (MA301 F0-66) that averages the jumps over a few periods. The filter adds a little lag, so the filter time constant is a trade-off. F9-21 builds it into the library. B1U1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

D as prediction. Another way to read P + D: Kp·e + Kd·de/dt = Kp·(e + (Kd/Kp)·de/dt), and e + Td·de/dt is a straight-line prediction of the error Td = Kd/Kp seconds ahead. With Kd = 4.8 and Kp = 8, the PD controller acts on where the error will be 0.6 s from now. Åström and Murray use this "prediction" reading; it explains why D helps against overshoot (it sees the arrival coming) and why it is useless when the prediction is poor (noisy measurements). B1D1

When D is not needed. The motor speed loop of F9-17 and F9-18 is a first-order plant: it does not swing by itself, and PI control is enough there. D earns its place on plants that store energy in two forms and swing between them (mass and spring, position and velocity), or on loops with significant lag. Many practical speed loops therefore run PI, and many position loops run PD or PID. B1

Limiting the derivative's gain at high frequency. An ideal derivative has a gain that grows without limit for fast signals; noise is fast. The filtered derivative used in practice has a gain that levels off: Åström and Murray parametrise the filter time constant as a fraction of Td (often written Td/N). The exact parametrisation and the values they consider reasonable must be taken from the book; this chapter does not quote them from memory. B1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The "Td/N" form of the derivative filter and any range of N recommended for it are recalled from general knowledge, not read in this build. Check "Feedback Systems" (Åström, Murray), PID chapter, the section on filtering the derivative, and record the form and values in the RB202 dossier (gate G1). F9-21 uses a filter time constant chosen and tested in simulation instead of a rule.

Setpoint weighting. Derivative on measurement is the extreme case of a more general idea: apply only a fraction of the setpoint change to the P and D terms. It reduces overshoot on setpoint steps without changing how the loop rejects disturbances. B1 treats it in the PID chapter. B1

How the hardware actually does it

The derivative is only as good as the measurement it is computed from. A position from an incremental encoder (F1-66) is quantised: it moves in whole counts. A rate computed from counts over one period jumps in steps of (one count)/T, so the finer the encoder and the longer the window, the smoother the rate. Some microcontrollers can also measure the time between encoder edges (input capture, HW204), which gives a better rate at low speeds; MA301 (F0-66) compares the two methods. U1

The actuator hears the D term directly. A jumpy D term means a jumpy PWM duty cycle, which a motor turns into audible noise, heat and wear in gears. When a fast loop "sounds wrong", log the D term first. The resolution of your kit's encoder and its PWM settings come from the kit's documents (F9-22). U1

Worked example

Problem. With Kp = 8 N/m on the 2 kg cart (c = 0.8 N·s/m), choose Kd for a damping ratio of 0.7. Predict the overshoot. Then predict the size of the derivative kick of a 1 m setpoint step if D were taken from the error, and the D jump per millimetre of sensor resolution at T = 10 ms and at T = 1 ms.

  1. Kd. c + Kd = 2ζ√(Kpm) = 2 × 0.7 × √16 = 5.6, so Kd = 5.6 − 0.8 = 4.8 N·s/m.
  2. Overshoot. exp(−π × 0.7/√(1 − 0.49)) = exp(−3.079) = 0.046: 4.6 %. R1 simulates 4.5 %.
  3. Kick. Kd × Δr/T = 4.8 × 1/0.01 = 480 N, far beyond the 10 N driver. R3 shows a D term of 480.00 N.
  4. Resolution. One millimetre per period: 4.8 × 0.001/0.01 = 0.48 N at 10 ms and 4.8 × 0.001/0.001 = 4.8 N at 1 ms. R4 shows D terms in multiples of 0.48 N at 10 ms and of 4.8 N at 1 ms.

The derivative gain that gives perfect damping in the equations also determines how hard every measurement jump hits the actuator. D1R1R3R4

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Amara judges her speed by eye from a continuous view of the road. The controller has only the samples of one sensor and estimates speed from differences of positions; with a coarse sensor and a short period, its "speed" is mostly jumps (forensic lab). R4
    • 

  • A shock absorber is a physical part that cannot react to a chalk line being redrawn. Derivative of the error can: it reacts to setpoint jumps as if the bicycle had moved, which no real damper would do (derivative kick). R3
    • 

  • Amara can brake early without limit. The controller's damping force is bounded by the actuator (±10 N here); a large D on a fast move can saturate the actuator, after which it no longer behaves like a damper. B1

Summary


    

  • Derivative action reacts to the rate of change: D = Kd·de/dt, or −Kd·dy/dt on the measurement.
    • 

  • On a cart, P is a spring and D a damper: ζ = (c + Kd)/(2√(Kpm)); with Kp = 8, Kd = 4.8 gives ζ = 0.7 and 4.5 % overshoot instead of 74 %.
    • 

  • Too much D over-damps and slows the response; first-order plants such as the speed loop rarely need D.
    • 

  • Derivative of the error kicks at setpoint steps (480 N asked in R3); derivative on measurement does not.
    • 

  • A difference quotient amplifies measurement steps by 1/T: one millimetre is 0.48 N of D at 10 ms and 4.8 N at 1 ms with Kd = 4.8. Filter the derivative.
    •
