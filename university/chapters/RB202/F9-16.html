Story hook

Analogy

Wei and Nia cycle to school on the same flat road. Wei has a rule: "I push the pedals the same way every morning, and that gives me my usual speed." Nia has a different rule: she glances at the little speedometer on her handlebar, and when the number drops she pushes harder; when it climbs she eases off.

On a calm morning both arrive at the same time. Then comes a windy day. Wei pushes the same way as always, the wind holds her back, and she arrives late. Nia sees her speed drop, pushes harder, and arrives almost on time. The next week Wei's tyres are a little flat, and she is late again. Nia's tyres are flat too, but her eyes on the speedometer fix that as well.

Wei is running an open loop: she decides once and never checks. Nia is running a closed loop: she checks, compares and corrects, again and again. This chapter shows, with a simulated motor and real numbers, why almost every machine that must hold a speed works like Nia, and what that costs.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the difference between open-loop and closed-loop control, with the bicycle analogy and with a block diagram.
  • Name the parts of a feedback loop: setpoint, error, controller, plant, actuator, sensor and disturbance.
  • Simulate a motor in C++ with a simple step-by-step model and run both kinds of controller against it.
  • Calculate the settled speed of open-loop and proportional closed-loop control with one line of algebra, and check it against the simulation.
  • Diagnose a loop that has silently become open loop, from a log.
Jargon box
Open-loop control
Simple words: deciding the command in advance and never checking what really happened. Analogy: Wei pushing the pedals "the usual way" whatever the wind does. Precise: control in which the command is computed from the setpoint (and perhaps a model) only; the measured output does not influence the command. B1
Closed-loop control
Simple words: measuring what happens and using it to choose the next command, again and again. Analogy: Nia watching her speedometer and pushing harder or softer. Precise: control in which a measurement of the output is fed back and compared with the setpoint, and the difference (the error) drives the command; also called feedback control. B1
Controller (control loop)
Simple words: the part of the program that decides the command. Analogy: the rider's brain. Precise: the rule, usually code run at a fixed rate, that maps the setpoint and (in closed loop) the measurement to an actuator command. Not the same as a hardware controller chip or a Kubernetes controller. B1
Plant
Simple words: the thing being controlled. Analogy: the legs and the bicycle together. Precise: the part of a control system whose output is to be regulated, including the actuator when it is not modelled separately; here, the motor driver plus the motor. B1
Disturbance
Simple words: anything from outside that pushes the output away from where you want it. Analogy: the wind, or a flat tyre. Precise: an input to the plant that the controller does not choose, such as a load torque or a change in supply voltage; good control keeps its effect on the output small. B1
Model (of a plant)
Simple words: a rule, written as maths or code, that predicts what the plant will do. Analogy: knowing "if I push like this, I go about this fast". Precise: an equation (here a step-by-step update) relating the plant's inputs to its output; never exact, and the gap between model and plant is called model mismatch. B1D1
Static gain (DC gain)
Simple words: how much output you finally get per unit of steady input. Analogy: how fast you end up going for a steady push. Precise: the ratio of settled output to a constant input in a stable system; for the pretend motor Kdc ≈ 98.04 rad/s per volt (run R1). R1U1
Time constant (τ)
Simple words: the "slowness" of a system: after one τ it has covered about 63 % of the way to its new value. Analogy: how long the bicycle takes to pick up speed after you push harder. Precise: for a first-order system, the time in which the output covers 1 − e−1 ≈ 63.2 % of a step change; the pretend motor's τ is 0.1961 s (run R1). U1R1
Control period
Simple words: the time between two runs of the controller. Analogy: how often Nia glances at the speedometer. Precise: the fixed interval T at which a digital controller reads its sensor and updates its command (the command is held constant in between); here T = 10 ms. MA301 calls the same interval the sample time Δt. B1D1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the picture behind this whole course. The top row is open loop: the controller computes a voltage from the setpoint, the plant turns the voltage into a speed, and nothing comes back. The bottom row is closed loop: a sensor measures the speed, a summing junction subtracts it from the setpoint to form the error, and the controller turns the error into the voltage. Both rows suffer the same disturbance; only one of them can notice it.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Some machines do a job by following a plan: a toaster heats for the time you set, whether the bread is thick or thin. Others keep watching and correcting: a person riding a bicycle feels the speed and the balance all the time and changes how hard they push. The first kind is open loop. The second kind is closed loop, and the "loop" is the path information takes: from the wheels, to the eyes, to the brain, to the legs, and back to the wheels.

Open loop is simple and needs no sensor, but it is only as good as your plan. If the wind blows or the tyres go soft, the plan is wrong and nobody notices. Closed loop needs a sensor and some care, but it fixes surprises by itself, because it reacts to what really happens instead of what was supposed to happen.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The parts of a loop

Every feedback loop in this course has the same parts, named in Figure 1. The setpoint r is the value you want (here a speed in rad/s). The plant is what you control: the motor driver and the motor. The actuator is the part of the plant that turns a command into physical action; for us it is the driver that applies a voltage V to the motor. The sensor measures the output y; here an encoder counts how far the shaft has turned, and the program turns counts into a speed. The controller is the code that chooses V. A disturbance is whatever else acts on the plant: a load on the shaft, a battery that sags. B1U1

In closed loop the controller works on the error e = r − y. The minus sign is the most important sign in control: it makes the loop push the output towards the setpoint. Get it backwards and the loop pushes away (MA301 shows such a loop running away in its F0-70 forensic lab). B1U1

A motor you can simulate

Note

Maths you need here. Only algebra from MA101 (F0-16 to F0-18). The model below is written as a rule that is applied again and again in small time steps. MA301 later shows that it is the step-by-step form of a differential equation; you do not need that yet.

This course uses one pretend motor everywhere, the one introduced in HW302 (F1-69) and reduced to a simple model in MA301 (F0-68). Its numbers are exercise values, not those of any real product. The model has one idea: at each moment the motor has a target speed it is heading for, and in every short time step dt it closes a fixed fraction dt/τ of the gap to that target: U1D1

speed_new = speed + (dt / τ) × (Kdc × V − Kload × Tload − speed)

Here V is the voltage applied, Tload is a braking torque on the shaft in N·m, and the three constants come from the motor's resistance, motor constant, inertia and friction (F1-69): τ = 0.1961 s, Kdc = 98.039 rad/s per volt, and Kload = 19,608 rad/s per N·m (printed by run R1). When the speed stops changing, the bracket must be zero, so the settled speed is D1R1

y = Kdc × V − Kload × Tload

That one line explains open-loop control. To get 300 rad/s with no brake, you apply V = 300 / 98.039 = 3.060 V. If a brake of 2 mN·m (0.002 N·m) appears, the speed settles 19,608 × 0.002 = 39.2 rad/s lower, at 260.8 rad/s, and the open-loop controller never knows. D1R1

Closing the loop

The simplest closed-loop rule is the one Lucía learned in F9-05: correct in proportion to how far off you are. For the motor, V = Kp × (r − y), with Kp = 0.2 V per rad/s in this chapter (F9-17 explains how to choose it). Put this rule into the settled-speed line and solve for y: D1

y = KdcKp(r − y) − KloadTload  ⇒  y = (L × r − KloadTload) / (1 + L),  with L = KpKdc

L is the loop gain: how much the plant's output changes, going once round the loop, per unit of error. With Kp = 0.2, L = 19.608. Two things follow from the formula. First, without a brake the speed settles at 300 × 19.608 / 20.608 = 285.4 rad/s, not 300: this proportional loop always leaves a small error (F9-17 studies it, F9-18 removes it). Second, the brake's effect is divided by 1 + L: the 39.2 rad/s drop becomes 39.2 / 20.608 = 1.9 rad/s. That division is the whole point of feedback. D1B1R2

A loop that runs at a fixed rate

A real controller is code. It wakes up every control period T, reads the sensor once, computes one command, sends it to the driver, and sleeps. The driver holds that command until the next wake-up. In this course T = 10 ms. The simulations reproduce this: the controller runs every 10 ms, while the motor model is advanced in 100 smaller steps of 0.1 ms in between, so that the "physics" is smooth and only the controller is sampled. B1D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Sensitivity to the plant. Suppose the motor is 10 % weaker than the model (Kdc 10 % lower). Open loop, the speed drops by the same 10 %, from 300 to 270 rad/s. Closed loop, L drops from 19.608 to 17.647 and the speed moves from 285.4 to 283.9 rad/s, about 0.5 %. In general, for small changes the relative change of the closed-loop output is the relative change of the plant gain divided by 1 + L; Åström and Murray develop this as the sensitivity of the loop. This is why feedback lets you build accurate systems from inaccurate parts. B1R1

What feedback costs. Feedback is not free. You need a sensor, and its errors enter the loop directly: if the encoder reads 5 % high, the loop holds the speed 5 % low, and no amount of gain fixes that. Sensor noise is passed to the actuator, multiplied by the gain. Too much gain, or too much delay round the loop, makes the loop oscillate or run away (F9-17). And if the sensor stops working, a closed loop can become an open loop without anyone noticing (this chapter's forensic lab). B1

Feedforward plus feedback. The two ideas combine well. A feedforward term computed from the model, Vff = r / Kdc, does most of the work when the model is right; a feedback term Kp(r − y) corrects what the model gets wrong. With an exact model this combination settles exactly on the setpoint; with mismatch, feedback shrinks the remaining error by 1 + L. The mini-project asks you to measure it. B1D1

Saturation. The driver cannot give more than its supply. The simulations clamp V to ±12 V (an exercise value). At the first control step, the closed-loop controller asks for 0.2 × 300 = 60 V and gets 12 V; while the command is clamped, the loop is effectively open. MA301 (F0-70) shows the effect on response time, and F9-18 shows the trouble it causes for integral action. U1R2

How the hardware actually does it

On a real robot the loop of Figure 1 is spread over a few pieces of hardware. A timer in the microcontroller fires every control period and starts the control code (timers are taught in HW204). The code reads the encoder's counter, which hardware increments as pulses arrive from the shaft (F1-66), and converts the change in count since the last period into a speed. It computes the command and writes it to a PWM (pulse-width modulation) unit, which switches the motor driver's transistors on and off fast enough that the motor sees an average voltage set by the duty cycle (F1-70). U1

Nothing in this chain is instant: the encoder count is a sample taken at one moment, the computation takes time, and the PWM unit applies the new duty at its next cycle. These small delays are one reason a real loop cannot use unlimited gain. The exact timer, counter and PWM features depend on the microcontroller and driver your kit uses; F9-22 tells you where to find them. U1B1

Worked example

Problem. The motor must turn at 300 rad/s. Today the battery is low and the driver delivers only 85 % of the voltage the controller asks for. Compare the settled speed with open-loop control (V = 3.060 V) and with closed-loop control (Kp = 0.2 V per rad/s).

  1. Open loop. The motor receives 0.85 × 3.060 = 2.601 V. Settled speed: 98.039 × 2.601 = 255.0 rad/s. Error 45.0 rad/s, or 15 %.
  2. Closed loop. The battery multiplies everything the controller sends by 0.85, so the loop gain becomes L = 0.85 × 0.2 × 98.039 = 16.667. Settled speed: 300 × 16.667 / 17.667 = 283.0 rad/s. Error 17.0 rad/s, or 5.7 %.
  3. Compare with the healthy battery. Closed loop gave 285.4 rad/s with a full battery, so the low battery cost it only 2.4 rad/s. Open loop lost 45 rad/s.
  4. Check by simulation. Listing 1's row "battery sag: driver gives 85 %" prints 255.0 and 283.0 (run R1).

Notice the honest conclusion: on a perfect day open loop was more accurate (300.0 against 285.4). Closed loop wins because perfect days do not happen, and its error hardly depends on what went wrong. D1R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nia sees the wind coming and can lean forward before her speed drops. The controller in this chapter only reacts to the measured speed after it has changed; using knowledge of a disturbance in advance is feedforward, and it needs a sensor or a model for the disturbance itself. B1
    • 

  • Nia glances at her speedometer whenever she likes and fills the gaps with her other senses. A digital loop sees the world only at its sample instants, every 10 ms here, and sees only what its sensor reports; between samples it is blind, and a broken sensor looks exactly like a perfect world (forensic lab). B1
    • 

  • A rider pushes as hard as it takes, within reason, and gets tired slowly. A motor driver has a hard limit (±12 V in the simulation); above it, the "closed" loop is open, whatever the code says. R2

Summary


    

  • Open-loop control computes the command from the setpoint and a model; it never looks at the output.
    • 

  • Closed-loop control measures the output, forms the error e = r − y and lets the error drive the command; the minus sign makes the feedback negative.
    • 

  • For the pretend motor the settled speed is y = KdcV − KloadTload; open loop inherits every disturbance and every model error in full.
    • 

  • With proportional feedback, y = (L r − KloadTload)/(1 + L): disturbances and mismatch are divided by 1 + L, at the price of a small steady error.
    • 

  • A digital controller runs once per control period and holds its command in between; the driver's limit can open the loop.
    • 

  • A stale sensor turns a closed loop silently into an open loop; check that readings are fresh.
    •
