Story hook

Analogy

Tomás still tows his trailer (F9-17), and with the "push harder the slower you are" rule he still rides a little below his speed. One morning he notices something about himself: after a minute of being slightly slow, he starts pushing a little more, and a little more, until the speedometer finally shows the right number. Then he keeps that extra push, without thinking, for as long as the trailer is behind him.

That growing extra push is a memory of how long he has been off, and by how much. It fixes the leftover error that the "how far off now" rule never could.

But memory can mislead. On a steep hill Tomás pushes as hard as his legs allow and is still slow for a long time. His memory keeps saying "more, more". At the top the road flattens, he is still pushing with everything his memory asked for, and he shoots past his speed before he can calm down. This chapter gives the controller the same memory, measures how well it works, and then shows, from a real log, how it goes wrong in exactly this way.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why integral action removes the steady-state error of a constant setpoint and a constant load.
  • Implement a PI controller in C++ with a running sum of the error, and state the units of Ki.
  • Choose a starting integral gain and recognise, from a step response, an integral gain that is too low or too high.
  • Diagnose integrator windup from a log in which the output saturates and the integral keeps growing, and explain the overshoot quantitatively.
  • Name the standard protections against windup that F9-21 implements.
Jargon box
Integral action (I term)
Simple words: a part of the command that grows for as long as there is an error, and stays when the error is gone. Analogy: Tomás's extra push that builds up while he is slow. Precise: the term Ki·S of the controller output, where S is the integral of the error over time; in discrete form S[k] = S[k−1] + e[k]·T. The I of PID. B1D1
Integral gain (Ki)
Simple words: how fast the extra push grows for each unit of error. Analogy: how quickly Tomás's patience runs out. Precise: the constant Ki multiplying the integral of the error; units of command per (error × time), here V per (rad/s · s) = V per rad. Some texts write Ki = Kp/Ti with an integral time Ti. B1
Running sum (discrete integrator)
Simple words: a total to which you add "value × T" at every sample. Analogy: a tally of "how slow, for how long". Precise: S[k] = S[k−1] + x[k]·T, the rectangle rule updated one sample at a time; MA301 (F0-65, F0-66) relates it to the integral. U1D1
PI controller
Simple words: a controller that adds "how far off now" and "how long and how far off". Analogy: Tomás's two habits together. Precise: u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt, clamped to the actuator's range; a PID controller with Kd = 0. B1
Integrator windup
Simple words: the integral keeps growing while the actuator is already at its limit, and later causes a big overshoot. Analogy: Tomás's "more, more" on the hill that makes him shoot past his speed at the top. Precise: the accumulation of the integral state while the actuator is saturated, so that the controller's output stays saturated after the error changes sign, until the excess integral has been removed by error of the opposite sign. B1R3
Anti-windup
Simple words: any trick that stops the integral from growing while the actuator is at its limit. Analogy: Tomás telling himself "I am already pushing as hard as I can, stop asking for more". Precise: a modification of the integral update during saturation, for example conditional integration (freeze it) or back-calculation (bleed it towards the value the limit allows); implemented in F9-21. B1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The proportional rule looks only at now: how far off am I at this moment? It forgets everything else. Integral action adds a memory: it keeps a tally of how far off you have been, and for how long. As long as you are even a little off, the tally grows, and the push grows with it. When you finally reach the goal, the tally stops growing and the push stays where it is: exactly the push needed to stay there.

That is why the memory removes the leftover error. Its danger is that it does not know when pushing more is useless. If the motor is already at full power and still slow, the tally keeps growing anyway. When the motor finally gets there, the huge tally keeps pushing, and the motor overshoots. That is windup.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The running sum

Every control period the PI controller does three things: B1D1

e = r − y;   S = S + e·T;   V = clamp(Kp·e + Ki·S, −12 V, +12 V)

S has units of error × time: rad/s × s = rad. So Ki, which turns S into volts, has units of volts per rad. In Listing 1, Kp = 0.05 V per rad/s and Ki = 0.25 V per rad: one second of a 1 rad/s error adds 0.25 V to the command.

Why the steady-state error disappears

Assume the loop settles: speed, error and command all stop changing. Then S stops changing too. But S changes by e·T every period, so S can only be constant if e = 0. A settled PI loop therefore has zero error, whatever the load, the battery or the motor's exact gain, as long as the setpoint and the load are constant and the actuator is not at its limit. B1D1

With e = 0 the P term is zero, so the I term alone must supply the whole voltage the motor needs. From F9-16, that voltage is (r + KloadTload)/Kdc: 300/98.039 = 3.060 V without a brake and 339.2/98.039 = 3.460 V with a 2 mN·m brake. Listing 1 prints an I term of 3.060 V before the brake and 3.460 V after it, with the P term at 0.000 (run R1). The integral has "learned" the load. D1R1

Figure 2 shows the result. The P-only loop with Kp = 0.05 settles at 249.2 rad/s (F9-17's formula) and the brake pushes it down to 242.5. The PI loop reaches 300 rad/s, is pulled down by the brake (297.2 rad/s at the 1.75 s sample), and climbs back to 300.0 within about a second, while its I term rises from 3.06 V to 3.46 V. R1

Choosing Ki

Listing 2 keeps Kp = 0.05 and tries five integral gains on a small step (0 to 100 rad/s), small enough that the command never reaches 12 V (largest command 8.00 V; run R2). R2

  • Ki = 0: the P-only error of 16.9 rad/s remains; the response never gets within 2 % (the program prints the run length, 3.00 s).
  • Ki = 0.1: no overshoot, but the last part of the error is removed slowly: 1.03 s to settle within 2 %.
  • Ki = 0.25: no overshoot and settled within 2 % at 0.14 s.
  • Ki = 1.0 and 3.0: faster at first, but 16.9 % and 37.6 % overshoot.

Too little integral is slow; too much makes the loop overshoot and ring, because the integral keeps growing while the speed is still rising, and must then be unwound. A useful starting point for a first-order plant like this motor is Ki ≈ Kp/τ, here 0.05/0.196 = 0.255 V per rad: the integral then builds up at the pace at which the motor itself responds. Åström and Murray discuss this kind of rule; MA301 (F0-70) and RB402 later give the reasoning in terms of poles and zeros. Treat it as a starting point, then test (F9-20). B1U1R2

Windup

The argument "S constant means e = 0" assumed the loop can settle. When the actuator is saturated it cannot follow the controller, and the argument breaks. Figure 3 shows the same PI gains on a large step, 0 to 900 rad/s. At the start the controller asks for 47 V and gets 12 V. For 0.45 s the applied voltage sits at 12 V, but the integral does not know: it adds up the large error every period, and the I term climbs from 2.25 V to 25.55 V. The motor needs only 9.18 V to hold 900 rad/s. When the speed passes 900 rad/s (at about 0.28 s), the I term is so large that P + I still asks for more than 12 V, and the motor keeps accelerating to 1078.6 rad/s, 19.8 % too fast (run R3). R3B1

The same gains on a 100 rad/s step, which never saturates, do not overshoot at all (the first part of R3). The gains are not the problem; the saturation is. R3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The unwinding budget. The I term can only shrink through negative error, that is, through overshoot. In R3 the I term must fall from 25.55 V (at 0.30 s) to 9.18 V. Since I = Ki·S, the running sum must lose (25.55 − 9.18)/0.25 = 65.5 rad: the speed must spend 65.5 rad of "error × time" above the setpoint. That area is the overshoot of Figure 3. This is a general result: without protection, every unit of integral collected during saturation must be paid back with overshoot. D1R3

Protections. Åström and Murray describe several anti-windup schemes; F9-21 implements the first: B1

  • Conditional integration (clamping): do not add to S when the output is saturated and the error would push it further into saturation.
  • Back-calculation: feed the difference between the asked and the applied command back into the integrator, so the integral is pulled towards the value that the limit allows. It has a tuning constant of its own.
  • Limits on the integral itself: keep the I term within the actuator range. Simple, and a useful backstop for the other two.
  • Avoid saturation: ramp the setpoint (F9-22) so the error, and therefore the command, stays small.

Windup without a big step. Saturation is not the only way to stop the plant from following. A motor that is jammed, unpowered by an emergency stop, or disconnected also leaves the integral collecting error. When power returns, the controller applies its full wound-up command at once. Real controllers therefore reset or freeze the integral whenever the actuator is disabled (F9-21 provides reset(), F9-22 uses it). B1U1

Ramp and sinusoid setpoints. The zero-error argument covers constant setpoints and constant loads. If the setpoint is a ramp (speed increasing steadily), a PI loop on this motor follows it with a constant lag; following ramps without error needs more integrators or feedforward. Åström and Murray treat this as the "type" of a loop. B1

How the hardware actually does it

On a microcontroller the running sum is one variable that lives as long as the controller does. Its type matters. In floating point (MA302) a sum that grows very large loses precision for small additions; in fixed-point integer arithmetic it can overflow and wrap around to a large negative value, which turns a full-forward command into full reverse (MA102 wrap-around, SP201 signed overflow). Both are reasons to keep the integral bounded, independently of the anti-windup logic. U1

The saturation itself is physical: the driver cannot give more than the battery voltage (simulated here as ±12 V) and the PWM duty cycle cannot exceed 100 %. Some drivers also limit current. Any such limit, wherever it sits in the hardware, opens the loop while it is active, so the controller must know the real limit to protect its integral. The limits of your kit come from its driver's documentation (F9-22). U1

Worked example

Problem. The PI loop of Listing 1 is running at 300 rad/s with no brake. A brake of 2 mN·m appears. (a) Where does the I term settle? (b) How much error × time does the integral need to collect to get there? (c) Check against R1.

  1. (a) In the end e = 0, so the I term supplies all the voltage: (300 + 19,608 × 0.002)/98.039 = 339.2/98.039 = 3.460 V.
  2. (b) The I term must rise by 3.460 − 3.060 = 0.400 V. With Ki = 0.25 V per rad, S must grow by 0.400/0.25 = 1.6 rad. That is the area between the setpoint and the speed curve during the dip.
  3. (c) R1 shows the I term at 3.060 V before 1.5 s and 3.460 V from 2.75 s; at 1.75 s it is 3.321 V while the speed is 297.2 rad/s, and the P term (0.138 V) adds the rest of the 3.458 V command. R1
  4. Size of the dip. If the 1.6 rad were collected at a constant error of about 2.8 rad/s (the dip at 1.75 s), it would take about 0.57 s; the real error shrinks as the loop recovers, so the recovery takes longer, about a second in R1.

The same bookkeeping, "the integral must change by ΔI/Ki of error × time", explains both the recovery from a load and the overshoot of windup. D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Tomás's memory fades: he forgets the hill after a while. The controller's running sum never forgets anything; an old error stays in S until an opposite error cancels it. That is exactly why windup lasts so long. D1
    • 

  • Tomás can feel that his legs are at their limit and stop asking for more. A plain PI controller cannot see the limit of the driver; anti-windup gives it that knowledge explicitly, and only if the programmer writes the real limit into the code (F9-21). B1
    • 

  • A rider adapts the strength of his memory to the situation. Ki is fixed: the same Ki that is ideal for small steps causes windup in large ones (R2 against R3). R2R3

Summary


    

  • Integral action adds Ki·S, with the running sum S = S + e·T; Ki has units of command per (error × time).
    • 

  • A settled PI loop has zero error for constant setpoints and loads: S can only stop changing when e = 0, and the I term then holds the whole command the plant needs.
    • 

  • Too little Ki is slow; too much overshoots and rings. Ki ≈ Kp/τ is a starting point for a first-order plant.
    • 

  • When the actuator saturates, the integral keeps growing (windup); the excess must be paid back as overshoot, ΔI/Ki of error × time.
    • 

  • Protections: conditional integration, back-calculation, bounding the integral, setpoint ramps, and resetting the integral whenever the output is disabled.
    •

Root cause: integrator windup during saturation, with no anti-windup in the controller (windup_log.cpp line 29 adds to the integral every period regardless of the clamp on line 33).

How the evidence was produced: windup_log.cpp runs a PI loop (Kp = 0.05, Ki = 0.25, T = 10 ms, ±12 V clamp, no anti-windup) on the pretend motor, first for a 100 rad/s step (0.4 s logged), then for a 900 rad/s step (1.5 s logged), one row every 50 ms (run R3).
