Story hook

Analogy

Keanu has practised on the playground for weeks. Today he rides on a real street for the first time, and his mother has a plan. First she checks the brakes with him standing still. Then he rides ten metres at walking pace while she walks beside him, one hand near the saddle. Only when that goes well does he ride to the corner and back. Before each step they agree on what would make them stop.

The street is not the playground. The road is bumpier, the bicycle pulls a little to one side, and a parked car hides the view. None of that was in the practice. What carries over is the skill; what has to be checked again is everything else.

This chapter takes the speed loop from simulation to a motor on a bench, the same way. It defines the boundary between program and hardware, measures the real motor before controlling it, builds safety into both the program and the wiring, and compares the bench log with the simulation. This build had no motor kit: every listing below ran against a simulated kit, and every step that needs real hardware is marked untested.

What you will be able to do

  • Separate control code from hardware access with a small interface, so the same program runs against a simulator and a kit.
  • Measure a motor's gain and time constant with an open-loop step test, and derive PI gains from them.
  • Build layered safety: a hardware emergency stop, and in software a sign test, a speed limit, a setpoint ramp and a stall watchdog.
  • Run a supervised bench session that brings a speed loop up one step at a time.
  • Explain differences between simulated and real logs, and diagnose a counter-wrap fault from a log.
Jargon box
Hardware interface (control code)
Simple words: a short list of functions through which the control program talks to the hardware, and nothing else. Analogy: the handlebars, pedals and brakes: the rider never touches the chain directly. Precise: an abstract class (here MotorIo: read the encoder counter, set the duty cycle) with one implementation per back end, such as a simulator or a particular kit. D1
System identification
Simple words: working out a machine's numbers by testing it, not by reading them off a label. Analogy: finding out how a new bicycle accelerates by pedalling it once with a fixed effort. Precise: estimating the parameters of a model (here the static gain and time constant of a first-order model) from measured input and output data. B1D1
Deadband
Simple words: a range of small commands that produce no motion at all. Analogy: pushing the pedal so gently that the bicycle does not move. Precise: a dead zone in an actuator's input–output curve, often caused by static friction; here an exercise value of 0.5 V below which the simulated motor does not turn. D1B1
Sign test
Simple words: a gentle check, before closing the loop, that "forward" on the motor really reads as "forward" on the sensor. Analogy: rolling the bicycle a little to check the speedometer counts up, not down. Precise: a short open-loop drive with a small positive command, accepted only if the measured speed is clearly positive; a sign error would turn negative feedback into positive feedback. D1R3
Stall watchdog
Simple words: a check that stops the motor when it is being pushed hard but is not moving. Analogy: stopping pedalling when the wheel is stuck in a drain grate. Precise: software that sets the output to zero when the command magnitude stays above a threshold while the measured speed stays near zero for longer than a set time (here duty above 0.5 and speed below 5 rad/s for 0.3 s). D1R3
Bench test
Simple words: testing a machine fixed in place on a table, with protection, before letting it do real work. Analogy: checking the brakes while standing still. Precise: a supervised test with the mechanism clamped, the load removed or controlled, limits set low and an emergency stop within reach. U1
Sim-to-real gap
Simple words: the differences between how something behaves in the simulator and on the real machine. Analogy: the street being bumpier than the playground. Precise: the effect of everything the model leaves out or gets wrong (friction, deadband, noise, delays, supply sag, wiring); BR-08 treats it in depth. U1
Safety

Before any bench session. (1) Simulator first: run this chapter's lab folder and pass it before touching hardware. (2) Adult supervision for the whole session: this is an L2 course. (3) A hardware emergency stop that cuts motor power, within the supervisor's reach, tested before the first run; the program's stop logic is extra, never a replacement. (4) Motor clamped to the bench, nothing attached to the shaft except what the lab plan names, fingers, hair and cables away from moving parts. (5) Low voltage only: the kit's own supply as its maker documents it; no LiPo batteries in this lab, no mains. (6) Limits first: the speed limit and the output limit are set in the program before the first run and are not raised during the session. (7) One change at a time, and stop at once on any unexpected sound, smell, heat or motion.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The robot's program does not know whether it is talking to a pretend motor in the computer or a real one on the table. It only knows two buttons: "tell me how far the wheel has turned" and "push this hard". That makes it possible to test everything on the pretend motor first.

A real motor is a little different from the pretend one, so first we measure it: push gently for one second, watch how fast it gets and how quickly. From that, we work out the controller's numbers. Then we turn it on carefully, with a grown-up and the big red stop button, starting slow, with the program checking its own work: is the wheel turning the right way? Is it trying hard but stuck? If anything is wrong, it stops.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The boundary: MotorIo

Listing 1 defines MotorIo with two operations the control program needs: readEncoder(), which returns a 16-bit free-running counter, and setDuty(d), which sets the PWM duty cycle between −1 and +1 (full reverse to full forward). A third, advance, lets time pass in the simulator; on a kit, time passes by itself and the loop waits for its timer instead. D1

SimulatedKit implements the interface with the pretend motor of F9-16 behind a 12 V supply, plus three things the earlier chapters left out: a deadband (0.5 V of friction), an encoder with 1024 counts per revolution whose counter wraps after 65535, and a counter that does not start at zero. It can also be set to have its wires swapped or its shaft jammed, to test the safety logic. All are exercise values. For a real kit you write KitMotorIo from the kit's documents; nothing else in the program changes. D1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. No motor kit, driver or microcontroller was available in this build. KitMotorIo is not written here, because the functions that configure a PWM output, read an encoder counter and run a periodic timer differ between microcontrollers and their libraries, and this chapter does not quote any of them from memory. Write it from the kit's own documentation: the microcontroller's reference manual (timer and encoder interface sections), the motor driver's datasheet (input logic, allowed PWM frequency, current limits, behaviour on fault) and the encoder's datasheet (counts per revolution, signal levels). Record the document titles and sections in your lab notes; the course owner records the chosen kit in the RB202 dossier.

The speed comes from two counter readings one period apart: speedFromCounts subtracts them as 16-bit unsigned numbers, so a wrap from 65535 to 0 between readings gives the right difference, as long as the shaft turns less than half the counter range (32768 counts) per period. At 1024 counts per revolution and 10 ms, that is up to about 20,000 rad/s, far above this motor's speed. D1U1

Measure before you control

The gains of F9-18 came from a model with known R, K, J and b. A kit comes with a label, not a model, and labels do not mention friction. So the first powered step is an open-loop step test: apply a fixed, modest duty cycle and log the speed. Listing 2 applies duty 0.25 (3 V) and logs the speed every 10 ms for one second (run R1, Figure 2). R1

Listing 3 reads that log and fits the first-order model of F9-16. The final speed is the mean of the last 20 samples, 242.5 rad/s, so the static gain is 242.5/3 = 80.8 rad/s per volt. The time constant is the time at which the speed first reaches 63.2 % of the final value (153.3 rad/s): the sample at 0.20 s, minus half a period because a speed from counts is the average over the previous period, gives τ = 0.195 s (run R2). R2D1

Compare with the model behind the simulated kit: τ = 0.1961 s, and 98.04 rad/s per volt above the deadband. The time constant was recovered well. The gain was not: 80.8 is the gain of the 3 V operating point including the deadband, (3 − 0.5)/3 × 98.04 = 81.7, a little lower again because the speed was still rising slightly at the end. A step at a different duty would give a different "gain". That is the deadband showing through, and it is why the integral term matters on real hardware. D1U1

From the model to PI gains

Listing 3 uses the rule of F9-18: Ki = Kp/τ, with Kp chosen so that the closed loop responds with a time constant of 0.05 s, about four times faster than the motor alone: Kp = τ/(K·Tcl) = 0.195/(80.8 × 0.05) = 0.0482 V per rad/s and Ki = 0.0482/0.195 = 0.2474 V per rad (run R2). Layer 3 shows where the formula comes from. R2D1

The supervised loop program

Listing 4 wraps the PID library of F9-21, unchanged, in four software safety layers:

  1. Sign test before the loop is closed: duty +0.10 for 0.2 s must give a speed above +5 rad/s. If the wires are swapped, a positive command reads as negative speed, and closing the loop would turn negative feedback into positive feedback: the controller would push harder and harder the wrong way.
  2. Speed limit: the requested setpoint is capped at 400 rad/s, a value fixed in the lab plan before the session.
  3. Setpoint ramp: the setpoint rises at most 600 rad/s per second, so the motor is never asked for a sudden jump and the integral has little to wind up.
  4. Stall watchdog: if the duty stays above 0.5 while the measured speed stays below 5 rad/s for 0.3 s, the program sets the duty to 0 and stops.

Run R3 exercises all of it on the simulated kit. Run A (correct wiring): the sign test reads +25.6 rad/s, the setpoint ramps to 300 rad/s in 0.5 s, the measured speed follows without overshoot and holds 300.0 rad/s with about 3.55 V (Figure 3). Run B (wires swapped): the sign test reads −25.6 rad/s and the program stops without closing the loop (exit value 1). Run C (shaft jammed 0.8 s into the run): the controller drives to full duty with zero speed, and the watchdog stops it at t = 1.10 s (exit value 2). R3

None of these software layers replaces the hardware emergency stop. If the program hangs, crashes or is being reflashed, only a switch in the power path stops the motor (Figure 1).

The bench session, step by step

On the real kit, the same programs run in the same order, each step only after the previous one passed, with the supervisor holding the stop: (1) e-stop test with the motor powered but commanded to zero; (2) encoder check by turning the shaft by hand with power off; (3) sign test; (4) step test at duty 0.25, then identification; (5) closed loop at a low setpoint (for example 100 rad/s), then 300 rad/s; (6) a gentle load, if the lab plan includes one. Log every run. The lab section lists what to compare. U1D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Where Kp = τ/(K·Tcl) comes from. Write the motor as τ·dω/dt = K·V − ω and the PI controller as V = Kpe + Ki∫e dt with Ki = Kp/τ. Then V = Kp(e + (1/τ)∫e dt). Differentiating the motor equation and substituting shows that the closed loop obeys Tcl·dω/dt = r − ω for a constant setpoint r, with Tcl = τ/(K·Kp): the PI zero cancels the motor's lag, and what is left is a first-order loop with the time constant you chose. The cancellation is only as good as the τ you measured; MA301 later shows the same result with transfer functions. B1 discusses this kind of design. D1B1

Note

Maths you need here. The step above uses differentiation (MA301). If you have not met it yet, keep the result: with Ki = Kp/τ, the closed loop behaves like a first-order system with time constant τ/(K·Kp). Run A checks it: during the ramp the measured speed trails the setpoint by 26 to 32 rad/s in the printed rows, close to the 600 × 0.05 = 30 rad/s a 0.05 s first-order loop would lag a 600 rad/s² ramp. The match is not exact because the deadband makes the real gain above 0.5 V higher than the identified 80.8, and because each printed setpoint has already moved one step ahead of the averaged speed.

Why choose Tcl = 0.05 s and not faster? A faster loop needs larger gains, which ask for more voltage on every change, saturate the driver more often, and amplify encoder quantisation (each count is 0.61 rad/s at 10 ms here). It also relies more on the model being right. Four times faster than the open-loop motor is a cautious first choice for a bench session; it can be raised once logs show margin. D1B1

Sim-to-real. The simulated kit already contains three effects the earlier chapters' motor did not (deadband, quantised counts, a wrapping counter). A real kit adds more: supply voltage that sags under load, PWM switching ripple, electrical noise on the encoder lines, gear backlash, temperature drift and timing jitter. BR-08 treats this gap systematically; this chapter is the course's first contact with it, so the rule is modest: identify the real motor, start from gains derived from that, and compare the logs. U1

How the hardware actually does it

The duty cycle becomes a PWM signal from a microcontroller timer (HW204, HW303); a motor driver (an H-bridge, HW302 F1-70) switches the supply across the motor at that duty, and the motor's inductance smooths the current. An incremental encoder (F1-66) produces two square waves; a timer in encoder mode, or an interrupt routine, counts their edges up or down into a counter that wraps. The control task runs on a periodic timer, reads the counter, computes the speed, calls update, and writes the new duty. U1

The driver's own protections (current limit, thermal shutdown, under-voltage lockout) and how it reports them are specific to the part; read them in its datasheet before the session. A driver that shuts down on over-current looks, in the speed log, exactly like a jammed shaft: high duty, no motion. The stall watchdog stops both. U1R3

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. The bench procedure of this chapter (steps 1–6 of Layer 2) and the expected behaviour of a real kit have not been observed in this build; only the simulated-kit runs R1–R4 have. The PWM frequency, the driver's protections, the encoder's counts per revolution, the supply voltage and the counter width of your kit must be taken from its documents, and the constants in Listings 1 and 4 (12 V, 1024 counts, 16-bit counter, 0.5 V deadband, 400 rad/s limit, watchdog thresholds) replaced by values your supervisor approves for that kit.

Worked example

Problem. On a bench, a step test at duty 0.30 on a 9 V supply gives a final speed of 180 rad/s and reaches 63.2 % of it at the sample marked 0.13 s (period 10 ms, speeds from counts). Find K, τ and the PI gains for Tcl = 0.05 s. Then check the counter: with 2048 counts per revolution, a 16-bit counter and 10 ms, how fast may the shaft turn before speedFromCounts gives wrong answers? How often does the counter wrap at 180 rad/s?

  1. K = 180/(0.30 × 9) = 180/2.7 = 66.7 rad/s per volt (at this operating point, deadband included).
  2. τ = 0.13 − 0.005 = 0.125 s.
  3. Kp = 0.125/(66.7 × 0.05) = 0.0375 V per rad/s; Ki = 0.0375/0.125 = 0.30 V per rad.
  4. Counter limit: fewer than 32768 counts per period: 32768/2048 = 16 revolutions per 10 ms, 1600 rev/s, about 10,000 rad/s.
  5. Wrap period: 180 rad/s is 180/(2π) × 2048 = 58,670 counts per second; 65536/58,670 = 1.12 s between wraps.

The same arithmetic, done before the session, tells you what the bench logs should show. D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Keanu feels the street change under him and adapts at once. The controller's gains stay what they were set to; if the real motor differs from the identified model, nothing adapts unless someone measures and retunes. D1
    • 

  • Keanu's mother can grab the saddle. A program cannot grab anything: the only reliable "hand on the saddle" is the hardware emergency stop in the power path. U1
    • 

  • A wrongly assembled bicycle usually feels wrong at once. A swapped wire in a control loop can look fine until the loop is closed, and then it runs away; that is why the sign test comes first. R3

Expected observations


Step 2: τ stays near 0.195 s, but the gain rises towards 98 rad/s per volt because the deadband is a smaller fraction of 6 V than of 3 V: (6 − 0.5)/6 × 98.04 ≈ 89.9, a little less because the speed is still rising at the end. Verification


Part A: all four runs behave as predicted and every program exits through a path that sets the duty to 0. Part B: the bench speed loop holds the setpoint within a band you state in advance, and you can explain each difference from simulation from your logs.


Troubleshooting


    

  • Sign test fails on the bench → power off; swap the motor leads or the encoder channels (one, not both), or negate in code at one place only; repeat.
    • 

  • Speed jumps between two values at standstill → encoder noise or a loose connection; power off and check wiring before continuing.
    • 

  • Motor whines but does not turn at small duties → deadband; the PI loop will push through it, more slowly.
    • 

  • Speed oscillates on the bench although the simulation was calm → gains too high for the real plant (the identification was off, or there is delay); halve Kp and Ki and identify again.

Summary


    

  • A small hardware interface (MotorIo) lets the same control program run against a simulator and a kit.
    • 

  • Measure before you control: a step test gave τ = 0.195 s and 80.8 rad/s per volt; the gain includes the deadband and depends on the operating point.
    • 

  • PI with Ki = Kp/τ and Kp = τ/(K·Tcl) gives a first-order closed loop; run A held 300.0 rad/s without overshoot.
    • 

  • Safety in layers: a hardware stop in the power path, and in software a sign test, a speed limit, a ramp and a stall watchdog (runs B and C).
    • 

  • Counter differences must be computed in the counter's own unsigned width; otherwise every wrap is a kick (R4). The bench steps of this chapter are untested on hardware in this build.
