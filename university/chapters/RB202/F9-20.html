Story hook

Analogy

Keanu is setting up a bicycle for a hill race. His coach hands him a card: "Reach the top of the first ramp in under a minute, never brake so hard the back wheel skids, and be able to stop on the marked line." Keanu does not change the saddle, the gears and the brakes all at once. He changes one thing, rides the practice ramp, writes the times down, compares them with the card, and only then changes the next thing.

After a dozen practice rides one setup meets every line on the card. Only then does he take it to the real hill, slowly, with his coach watching.

Tuning a PID controller works the same way. The card is a written specification. The practice ramp is a simulation. Each ride is an experiment that changes one gain for a reason you can say out loud. This chapter runs that process on a simulated cart, first by hand and then with a program that tries 2550 settings, and shows how a tuning report can lie.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a step-response specification with measurable requirements: overshoot, settling time, final error and actuator effort.
  • Measure those quantities from a simulated response in C++, with definitions you can defend.
  • Tune P, I and D by hand, one change at a time, explaining each change with the ideas of F9-17 to F9-19.
  • Run a gain sweep that tests every combination on a grid and ranks the passing ones.
  • Judge a tuning result: its margin against the specification, and how far it can be trusted on a real machine.
Jargon box
Tuning
Simple words: choosing the controller's gains so the system behaves the way you want. Analogy: Keanu adjusting his bicycle between practice rides. Precise: selecting Kp, Ki, Kd (and filter and limit settings) so that the closed loop meets a stated specification, by experiment, by rule or by optimisation. B1
Specification (control)
Simple words: a written list of measurable things the controlled system must do. Analogy: the coach's card. Precise: requirements such as maximum overshoot, maximum settling time, maximum steady-state error and maximum actuator effort for a defined test (here a 1 m step), each with a number and a pass/fail rule. B1D1
Rise time
Simple words: how long the output takes to get most of the way to the new target. Analogy: the time from leaving the start line to nearly reaching the top of the ramp. Precise: here the time from 10 % to 90 % of the step; other definitions exist, so a specification must state which one it uses. B1U1
Overshoot
Simple words: how far the output goes past the target, as a percentage of the step. Analogy: rolling past the stop line. Precise: 100·(peak − final value)/(step size); here the final value is the setpoint. U1
Settling time
Simple words: how long until the output stays close to its final value for good. Analogy: the moment Keanu stands still on the line and stays there. Precise: the last time the response is outside a band (here ±2 %) around its final value. U1
IAE (integral of absolute error)
Simple words: one number that adds up how wrong the output was, and for how long. Analogy: the total distance-times-seconds Keanu spent away from the line. Precise: IAE = ∫|e(t)| dt, computed here as the sum of |e[k]|·T; units m·s. Smaller is better; a common cost for comparing tunings. B1D1
Gain sweep
Simple words: trying every combination of gains on a grid and keeping the ones that pass. Analogy: riding the practice ramp with every saddle height and every gear, and keeping the setups that meet the card. Precise: an exhaustive search over a finite set of gain vectors, each evaluated by simulation against the specification and ranked by a cost such as IAE. D1

The idea in pictures

The test plant of this chapter is the 2 kg cart of F9-19 (friction 0.8 N·s/m, force limited to ±10 N), now standing on a slight slope that pushes it back with a constant 1.5 N (an exercise value). It must move 1 m forward. The written specification has four requirements: D1

  • S1 overshoot at most 5 %;
  • S2 settling time (±2 % band) at most 3.0 s;
  • S3 final error at most 2 mm, measured as the largest error during the last 2 s of a 10 s run;
  • S4 the controller never asks for more than 10 N, so the force limit is never reached.

Figure 1 draws S1 and S2 on a response that fails both. Figure 2 shows the tuning loop. Figure 3 compares three of the experiments of the hand-tuning session.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A controller has three knobs: P (how hard to push for each bit of distance still to go), I (how hard to push for having been off for a long time) and D (how hard to brake for moving fast). Turning a knob changes how the robot moves, and each knob has side effects. More P makes it quicker but swingier. More I removes the last bit of error but can overshoot. More D calms the swinging but makes it slower and jumpier.

Tuning means writing down what "good" means first, then trying settings one at a time on a pretend robot inside the computer, and checking each try against what you wrote. The pretend robot cannot break and cannot hurt anyone, so you can try a hundred settings in a second. When one setting passes every check, you try it on the real robot, gently, with an adult and the stop button ready.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Write the specification first

Without a specification, tuning has no end: every response could be "a bit better". A useful requirement names a quantity, how it is measured, a limit and the test that produces it. S1 to S4 all refer to the same test (a 1 m step from rest, 10 s long). S4 is there for a reason from F9-18: if the controller never asks for more than the motor can give, the actuator never saturates, the loop stays linear, and the integral cannot wind up. D1U1

The definitions are part of the specification. Listing 1 (the test bench) measures settling time as the last time the error is larger than 2 cm, not the first time it gets smaller than 2 cm. The forensic lab shows what happens when a tool uses the other definition.

A tuning session by hand

Listing 2 runs seven experiments; every number below comes from its run (R1). Each experiment changes one thing for a reason: R1

  1. P only, Kp = 8 N/m. As in F9-19 the cart swings (41.4 % overshoot), and it never settles within 10 s.
  2. Add D, Kd = 4.8 N·s/m, the value F9-19 chose for ζ = 0.7. The swing is gone, but the cart stops short: the error during the last 2 s is 187.5 mm and it never reaches 90 % of the step, so the rise time is reported as −1. At rest the P force must hold the cart against the slope: Kp·e = 1.5 N, so e = 1.5/8 = 0.1875 m. This is the steady-state error of F9-17 and F9-18 in a new plant. D1
  3. Add I for the slope, Ki = 4. The final error drops to 1.3 mm, but overshoot jumps to 21.0 % (Figure 1): the integral keeps growing while the cart is still short, and must be unwound by overshooting (F9-18).
  4. Halve I, Ki = 2: 5.1 % overshoot and 3.38 s settling. Close, but S1 and S2 both fail.
  5. More D, Kd = 6: overshoot falls to 4.3 %, but settling grows to 5.77 s and the final error to 9.3 mm. The extra damping slows the approach, so the cart creeps the last centimetres while the integral builds up slowly.
  6. A little less I, Ki = 1.5 with Kd = 6: no overshoot, settles in 2.57 s, final error 1.5 mm. All four pass.
  7. Between 4 and 6, Ki = 1.75, Kd = 5.5: 1.9 % overshoot, 2.05 s settling, final error 1.5 mm. Also passes, and settles faster.

Experiment 5 is the instructive failure. More D was the natural cure for overshoot, and it did reduce the overshoot, but it broke two other requirements. One change can fix one requirement and break another; that is why each experiment is checked against the whole specification.

A gain sweep

The hand session found two passing sets in seven experiments. Listing 3 lets the computer try every combination on a grid: Kp from 5 to 10 in steps of 1, Ki from 0 to 4 in steps of 0.25, Kd from 0 to 12 in steps of 0.5. That is 6 × 17 × 25 = 2550 sets. Only 5 meet the specification (run R2). The best by IAE is Kp = 9, Ki = 1.75, Kd = 6, with IAE 0.872 m·s; the hand-tuned experiment 7 is second with 0.940 m·s, and experiment 6 is third. R2

The sweep is a good check of a hand tuning, and it shows how narrow the passing region is: 5 out of 2550, about 0.2 %. It does not replace understanding. It only finds what lies on its grid, and it ranks by one number that you chose. R2D1

Choosing among passing sets

The top-ranked set asks for up to 9.02 N, very close to the 10 N of S4. Experiment 7 asks for 8.02 N. A set that passes with no margin may fail on the real machine, where mass, friction and slope differ from the model. A sensible engineer often prefers a set with a little worse IAE and more margin on every requirement. The choice is a decision, written down with its reason. R1R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The integral's bill. At rest the integral must supply the whole slope force: Ki·∫e dt = 1.5 N, so the signed area under the error curve must end at 1.5/Ki m·s. For Ki = 1.75 that is 0.857 m·s. IAE counts every area as positive, so IAE ≥ 1.5/Ki, with equality only if the error never changes sign. Experiment 7 has IAE 0.940 m·s; the difference, 0.083 m·s, comes from the overshoot, whose negative area is counted once in the bill and once more in the IAE, so the overshoot's area is about 0.04 m·s. The sweep's winner, IAE 0.872, sits only slightly above its bound of 0.857. This explains a pattern in the sweep: a lower Ki raises the floor under the IAE, a higher Ki lowers it but brings overshoot. D1R1R2

Cost functions. IAE is one choice. Squaring the error (ISE) punishes large errors more; weighting by time (ITAE) punishes errors that last. B1 discusses these and other criteria; the choice changes which passing set ranks first, so the cost belongs in the specification too. B1

Tuning rules. Before computers made sweeps cheap, engineers used rules that compute gains from one or two measured numbers of the plant, such as the step-response method and the ultimate-gain method associated with Ziegler and Nichols. B1 describes them and their weaknesses; F9-22 uses a simpler model-based choice for a speed loop. B1

Not verified — check before relying on this

That B1 presents the Ziegler–Nichols step-response and frequency-response (ultimate-gain) methods, and criticises them for giving poorly damped loops, is recalled from general knowledge, not read in this build. This chapter quotes none of their formulas or tables. The Source Researcher must check the PID chapter of "Feedback Systems" and record the section in the RB202 dossier (gate G1).

Robustness: a tuning fits one model. All 2550 runs used one cart with one mass, one friction and one slope. A real cart differs. In a scratch run of this build, changing only the mass from 2.0 to 2.5 kg made all seven hand-tuned experiments fail, including the two that passed (lab step 3 lets you repeat it). A specification met with no margin on the nominal model may not survive the first contact with hardware. Remedies: sweep or test over a range of plant parameters, prefer sets with margin, and keep the gains adjustable on the bench. D1B1

The cost of a sweep. Each run simulates 1000 control periods with 100 physics steps each: 105 steps per gain set, 2.55 × 108 for the sweep. A three-gain grid grows with the cube of its resolution, so halving every step size costs about 8 times as much. Coarse-to-fine searches and optimisers (B1, and CS courses on optimisation) reduce the cost; for a three-gain controller and a fast simulator, a brute-force sweep is often good enough. D1

How the hardware actually does it

The simulator contains only what someone wrote into it. The cart here has no backlash, no static friction, no encoder quantisation, no delay between measuring and acting, and an actuator that delivers exactly the requested force. Each of these exists on a real machine and each can move a response outside the specification (F9-19 showed quantisation; F9-22 adds a deadband and wiring faults). U1

That is why tuning on hardware starts from simulated gains, but not at full scale: reduce the gains or the setpoint step, keep the speed limit and the emergency stop ready, record the same metrics from logs, and compare them with the simulated ones. Large differences tell you the model is wrong, and the model should be fixed before the gains are. U1B1

Worked example

Problem. For the cart on the slope (push back 1.5 N), predict before simulating: (a) the final error of a PD controller with Kp = 8; (b) the Kp a PD controller would need to meet S3 (2 mm) without any I; (c) whether that Kp could pass S4 for a 1 m step; (d) the signed error area that a PI or PID controller with Ki = 1.5 needs to build.

  1. (a) At rest Kp·e = 1.5 N, e = 1.5/8 = 0.1875 m. Experiment 2 reports 187.5 mm.
  2. (b) Kp ≥ 1.5/0.002 = 750 N/m.
  3. (c) No. At the step the error is 1 m, so P alone asks for 750 N, far above 10 N. Without I, S3 and S4 cannot both be met: the slope forces an integral term into the design.
  4. (d) Ki·∫e dt = 1.5 N, so ∫e dt = 1.0 m·s. Experiment 6 (Ki = 1.5) has IAE 0.996 m·s in R2, close to 1.0 and consistent with its 0.0 % overshoot (the error never changes sign). It is slightly less than 1.0 because at the end the remaining 1.5 mm of error lets P supply 8 × 0.0015 = 0.012 N of the 1.5 N.

A few lines of algebra rule out whole families of gains before a single run, and tell you what the runs should show. D1R1R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Keanu's practice ramp is a real hill, only shorter. The simulator is a model; it can be wrong in ways no practice ride would be (a missing friction effect, a wrong mass). Passing in simulation is a reason to try hardware carefully, not a proof. D1
    • 

  • Keanu feels why a setup is better. The controller's tuning knows only the numbers the bench measures; a requirement left off the specification (noise, energy use, wear) is not protected at all. B1
    • 

  • Keanu's rides take minutes. A simulated step test takes milliseconds, so "try everything" becomes possible, which no rider could do. R2

Lab


Goal


Tune a PID controller for the simulated cart against a written specification, by hand and by sweep, and test how robust the result is to errors in the model.


Hardware and software needed


No hardware. A C++20 compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 through university/labs/run_lab.sh.


Safety


None beyond normal computer use. Simulated gains are not ready for a real machine: the first hardware test of any tuning uses reduced gains or steps, a speed limit and an emergency stop, under supervision (F9-22).

Expected observations


Step 3: in a scratch run of this build, no experiment passed with 2.5 kg; experiment 6 failed only S3 (2.5 mm) and experiment 7 failed S2 (3.72 s). Step 4: the top set does not change (2.04 s, against 2.05 s for experiment 7's set); only the last two ranks swap, because Kp = 5 settles in 2.74 s and Kp = 9, Ki = 1.5 in 2.82 s. Step 5: in a scratch run of this build, none of the 2550 sets survived. That is a finding, not a failure of the lab: this specification has no margin for a ±25 % mass error, and the honest response is to report it and propose a looser limit or a better-known mass, with numbers. Your runs are the check.


Verification


You have one gain set of your own that passes S1–S4 on the nominal cart, a written reason for each change that led to it, and a statement of how it behaves when the mass changes by 25 %.


Troubleshooting


    

  • The sweep takes very long → the sanitizers slow it down; that is why its folder allows 60 s. For your own large sweeps, build a copy with -O2 and without sanitizers.

  • Every set fails S3 → check that the slope sign and the integral are in the code you are running.

Mini-project


Practice for the practical exam (P): tune to a written specification. Your instructor gives you a modified test bench (a different mass, friction, slope or force limit) and a written specification. Produce: the tuning log (each experiment with its reason and its metrics), the chosen gain set, a gain sweep that confirms or improves it, a robustness check over at least two other plant values, and one paragraph defending your choice. For self-practice, use a 3 kg cart with friction 1.0 N·s/m, a slope force of 2.0 N and the S1–S4 limits of this chapter.


Rubric (5 points): a passing set on the nominal plant, confirmed by the test bench (1); a tuning log in which every change has a reason that uses P, I or D behaviour correctly (1); a correct sweep with stated grid and cost (1); a robustness check with numbers (1); a defended choice that discusses margin, not only the cost (1).



Summary


    

  • Write the specification first: quantities, definitions, limits and the test (here S1–S4 for a 1 m step).
    • 

  • Tune by hand one change at a time, each for a stated reason, and check every experiment against the whole specification; one fix can break another requirement (experiment 5).
    • 

  • A constant disturbance makes I necessary: PD leaves 1.5/Kp of error, and I must build a signed error area of 1.5/Ki.
    • 

  • A gain sweep checks a hand tuning: 5 of 2550 sets passed here; the IAE winner had less margin than the hand-tuned set.
    • 

  • A tuning fits one model: with 2.5 kg instead of 2 kg every hand-tuned set failed. Prefer margin, test robustness, and treat hardware as the final judge.
