Story hook

Kwame's bicycle computer records the ride: speed every second, heart rate, the route. One afternoon he wobbles and nearly falls at a crossing. That evening he cannot remember why. He looks at the recording. His speed was steady, his heart rate was steady, and then for two seconds there is nothing: no speed, no position, a flat line. Then the recording continues as if nothing happened. The recorder did not fail: it was blinded. His phone, mounted on the same handlebar, had started a large update, and the cycle computer, which shares the phone's processor through an app, got no time to run.

The gap in the recording is the evidence. Without it, "I wobbled" is all anyone knows. With it, and with the phone's own log of the update, the cause is clear, and so is the fix: give the cycle computer its own processor, or stop the phone updating during rides.

The RB403 forensic case is this story with a robot. An operator reports that the robot "froze for two seconds". Nothing crashed; the robot recovered by itself. This chapter builds the recorder that makes such a report answerable, reproduces the freeze with real real-time threads on the build machine, finds its cause in the evidence, and re-measures after moving the control loop to its own CPU.

What you will be able to do

  • Distinguish logs, telemetry, traces and the flight recorder, and say what each is for, where it is stored, and what it costs the real-time loop.
  • Build a flight recorder that a real-time loop can write without allocating, locking or calling the kernel, and test its wrap-around.
  • Reconstruct an incident timeline from several sources on a common time base, and separate observation from hypothesis.
  • Diagnose a control-loop freeze caused by a higher-priority activity on a shared CPU, from a driver log, the loop's own records and the machine's real-time settings.
  • Re-measure after a fix and state what the new measurement proves and what it does not.
Jargon box
Flight recorder
Simple words: a small memory that always holds the last few seconds of what the robot's control loop did, so it can be read after something went wrong. Analogy: the cycle computer's recording, but only the last minute, kept forever fresh. Precise: a fixed-size ring buffer of small binary records (time, cycle number, values, event code) written by the real-time code without allocation, locks, system calls or formatting; the oldest records are overwritten; it is dumped to storage on an incident trigger or read after the loop stops. R1 (Lock-free ring buffers: F9-49.)
Telemetry
Simple words: numbers the robot sends while it runs so that people can watch its health from elsewhere. Analogy: the cycle computer sending your speed and heart rate to a friend's phone. Precise: a periodic, usually down-sampled stream of named measurements (rates, temperatures, battery, loop lateness percentiles, counts of faults) sent off the robot over a network to a monitoring system; best effort, never on the control path; distinct from logs (events) and the flight recorder (full-rate, local, recent). C1
Incident timeline
Simple words: the list of everything that happened around a problem, in time order, from all the records you have. Analogy: putting the cycle computer's track and the phone's update log side by side. Precise: a merged, time-ordered sequence of observations from logs, traces, recorders and witnesses, each with its source and its clock, used to separate what was observed from what is inferred; requires a common time base or known offsets between clocks. R2
Common time base
Simple words: all the records use the same clock, so you can put them in order. Analogy: the cycle computer and the phone both set to the same time. Precise: a clock shared by every record that will be correlated: on one Linux machine the monotonic clock (which never jumps) for intervals and ordering, plus a wall-clock time for matching against the outside world; across machines a synchronised clock with a known error. D2
Kernel trace
Simple words: a recording, made by the operating system, of which program ran on which CPU and when, and why it stopped. Analogy: a log of who was holding the handlebars at every moment. Precise: a time-stamped stream of kernel events (scheduler switches and wake-ups, interrupts, system calls, driver trace points) recorded into per-CPU buffers by the kernel's tracing infrastructure and read with a tool; it shows which task ran on a CPU while another was waiting. D1
Log rate limiting
Simple words: letting a repeating message through only a few times per second, and counting the rest. Analogy: the friend who says "you're slow" once a minute, not every second. Precise: a limit on how many instances of a message (or of a source) are written per interval, with a count of suppressed instances; protects storage, the CPU and the readability of the log during fault storms, which are exactly the moments that matter. C1
Shared-CPU interference
Simple words: one program making another late because they take turns on the same processor. Analogy: the phone update starving the cycle computer app. Precise: the delay a task suffers because another task of equal or higher priority (or a non-preemptible kernel path, or an interrupt handler) runs on the same CPU; removed by placing the time-critical task on a CPU nothing else is allowed to use (CPU isolation and affinity) or by changing priorities. R2 R3

Terms used from earlier courses: structured log, ring buffer (SPSC) (see the glossary); log level and monotonic clock (OS302); SCHED_FIFO, CPU isolation (F9-66); real-time throttling and threaded interrupt handler (RB304, F9-47); bag and tracing in ROS 2 (RB303, F9-43).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot keeps a diary. Some of it is written slowly, in words, like "the camera started" or "the battery is low". Some of it is a fast list of numbers, a thousand times a second, about what the robot's balance-and-steering program did. That fast list is kept in a small circle of memory: when the circle is full, the newest entry goes where the oldest was. So the robot always remembers the last few seconds, which is exactly what you want to look at when something strange has just happened.

One day a robot stops moving for two seconds and then carries on. Nobody knows why. We look at the fast list and find a hole: two seconds with no entries. The steering program did not run at all. Then we look at the slow diary and see that at the same moment the camera program was restarting the camera, and it was using the same part of the computer, and it was more important. So the camera took the computer's whole attention for two seconds. The fix is to give the steering program its own part of the computer. Then we test again, and the hole is gone.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Four kinds of record answer four different questions.

  • Logs answer "what happened, in words?" They are events: a unit started, a device was reset, a parameter was rejected. They should be structured (fields such as time, unit, level, event, values, not free sentences), have levels, and be rate-limited so that a fault storm cannot flood the disk or hide the first message. They are written by ordinary units, never by the control loop directly.
  • Telemetry answers "is the robot healthy right now?" A few numbers per second (battery, temperatures, loop lateness percentiles, fault counters) sent over the network to a monitoring system. Down-sampled and best effort; if the network is down, it is lost, and nothing on the robot waits for it.
  • Traces answer "who was running on which CPU, and why was my task waiting?" They are recorded by the kernel (scheduler switches, wake-ups, interrupts) and by instrumented programs, usually only when enabled, because they produce a lot of data.
  • The flight recorder answers "what exactly did the control loop do in the seconds before the incident?" Full rate, binary, in memory, local, recent; written by the control loop itself; dumped when something triggers it.

The flight recorder must not disturb what it records. A control loop with a 1 ms period cannot afford to format text, allocate memory, take a lock that a lower-priority thread might hold, or write to a file. So it copies one small record into a ring buffer and moves on. Everything else (formatting, saving, analysing) happens later and elsewhere. R1

Forensics is the discipline of answering "what happened?" from records. The method is the one this university uses everywhere: collect the evidence before changing anything; put it on one time line; write down what was observed separately from what you think; form several hypotheses; for each one, say what evidence would rule it out and look for it; fix; and then re-measure, under the same conditions, to show that the symptom is gone. A fix without a re-measurement is a guess.

The course case is a classic: a control loop that stops for two seconds while nothing crashes. Two seconds is far longer than any normal scheduling delay; something prevented the loop from running at all. The loop's own records show the hole and its exact edges. A driver's log, on the same time base, shows what started and ended at those edges. The machine's settings show why a busy higher-priority thread could hold the CPU that long. And moving the loop to a CPU the driver does not use, then measuring again, shows whether that was the whole story. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The flight recorder. Listing 1, flightrec.h, is a template ring of N records of 24 bytes (time in nanoseconds from CLOCK_MONOTONIC, cycle number, one value, an event code). push stores the record at head % N and publishes the new head with a release store; it never fails and never blocks. for_each visits the records still held, oldest first. The design is single-writer: the control thread writes, and the analysis reads after the writer has stopped. A reader running concurrently would need a per-record sequence number to detect records overwritten under it (lab extension). Before trusting it, test the wrap-around, which is where ring buffers break:



Ten records into a ring of four: six overwritten, and the four held are 6, 7, 8, 9, oldest first. R1


Sizing. The lab's recorder holds 8192 records: 8.192 s at 1 ms, 192 KiB of memory (8192 × 24 bytes). The rule: hold at least the longest interval between the start of an incident and the trigger that dumps the ring, with a margin. A freeze of two seconds, noticed by an operator who presses a "mark" button a few seconds later, needs about ten seconds; the dump trigger can also be automatic (the watchdog of F9-69 firing, a lateness above a threshold).


Reproducing the freeze. Listing 2, freeze.cc, uses two real threads with the real-time policy SCHED_FIFO, each pinned to a CPU given on the command line. The control thread (priority 80) runs a 1 ms loop to absolute deadlines and records every cycle with its lateness. The camera thread (priority 90) is the university's stand-in for a USB camera driver's error path: one second after the start it logs "no frame for 500 ms, resetting device" and then keeps its CPU busy for two seconds (retries and polling), and logs "reset complete". It is a simulation, not the Linux USB driver, and its log lines are written by the program in a kernel-log-like layout; the lab is honest about that because the build container has no USB camera. After four seconds the program prints the camera log, the gaps in the flight recorder, and a lateness histogram. R2

Read it like an investigator. The flight recorder has one gap longer than 5 ms: from a cycle recorded just before 1.000 s to the next cycle, recorded just after 3.000 s, about 2000 ms. The camera log, on the same monotonic clock, has its reset starting just after 1.000 s and completing just after 3.000 s. The edges match to within a millisecond. Only about 2000 records were written in four seconds instead of about 4000, and the histogram has exactly one cycle in the "≥ 100 ms" bin: the first cycle after the gap, two seconds late. Note what the cycle numbers do not show: the two cycles on either side of the gap have consecutive numbers. The loop counts iterations, not releases; after an overrun it skips the missed releases (Listing 2 lines 94–96). Only the time stamps reveal the gap. A recorder that stored cycle numbers without times would have hidden the freeze. R2


Why two whole seconds? Both threads are SCHED_FIFO, and on a CPU the scheduler always runs the highest-priority runnable real-time thread, without time slices among different priorities. The camera thread (90) was runnable for two seconds and the control thread (80) was runnable from its next release onward; the camera thread won every decision. The machine's real-time throttling (sched_rt_runtime_us=950000 of a 1,000,000 µs period) reserves a part of each period for non-real-time tasks; it did not help the control thread, which is itself a real-time task. Priorities were set by someone who thought "the camera driver is a driver, so it should be higher", without asking what the control loop's deadline was. R2 D3

Logs that help. The camera log line worked as evidence because it had a precise time stamp on the same clock as the recorder, a unit name, and both edges of the event ("resetting", "complete"). Rules for the course robot's logs: every line has a monotonic time stamp and a wall-clock time stamp, the unit, a level, an event name and key-value fields; long operations log their start and their end; repeated messages are rate-limited with a count of suppressed lines; the log is written to the logs partition of the image (F9-67), which survives updates, and is rotated by size. Logs that matter for safety (e-stops, watchdog trips, resets, arming) are never rate-limited and are kept longest. C1


Telemetry that helps. A monitoring system that had received, every second, the control loop's worst lateness and its count of cycles, would have shown the incident as a spike to 2000 ms and a drop in the cycle count at the same second, across the whole fleet, and would have shown whether it happened only on robots with a certain camera. Telemetry finds patterns across robots and time; the flight recorder explains one incident in detail. C1



How the hardware actually does it


On a robot computer, the storage for logs is usually flash (SD card or eMMC), which wears out with writes; continuous high-rate logging can shorten its life, which is one more reason the flight recorder lives in RAM and is written only on a trigger. A power loss can lose the last seconds of a log that was still in a file-system cache; the incident logger must flush (and the robot can keep a capacitor-backed shutdown, or a microcontroller with its own small recorder). Clocks: the monotonic clock comes from a hardware counter; wall-clock time on a robot without a real-time-clock battery starts wrong at every boot until it is synchronised over the network, so time stamps of early boot messages can be years off, and correlating with the outside world needs the offset. USB devices share a host controller, and its interrupts land on whichever CPU the kernel or the configuration chose; a camera reset can occupy that CPU in the interrupt handler or in the driver's threads. None of these hardware behaviours were measured in this build. C1


Safety
A robot whose control loop stops for two seconds is a robot driving on its last command for two seconds: the watchdog of F9-69 exists for this, and in the course case it should have stopped the robot after its timeout. If the incident report says "froze" and not "stopped by the watchdog", check the watchdog too. When reproducing an incident on a real robot, do it with the wheels off the ground or motor power off at the e-stop chain. Preserve the evidence before any test: copy the logs partition and the recorder dump first, because the reproduction can overwrite them. C2

Worked example


A. Reading the gap. Take the four time stamps from freeze_shared.out (microsecond resolution; the exact digits differ from run to run). Gap: first cycle after minus last cycle before, a little over 2.000 s, printed in whole milliseconds. Start edge: the camera's "resetting" time minus the last cycle before the gap, less than one control period (1 ms): the control thread had just finished a cycle and was sleeping until its next release at about 1.000 s, when the camera thread became runnable at a higher priority. End edge: the first cycle after the gap minus the camera's "complete" time, a fraction of a millisecond: the control thread had been runnable for two seconds and ran as soon as the CPU was free. Each edge lies within one period of a camera event. The two sources agree because they share CLOCK_MONOTONIC. R2


B. The record count as evidence. In four seconds a 1 ms loop should write about 4000 records. Run 1 wrote about 2000: about 1000 before the gap, about 1000 after it, and none for two seconds. Run 2 wrote close to 4000. Any shortfall in run 2 comes from cycles that overran and skipped their missed releases; compare it with the number of cycles in run 2's histogram that were late by more than 1 ms, each of which can skip one or more releases. Counting is a cheap check that every telemetry stream can carry.


C. Choosing the priorities. Order by deadline (rate-monotonic, F9-65): the control loop has a 1 ms period; the camera driver's work concerns frames every 33 ms or so and a reset that can take seconds. The control loop must be higher. With the control loop at 90 and the camera at 80, both on CPU 1, the camera's reset would run in the control loop's idle time, which is most of every millisecond, and finish later; the control loop would be delayed by at most the camera's non-preemptible sections. Separate CPUs remove even that, and protect the loop against any future high-priority work on the camera's CPU.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Kwame's cycle computer records for hours. A flight recorder deliberately forgets everything older than a few seconds; it is useful only if something dumps it in time.
    • 

  • The phone and the cycle computer are two devices. On the robot, the camera driver and the control loop are on one computer, and which CPU each uses is a setting that someone chose, often without knowing it.
    • 

  • A rider can say "I wobbled". A robot cannot report what it did not record; the hole in the record is the only witness of the freeze.
    • 

  • Kwame's fix (stop the phone updating) is a habit. The robot's fix must be a configuration that is checked in the image build and re-measured on every release.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Logging from the control loop with printf or a logger. Symptom: lateness spikes that disappear when logging is off. Fix: a flight recorder; format outside the loop.
    • 

  • Recording cycle numbers without time stamps. Symptom: the record looks continuous although the loop stopped. Fix: always record the monotonic time.
    • 

  • Different clocks in different logs. Symptom: events that "happen" before their cause. Fix: a common time base; record both monotonic and wall-clock time; know the offsets.
    • 

  • Changing the system before collecting evidence. Symptom: the incident cannot be explained because the reboot overwrote the logs. Fix: copy the logs partition and dump the recorder first.
    • 

  • Priorities by importance of the component, not by deadline. Symptom: a "driver" at a higher priority than the 1 ms control loop. Fix: order by deadline; separate CPUs for the hardest deadlines.
    • 

  • Declaring victory without re-measuring. Symptom: the freeze returns on another robot. Fix: the same measurement, same conditions, before and after, on the robot.
    • 

  • Believing the isolation setting. Symptom: lateness stays although the CPU "is isolated". Find it: read the isolated-CPU list and the interrupt affinities, and measure. Fix: configure what was missing.
    •

  3. State three hypotheses for a two-second gap in the control loop (for example: the loop blocked on a lock or a system call; the process was stopped; a higher-priority thread held the CPU) and the evidence that supports or rules out each.
    4. 

  4. Why did the safety supervisor log no fault? What should it have done?
    5. 

  5. Propose the fix and the re-measurement. What result do you expect, and what will it not prove?
    6. 



Method hints. Put the recorder's gap edges and the camera log on one line. Then ask, for each hypothesis, what the recorder would show: a blocked loop and a preempted loop look the same in the recorder, so look for evidence about who else was running, and about the configuration of CPUs and priorities.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. Listing 2 was run with both threads on CPU 1 (./freeze 1 1, R2) on the build container. The camera thread simulates a driver's reset by keeping its CPU busy for two seconds at SCHED_FIFO 90.


Timeline (times as printed in the evidence, rounded). Just before 1.000 s, recorder: the last cycle before the gap, on time. Just after 1.000 s, camera log: "no frame for 500 ms, resetting device". From 1.000 s to 3.000 s: no records. Just after 3.000 s, camera log: "reset complete". A fraction of a millisecond later, recorder: the next cycle (consecutive number), about two seconds late (the one entry in the "≥ 100 ms" bin). The recorder's gap starts within one control period of the reset and ends within one control period of its completion.


Hypotheses. (1) The control loop blocked on a lock or a system call: the loop in Listing 2 takes no lock and its only system calls are the clock and the sleep; and the gap's edges coincide with another thread's activity, which a self-inflicted block would not. (2) The process was stopped (a signal, a debugger, the machine suspended): the camera thread of the same process kept running and logging during the gap, so the process was not stopped. (3) A higher-priority thread held the CPU: both threads are SCHED_FIFO on CPU 1, the camera thread at 90 was busy for exactly the gap, and throttling does not protect one real-time task from another. Hypothesis 3 fits all the evidence. On a real robot, a scheduler trace would show it directly.


The safety supervisor. It should have stopped the robot: no kick for two seconds is far beyond a 50 ms timeout (F9-69). If the supervisor ran on the same CPU at a lower priority than the camera, it was frozen too, and could not notice. That is why the final watchdog belongs on the motor microcontroller, and why "no fault was logged" is a second finding to fix.


Fix and re-measurement. Put the control loop on its own CPU (and the supervisor on a CPU the camera cannot block), order priorities by deadline, steer the camera's interrupts away from the control CPU, and isolate the control CPU from general work. Re-measure with the same scenario: run 2 (./freeze 2 1, R3) shows no gap longer than 5 ms. It does not prove the robot's behaviour; repeat it on the robot with the real camera and record a scheduler trace.
