Story hook

Ama and her brother ride down a long hill, one behind the other. Halfway down, a dog runs into the road. Ama does not stop pedalling and hope: she squeezes both brakes, hard but not so hard that the wheel locks, and stops with her feet on the ground. That is an emergency stop: a fast, controlled action that ends in a state where nothing moves.

Her brother has a different job. He rides behind her and calls out every few seconds. If Ama does not answer twice in a row, he does not wait to find out why. He shouts "stop!" and gets ready to grab her saddle. He does not know whether she is daydreaming, has a cramp, or has fainted; he only knows that the answer that should have come did not. That is a watchdog.

And once they are both standing at the side of the road, neither rides on just because the dog has gone. They look, they agree, and one says "go". That is a reset.

A robot needs all three: a stop that works even when its software does not, a watchdog that notices when the software stops answering, and safe states with rules for leaving them. This chapter builds the software part of each, tests the e-stop on the course image, and shows how a watchdog that is fed by the wrong thing watches nothing at all.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the layers that stop a robot (hard-wired e-stop chain, drive enable, microcontroller watchdog, host supervisor) and which of them must work with no software at all.
  • Define the safe state of a robot for a given hazard, and the difference between an immediate power cut and a controlled stop followed by a power cut.
  • Design a watchdog: what kicks it, its timeout, its check period, and the bound on detection time that follows from them; and measure that bound with real threads.
  • Implement a safety supervisor as a state machine (ready, stopping, locked) with latching and reset rules, and check invariants on every step.
  • Test an e-stop end to end on the course image: press it from outside while the control loop runs and verify that no non-zero actuator command follows.
Jargon box
Safe state
Simple words: the condition the robot goes to when something is wrong, chosen so that it cannot hurt anyone while it stays there. Analogy: standing at the side of the road with both feet down. Precise: a defined state of the machine in which the hazard under consideration is absent (for a wheeled robot: stationary, drive disabled, brakes applied where fitted); entered by a safety function and left only by a defined reset; it depends on the hazard: for an arm holding a load, "power off" may drop the load, so the safe state includes holding brakes. D1 D3
Controlled stop
Simple words: slowing the robot down along a planned ramp first, and only then switching the motors off. Analogy: squeezing the brakes firmly instead of jumping off. Precise: a stop in which the drives remain powered and are commanded to decelerate at a set rate to zero speed, after which power to the drives is removed; contrasted with an uncontrolled stop by immediate removal of power, where the machine coasts or is held by brakes. D1
Watchdog (software)
Simple words: a checker that expects a sign of life at regular times and acts when it stops coming. Analogy: the brother who calls and shouts "stop!" when there is no answer. Precise: a monitor with a timeout T that is reset ("kicked", "fed") by the monitored activity; checked every P; detection of a stop happens at most T + P (plus the monitor's own scheduling delay) after the last kick; the action is to request the safe state. A hardware watchdog timer does the same in a counter that resets the processor (HW204). R2
Kick source
Simple words: the thing that tells the watchdog "still alive". Analogy: Ama's answer, not the sound of her wheels. Precise: the code path that resets the watchdog; it must be the evidence of useful progress of the monitored function (for example the end of a control cycle that used fresh data), never an independent timer or a thread that runs whatever the function does. R4
Latched safe state
Simple words: once the robot has stopped for a safety reason, it stays stopped even if the reason goes away, until a person resets it. Analogy: the dog has gone, but nobody rides on until someone says "go". Precise: a state that persists after its triggering condition clears; leaving it requires (a) the cause to be gone, (b) the system to be healthy, and (c) a deliberate reset action, and the reset itself must not start motion. D2 (RB101's F9-06 introduced latching.)
Drive enable
Simple words: the switch, separate from the speed command, that allows a motor drive to make the motor turn at all. Analogy: the chain on the bicycle: without it, pedalling does nothing. Precise: a signal or contactor that permits the power stage of a drive to produce torque; removing it (for example by a safe torque off input of the drive, or a contactor in the motor supply) stops torque regardless of the command value; the e-stop chain acts on this, not on the command. D4
Host heartbeat
Simple words: a short message the robot computer sends to the motor microcontroller many times a second to say it is alive. Analogy: Ama's regular answer to her brother. Precise: a periodic message with a sequence number (often carried with the command stream) from the high-level computer to the real-time controller; the controller enters its safe state when no valid heartbeat arrives within its timeout, so the failure of the whole robot computer is detected by the one component that does not depend on it. C1

Terms used from earlier courses: emergency stop and latched (RB101, F9-06); watchdog timer (HW204); fail-safe default, heartbeat and motor power cut (emergency-stop path) (see the glossary); companion microcontroller (F9-65); readiness gate (F9-68).

Safety

Read this before the chapter. The software in this chapter is a teaching model of what a safety supervisor does. It is not a safety function in the sense of any functional-safety standard, it has not been assessed, and it must never be the only thing between a person and a moving robot. On every robot in this university, the emergency stop is a hard-wired chain that removes power from the motor drives (or the drives' torque) without any software in the path (RB101; faculty F9 rules). Software monitors that chain, stops motion in a controlled way, and records what happened; it does not replace it. Industrial and service robots are designed to the standards named in this chapter, by people trained in them. C2 D3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot needs a big red button that stops it, and that button must work even if the robot's computer is broken. So the button is not a question to the computer. It is a switch that cuts the motors' power with a wire. The computer is told that the button was pressed, so it can stop asking the motors to move, but the stopping does not wait for the computer.

The robot also needs a helper that checks whether its brain is still working. The brain sends a little "I'm fine" message all the time. If the message stops, the helper does not wait to find out why: it slows the robot down and stops it. That helper is called a watchdog.

When the robot has stopped because something was wrong, it stays stopped. Even if the button is pulled back up, the robot waits until a person checks that everything is all right and presses "reset". And after the reset the robot starts slowly again from zero, never with a jump.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What is the safe state? Start with the hazard, not with the software. A wheeled robot that might drive into a person is safe when it is stationary and its drives cannot produce torque. A robot arm holding a heavy part is not safe with its power simply cut: the part falls, and the arm may sag under gravity; its safe state includes holding brakes that engage when power is lost. A drone has no stationary safe state in the air at all; its "safe state" is a controlled landing. The same software pattern serves all three, but the state it drives to is a design decision made per hazard, written down, and reviewed. The course robot is wheeled: its safe state is "speed 0, drive enable off". D3

Stopping: cut or ramp. There are two ways to get to that state. Cut the power at once: simple, and certain, but a fast robot coasts (or skids, if it has brakes), and a robot arm stops wherever its brakes catch it. Or keep the drives powered, command a controlled deceleration to zero, and then cut the power: shorter stopping distance for a wheeled robot whose drives can brake, and no uncontrolled motion, but it relies on the drives and the controller working during the ramp. Real machines often do the second with the first as a backstop: the safety relay of the e-stop chain removes power after a fixed delay whatever the software did. The standards define named stop categories for these cases (box in Layer 3). D1

The emergency stop is a hard-wired chain: a latching mushroom button with normally-closed contacts (so a broken wire reads as "stop"), in series with a safety relay that drops the motor contactor or the drives' torque-off input. The robot computer has an input from that chain, the e-stop monitor, so the software knows the robot has been stopped: it must stop commanding motion, ramp its own commands to zero, log the event, and refuse to resume until reset. If the software misses the input, the motors still stop, because the software was never on the stop path. D2

Watchdogs detect the failures that nobody presses a button for: a control task stuck in a driver call, a process that crashed, a robot computer that froze. A watchdog has three parameters: what kicks it, its timeout T, and how often it is checked, P. If the last kick was at time k, the watchdog notices no later than k + T + P, plus the time it takes the watchdog itself to get the CPU. The most important of the three is the first: the kick must come from the thing you want to watch, at the point where it has done its job. A watchdog kicked by a timer watches the timer.

Watchdogs are layered like Figure 1. Inside the robot computer, a supervisor watches the control loop (this chapter's Listing 1 and Listing 2). The motor microcontroller watches the robot computer through the host heartbeat: if the heartbeat stops, it ramps the motors down and drops the drive enable on its own. And the microcontroller has its own hardware watchdog timer (HW204), which resets it if its firmware hangs, with its outputs designed so that a reset leaves the drives disabled. C1

Leaving the safe state is where many accidents start. Rules: the safe state is latched; a reset is accepted only if the cause is gone (e-stop released, watchdog healthy); the reset is a deliberate operator action at a place where the operator can see the robot; and the reset does not start motion: it returns the robot to READY, and the controller starts from zero speed. The lab's supervisor implements all four, and checks after every simulated millisecond that it has not broken them.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The supervisor as a state machine. Listing 1, safety.cpp, simulates one drive in 1 ms steps. The control task ramps its command to a cruise speed of 500 mm/s and kicks the watchdog every step it runs. The supervisor is separate from the control task: it owns the state (Figure 2) and the drive output, and the control task can only ask for a speed. Faults (e-stop, no kick for more than 50 ms) take READY to STOPPING, which ramps the drive command down by 2 mm/s per ms (2 m/s²); at zero, the supervisor disables the drive and enters LOCKED. Three invariants are checked on every step: the drive is never commanded non-zero while disabled; the command never increases outside READY; the command never jumps up by more than the control task's ramp of 1 mm/s per ms. The scenario file:





Read it with Figure 3. The control task stalls at 1000 ms (it is stuck for 200 ms, as if blocked in a driver call). Its last kick was at 999 ms; at 1050 ms the gap is 51 ms, more than 50, and the supervisor starts the controlled stop from 500 mm/s. It takes 250 steps of 2 mm/s, so the drive is at zero at 1299 ms and the supervisor locks it. Note what does not happen at 1200 ms, when the control task comes back: nothing. The robot stays stopped, because the safe state is latched. The reset at 1600 ms is accepted (no e-stop, the watchdog is healthy again), and the control task, which sees the state, ramps from 0. At 2000 ms the e-stop arrives with the drive at 400 mm/s; the stop takes 200 steps. The reset at 2400 ms is refused because the button is still pressed; after the release at 2500 ms, the reset at 2600 ms is accepted. The last two lines are the checks: the drive followed a command the control task had not refreshed for at most 50 ms, and no invariant was violated. R1


The stale-command window. Between the stall at 1000 ms and the fault at 1050 ms, the drive kept following the control task's last command, 500 mm/s. That window is the cost of the watchdog's timeout: during it, the robot moves on a decision made 50 ms ago, and at 0.5 m/s it covers 25 mm. The timeout is therefore a safety parameter, chosen from the distance the robot may travel on a stale command, not from what is convenient for the software. Too short a timeout causes nuisance stops on ordinary jitter (F9-66 measured how late a Linux thread can be); too long a timeout lets the robot drive blind. R1


The detection bound, measured. The simulation detects a stop at exactly T + 1 step. A real watchdog is a thread that is itself scheduled. Listing 2, watchdog.cpp, runs a control thread with a 10 ms period that hangs for 300 ms in its 31st cycle, and a watchdog thread that checks every 5 ms with a 50 ms timeout. It prints the detection gap and compares it with the bound T + P = 55 ms:




Compare the printed gap with the bound: in the runs made during this build on the (otherwise idle) container it stayed within it, a little over the 50 ms timeout. On a loaded machine the watchdog thread can be woken late (F9-66's measurements), and the program prints the excess as "extra scheduling delay". That is the argument for running the watchdog at a high real-time priority and, better, for putting the final watchdog outside the robot computer altogether, in the microcontroller, where Linux scheduling cannot delay it. Note also the last line: once the safe state was requested, the control thread stopped kicking. A component that has been declared failed must not be able to clear its own fault by kicking again. The e-stop test on the course image. The robot image of F9-67 polls its e-stop line (GPIO line 3 of the PL061) in every 1 ms control cycle; on a press it commands 0, latches the safe state, holds it for 300 cycles while counting any non-zero command, and exits with 0 only if there was none. The e-stop test boots that image, asks it to run 8000 cycles (8 s), and presses the button from outside after about one second. The "button" is QEMU's: the monitor command system_powerdown raises the power-key input that QEMU's virt machine wires to line 3 of its PL061 when ACPI is off, and the robot's devicetree names that line as its e-stop. The helper press.py types the command into the monitor's socket.




The serial console shows the whole story: the image armed, the press arrived during the loop, the image entered the safe state at that cycle with actuator command 0, the input was already released again 300 cycles later (QEMU's key is a pulse) but the state stayed latched, and the check counted zero non-zero commands. The test's exit code is the image's own verdict. What this test does not show is the time from the press to the reaction, because the press happens in host time and the image counts in emulated time; on a real robot that is measured with an oscilloscope on the button contact and the drive enable. Emergency stops, stop categories and safety functions of machines are specified in international standards, among them ISO 13850 (emergency stop function), IEC 60204-1 (electrical equipment of machines, which the author recalls defines stop categories 0, 1 and 2: immediate power removal, controlled stop then power removal, and controlled stop with power maintained), ISO 13849-1 (safety-related parts of control systems, performance levels), IEC 61508 (functional safety of programmable electronic systems, safety integrity levels), IEC 61800-5-2 (safety functions of adjustable-speed drives, including safe torque off), ISO 10218 (industrial robots) and ISO 13482 (personal care robots). No requirement of any standard is claimed to be met by the course software. D1 D3 D4


Designing the kick. Where in the control loop should the kick go? At the end of a cycle that read fresh sensor data, computed a command and handed it to the drive, and only if all three succeeded. Not in a separate thread (it would keep kicking while the control thread is stuck), not at the start of the cycle (a cycle that hangs half-way would already have kicked), and not on every message received (a flood of stale messages would keep it alive). For a stack with several critical tasks, each one gets its own deadline in the supervisor, or the kick is the conjunction: a "window" that closes only when every critical task has reported in. Some hardware watchdogs also refuse kicks that come too early (window watchdogs), which catches a loop that has started running far too fast, for example after a timer misconfiguration. R4


Heartbeat to the microcontroller. The host's commands to the motor microcontroller carry a sequence number and are sent every control cycle; the microcontroller treats a repeated or missing sequence number for longer than its timeout as "host lost", ramps the motors down with its own limits and drops the drive enable. That one rule covers a crashed control process, a hung Linux, a kernel panic, a cut cable and a power-down of the robot computer, which is why it belongs in the microcontroller and not on the host. The microcontroller's own firmware is watched by its hardware watchdog timer, and its outputs are designed so that a reset of the microcontroller leaves the drive enable off (pull resistors and the reset state of its pins). C1



How the hardware actually does it


In hardware, the e-stop chain is wiring and relays designed for this purpose: a button with positively opening, normally-closed contacts, often two channels so that a single wire fault is detected; a safety relay or safety controller that monitors both channels, needs a reset input, and switches the motor contactor or the drives' torque-off inputs, possibly with a delay so that the drives can first ramp down. Motor drives with a safe torque off input remove the motor's ability to produce torque without removing the supply, so they restart faster. Mechanical brakes on vertical axes hold when power is lost. The robot computer's part is an input that reads the chain's state through an isolated input, and an output (on the microcontroller) to the drive enable. A hardware watchdog timer is a counter in the SoC or the microcontroller that resets the chip if it is not reloaded in time. None of this hardware was available in the build container; the QEMU e-stop is a key on an emulated GPIO controller. D4 C2


Safety
Never wire an e-stop as an input that software reads and then acts on as the only stop: that is a "software e-stop" and it fails exactly when the software fails. Never connect the course robot's motors to power without the hard-wired chain in place and tested. Test the e-stop at the start of every session, with the robot on blocks for the first test after any change to wiring, firmware or image. When testing watchdogs on a real robot, induce the fault (a stall, a killed process, an unplugged cable) with the wheels off the ground or with motor power off at the chain, and only then on the floor in a clear area. Electrical work on the chain itself (mains, contactors, relays) is for qualified people. C2

Worked example


A. The stops of safety.out by hand. Watchdog: last kick at 999 ms (the control task ran at 999, not at 1000); the condition is "now − last kick > 50", first true at 1050 (51 > 50). From 500 mm/s at 2 mm/s per ms, the command reaches 0 after 500 / 2 = 250 steps; the first decrement is at 1050, so the 250th is at 1050 + 249 = 1299. E-stop: after the reset at 1600 the control task ramps by 1 mm/s per ms from 0: its command is 1 at 1600 and 400 at 1999. The supervisor detects the e-stop at 2000 before it copies a new command, so the drive command it prints is the one of the previous step, 400 mm/s. 400 / 2 = 200 steps, the first at 2000: 2000 + 199 = 2199. Both match the output. R1


B. Choosing the timeout from a distance. The course robot's maximum speed is 0.5 m/s, and the safety review allows at most 50 mm of travel on a stale command before the stop begins. The worst-case detection time is T + P + the watchdog's own delay d. With P = 5 ms and a budget of d = 5 ms: 0.5 m/s × (T + 10 ms) ≤ 0.05 m gives T ≤ 90 ms. Then add the stopping distance of the ramp: from 0.5 m/s at 2 m/s², v² / (2a) = 0.25 / 4 = 62.5 mm. The robot needs 50 + 62.5 = 112.5 mm of clear space in front of it at full speed, a number the planner's safety margin must respect.


C. The reset rule as a truth table. Inputs: state is LOCKED (L), e-stop released (R), watchdog healthy (H). Accept only if L ∧ R ∧ H. At 2400: L true, R false: refused, and the message names the reason. At 2600: all three true: accepted. A reset in READY or STOPPING is refused: the reset is not a way to cancel a stop in progress.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Ama's brother can see her and guess what is wrong. A watchdog sees only the kick; it cannot tell a stuck task from a crashed one, and it should not try before acting.
    • 

  • Ama brakes herself. A robot's e-stop must not depend on the robot's "self" (its software) at all; the analogy would be brakes that clamp when anyone pulls a cord, whatever the rider is doing.
    • 

  • Standing at the roadside is safe for a cyclist. For a robot arm with a load, or a drone, "stopped" is not automatically safe; the safe state is designed per hazard.
    • 

  • A brother who shouts too often is merely annoying. A watchdog with too short a timeout stops the robot on ordinary jitter, and people then raise the timeout or disable it; nuisance trips are a safety problem, not a convenience one.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • A software e-stop. The button is read by software and the software stops the motors. Fix: the hard-wired chain; software only monitors.
    • 

  • Kicking from a timer or a separate thread. Symptom: the watchdog never fires, even in a test where the control task is stuck. Find it: a stall test with the stale-command measurement (forensic lab). Fix: kick at the end of a good control cycle.
    • 

  • Auto-resume. Symptom: the robot moves again by itself when the e-stop is pulled up or the fault clears. Fix: latch; reset needs an operator; reset does not start motion.
    • 

  • Resuming at the old speed. Symptom: after a reset the robot jumps to the speed it had before the stop. Cause: the controller kept its command or integrator. Fix: controllers reset to zero outside READY (Listing 1 line 103) and an invariant that forbids jumps.
    • 

  • Timeout chosen for convenience. Symptom: nuisance trips, or none in a real stall. Fix: derive it from allowed travel and measured jitter (worked example B).
    • 

  • A failed component clearing its own fault. Symptom: the watchdog fires and is immediately satisfied by a late kick. Fix: once tripped, only a reset clears it (Listing 2 line 42).
    • 

  • Testing the e-stop only at commissioning. Symptom: a contact welded or a wire came loose months ago. Fix: test at the start of each session and log the test.
    •

Summary


    

  • The safe state is chosen per hazard; for the course's wheeled robot it is "speed 0, drive enable off".
    • 

  • The e-stop is a hard-wired chain that removes drive power or torque with no software in the path; software monitors it, ramps its commands to zero and refuses to resume.
    • 

  • Stops are immediate power removal or a controlled ramp followed by power removal; the chain's delay backs up the ramp.
    • 

  • A watchdog detects a stop within T + P plus its own scheduling delay; the timeout is a safety parameter derived from the travel allowed on a stale command.
    • 

  • The kick must come from the end of a good control cycle; a timer kick watches nothing, as the forensic lab shows.
    • 

  • Safe states are latched; reset needs the cause cleared, the system healthy and an operator, and never starts motion.
    • 

  • Watchdogs are layered: host supervisor, microcontroller heartbeat timeout, hardware watchdog timer. The course image's e-stop test in QEMU passed with zero non-zero commands after the press; it was not tested on hardware.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. safety_forensic.in replays the demonstration: from time 0 the kick source is a timer (release 3's change), and at 1000 ms the control task stalls for 400 ms. Listing 1 ran on it (R4).


Observation. In release 2's run a 200 ms stall produces "FAULT: watchdog" 51 ms after the last kick and a stop. In release 3's run there is no FAULT line, no STOPPING, no LOCKED: the robot stays READY at 500 mm/s throughout. The longest stale-command time is 400 ms: the drive followed a command that nobody had refreshed for 400 ms, during which the robot travelled 0.5 m/s × 0.4 s = 200 mm. That matches "drove into the table edge at walking pace".


Root cause. Release 3 kicks the watchdog from a timer (kick_source timer; Listing 1 lines 109–111): every step, whatever the control task does. The watchdog therefore watches the timer, which never stops. The nuisance stops that motivated the change should have been fixed by a timeout derived from measured jitter (worked example B) or by fixing the jitter.


Why the invariants passed. They check the supervisor's own behaviour (no command while disabled, no increase outside READY, no jump). None of them says "the drive must not follow a stale command"; that property was only measured and printed, not checked. Lab step 2 adds it, and then this run fails.


Other hypotheses. "The timeout was raised above 400 ms": the first line of the output shows 50 ms. "The watchdog fired but the stop was slow": there is no FAULT line at all, and the stale time equals the whole stall.


Fix and prevention. Kick from the end of a good control cycle (release 2's behaviour). Add the stale-command invariant. Keep a stall scenario in the release tests: it fails release 3. Second layer: the microcontroller's heartbeat timeout (lab step 6), fed by sequence numbers that only the control task produces, would have ramped the robot down about 20 ms after the stall began, whatever the host's watchdog did.
