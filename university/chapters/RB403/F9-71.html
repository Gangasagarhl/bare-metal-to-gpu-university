Story hook

All winter, Efua trains on a stationary bicycle trainer in her living room. The trainer is perfect: the road is flat, there is no wind, the "speed" on its display is exactly what the rollers turn. She learns to hold 30 km/h without thinking. In spring she rides outside for the first time. The road has gravel, a headwind pushes back, her tyres are a little soft, and her new bicycle computer, which she set up in a hurry, is configured for the wrong wheel size. She pedals exactly as she learned, the computer says 30, and her friend on the next bicycle, with a correctly set computer, says she is doing 24.

Nothing she learned was wrong. The trainer simply left out everything that makes the road hard, and the one thing she trusted to tell her how she was doing had a wrong number in it.

Robots go from simulator to floor the same way. A controller tuned in simulation meets delays, friction, battery sag, sensor noise and calibration errors it has never seen. Most of these make it worse; one kind, a wrong parameter in the sensor path, makes it confidently wrong. This chapter runs one controller against a series of increasingly real motors, finds which differences matter and how much, and investigates a bench robot that reports the right speed while driving at half of it.

What you will be able to do

  • List the effects that simulators commonly leave out, and predict how each changes a closed-loop controller's behaviour.
  • Measure the effect of each difference separately, changing one thing at a time, with fixed metrics.
  • Compute the delay margin of a simple speed loop and check it against simulation runs.
  • Explain why a calibration error in the sensor path is invisible to the controller and how an independent reference (ground truth) exposes it.
  • Plan a staged path from simulation to the floor (software-in-the-loop, hardware-in-the-loop, bench, supervised floor) with gates and safety rules, as MP6 requires.
Jargon box
Reality gap
Simple words: the differences between the simulated robot and the real one that change how the software behaves. Analogy: the living-room trainer versus the gravel road. Precise: the set of unmodelled or mis-modelled effects (delays, friction, saturation, quantisation, noise, parameter errors, compliance, contacts, timing jitter) between a simulation and the physical system, measured by the difference in closed-loop behaviour of the same software under the same scenario. C2 R1
Delay margin
Simple words: how much extra delay a control loop can take before it starts to oscillate without settling. Analogy: how long you can look away from the road before your steering corrections start to make you weave. Precise: for a stable loop with gain crossover frequency ωc and phase margin φm (in radians), the largest added pure time delay that keeps it stable, φm / ωc. D1
System identification
Simple words: measuring the real robot to find the numbers that the simulator should use. Analogy: timing yourself on the real road to set the trainer's resistance. Precise: estimating the parameters (and sometimes the structure) of a dynamic model from measured inputs and outputs, for example a motor's gain, time constant, dead time and friction from a recorded step response. D2
Domain randomisation
Simple words: testing (or training) in many simulated worlds whose parameters are varied, so that what works does not depend on one exact guess. Analogy: training on the trainer with the resistance changed every few minutes. Precise: sampling the uncertain simulation parameters (delays, friction, masses, sensor noise, calibration) from ranges that cover the real system, and requiring the controller or policy to meet its criteria over the whole range. D3
Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL)
Simple words: running the real robot computer and its real software against a simulated robot body. Analogy: the real bicycle mounted on the trainer. Precise: a test set-up in which the target hardware runs the production software and exchanges real signals (or messages on the real interfaces) with a real-time simulation of the plant; contrasted with software-in-the-loop (SIL), where the production software runs on a development computer against the simulator. C2
Calibration parameter
Simple words: a number in the software that describes the real hardware, such as how many encoder counts make one turn or how big the wheel is. Analogy: the wheel size set in the bicycle computer. Precise: a constant in the measurement or actuation path (encoder counts per revolution, gear ratio, wheel radius, sensor offset and scale) that converts between raw hardware units and physical units; an error in it scales or offsets every measurement, and a feedback loop closed on that measurement faithfully tracks the wrong quantity. R2
Encoder quantisation
Simple words: an encoder counts whole steps, so speed computed from counts jumps in fixed steps. Analogy: counting fence posts to guess your speed. Precise: speed estimated as Δcount × 2π / (CPR × Δt) can only take multiples of 2π / (CPR × Δt); with 1024 counts per revolution and Δt = 1 ms, that step is 6.14 rad/s. R2

Terms used from earlier courses: robot simulator (physics), simulated clock (sim time), real-time factor and ground truth (RB303, F9-44); PID and anti-windup (RB201 to RB301); encoder (RB101); flight recorder (F9-70).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A simulator is a pretend robot inside the computer. It is very useful, because nothing can break and nobody can get hurt. But the pretend robot is too perfect. Its wheels never slip, its motors answer at once, its battery never gets tired, and its sensors never make mistakes. So a robot program that works perfectly on the pretend robot can wobble, overshoot or be slow on the real one.

The trick is to add the real robot's problems to the pretend robot one at a time and watch what each does: a little delay, some friction, a tired battery, a sensor that counts in steps. Then you know which problems matter. And before you trust the real robot's own speed reading, you check it with something else, like a friend measuring with a stopwatch, because if the robot has one wrong number in its settings, it will believe it is going at the right speed when it is not.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The lab's controller is a PI speed controller for one wheel motor, tuned on an ideal model: a first-order motor (gain 10 rad/s per volt, time constant 50 ms), a perfect speed sensor, no delay, no friction, a 12 V supply. It gets a step from 0 to 60 rad/s. Then the same controller, with the same gains, drives seven other motors, each adding effects that simulators commonly leave out:

  • Delay. The command reaches the motor later than it was computed: communication to the motor microcontroller, the drive's own loop, the scheduling of the control thread (F9-66). Delay is the effect a feedback loop tolerates least, because the controller reacts to the past.
  • Friction. A constant force against motion (Coulomb friction): the integral term must supply extra command to overcome it.
  • Battery sag. A lower supply voltage limits the largest command; the controller saturates longer and responds more slowly.
  • Encoder quantisation and noise. Speed is computed from whole counts; at 1 ms sampling it moves in steps of 6.14 rad/s with 1024 counts per revolution, and jitter on the count adds noise.
  • A calibration error. The software's parameter for counts per revolution differs from the encoder that was fitted.

The rules of the comparison come from BR-08: the same software, the same scenario, the same metrics (overshoot, settling time, steady-state speed, time in saturation), and one change at a time. Changing two things at once is a trap: when the behaviour changes, you cannot tell which change did it. C2

The results (Layer 3) teach three lessons. First, most effects cost performance gracefully: the robot settles a little later. Second, delay has a cliff: below a certain delay the loop barely notices, above it the loop never settles. Where the cliff is can be computed, and should be, before the robot ever moves. Third, a calibration error is in a class of its own: the loop does exactly what it was designed to do, and its own measurements say everything is fine. Only an independent reference, ground truth in the language of RB303, reveals it.

The way across the gap is staged (Figure 3): randomise the simulation's uncertain parameters over ranges that cover the real robot; run the production software and image against the simulator (software-in-the-loop); run it on the real robot computer and microcontroller against a simulated motor (hardware-in-the-loop); then the bench, with the wheels off the ground and an external speed reference; then the floor, supervised, at reduced speed, after the safety review. Keep the same logs and flight recorder (F9-70) in every stage, so that the comparison is between records, not impressions.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

One controller, eight motors. Listing 1, sim2real.cpp, runs each case for 1.5 s in 1 ms control steps, integrates the motor in ten sub-steps per control step, and reports overshoot, settling time (to within 5 % of the target, judged on the true speed), the mean true and measured speed over the last 0.2 s, and the share of steps in saturation. All "robots" are models in the program; they stand in for hardware the build container does not have and are only as true as their equations. R1





Read it one row at a time against ideal_sim (settles in 77 ms, no overshoot). Adding the encoder costs 5 ms and a trace of overshoot: quantisation noise. A 5 ms delay costs 14 ms of settling time, a 15 ms delay costs 89 ms and brings 4.7 % overshoot, and a 30 ms delay makes the loop oscillate for the whole run (28.2 % overshoot, never settled, 30.6 % of the time in saturation, a mean of 46 rad/s instead of 60). Friction costs 11 ms: the integrator supplies the extra command. A 7 V battery costs 47 ms: the command saturates three times as long. All effects together (5 ms, friction, noise, 9 V, the encoder) settle only after 1305 ms and spend 16 % of the time saturated: the effects interact, which is why each must first be understood alone. R1


Why delay has a cliff: the delay margin. The PI controller is C(s) = 0.4 + 8/s = 0.4(s + 20)/s and the motor is P(s) = 10/(0.05s + 1) = 200/(s + 20). The controller's zero at s = −20 cancels the motor's pole, so the open loop is L(s) = 80/s: an integrator. Its gain is 1 at ωc = 80 rad/s and its phase is −90° everywhere, so the phase margin is 90° = π/2 rad. A pure delay Td subtracts ωTd radians of phase at ω without changing the gain, so the loop loses its margin when 80 × Td = π/2: Td = π/160 s ≈ 19.6 ms. The digital controller adds roughly one more millisecond of its own (it samples, then applies the command for a whole step), so the cliff should lie between about 18 and 20 ms of added delay. The lab checks it: D1






With 17 and 18 ms the loop still settles, slowly (353 ms, 667 ms) and with growing overshoot; from 19 ms it does not settle in the run. The calculation was right to about a millisecond. Two details deserve care. The "1398 ms" settling time of the 22 ms case is an artefact of the metric: the oscillating speed happened to be inside the 5 % band for the last steps of the run, and the mean of 53.9 rad/s shows that it was still oscillating; a metric must be read together with others. And delay_22_nosat, the same delay with practically unlimited voltage, diverges (−319 rad/s by the end): beyond the margin the linear loop is unstable, and only saturation keeps the real motor in a bounded oscillation. On a robot, that bounded oscillation is a violently shaking wheel. R3


The same algebra explains the ideal case: a closed loop of L = 80/s responds like a first-order system with time constant 1/80 s = 12.5 ms and is within 5 % after 3 time constants, 37.5 ms; no_saturation settles in 35 ms (the sampled loop is slightly faster than the continuous estimate at this resolution). The 77 ms of ideal_sim come from the 12 V limit: the first command, 0.4 × 60 = 24 V, is clamped, and the motor accelerates at its maximum until the error is small. A simulator that models the controller but not the supply limit will promise 35 ms.


What the delay margin means for the stack. Every millisecond between "sensor read" and "command applied" counts against the 19.6 ms: the encoder read, the control thread's scheduling latency (F9-66), the message to the motor microcontroller, the microcontroller's own loop, the drive's update rate. A control loop that runs on Linux and sends its command over a bus with a 10 ms period has spent half its margin before anything goes wrong. That is the quantitative argument for the split architecture of F9-66: put the speed loop on the microcontroller, where the delay is a fraction of a millisecond, and send it set points from Linux. D1

The delay-margin formula φm/ωc and the loop-shaping argument are standard control theory recalled by the author; the textbooks named in the Sources were not opened in this build. The numbers in this chapter come from the lab's own model and runs, not from any real motor. A real motor has more dynamics (electrical time constant, gearbox compliance, backlash, a current loop in the drive), each of which takes phase away and shrinks the margin; identify the real motor (lab step 5) before relying on 19.6 ms or anything near it.


Calibration errors are invisible from inside. A wrong encoder parameter scales every speed measurement. The controller, which is designed to make the measured speed equal the target, does exactly that, and its logs, its telemetry, its flight recorder and its "settled" checks all report success. Only a measurement that does not pass through the same parameter can show the error: an external tachometer, a motion-capture system, a measured distance over a timed run, or the simulator's ground truth in a simulation that models the same error. This is the subject of the forensic lab. The direction of the error decides the danger: if the encoder has more counts than the software believes, the robot moves slower than it reports; if it has fewer, the robot moves faster than it reports, and every software speed limit and every stopping-distance calculation of F9-69 is wrong by the same factor. margin.out's last line shows the second case: true 117.7 rad/s against a measured 58.9. R3


Simulators and their clocks. A simulator runs on a simulated clock, which may run faster or slower than real time (the real-time factor, RB303). Software that measures time with the wall clock instead of the simulated clock behaves differently in simulation and on the robot; software that assumes the simulator's perfect periodicity breaks on the robot's jitter. The deployment of F9-68 should switch only the clock source and the hardware interface between simulation and robot, nothing else, so that the same image, configuration and units run in both. D4

How the hardware actually does it


On the kit robot of MP6, the speed loop runs on the motor microcontroller at a rate of a kilohertz or more; Linux sends set points. The encoder is read by a hardware counter (a timer in encoder mode) so that no count is missed; speed at low rates is better measured from the time between edges than from counts per period, which avoids the 6.14 rad/s steps of the lab. The drive limits current, which the lab does not model, and the battery's voltage falls with load and charge state. Friction differs between wheels and changes with temperature and floor. The external reference for the bench is a hand-held or clamped tachometer, or a known distance and a stopwatch; for the floor, a motion-capture system if one is available. System identification on the bench: record a voltage step with the wheels off the ground, fit gain, time constant and dead time, and put the numbers into the simulator. None of this hardware was used in this build. D2 C1


Safety
The first run of any controller on real hardware is a bench run: the robot on blocks with its wheels off the ground, the hard-wired e-stop tested and in hand, speed limits set in the motor microcontroller, and nobody's fingers near the wheels or the gearbox. Expect the worst case of Layer 3: a loop beyond its delay margin, or a calibration error that makes the robot faster than it believes. Then the floor, supervised, in a clear zone, at reduced speed, only after the MP6 safety and readiness review (gate R3: e-stop tested, speed limits set, clear zone, supervision arranged, battery handling per the maker's instructions). Change one thing at a time between runs. For drones (BR-08, MP7), propellers off on the bench and the local rules read and recorded. C2

Worked example


A. The quantisation step. With 1024 counts per revolution and Δt = 1 ms, one count per sample is 2π / 1024 rad per 0.001 s = 6.136 rad/s. At 60 rad/s the encoder advances 60 / (2π) × 1024 × 0.001 = 9.78 counts per sample, so the measurement alternates mostly between 9 and 10 counts (55.2 and 61.4 rad/s), and with the encoder jitter of the bench case sometimes 8 or 11 (49.1 and 67.5). Those four values are exactly the ones in the forensic trace (whose encoder also gives about 9.8 counts per sample at its true 30 rad/s). Averaging over 10 samples gives steps of 0.61 rad/s at the cost of 5 ms of delay (half the window); measuring the time between edges avoids both. R2


B. The delay budget of the course robot. Margin 19.6 ms; keep at least half in reserve against unmodelled dynamics: budget 9.8 ms. Spend it: sampling and zero-order hold ≈ 1 ms; worst-case scheduling latency of the control thread as measured on the robot (F9-66), say 1 ms with PREEMPT_RT and an isolated CPU; one message to the microcontroller on a bus with a 2 ms period, worst case 2 ms; the drive's update, 1 ms. Total 5 ms: within budget. Running the loop on Linux with commands over a 10 ms bus would exceed it before any scheduling problem. (The 1 ms latency is an assumption to be replaced by your measurement.)


C. The steady state with a calibration error. The controller drives the measured speed to 60 rad/s. Measured = true × CPRtrue / CPRassumed. With 2048 true and 1024 assumed, true = 60 × 1024 / 2048 = 30 rad/s; with 1024 true and 2048 assumed, true would be 120 rad/s, which this motor can reach only near its 12 V limit (10 × 12 = 120 rad/s), so the run saturates more than half the time and reaches 117.7. Both match the outputs. R2 R3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Efua feels the headwind and the gravel and adapts at once. A controller with fixed gains does not adapt; it does what its gains say, whatever the road.
    • 

  • Her friend's correctly set computer is an obvious reference. On a robot, an independent reference has to be planned and brought: nothing on the robot is independent of its own parameters unless it was designed to be.
    • 

  • A trainer that is too easy makes Efua slow on the road. A simulator that is too easy can make a robot unstable: below the delay margin it is merely slower, above it it shakes.
    • 

  • Riding outside for the first time is a nice day out. Running a robot on the floor for the first time is a hazard to be controlled, with a bench stage, an e-stop and a review.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Trusting simulation gains on hardware. Symptom: oscillation or shaking at the first floor run. Fix: bench first; compute the delay margin with the real delays; identify the real motor.
    • 

  • Changing two things at once. Symptom: behaviour changed and nobody can say why. Fix: one change per run, logged.
    • 

  • Validating against the robot's own sensors only. Symptom: "it reaches 60 rad/s" while the robot is visibly slow (or fast). Fix: an external reference on the bench.
    • 

  • Reading one metric alone. Symptom: a "settled" result for a loop that oscillates (the 22 ms artefact). Fix: read overshoot, settling, means and saturation together; look at the trace.
    • 

  • No delay in the simulation. Symptom: the simulator promises a fast, crisp response. Fix: model the measured delays of your stack (F9-66, F9-70) and randomise them.
    • 

  • Calibration parameters not tied to the hardware revision. Symptom: a new motor or wheel and an old parameter file. Fix: calibration in a per-robot file checked at start-up against a hardware identity (F9-68's readiness checks).
    • 

  • Skipping the bench. Never. BR-08 and MP6 make it a gate.
    •

Forensic lab


Scenario. Release 6 of the course robot went to the bench after a hardware revision: new wheel motors from another supplier. The simulator run of release 6 was perfect. On the bench, the robot's own log says the wheel reaches its 60 rad/s set point within a few hundred milliseconds and holds it. But the technician, who had clamped a tachometer to the wheel as the bench procedure requires, reads about 30 rad/s, and the robot needs twice as long as planned to cover a measured distance. The software team says "the tachometer is broken; our logs show 60".

Summary


    

  • The reality gap is measured, not argued: same software, same scenario, same metrics, one change at a time.
    • 

  • Friction, battery sag, quantisation and noise cost performance gradually; in the lab's runs each added tens of milliseconds to settling.
    • 

  • Delay has a cliff at the delay margin φm/ωc; for the lab's loop π/160 s ≈ 19.6 ms, confirmed by runs to within about a millisecond. Beyond it, only saturation bounds the oscillation.
    • 

  • Every millisecond between sensing and actuation counts against the margin, which argues for closing fast loops on the microcontroller.
    • 

  • Calibration errors are invisible from inside the loop; an independent reference exposes them, and an error in the dangerous direction defeats speed limits.
    • 

  • The path to the floor is staged: randomised simulation, SIL, HIL, bench with e-stop and reference, supervised floor after the safety review.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. sim2real_forensic.in: the simulator case uses the 1024-count encoder the software assumes. The bench case models the revised hardware: a 2048-count encoder while the parameter still says 1024, plus a 3 ms delay, friction 60 and encoder jitter; the "true" speed plays the tachometer (R2).


Observation. Measured mean 60.0 rad/s, true mean 30.0: a ratio of exactly 0.5. The trace shows the measured speed taking only the values 49.09, 55.22, 61.36 and 67.50, steps of 6.14 rad/s (one count per sample at the assumed 1024 CPR), and the true speed steady near 30. Saturation is 0.7 %: the controller has plenty of voltage left.


The tachometer is not broken. Its reading is steady and plausible, and it tracks the start of the step at the same time as the robot's measurement (both rise between 100 and 200 ms). A broken tachometer would not produce an exact factor of two with the robot's measurement.


Hypotheses. Friction of the new motors: the integral term compensates friction, so it would not leave a lasting offset between measured and true speed; and both measures would agree. A weak battery: the controller would saturate, and saturation is 0.7 %; also the measured speed would fall short too. A calibration parameter: an exact factor of 2 is what a CPR mismatch of 2048 against 1024 produces (worked example C); the new motors' encoder has 2048 counts. This hypothesis explains all the evidence.


Why "settled" was reported. The robot's check uses the measured speed, which is at 60 (within its quantisation). The model's own settle metric, judged on the true speed, says "never".


The reverse error. With a 1024-count encoder and a 2048 parameter, the robot drives at about twice the commanded speed while reporting the commanded speed (margin.out, last line: true 117.7, measured 58.9, saturated 56 %). Every speed limit and stopping-distance calculation would be wrong by a factor of two, in the dangerous direction, and the logs would still look fine.


Fix and prevention. Set CPR to 2048 in the per-robot calibration file. Prevention: tie calibration to a hardware identity read at start-up (the motor microcontroller reports its encoder type; a mismatch blocks readiness, F9-68); and keep the bench procedure's external-reference check as a release gate, with a tolerance (for example 5 %) between measured and reference speed.
