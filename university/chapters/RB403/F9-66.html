Story hook

Lucía's band needs a drummer for its tour. Two people apply. Tomás is brilliant: he knows every style, talks to the audience, fixes the sound desk and remembers every song the band has ever played. But once or twice an evening, when someone asks him a question in the middle of a song, he comes in a fraction late. Ines plays only the beat, nothing else, and has never once been late in her life.

Lucía cannot decide, so she asks the band what a missed beat would cost. In the slow songs, nothing much. In the opening number, where the dancers jump on the beat, a late beat means somebody lands badly. So the band hires both: Ines keeps the beat for the dancers, Tomás does everything else and plays along. They agree on one rule: Tomás never tells Ines what to do in the middle of a bar.

Choosing an operating system for a robot is the same decision. Linux is Tomás. A real-time operating system on a microcontroller is Ines. This chapter shows how to decide, with requirements, measurements and arithmetic rather than habits, and measures what the build machine's Linux actually does.

What you will be able to do

  • Compare Linux with the real-time preemption model, an RTOS on a microcontroller, and a split architecture, against requirements that you write down first.
  • Measure the wake-up latency of a periodic thread under different scheduling policies and loads, and present it as a histogram with percentiles and a maximum.
  • Explain why a measured maximum is a lower bound on the true worst case, and what that means for a hard deadline.
  • Build a decision matrix with hard requirements and weights, and test how sensitive the choice is to the weights.
  • Diagnose a latency histogram whose tail is caused by a wrongly prioritised real-time thread on the same CPU.
Jargon box
Latency histogram
Simple words: a table that counts how often each delay happened. Analogy: counting how many beats were on time, a little late, or very late, over a whole concert. Precise: the distribution of a measured latency over many samples, binned by value, reported with minimum, median, high percentiles (for example p99) and the maximum; for real-time work the tail and the maximum matter more than the mean. D1 R1
SCHED_FIFO
Simple words: a Linux setting that lets a thread run before all normal threads, until it sleeps or a more important one comes. Analogy: the drummer who plays first, before anyone may speak. Precise: a POSIX real-time scheduling policy in Linux: fixed priorities (1 to 99 on Linux), preemptive, first-in-first-out among threads of equal priority, no time slicing; a SCHED_FIFO thread runs ahead of every SCHED_OTHER (normal) thread on its CPU. D4 H1
Split architecture (Linux plus microcontroller)
Simple words: a robot with two kinds of computer: Linux for the rich work, a microcontroller with an RTOS for the fast, strict work. Analogy: the band with two drummers and one rule between them. Precise: a design in which hard-deadline loops run on a separate processor under an RTOS or bare-metal firmware, the application processor runs Linux (often with PREEMPT_RT) for perception, planning, middleware and coordination, and a defined protocol over a bus carries setpoints down and state up, with each side able to reach a safe state if the other stops. C2 C1
CPU isolation
Simple words: keeping some processor cores free of everything except the work you choose. Analogy: a practice room where only the drummer may go. Precise: configuring the kernel and the system so that chosen CPUs receive no general tasks, as few interrupts and kernel housekeeping as possible, and only the threads pinned to them; done with kernel boot parameters, interrupt affinity and CPU affinity of threads (the exact parameters are listed, unverified, in Layer 3). D1
Decision matrix
Simple words: a table that scores each choice against what matters, so the decision can be checked and argued. Analogy: the band's list of what a missed beat costs in each song. Precise: a table of candidate options against weighted criteria; options failing a hard requirement are excluded first, the rest ranked by weighted score, and the ranking is tested for sensitivity to the weights (a choice that flips when one weight changes slightly is not a decision yet). R2

Terms from RB304 used here without redefinition: wake-up latency, jitter, PREEMPT_RT, threaded interrupt handler and real-time throttling (F9-45, F9-47); RTOS from OS305.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Some computers are like a clever friend who can do anything but sometimes answers a moment late. Others are like a metronome: they do very little, but always on time. A robot often needs both. So engineers write down which jobs must never be late, measure how late the clever computer really is, and give the strict jobs to the metronome. Then they let the two talk, with one rule: the clever one may ask, but it may never make the metronome wait.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

RB304 showed that "fast" is not "real-time": what matters is the worst case, not the average (F9-45), and that Linux's latency depends on its preemption model, its configuration and the hardware (F9-47). This chapter turns those facts into a decision for the whole robot. D1

Linux with PREEMPT_RT gives you the drivers (cameras, LiDARs, networks, storage), ROS 2, a file system, large memory and the tools you already know. The real-time preemption model makes most kernel code preemptible and runs interrupt handlers as threads with priorities, so the wake-up latency of a high-priority thread becomes small and is bounded by short non-preemptible sections; how small, on your hardware, you must measure. The price: a large code base whose worst case you cannot analyse line by line, and timing that depends on drivers and firmware you do not control. D1

An RTOS on a microcontroller (Zephyr, FreeRTOS and others, OS305) gives you a small kernel with fixed-priority preemptive scheduling, short and analysable interrupt latency, and a program small enough to review. The price: few drivers, little memory, no full ROS 2 (micro-ROS bridges to it, F9-51), and more of the work written by you. D2 D3

Both is the split architecture of Figure 1C, the pattern BR-05 and RB304 already pointed at: motor loops on the microcontroller, coordinating control and everything rich on Linux. Its new cost is the interface: a protocol, its timing, and what each side does when the other goes silent. C2

The decision is made in four steps. (1) Write the requirements: for every loop its rate, deadline and criticality (F9-65). (2) Measure what each candidate gives for the loops that might run on it: the wake-up latency histogram under the real load (Listing 1). (3) Exclude candidates that fail a hard requirement. (4) Rank the rest with a weighted matrix and test the ranking's sensitivity (Listing 2). R1 R2

Watch out
A measured maximum is a lower bound on the true worst case: the worst case is at least that bad, and may be worse, because the run may not have met the worst combination of events. For a hard deadline you need either an argument for the bound (analysable code, as on the microcontroller) or measurements long enough, under realistic worst-case load, that the remaining risk is acceptable, plus a safe reaction when the deadline is still missed (F9-69). Never present a median as "the latency". D2



Making Linux suitable: the configuration checklist


If Linux runs a loop with a deadline, the image (F9-67) and the system must be configured for it. The real-time preemption documentation of the kernel is the source to read; the checklist below is this course's summary of what must be decided, and every item is measured, not assumed. D1


    

  1. The kernel's preemption model: the fully preemptible (real-time) model selected in the kernel configuration.
    2. 

  2. CPU isolation for the real-time threads: no general tasks, as few interrupts and kernel housekeeping activities as possible on those CPUs.
    3. 

  3. Interrupt affinity: device interrupts (cameras, network, storage) steered to the non-isolated CPUs; the threaded handlers' priorities set below the control loop unless the loop depends on that device.
    4. 

  4. The real-time threads: SCHED_FIFO with priorities assigned by the design (rate-monotonic among them, F9-65), memory locked and pre-faulted, no allocation, no blocking I/O in the loop (F9-49).
    5. 

  5. Power management: deep idle states and frequency changes on the isolated CPUs decided and measured, because leaving a deep idle state takes time.
    6. 

  6. Firmware settings of the board or PC that take the processor away from the OS, checked against the board's documentation.
    7. 

  7. Measurement under the worst realistic load, for long periods, after every image change.
    8.

The RTOS side and the interface


On the microcontroller, the RTOS gives fixed-priority preemptive tasks and interrupt handlers whose worst-case latency is set by the hardware's interrupt entry, the longest section with interrupts disabled, and the RTOS's own critical sections; the RTOS documentation states how it handles each (OS305). Because the program is small, you can reason about its worst case with RB304's response-time analysis. D2 D3


The split architecture's risk moves to the interface. Write it as a contract: message types and units; the rate of setpoints from Linux; the microcontroller's timeout (if no valid setpoint arrives within a stated time, ramp to zero and disable the drive); sequence numbers or counters to detect stale or repeated messages; a heartbeat in both directions; and the state machine each side runs when the other side goes silent. This contract is what lets the microcontroller stay safe when Linux is late, which is the whole point of the split. F9-69 implements the supervisor side. C1



Other ways to get "both"


Two further patterns put an RTOS and Linux on one chip: asymmetric multiprocessing (different cores of the same SoC run Linux and an RTOS, sharing memory and an inter-processor interrupt), and partitioning by a hypervisor (each OS in its own partition). They save a chip and a bus, and they share more: memory controllers, caches and sometimes clocks and power. Whether the isolation is good enough is a question for the SoC's documentation and for measurement. C3

How the hardware actually does it


A wake-up begins in hardware: a timer in the processor (on x86 the local APIC timer, on Arm the generic timer, programmed through the kernel's high-resolution timer code) raises an interrupt at the programmed time. The core must finish or abandon the instruction it is executing, save state and enter the kernel's interrupt path. If interrupts are disabled at that moment (by a non-preemptible kernel section, a driver's critical section, or firmware such as system management mode on PCs), entry waits. Then the scheduler must run, which on a non-PREEMPT_RT kernel may wait for a preemption point, and the context switch restores the thread's state; its first memory accesses may miss in cache. On a virtual machine there is one more layer: the host must be running the virtual CPU at all. D1 D4


A microcontroller removes most of these layers: no MMU, often no cache or a small one, no firmware taking the core away, a vectored interrupt controller whose entry sequence its architecture manual specifies, and a program small enough that the longest interrupt-disabled section can be found by reading it. That is why its worst case can be argued instead of only measured. C1 D2

Safety
No operating system choice makes a robot safe on its own: Linux with PREEMPT_RT, an RTOS or both only change how predictable the software is. The emergency stop stays a hard-wired chain with no software in the path (F9-69). Latency tests with real-time load generators can make a computer unresponsive for seconds; run them on a bench machine or on the robot computer only with motor power off at the e-stop chain, never while it controls a robot that can move. C3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A drummer who is late once is late by a fraction of a beat. A Linux thread can be late by many periods at once (the forensic lab shows a whole 20 ms burst), and nothing in the analogy prepares you for that tail.
    • 

  • Ines never fails in the story. A microcontroller with an RTOS can fail too: a stack overflow, a stuck bus, a brown-out. The split architecture is safe only because each side is designed to make the motors safe when the other goes silent, not because one side is perfect.
    • 

  • Two drummers hear each other directly. Linux and the microcontroller talk through a bus with its own latency, losses and failure modes, which the contract must cover.
    • 

  • The band chose by asking what a missed beat costs. That part of the analogy holds exactly: requirements first, then technology.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Reporting the mean. Symptom: "average latency 50 µs" in a review, and a robot that misses deadlines. Fix: histogram, p99, max, run length and load, always together.
    • 

  • Measuring on an idle machine. Symptom: excellent numbers in the lab, misses in the field. Cause: the field has cameras, logging and the network running. Find it: compare the idle and busy runs of this lab. Fix: measure with the real stack running, and with a stress load on top.
    • 

  • SCHED_FIFO silently not applied. Symptom: SCHED_FIFO numbers identical to normal ones. Cause: the program lacks the privilege, ignores the error, and runs as a normal thread. Find it: Listing 1 prints "cannot set scheduling policy" and exits with 3. Fix: grant the capability or run as the service user configured for it (F9-68), and always check the return code.
    • 

  • Relative sleeps. Symptom: the loop's frequency drifts lower under load. Cause: sleeping "1 ms from now" instead of "until t + 1 ms". Fix: absolute deadlines (Listing 1 line 105).
    • 

  • A "very important" helper thread at a high real-time priority. Symptom: periodic large latencies in the control loop. Cause: the forensic lab. Fix: priorities follow the design's analysis, not the author's feelings about importance.
    • 

  • Measuring in a virtual machine and concluding about hardware. This chapter's numbers come from one. Say so in every report.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. Listing 1 with arguments 2000 1000 1 fifo:80 hog:90:20:100: the measuring thread at SCHED_FIFO 80 on CPU 1, and a SCHED_FIFO 90 thread on the same CPU busy for 20 ms then sleeping 80 ms (R3).


Root cause. SCHED_FIFO always runs the highest-priority runnable thread on a CPU. During each 20 ms burst the daemon (90) is runnable and the control thread (80) cannot run, whatever its own priority; every wake-up that falls into a burst waits until the burst ends. With bursts of 20 ms every 100 ms, about 20 % of wake-ups fall into a burst, with waits spread between 0 and 20 ms: the histogram's bins above 1 ms hold roughly one fifth of the samples, and the maximum is close to 20 ms (the burst length), as the run shows. The idle SCHED_FIFO run has neither.


Wrong hypotheses. "The kernel is not PREEMPT_RT": true, but the idle run on the same kernel has no such tail. "The busy load": there is none in this run. "The virtual machine": it adds isolated large values in every run, not a fifth of all samples with a maximum equal to the burst.


Fixes. (1) Run the daemon as a normal-policy thread, or at a SCHED_FIFO priority below the control loop: it may lag, which is acceptable for diagnostics. (2) Move it to a non-isolated CPU. Preferred: both, because the CPU-isolation rule of F9-65 says the control CPU carries only control work. Prevention: the start-up configuration of F9-68 lists every real-time thread with its priority and CPU, a check fails the image build when a thread not in the design list asks for a real-time priority on an isolated CPU, and the latency test runs with the whole stack, including diagnostics, after every image change.
