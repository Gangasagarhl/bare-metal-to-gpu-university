Story hook

Getting on a bicycle has an order that nobody writes down. You hold the brakes before you lift your foot off the ground. You put one foot on a pedal before you push off with the other. You look over your shoulder before you pull out. If you push off first and check the brakes later, you will usually get away with it, until the day the brake cable has snapped. Then the order was the only thing that could have saved you, and you skipped it.

A robot computer that has just booted has the same problem with twenty programs instead of two feet. The camera driver, the motor bridge, the e-stop monitor, the state estimator, the controller and the planner all start at once unless somebody says otherwise. Most of the time they find each other and everything works. One morning the motor microcontroller is slow to answer, the controller starts commanding anyway, and the robot jerks forward the moment its motors are enabled. Nothing crashed. Every program did what it was written to do. What went wrong was the order.

This chapter writes the order down, checks it with a tool, runs it with a small real supervisor, and shows how one word in a unit file can make a robot say "ready" when it is not.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe a robot's software as a set of deployable units, each with a place to run, a configuration, a start command and a readiness signal.
  • Distinguish an ordering dependency ("start after B is running") from a requirement dependency ("start only when B is ready, and not at all if B fails"), and choose the right one for each edge.
  • Compute a start-up timeline by hand and with a tool, find its critical path, and detect dependency cycles.
  • Build a small supervisor that starts real processes in dependency order, waits for a READY message, restarts a failed unit with back-off, gives up after a limit, and stops everything in reverse order.
  • Design the "robot ready" gate so that the robot can never be armed while a unit on its safety or motion path is missing.
Jargon box
Unit (deployable unit)
Simple words: one program of the robot, together with everything needed to start it: where it runs, with which settings, and how it says "I am ready". Analogy: one part of getting on the bicycle (brakes, pedal, look back), each with its own check. Precise: a process (or group of processes, or a container) described by a start command, a target CPU or machine, a configuration (parameters, environment), its dependencies on other units, a readiness signal, and a restart policy; service managers call this a service or a unit, ROS 2 starts nodes through launch files. D1 D2
Ordering dependency
Simple words: "start me after that one has started". It says nothing about whether the other one works. Analogy: sitting down after a foot is on the pedal. Precise: an edge B → A meaning A is launched no earlier than B's process is running; if B never becomes ready, or fails, A is still started. In the lab's format: after=B. R1
Requirement dependency
Simple words: "start me only when that one is working, and not at all if it fails". Analogy: do not push off until the brakes have held. Precise: an edge B → A meaning A is launched only after B has reported ready; if B fails, A is blocked, and so is everything that requires A. In the lab's format: needs=B. R1
Readiness signal
Simple words: a message a program sends when it has finished getting ready, which is later than when it started. Analogy: the squeeze on the brake lever that tells you the brakes hold. Precise: an explicit notification from a unit to its supervisor (here the text READY on file descriptor 3) sent after the unit has opened its devices, loaded its parameters and seen its peers; "the process exists" is not readiness. R2 D2
Topological order
Simple words: a list of the units in which every unit comes after all the units it waits for. Analogy: the order of getting on the bicycle written as a list. Precise: a linear order of the vertices of a directed acyclic graph consistent with every edge; found by Kahn's algorithm (repeatedly take a vertex with no unprocessed incoming edges); exists if and only if the graph has no cycle. D3
Critical path (start-up)
Simple words: the chain of units that decides how long start-up takes; making anything else faster does not help. Analogy: the slowest chain of steps between stopping at the kerb and riding away. Precise: the path in the dependency graph, ending at the target unit, along which each unit's launch time was set by its predecessor's running or ready time; its length is the target's ready time. D3 R1
Restart policy
Simple words: the rule for what the supervisor does when a unit dies: try again, wait longer each time, and give up after a few tries. Analogy: the chain slips off; you put it back once or twice, but if it keeps coming off you stop riding. Precise: a per-unit specification of whether to restart on failure, the delay (often growing: back-off), the maximum number of attempts in a time window, and the action when the limit is reached (here: stay in the safe state and do not arm). R2
Readiness gate
Simple words: one special unit that becomes ready only when every unit the robot needs to move safely is ready; the robot may be armed only after that. Analogy: the moment you are sitting, feet on pedals, brakes checked. Precise: a target unit (here robot_ready) with requirement dependencies on the safety and motion path; arming is refused until it is ready, and lost if one of its requirements fails later. R1

Terms used from earlier courses: node, launch file, parameter file, lifecycle node, discovery and QoS (RB303, F9-40 to F9-42); process, fork and exec (OS201); image and driver binding (F9-67).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When you get on a bicycle, some things must happen before others. You hold the brakes before you push off. You do not need to wait for your bell to work before you ride, but you do need the brakes. A robot is the same. Its programs wake up one by one. The program that listens to the big red stop button wakes first. The program that drives the motors waits for it. The program that decides where to go waits for the program that knows where the robot is. And the robot is not allowed to move until a special "everything is ready" sign is on. If the motor program never wakes up, the sign never comes on, and the robot stays still. That is the whole idea: write down who waits for whom, and make the robot wait for the things that keep it safe.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Deployment answers three questions for each program of the stack: where does it run (which computer, which CPU, which process), with what (which binary, which parameter file, which environment), and when (after what, and with what check). F9-65 placed the components on CPUs and F9-67 built the image that carries them. This chapter is about the "when".

Each program becomes a unit. A unit has two moments that matter: the moment its process is running, and the later moment it is ready. The motor bridge's process runs within milliseconds of being started; it is ready only when the motor microcontroller has answered, which in the course's design takes 400 ms. A unit tells its supervisor that it is ready with an explicit readiness signal. "The process exists" is the most common wrong substitute for readiness.

Units wait for each other in two different ways. "after=B": start me once B is running, whatever happens to B afterwards. That is pure ordering, used where the only need is that B goes first (B creates a directory, B should get its log in first). "needs=B": start me only once B is ready, and do not start me at all if B fails. That is a requirement, used where A cannot do its job without B. The two look almost the same in a unit file. They behave completely differently when something fails, and the forensic lab of this chapter is about exactly that difference.

Write the dependencies as a directed graph and the start-up order falls out: a topological order, computed with Kahn's algorithm. If the graph has a cycle (A needs B and B needs A), there is no order, and the tool must say so instead of starting anything. Give each unit a time to start and a time to become ready, and the same pass computes when every unit will be ready, and which chain of units, the critical path, decides when the whole robot is ready. Making a unit off the critical path faster does nothing for the start-up time.

The robot's readiness is itself a unit, robot_ready, with requirement dependencies on everything the robot needs to move safely: the e-stop monitor (through the motor bridge), the motor bridge, the controller, the state estimator, the planner. The robot can be armed only after robot_ready is ready. A camera may be excluded on purpose: the robot can drive slowly in a degraded mode without it. That is a design decision, made once and written in the unit file, not an accident.

Units also fail. A restart policy says what the supervisor does: restart after a delay that grows each time (back-off), up to a limit, and then stop trying and keep the robot in its safe state. At shutdown, units are stopped in reverse order, so the controller stops before the motor bridge it talks to. R1 R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The unit file and the tool. The lab describes the robot's units in a small text format of the course's own, modelled on what service managers do: unit NAME START_MS READY_MS [needs=...] [after=...] [fail]. The times are the design's estimates (or measurements from a previous release); fail injects a unit that never becomes ready. Listing 1, startup.cpp, reads the file, builds the graph, resolves it in topological order, computes launch, running and ready times, prints them in launch order, and walks back from robot_ready along the "decided by" links to print the critical path. Second, the last unit to be ready is the camera at 980 ms, but robot_ready is ready at 720 ms: the planner only waits for the camera to be running. Cycles. Kahn's algorithm takes units with nothing left to wait for, one at a time, and removes their outgoing edges. If units remain when no unit is free, those remaining units wait for each other: a cycle. The tool reports them and exits with 1 without simulating anything:




Cycles appear in real stacks more often than one would think: a parameter server that waits for the logger, and a logger that reads its configuration from the parameter server. The fix is always to break one edge, usually by making one side start with a built-in default and pick up the other side later. D3 R3


A real supervisor. The simulator computes times; it starts nothing. Listing 2, launcher.cpp, is a small supervisor that does start real processes. Its units are copies of itself in "child" mode, so the lab needs no other programs. It uses the POSIX process interface: pipe to make a channel, fork to create a child, dup2 to put the write end of the pipe on file descriptor 3 in the child, execl to run the unit's program, poll to wait for READY messages, waitpid with WNOHANG to notice children that died, and kill with SIGTERM to stop them. The motor bridge is made to crash on its first attempt, as if the motor microcontroller had not answered. D4




Read the log in order. The e-stop monitor is launched first and reports ready; only then are the IMU driver and the motor bridge launched (both need it). The motor bridge dies with exit code 3 before READY. The supervisor waits (50 ms on the first failure) and launches attempt 2, which reports ready. Only then is the controller launched; when it is ready, all units are ready and the robot may be armed. At the end the units are stopped in reverse order: controller first, e-stop monitor last. The millisecond values are wall-clock times of this one run in the build container; they include the program start-up of each child and vary from run to run. R2


Why readiness must be explicit. The supervisor cannot see inside a unit. The process being alive says nothing about whether the motor microcontroller answered, the camera enumerated, or the DDS participant has discovered its peers. Only the unit knows, so the unit must say. The lab's convention, a line on an inherited file descriptor, is one of several used in practice; service managers and middleware have their own (next box). A unit should report READY after it has: opened and checked its devices; loaded and validated its parameters; created its publishers and subscribers; and, for units on the motion path, seen the data it needs at least once. A unit that reports READY early makes every dependency that waits on it meaningless.

Middleware start-up. In a ROS 2 stack each unit is one or more nodes, started by a launch file, configured by parameter files, and talking through DDS (RB303). Two parts of middleware start-up are slow and variable. Discovery: participants find each other by exchanging announcements, and a subscriber receives nothing until discovery between it and the publisher has completed; that is why dds_discovery has the longest ready time on the critical path of startup.in. Configuration: parameters must be loaded and validated before the node can work. ROS 2's lifecycle nodes (managed nodes) separate these steps: a node is created unconfigured, is configured (reads parameters, allocates), and is activated (starts publishing) by an external manager. That gives the deployment exactly the hook it needs: configure everything, check that everything configured, then activate in dependency order, and only then open the readiness gate. D1 D5

Deployment decisions that start-up depends on. Where a unit runs changes its start-up: a node in its own process can be restarted alone; nodes composed into one process start faster and talk without serialisation but fail together. A unit on an isolated real-time CPU (F9-66) must be started with its CPU affinity and priority already set, by the supervisor, not by itself after it has run for a while. Containers add an image-pull and a namespace set-up to the start time and a second supervisor (the container runtime) between the service manager and the unit; whether that is worth it on a robot is an owner decision, not tested here. Every one of these choices should appear in the unit description, so that the start-up tool and the deployment agree. C1


Shutdown. Stopping matters as much as starting. Reverse order makes the controller stop sending commands before the motor bridge stops listening; the motor bridge, when it stops, must tell the motor microcontroller to bring the motors to their safe state (F9-69) and wait for the acknowledgement before it exits. A supervisor that kills everything at once leaves the microcontroller to notice the silence by its own watchdog; that must work too, but should not be the normal path.



How the hardware actually does it


On a robot computer the start-up order runs on top of hardware that has its own order. The motor microcontroller boots in milliseconds, long before Linux, and must hold the motors in their safe state (drives disabled, brakes engaged) until it receives a valid command stream and a heartbeat from the host; F9-69 builds that. The e-stop chain is hard-wired and works with the robot computer switched off; the e-stop monitor unit only tells the software that the chain is open. USB devices such as cameras are enumerated by the kernel during and after boot, in an order that can change between boots, which is why a camera unit must find its device by a stable identity, not by "the second video device". Storage speed decides how long it takes to load the binaries and the parameter files, and is often the largest part of cold-start time on SD cards. None of these hardware times were measured in this build. C1


Safety
Start-up is a dangerous moment for any machine that moves: units start in a new order after an update, devices appear in a new order, and a controller may command motion with stale or missing data. Rules for the course robot: motors are disabled by the motor microcontroller until the host has sent a valid heartbeat and the robot has been armed; arming is possible only after robot_ready is ready and only by an explicit operator action, never automatically at boot; the hard-wired e-stop chain cuts motor power independently of all software (RB101, F9-69); and the first boot of a new image or a new unit file is done with the robot on blocks or with motor power off at the e-stop chain. A readiness gate is a software convenience, not a safety function; it reduces surprises but does not replace the e-stop. C2

Worked example


A. The timeline of startup.in by hand. Rules: a unit is launched at the latest of (the ready time of each needs= unit) and (the running time of each after= unit), or at 0 if it waits for nothing; running = launch + START_MS; ready = running + READY_MS.


    

  • estop_monitor: 0, 0 + 5 = 5, 5 + 5 = 10. logger: 0, 10, 30.
    • 

  • imu_driver needs estop_monitor (ready 10): 10, 40, 120. motor_bridge needs estop_monitor: 10, 40, 440.
    • 

  • dds_discovery needs logger (ready 30): 30, 50, 250. camera_driver needs logger: 30, 80, 980.
    • 

  • state_estimator needs imu_driver (120) and dds_discovery (250): launch at max = 250; 290; 320. Decided by dds_discovery.
    • 

  • controller needs state_estimator (320) and motor_bridge (440): 440, 480, 490. Decided by motor_bridge.
    • 

  • planner needs state_estimator (ready 320) and is after camera_driver (running 80): 320, 420, 720. Decided by state_estimator.
    • 

  • robot_ready needs controller (490) and planner (720): 720, with START and READY 0. Decided by planner.
    • 



Walk back the "decided by" links from robot_ready: planner ← state_estimator ← dds_discovery ← logger. Every number matches startup.out. R1


B. What is worth optimising? The team can afford one improvement: halve the motor bridge's READY_MS (400 → 200) or halve DDS discovery (200 → 100). Motor bridge: ready at 240, controller launched at 320 (now decided by the state estimator), ready at 370; robot_ready still waits for the planner at 720. No gain. Discovery: ready at 150, state estimator 150, 190, 220; planner 220, 320, 620; controller still 440, 480, 490; robot_ready at max(490, 620) = 620. A gain of 100 ms, and the critical path is unchanged. Only work on the critical path shortens start-up, and only until another path becomes critical.


C. Back-off budget. The launcher waits 50 ms × attempt after each failure and gives up after 3 attempts. A unit that fails three times costs at most its three start attempts plus 50 + 100 = 150 ms of waiting before the supervisor gives up (the third failure gives up without waiting). If the motor bridge needs 80 ms per attempt, the worst case before "giving up" is about 3 × 80 + 150 = 390 ms plus process start times: a bound the operator interface can use to decide when to show "motor bridge failed" instead of "starting".

The launcher's limits, stated plainly: the back-off sleeps the whole supervisor (a real one schedules a timer and keeps serving other units); it does not watch units after start-up (Figure 3's missing arrow); it has no time-out for a unit that neither dies nor reports READY; and it reads any bytes on the pipe as READY. Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A rider checks the brakes by feeling them. A unit's readiness is only as good as what the unit checks before saying READY; a unit can say it and be wrong.
    • 

  • Getting on a bicycle has four or five steps that never change. A robot's start-up graph changes with every release, and the order that worked last time is not evidence about the new one.
    • 

  • A rider who skips the brake check is the one at risk. When a robot starts in the wrong order, the people near it are at risk, which is why the gate and the arming rule exist.
    • 

  • Getting off a bicycle needs no plan. Stopping a robot's units does: the reverse order and the motor bridge's safe-state hand-over are part of the design.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • "Running" taken as "ready". Symptom: the controller's first commands go nowhere, or it logs "no feedback" warnings at boot. Cause: a dependency on process start, not on readiness. Find it: compare the dependent's launch time with the dependency's ready time in the start-up log. Fix: an explicit readiness signal and needs=.
    • 

  • Ordering used where a requirement was meant. Symptom: the robot reports ready although a unit failed. Cause: after= (or an ordering-only directive of the service manager). Find it: inject the failure (fail) and see whether robot_ready is still reached. Fix: the forensic lab.
    • 

  • A unit reports READY too early. Symptom: dependants start and then fail on their first message. Cause: READY sent before devices or peers were checked. Fix: move the signal to the end of initialisation; test with a slow dependency.
    • 

  • Restart loops that never end. Symptom: the log fills with launches of the same unit; CPU load at boot is high. Cause: restart without a limit or back-off. Fix: back-off and a limit, then the safe state and a clear operator message.
    • 

  • Dependency cycles. Symptom: the service manager starts the units in some order anyway and one of them misbehaves, or nothing starts. Find it: the tool's cycle check (startup_cycle.out). Fix: break one edge with a built-in default.
    • 

  • Optimising off the critical path. Symptom: a unit became much faster and the robot did not start sooner. Find it: the critical path line. Fix: optimise along it (worked example B).
    • 

  • Killing everything at once at shutdown. Symptom: the motor microcontroller's watchdog trips at every shutdown. Fix: reverse order, and a motor bridge that hands over to the safe state before it exits.
    •

Summary


    

  • Deploying the stack means saying, for each unit, where it runs, with what configuration, after what, and how it reports ready.
    • 

  • Running is not ready: units must signal readiness explicitly, after they have checked their devices, parameters and peers.
    • 

  • Ordering dependencies only order; requirement dependencies also block on failure. The difference is invisible until something fails.
    • 

  • A topological order gives the start-up sequence; cycles must be reported, not run; the critical path decides start-up time.
    • 

  • Restart with back-off and a limit, then stay in the safe state; stop in reverse order.
    • 

  • A readiness gate with requirement dependencies on the safety and motion path decides when the robot may be armed, and only an operator arms it.
    • 

  • Inject failures to test the gate: in this build, a one-word change from needs= to after= made the robot reach "ready" without a motor bridge.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. after=B only orders: A is launched once B's process runs, and A is still launched if B later fails. Right where A only needs B to go first, for example the planner starting after the camera driver so that its first log lines are not mixed, when the planner can work without images. needs=B waits for B's READY and blocks A if B fails. Right where A cannot do its job without B: the controller and the motor bridge.
    2. 

  2. The process runs within milliseconds; the motor bridge is useful only when the motor microcontroller has answered, which takes much longer and can fail. Only the motor bridge knows when that happened, so it must report it.
    3.

It is safe only if the design says the robot may operate without the camera (degraded mode, reported to the operator) and the planner handles missing images; otherwise the planner must needs=camera_driver.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. startup_forensic.in is startup.in with one change on the controller's line (and its comment line): needs=state_estimator,motor_bridge became needs=state_estimator after=motor_bridge. The tool was run on it (R4).


Observation. Only the controller's line differs: launch 320 instead of 440, running 360 instead of 480, ready 370 instead of 490, launched when "state_estimator ready" instead of "motor_bridge ready". robot_ready (720) and the critical path are unchanged, which is why the review saw only an improvement.


The failure test. With fail appended to the motor bridge's line, release 4 blocks the controller and robot_ready and exits with 1:




Release 5 reaches robot_ready at 720 ms with the motor bridge FAILED, exit code 0:




Root cause. In release 5 the controller only orders itself after the motor bridge. robot_ready reached the motor bridge only through the controller's requirement; with that edge turned into ordering, nothing on the gate requires the motor bridge, so the gate opens without it. The bench robot matched exactly this case.


Other hypotheses. "The motor bridge falsely reported READY": in the evidence its ready time is still 440 and it is not on the gate's path at all; and with the cable unplugged a correct bridge never reports READY, which the failure test models. "The panel shows the wrong unit's state": the tool, which knows nothing about the panel, also reaches robot_ready, so the unit file alone explains it.


What the controller could do. In a normal start it runs for 70 ms (370 to 440) with no motor bridge: commands are lost, and a controller with an integral term accumulates error while the robot does not move. When the bridge appears (in the bench case, when the cable was plugged in and the bridge restarted) the controller's first command could be large: a lurch. The motor microcontroller holds the motors safe until it gets a heartbeat and the robot is armed, and the hard-wired e-stop works regardless; those layers, not the start-up order, prevented harm.


Fix and prevention. Restore needs=state_estimator,motor_bridge; additionally make robot_ready need motor_bridge and estop_monitor directly, so that no single edge on another unit can remove them from the gate. The controller should also hold its integrator and output at zero until it has received motor feedback. Prevention: the mini-project's build check, which injects a failure of every unit on the safety path and requires robot_ready to be blocked; it fails release 5.
