Story hook

Amara rides her bicycle down a busy street. Her eyes read the traffic far ahead and her mind plans the turn at the next corner. Her inner ear feels every small tilt, and her arms and legs correct it many times a second, without her thinking about it. When her front wheel hits a stone, her hands tighten on the bars before she knows what happened. And if a child steps out, she brakes hard: the planning stops, the reflex wins.

All of this runs in one body, but not in one place and not at one speed. The slow, clever thinking happens in one part; the fast, simple corrections in another; the "stop now" reaction does not wait for the thinking at all. If Amara's balance had to wait until she finished planning the route, she would fall at the first corner.

A robot is built the same way, on purpose. This chapter takes the robot of this course apart into its layers, shows which part runs where and how fast, and gives you a small tool that checks a stack description before anybody builds it.

What you will be able to do

  • Draw the layers of a robot software stack, from the hard-wired emergency stop and the motor microcontroller to perception, planning and operations, and place each component on a processor with a reason.
  • Classify each component by timing (hard, firm, soft deadline) and by criticality, and explain why the classes should not share a processor carelessly.
  • Compute processor utilisation, apply the rate-monotonic utilisation bound, and run exact response-time analysis when the bound is not enough.
  • Estimate the worst-case data age of a sensor-to-actuator chain from periods and response times.
  • Diagnose from a stack description and the checker's report why a release made the robot react late.
Jargon box
Robot software stack
Simple words: all the programs of a robot, from the ones that drive the motors to the ones that decide where to go, arranged in layers. Analogy: everything in the body that turns seeing into moving: senses, reflexes, balance, thinking. Precise: the set of firmware, operating systems, drivers, middleware and applications of a robot, organised as layers with defined interfaces (messages, rates, units, coordinate frames) and mapped onto the robot's processors; in this course: hardware and safety circuits, microcontroller firmware, the robot computer's OS, middleware, estimation and control, perception and planning, operations. C1 D5
Criticality
Simple words: how bad it is if a part of the robot fails or is late. Analogy: losing your balance is worse than missing a street sign. Precise: a property of a requirement, decided by hazard analysis, that ranks the consequences of a component's failure; a system is mixed-criticality when components of different criticality share resources such as a processor, memory or a bus, so that a fault in a low-criticality component can interfere with a high-criticality one. D2 D3
Processor utilisation (U)
Simple words: the share of a processor's time that a set of programs needs. Analogy: how much of every second your legs spend pedalling. Precise: for periodic tasks with worst-case execution budgets Ci and periods Ti on one processor, U = Σ Ci/Ti; U > 1 means no scheduler can meet every deadline. D2 D4
Rate-monotonic utilisation bound
Simple words: a quick test that says "these tasks surely fit" if the total load is small enough. Analogy: a rule of thumb that you can carry a bag safely if it weighs less than a fixed share of your strength; heavier bags might still be fine, but you must check. Precise: for n independent periodic tasks with deadlines equal to periods under rate-monotonic priorities, U ≤ n(21/n − 1) is sufficient for schedulability (Liu and Layland); it is not necessary. D4 (RB304's F9-46 derives it.)
Response-time analysis (RTA)
Simple words: a calculation of the longest time a task can take from being ready to being done, counting the higher-priority tasks that interrupt it. Analogy: how long a sentence takes when you must stop for every wobble. Precise: for fixed-priority preemptive scheduling, the smallest R with R = Ci + Σj ∈ hp(i) ⌈R/Tj⌉ Cj, found by iteration from R = Ci; the task meets its deadline if R ≤ Di; exact for this task model. D2 D3
Sensor-to-actuator chain (data age)
Simple words: the path a measurement takes through the programs until it moves a motor, and how old the measurement can be when it does. Analogy: from the moment the stone hits the wheel to the moment your hands tighten. Precise: an ordered list of periodic components that pass data forward; with independent periods, each stage can add up to one period of waiting plus its response time, so Σ (Ti + Ri) is a simple upper estimate of the age of the input behind an actuator command (the course's estimate, explained in Layer 3). D2
Companion microcontroller
Simple words: a small, separate computer on the robot that runs the fastest motor loops by itself. Analogy: the reflex that tightens your grip before you think. Precise: a microcontroller, usually running bare-metal code or an RTOS, connected to the robot computer by a bus (CAN, UART, SPI, USB or Ethernet), which owns time-critical I/O such as motor current loops and encoder counting and reports to the main computer; curriculum section 17 describes this class of device firmware. C1 (RB304's F9-51 puts micro-ROS on one.)

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot has many small programs, not one big one. Some are fast and simple, like the reflex that keeps you upright on a bicycle. Some are slow and clever, like deciding which street to take. The fast ones must never wait for the slow ones. So engineers put them in different places: the fastest on a small separate chip, the clever ones on the main computer, and the emergency stop on a wire that does not need any program at all. Then they check, with arithmetic, that every program has enough time to do its job.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Riding a bicycle mixes three speeds: balance corrections many times a second, steering around a stone, and route planning. A robot stack has the same three speeds and a fourth, the "stop" path, that must work even when everything else has failed. The layers of Figure 1 follow from that.

Hardware and the safety chain. Motors, motor drivers, sensors and the battery are hardware (RB201). The emergency stop is a hardware function too: the stop button opens a circuit that removes power from the motor drivers. Software can watch the button and react, but the stop must not depend on software working (F9-69). D6

The motor microcontroller. The fastest loops (motor current and speed, encoder counting) run on a microcontroller with bare-metal code or an RTOS, because a small program on a dedicated processor has small, analysable timing. Curriculum section 17 describes this class of firmware: a microcontroller inside the hardware, talking to the host through a bus and a protocol. C1 C2

The robot computer. A multi-core SoC or PC runs Linux. Linux gives drivers for cameras, LiDARs, networks and storage, and it runs ROS 2; it meets timing requirements only when configured for it (the real-time preemption model, CPU isolation, priorities), as RB304 showed (F9-47). D1 C2

Middleware. ROS 2 nodes exchange messages on topics through DDS; launch files start them and parameters configure them (RB303). D5

Applications. The state estimator fuses IMU and wheel odometry (RB302); the controller computes motor commands (RB202, RB402); a hardware interface passes them to the microcontroller (ros2_control in RB304); perception turns camera images and LiDAR scans into obstacles; the planner chooses a path (RB401).

Operations. Logging, telemetry, health monitoring and software updates keep the robot inspectable and maintainable (F9-70).

Two properties are assigned to every component: a timing class (how bad is lateness?) and a criticality (how bad is failure?). RB304 named the timing classes: a hard deadline whose miss can cause a failure, a firm deadline where a late result is useless, a soft deadline where a late result is worth less (F9-45). Then each component is placed on a processor, and the placement is checked with arithmetic: utilisation per processor, response times, and the age of data along each chain. Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

One paragraph of analogy first: a good rider does not think about balance, and does not let thinking about the route interrupt balance. The stack design below is how you make the same promise in software, and how you check it before the robot moves.

From requirements to a placement

A stack design starts from requirements, not from software packages. For each function write down: its rate (or the events that trigger it), its deadline, its worst-case execution budget on the target processor (measured later, F9-66 and RB304's F9-48), its criticality (from hazard analysis, F9-69), its inputs and outputs (message types, units, frames), and the chain it belongs to. Then decide placement. The course uses four placement rules, which are design principles, not laws: D2 D3

  1. The stop path is hardware. No processor appears on the path from the e-stop button to motor power. Software watches the button and reacts as well, but the removal of power does not wait for it. (F9-69 covers the standards that govern this; their requirements are not reproduced here.) D6
  2. The fastest loop lives closest to the hardware. Motor current and speed loops run on the microcontroller. The robot computer sends setpoints at a lower rate, and the microcontroller has its own watchdog that stops the motors if the setpoints stop arriving. C1
  3. Criticality separates before timing does. High-criticality work (estimation, control, health monitoring) gets processors of its own on the robot computer (CPU isolation, F9-66 and F9-70); low-criticality work (perception, logging, the user interface) shares the rest. A processor shared between the two classes makes the high-criticality component's timing depend on the low-criticality component's behaviour, which is exactly what you did not analyse.
  4. Every chain has a data-age budget. A controller that reacts to an obstacle 300 ms after it appeared may be correct and still too late. The budget is checked on paper (this chapter), then measured on the robot (F9-70).

Utilisation and the bound

For each processor, sum C/T. Above 1, the processor is overloaded: over a long enough interval it is asked for more time than exists, and some deadline must be missed whatever the scheduler does. Below 1, it depends on the scheduler. For fixed priorities assigned by rate (shorter period, higher priority: rate-monotonic), Liu and Layland proved that U ≤ n(21/n − 1) suffices for n tasks with deadlines equal to periods; the bound falls from 1 for one task towards ln 2 for many. Above the bound, the task set may still be schedulable: you must check exactly. D4 D2

The exact check for this model is response-time analysis. The worst case for task i under fixed priorities is the moment all higher-priority tasks are released together with it (the critical instant); its response time is then the smallest fixed point of R = Ci + Σj ∈ hp(i) ⌈R/Tj⌉ Cj. Iterate from R = Ci; stop when R repeats (the answer) or exceeds the deadline (a miss). D2 D3

The analysis rests on assumptions you must state in a design review, because a real robot computer breaks most of them a little: independent tasks (no shared locks; with locks, add a blocking term, F9-46), no release jitter, budgets that are true worst cases, zero scheduling overhead, and one processor per analysis (tasks pinned, no migration). The checker in Listing 1 implements the textbook model and nothing more; it is a design-time filter, not a proof that the robot will meet its deadlines. D2

Data age along a chain

When components run at their own periods and pass data through "latest value" buffers (the usual pattern with topics), a new sensor sample can wait up to one full period of the next stage before that stage picks it up, and then takes up to its response time to produce an output. Summing over a chain gives Σ (Ti + Ri) as a simple upper estimate of the age of the oldest input behind an actuator command. It is pessimistic when stages are synchronised (a stage triggered by the arrival of its input waits no period at all), and it ignores communication delays, which on a DDS transport you must measure. The estimate is the course's: the literature on cause-effect chains distinguishes reaction time from data age and gives tighter bounds; treat this as the first number in a design review, not the last. Mixed criticality on one robot computer

Interference between components on one SoC does not only come through processor time. Shared caches and memory bandwidth slow a control loop when a perception task streams images; a driver's interrupt handler runs on whichever CPU receives the interrupt; a kernel lock taken by a logging write can block a real-time thread; a memory leak in a planner can make the kernel reclaim memory everywhere. The defences are layered: separate processors (the microcontroller), separate CPUs with interrupts steered away (F9-66), memory locked and pre-allocated in real-time threads (RB304's F9-49), bounded queues instead of unbounded ones, and a health monitor that notices and stops the robot. The real-time preemption documentation of the Linux kernel discusses the kernel-side sources of latency; the rest is design. D1 D3

How the hardware actually does it

The course robot has two kinds of processors. The robot computer is a multi-core application processor (an SoC as in DR403 or a PC): out-of-order cores with caches shared between them, an MMU, and a memory system optimised for throughput. The motor controller is a microcontroller: one in-order core, on-chip SRAM and flash, peripherals for PWM, encoder capture and CAN, and an interrupt controller with fixed priorities. The first gives you Linux, drivers and large memory; the second gives you timing you can reason about instruction by instruction. The bus between them (CAN, UART, SPI, USB or Ethernet; RB201's F9-14) is part of every control chain and has its own latency, which you measure on your kit. C1 C2

Shared resources on the application processor are the reason for CPU isolation. Two cores share at least the last-level cache and the memory controller; a core running a perception task that streams images evicts cache lines the control thread needs and competes for memory bandwidth, so the control thread's execution time grows even when nothing preempts it. Measured budgets must therefore be taken with the real load running next to the measured thread. D1

Safety

A stack diagram is not a safety design. Whatever the layers and CPUs, the course robot's emergency stop is a hard-wired chain that removes motor power or torque with no software in the path, and the motor microcontroller holds the motors in their safe state when the robot computer goes silent (F9-69; RB101). Placing a component on a "high-criticality" CPU makes its timing more predictable; it does not make the component a safety function. Before any stack runs on a robot that can move, the wheels are off the ground or motor power is off at the e-stop chain. D6 C3

Worked example

CPU 3 of the course stack runs the detector (T = 33,333 µs, C = 20,000 µs) and the planner (T = 100,000 µs, C = 30,000 µs). Is it schedulable under rate-monotonic priorities?

  1. Utilisation. U = 20,000/33,333 + 30,000/100,000 = 0.600 + 0.300 = 0.900.
  2. Bound. n = 2: 2(21/2 − 1) = 2 × 0.4142 = 0.828. U is above the bound, so the quick test says nothing.
  3. Priorities. Rate-monotonic: the detector (shorter period) has the higher priority.
  4. Detector. No higher-priority task: R = C = 20,000 µs ≤ 33,333. It meets its deadline.
  5. Planner. R0 = 30,000. R1 = 30,000 + ⌈30,000/33,333⌉ × 20,000 = 30,000 + 1 × 20,000 = 50,000. R2 = 30,000 + ⌈50,000/33,333⌉ × 20,000 = 30,000 + 2 × 20,000 = 70,000. R3 = 30,000 + ⌈70,000/33,333⌉ × 20,000 = 30,000 + 3 × 20,000 = 90,000. R4 = 30,000 + ⌈90,000/33,333⌉ × 20,000 = 30,000 + 3 × 20,000 = 90,000 = R3. Fixed point: R = 90,000 µs ≤ 100,000. It meets its deadline, with 10 ms to spare.
  6. See-and-avoid chain age. camera_driver (T 33,333 + R 5,500) + detector (33,333 + 20,000) + planner (100,000 + 90,000) + controller (1,000 + 280) + motor_bridge (1,000 + 130) = 284,576 µs, about 0.28 s.

The checker prints the same numbers (the response times of the CPU 1 and CPU 2 tasks come from the same iteration with more terms): R1



Two lessons. First, "above the bound" is not "unschedulable": the exact test passed. Second, the chain estimate shows where the age comes from: the planner's period and response time are two thirds of it. If the robot must react to an obstacle faster, a faster detector does little; a reactive layer that bypasses the planner (an obstacle stop in the controller, fed by the detector) changes the chain itself.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A body's reflexes and thinking grew together and share one blood supply, but nobody had to prove that balance never waits for thought. A robot's layers are chosen by engineers and shared through caches, buses and kernels; the separation exists only if you design it and measure it.
    • 

  • A rider's reflex is local and has no "message bus". In a robot even the fast loop crosses a bus between the robot computer and the microcontroller, and that bus adds latency, can lose messages, and can fail; the analogy hides all of this.
    • 

  • When a rider faints, the bicycle falls. A robot can be designed to reach a safe state when its "brain" fails (watchdogs and hard-wired stops, F9-69); the analogy suggests failure is all-or-nothing, which is the opposite of the design goal.
    • 

  • The body has no CPU budget. The utilisation arithmetic of this chapter has no counterpart in the analogy, and you should not look for one.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Budgets from an idle machine. Symptom: the analysis passes and the robot still misses deadlines. Cause: execution times measured with nothing else running. Find it: measure each task's execution time with the full stack running (camera streaming, logging on). Fix: use the loaded worst case plus a margin, and re-measure after every release.
    • 

  • Mixing units. Symptom: absurd utilisations (0.0003 or 300). Cause: periods in milliseconds, budgets in microseconds. Find it: the checker's per-task T and C columns. Fix: one unit per file, written in its header.
    • 

  • Forgetting the microcontroller's chain stages. Symptom: the measured motor response is slower than the chain estimate. Cause: the bus and the microcontroller loop are left out of the chain. Fix: add them as stages with their own periods.
    • 

  • Treating the bound as a verdict. Symptom: a design is rejected that the exact test would accept (the worked example). Fix: always run response-time analysis above the bound.
    • 

  • Trusting the analysis model. Symptom: a task that "cannot miss" misses. Cause: shared locks, interrupts, cache interference or migration break the model. Fix: state the assumptions; pin tasks; measure (F9-66, F9-70).
    • 

  • A stop path through software. Symptom: the e-stop works in every test, until the day the computer hangs. Cause: the button is read by a program that then commands the motors off. Fix: the hard-wired chain of F9-69.
    •

Summary


    

  • A robot stack has layers that run at different speeds and criticalities: hardware and the e-stop chain, microcontroller firmware, the robot computer's OS, middleware, estimation and control, perception and planning, operations.

  • Utilisation above 1 is overload; the Liu–Layland bound is sufficient, not necessary; response-time analysis is exact for the textbook model.

  • Σ (T + R) along a chain estimates the age of data behind an actuator command; it shows which stage to change.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. stack_forensic.in is stack.in with two lines changed: the camera driver and the detector moved to CPU 1, their period halved to 16,667 µs (the faster camera), and the detector's budget set to 9,000 µs per frame. The checker was run on it (R2).


Root cause. CPU 1's utilisation went from 0.440 to 1.160: 0.440 + 3,000/16,667 + 9,000/16,667 = 0.440 + 0.180 + 0.540 = 1.160 > 1. Under rate-monotonic priorities the detector has the longest period on that CPU (16.7 ms, tied with the camera driver, which the checker ranks higher because it is listed first), so it is the lowest-priority task, and it misses its deadline: frames are processed late or dropped, so obstacles reach the planner late or not at all. The see-and-avoid chain has no bound.


Why control still looks fine. The control tasks have shorter periods and therefore higher priorities; the overload falls on the detector. This is fragile: any priority mistake (the detector given a high priority to "fix" the drops) would move the overload onto the controller. The mixed-criticality warning on CPU 1 says the same thing in one line.


Wrong hypotheses. "The new camera is slower": its driver's response time on CPU 1 is 5,680 µs, within its period; the evidence shows the detector, not the camera, missing. "The planner is too slow": the planner alone on CPU 3 has R = 30,000 ≤ 100,000.


Fix. Put the camera driver back on CPU 2 (U becomes 0.290 + 0.180 = 0.470) and the detector on CPU 3 with the planner: 0.540 + 0.300 = 0.840 > 0.828 bound, so run RTA: detector R = 9,000; planner R: 30,000 → 30,000 + 2·9,000 = 48,000 → 30,000 + 3·9,000 = 57,000 → 30,000 + 4·9,000 = 66,000 → 30,000 + 4·9,000 = 66,000 ≤ 100,000. CPU 1 returns to 0.440 with high-criticality work only. Prevention: run the checker in CI on every change to the stack description (the mini-project), and measure the chain age in the field test with the flight recorder of F9-70.
