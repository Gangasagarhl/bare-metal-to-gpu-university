Story hook

Analogy

Eight cooks sit around the round table, passing bowls to their right. On every turn, each cook must receive a bowl before passing one on. One evening, the cook in seat 3 stops. Not falls over, not walks out: stops, hands on the table, eyes open, saying nothing. Seat 4 waits for a bowl that never comes; seat 5 waits for seat 4; soon the whole table is waiting, each cook patiently holding out a hand. Nobody shouts. The kitchen is silent until morning, when the hall manager finds the dinner never served and the night's work lost.

Ingrid, the steward, introduces three rules. First, every cook keeps a kitchen timer: anyone who waits more than two minutes for a bowl rings the alarm, and the whole table stops. Second, every ten turns, the head cook writes the state of the dinner on a card in ink and files it in the safe. Third, when the alarm rings, the hall manager calls a fresh table, and it starts from the last card in the safe.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why one failed rank stops the whole job in synchronous training, and why a frozen rank is worse than a dead one.
  • Build a watchdog that turns a silent hang into an abort within a set timeout.
  • Write checkpoints that survive a crash during writing (temporary file, flush to disk, atomic rename, checksum, an older fallback).
  • Restart a job automatically and show that the result equals an uninterrupted run (milestone F8).
  • Reconstruct a hung job from per-rank logs and traces: which rank stopped, where, and when.
Jargon box
Hung job
Simple words: a job that is still running but no longer makes progress, often without any error message. Analogy: the round table waiting for a bowl that never comes. Precise: a distributed job in which some processes block indefinitely, most often in a collective or receive whose matching operation on another rank will never be posted, because that rank is stopped, slow, deadlocked or on a different code path; no process exits, so exit-based failure detection does not trigger. R4
Watchdog (collective timeout)
Simple words: a timer that stops the job when an operation takes much longer than it should. Analogy: each cook's kitchen timer. Precise: a mechanism that bounds how long a rank waits for a communication operation; after the timeout it reports the stuck operation and aborts the job (or the communicator), turning a hang into a failure that a restart mechanism can handle. In Listing 1, the gradient all-reduce is started with MPI_Iallreduce and polled with MPI_Test; after watchdog seconds the rank calls MPI_Abort. L1 R2
Abort
Simple words: stopping every process of the job at once, on purpose. Analogy: the alarm that stops the whole table. Precise: in MPI, MPI_Abort(comm, errorcode) makes a best attempt to terminate all tasks in the group of comm; Open MPI terminates all processes of the job and mpirun returns the error code. In NCCL, a communicator can be aborted so that pending operations stop and resources are released (unverified box). D2 R2
Atomic checkpoint
Simple words: a saved state that is either completely old or completely new, never half-written. Analogy: the card written in ink and only then put in the safe. Precise: a checkpoint written to a temporary file, flushed to stable storage (fsync), and moved into place with rename, which POSIX specifies to replace the target atomically; readers verify size, a magic number and a checksum, and fall back to the previous checkpoint if the newest fails. D3 R3
Restart from checkpoint
Simple words: starting the job again from the last saved state instead of the beginning. Analogy: a fresh table starting from the card in the safe. Precise: a new instance of the job (often on new processes or new machines) that loads the newest valid checkpoint and continues from its recorded step; with deterministic data order and computation, the result equals that of an uninterrupted run. R2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When many computers train one model together, they wait for each other at every step to add up their results. If one computer breaks loudly, the others are told and everything stops. If one computer freezes instead, the others wait forever, politely, and nothing tells you. So you add three things. A timer: if waiting takes far too long, stop everything. A save point: every few steps, write down where you are, carefully, so a crash cannot leave a half-written page. And a restart: when the job stops, start it again from the last save point. Done well, the final model is exactly the same as if nothing had gone wrong.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why one rank stops everyone

The round table cannot pass bowls past an empty seat. In synchronous data parallelism (F8-12), every step ends with an all-reduce over all ranks; a collective completes only when every rank in the communicator has taken part. One missing rank therefore blocks every other rank in the same collective, and they cannot tell, from inside the call, whether the missing rank is slow, frozen, dead or doing something else. D2

Dead and frozen are different failures

Listing 1 can inject two kinds of failure into one rank, both silent:

  • Killed (SIGKILL): the process is gone. The launcher, mpirun, sees one of its processes exit and ends the whole job. In R2's ft_kill run, Open MPI printed "mpirun noticed that process rank 1 … exited on signal 9 (Killed)" and returned 137 (128 + 9, the shell's way of reporting death by signal 9). R2 L2 Detection here costs nothing; the launcher does it.
  • Frozen (SIGSTOP): the process still exists but never runs again. No process exits, so the launcher notices nothing. The other ranks wait in the all-reduce forever. This is the hung job. Real causes include a GPU kernel that never finishes, a deadlock inside one rank, a network path that drops a connection without an error, or a rank that took a different branch of the code and calls a different collective. R4

The watchdog: turn a hang into an abort

A blocking MPI_Allreduce gives the program no chance to look at the clock. Listing 1 instead starts the all-reduce with MPI_Iallreduce and polls it with MPI_Test, sleeping 1 ms between polls. While it waits, it prints a warning every warn seconds and writes "still waiting" to its trace; after watchdog seconds it calls MPI_Abort(MPI_COMM_WORLD, 42). L1 In R2's ft_hang run, rank 2 froze at step 37; ranks 0, 1 and 3 printed "allreduce not complete after 1 s", then "WATCHDOG: no progress for 2.0 s, calling MPI_Abort", and the job ended with exit code 42. R2 The value 42 is this program's own choice; restart.sh only checks that the exit code is not 0.

Checkpoints: write so that a crash cannot destroy them

Every ckpt steps (10 by default), rank 0 writes the parameters and the next step number. Plain data parallelism keeps identical parameters on every rank (F8-12), so one rank's copy is the whole state; with sharded state this is wrong (F8-15's forensic lab). The write follows Figure 2: write ckpt.tmp, fflush and fsync it, rename the current ckpt.bin to ckpt.prev, rename ckpt.tmp to ckpt.bin. L3 On start, every rank reads ckpt.bin and checks its size, a magic number and an FNV-1a checksum of its contents; if any check fails it tries ckpt.prev; if that fails too, it starts from step 0 and says so. In R3, ckpt.bin was cut to 100 bytes after a 45-step run, and the next run printed "ckpt.bin rejected (short file (100 of 272 bytes)); resuming from ckpt.prev at step 30". R3

Restart, and the proof that it worked

restart.sh runs the job under a time limit (timeout -s KILL) and starts it again when the exit code is not 0, up to a maximum number of attempts. The injected failure is passed to the first attempt only, because it stands for a transient fault. The proof that a restart is correct is that the final parameters equal those of an uninterrupted run: Listing 1 prints a checksum of the parameters, and all three faulty runs (hang, kill, damaged checkpoint) ended with e31e3fa0a92a7485, the same as the clean run. R1 R2 R3 They are bitwise equal, not merely close, because the data of every step are a function of the step number only (a hash), the checkpoint stores the exact 32-bit floats, and the reduction runs the same way on the same number of ranks; the steps between the checkpoint and the failure are computed twice, with the same result.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Choosing the timeout

A watchdog that fires too early kills healthy jobs that hit a slow step (a long checkpoint write, a slow first step while kernels are compiled or loaded, a busy network); one that fires too late wastes GPU hours every time a rank hangs. Measure the distribution of step times (and of the all-reduce wait in particular) on healthy runs, and set the timeout well above the largest healthy value, with a separate, longer timeout for known slow phases (start-up, checkpoints). Listing 1 uses 2 s because its steps take about 22 ms; R1 shows steps 10 apart about 0.21–0.23 s apart. R1 The warnings before the abort matter as much as the abort: they put the time and step of the stall in the log.

Who should time out?

In Listing 1 every waiting rank has its own watchdog; the first to fire calls MPI_Abort, and R2 shows that several ranks reached the timeout within a millisecond and each called it. In some trial runs Open MPI also printed its own error messages ("ORTE_ERROR_LOG: …") while several aborts arrived at once; such lines differ between runs and are a side effect of the simultaneous aborts, not a second failure. R2 The frozen rank never fires its own watchdog: it is not running. That asymmetry is evidence in the forensic lab.

MPI's model of failure

The MPI standard's default error handler for communicators, MPI_ERRORS_ARE_FATAL, aborts the job on an error; MPI_ERRORS_RETURN returns error codes instead, set with MPI_Comm_set_errhandler. L1 D2 Neither helps with a hang: there is no error, only waiting. And after a real process failure, MPI-3.1 does not define how to continue in the surviving processes; the practical recovery is the one in this chapter: abort everything, start a new job, load a checkpoint. D2

Not verified — check before relying on this

From memory, not opened in this build: the MPI Forum's User-Level Failure Mitigation proposal (ULFM) adds calls to detect, revoke and shrink a communicator after a process failure, so that the survivors can continue, and Open MPI ships a ULFM implementation in later releases than the 4.1.6 used here. Confirm the status of ULFM in the current MPI standard and in your MPI library before planning on it.

The GPU version: NCCL and RCCL

On GPUs the all-reduce runs in a NCCL or RCCL kernel on a stream; the host thread that launched it is free, so the watchdog runs on the host and checks whether the collective's work on the stream has completed (for example with an event). If it has not, the communicator must be aborted so that the kernels stop and the GPU memory is released; then the process exits and the job restarts. D1 Milestone F8 asks you to read "NCCL documentation on asynchronous error handling and communicator abort". M1

Not verified — check before relying on this

From memory, not checked against the documentation or headers in this build (no NCCL headers were available): NCCL provides ncclCommAbort (frees a communicator's resources and aborts its outstanding operations) and ncclCommGetAsyncError (polls for asynchronous errors such as a failed network connection); recent NCCL versions can be told to return instead of blocking forever (a non-blocking communicator configuration); RCCL mirrors the NCCL API. PyTorch's process groups wrap NCCL with a collective timeout argument (timeout of init_process_group) and a watchdog thread controlled by environment variables whose names have changed across releases (from NCCL_ASYNC_ERROR_HANDLING to TORCH_NCCL_ASYNC_ERROR_HANDLING, among others). Read the NCCL "Fault tolerance" or error-handling section and your framework's documentation for the version you install; do not copy these names into a job script on the strength of this box.

Checkpoint frequency and cost

Each checkpoint costs time; each failure loses, on average, half a checkpoint interval of work plus the restart time. F8-17 derives the interval that balances the two (Young's approximation) and simulates it. Two engineering rules apply already here: a checkpoint must contain everything that the next step depends on (parameters, optimizer state, step number, data position, random-number state, learning-rate schedule position); and a restart should be tested, not assumed: run the clean and the faulty jobs and compare their results, as run.sh does.

What "atomic" covers, and what it does not

POSIX specifies that rename replaces the target name atomically: another process sees either the old file or the new one under that name. D3 That is about names, not about the data reaching the disk; hence the fsync of the file before the rename. For a power failure, the directory entry itself must also reach the disk, which needs an fsync of the directory; Listing 1 does not do that, and on a shared cluster file system the guarantees depend on the file system. D3 The checksum and the fallback to ckpt.prev are the second line of defence: they detect a damaged file whatever the cause, as R3 shows.

How the hardware actually does it

On a GPU cluster the same three mechanisms exist at different layers. Detection: the launcher or the scheduler notices exited processes and lost nodes (through its own heartbeats with each node's agent), the communication library notices broken connections, and the training framework's watchdog notices collectives that do not finish. Abort: the framework aborts the NCCL/RCCL communicator and exits; the scheduler kills the remaining processes of the job on every node. Restart: the scheduler requeues the job (F8-17), often on different machines, so the checkpoint must be on storage that every node can read, and the job must not depend on the old node names or GPU numbers. Common hardware causes of hangs and failures include GPU errors reported by the driver, network link flaps and failing memory; the node health checks of F8-17 try to catch them before a job starts. D1

Worked example

How much work did the ft_hang run of R2 lose, and how long did detection take?

  1. The last checkpoint before the failure: "checkpoint written: next step 30" at about t = 0.71 s. Rank 2 froze at step 37 (the fault injected).
  2. Work redone after the restart: steps 30 to 36, 7 steps. At about 22 ms per step (R1: steps 10 apart are about 0.21–0.23 s apart), about 0.15 s of work.
  3. Detection: the last progress of ranks 0, 1, 3 was the all-reduce of step 37 posted at about 0.9 s; the watchdog fired at about t = 2.9 s, 2 s after posting, as configured.
  4. Restart: attempt 2 printed "resuming from ckpt.bin at step 30" and finished in about 0.7 s of its own clock.
  5. Total cost of the failure: the 2 s detection timeout + about 0.15 s redone + the restart time (process start, 1 s pause in restart.sh).

The numbers are read from R2 and R1. R2 R1 The lesson scales: at real sizes the timeout is minutes and the redone work is up to a checkpoint interval of a large job, so both the timeout and the interval are design decisions with a cost (F8-17).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A frozen cook can be seen. At the table, a neighbour would notice. A frozen process looks exactly like a slow one from the other ranks; only time tells them apart, which is why the watchdog is a timer and not a test.
    • 

  • The alarm in the kitchen is heard by everyone. MPI_Abort asks the runtime to end every process; a frozen or unreachable process may only be ended by the operating system or the scheduler on its node, and on a lost node not at all until the node returns.
    • 

  • The card in the safe holds the dinner. A checkpoint holds only what the program wrote into it; anything forgotten (optimizer state, data position, random state) is silently reset by a restart.
    • 

  • The new table is not the same table. A restarted job may get different machines, different GPU numbers, even a different number of ranks; the checkpoint format must not assume the old ones.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • No timeout at all. Symptom: a job uses its whole time limit with no output after some point. Find it: per-rank traces (forensic lab). Fix: a watchdog on every collective.
    • 

  • Timeout too short. Symptom: aborts during checkpoints or the first step. Fix: measure healthy times; longer timeouts for known slow phases.
    • 

  • Writing the checkpoint in place. Symptom: after a crash during the write, the job cannot resume at all. Fix: Figure 2.
    • 

  • No check on read. Symptom: a job resumes from a damaged checkpoint and trains on garbage. Fix: size, magic, checksum, fallback.
    • 

  • Restart loop on a permanent fault. Symptom: the same failure on every attempt. Fix: a maximum number of attempts, and record the failing node so the next attempt avoids it.
    • 

  • Restart result "close enough". Symptom: a resumed run differs slightly from the uninterrupted one. Find it: compare checksums; look for state missing from the checkpoint (data order, random state). The milestone accepts a difference only if it is explained.
    • 

  • Logs that do not survive. Symptom: the last lines before a kill are missing. Fix: flush after each line (Listing 1, line 106).
    •

Summary


    

  • In synchronous training one missing rank blocks every collective; a dead rank is noticed by the launcher, a frozen one is not.
    • 

  • A watchdog (non-blocking collective, polled with a timeout, then abort) turns a hang into a failure within a set time.
    • 

  • Checkpoints are written to a temporary file, flushed, and renamed into place; readers check size, magic and checksum and fall back to the previous one.
    • 

  • A restart loop plus a deterministic job gives a final model bitwise equal to the uninterrupted run: R2 and R3 show the same checksum after a hang, a kill and a damaged checkpoint.
    • 

  • Per-rank traces, flushed line by line, reconstruct which rank stopped and where.

Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. Last progress: rank 0's "step 40 loss" line at about t = 0.97 s and the checkpoint "next step 40" at about 0.95 s. The traces show ranks 0–2 posting the step 41 all-reduce at about 0.99 s and waiting from then on. The scheduler killed the job at 6 s (exit code 137): about 5 s of the 6 s limit, over 80 %, was wasted.
    2. 

  2. Rank 3. Its last trace line is "step 41 begin" at about 0.97 s: it never posted the all-reduce of step 41. Ranks 0–2 posted it at about 0.99 s and wrote "still waiting" once a second until the kill.
    3. 

  3. Alive. A dead process ends an Open MPI job at once with "mpirun noticed that process rank … exited" (R2, ft_kill); there is no such message, and the job ran until the time limit. Rank 3 still existed but was not making progress.
    4. 

  4. The warnings come from the waiting loop around MPI_Test. Rank 3 never reached it: it stopped between "step 41 begin" and posting the all-reduce, in the computation part of the step (or it was not running at all).
    5. 

  5. (a) The process was stopped (by a signal, a debugger, or the system). (b) An endless loop or a deadlock in the computation of step 41 (for example, waiting for a lock or a device). (c) A device call that never returned (on GPUs: a kernel that never finishes, a driver fault). Evidence: process state on the node (ps shows "T" for stopped, "R" for running, "D" for waiting on I/O), CPU time growing or not, a stack trace with a debugger, the kernel and driver logs, the scheduler's node health record. Here: the evidence was produced by stopping rank 3 with SIGSTOP at step 41 (fail=3:41:hang), and the process state would have shown "T".
    6. 

  6. Step 40: the last checkpoint said "next step 40", written after step 39 at about 0.95 s; only step 40 (and the started step 41) would be redone.
    7. 

  7. Turn on the watchdog (the job ran with watchdog=0), with a timeout above the largest healthy wait (2 s for steps of about 22 ms), and run under restart.sh or a requeueing scheduler: the job would have aborted at about 3 s and resumed from step 40, as R2's ft_hang run shows for a similar fault.
    8.
