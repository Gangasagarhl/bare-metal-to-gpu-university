Story hook

Analogy

The halls do not belong to Wiremu's banquet team. They are booked through the booking office, which shares them among many teams: a banquet gets a set of halls, all at once, for a fixed evening, and when the evening ends, or when a more urgent booking arrives, the office knocks on the door. Two minutes later the halls must be empty.

Wiremu's team learned to work with the knock: when it comes, they write the state of the dinner on a card, and ask the office to book them again. The next evening, the office gives them other halls across the courtyard. The card is still in the cupboard of the old hall. They start the dinner from the beginning.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe what a distributed training job needs from a cluster scheduler: all its GPUs at once, on suitable nodes, for a bounded time, with a warning before it is stopped.
  • Write a batch script that turns the scheduler's warning into a checkpoint and a requeue.
  • Map ranks to GPUs from the node-local rank, and refuse an impossible job shape.
  • Derive the first-order optimum checkpoint interval, T* = √(2CM), and check it with a simulation.
  • Diagnose a requeued job that restarts from step 0.
Jargon box
Requeue
Simple words: putting a stopped job back in the queue so that it runs again later, possibly on other machines. Analogy: asking the booking office for another evening. Precise: a scheduler operation that returns a job that was running (or was stopped by preemption, failure or request) to the pending state under the same job identity; when it runs again it starts its batch script from the beginning, so the job itself must resume from its checkpoint. D1
Preemption warning signal
Simple words: a signal the scheduler sends a short time before it stops a job, so that the job can save its work. Analogy: the knock on the door two minutes before the halls must be empty. Precise: a signal (commonly SIGTERM or a user-chosen one) delivered to the job's processes a configured time before the time limit or before preemption, followed by a forced kill if the job has not ended; the job's handler should checkpoint and exit. D1 R2
Node-local rank
Simple words: a process's number among the processes of the same job on the same machine. Analogy: a cook's seat number within one hall, not within the whole banquet. Precise: the rank of a process within the group of processes sharing a node; it is used to choose one GPU per process; in MPI it can be computed with MPI_Comm_split_type(…, MPI_COMM_TYPE_SHARED, …), and launchers also set it in the environment (Open MPI: OMPI_COMM_WORLD_LOCAL_RANK). L1 R3
Optimum checkpoint interval
Simple words: how often to save so that the time spent saving and the work lost to failures add up to as little as possible. Analogy: writing the card often enough, but not so often that writing takes the evening. Precise: for checkpoint cost C and mean time between failures M (with C much smaller than M), the interval T that minimises the expected overhead is approximately T* = √(2CM) (Young's first-order approximation); higher-order estimates refine it when C is not small next to M. D2 D3 R1

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A big computer cluster is shared by many people, so a program for training a model does not start at once: it asks for a number of GPUs and waits until the cluster's booking system gives them all at once. It gets them for a limited time, and sometimes it is stopped early because someone more urgent needs them. So the program has to be polite and prepared: when it is warned that time is up, it saves its work, and asks to be put back in the line. Next time it may get different computers, so it must keep its saved work somewhere every computer can see.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What a training job asks of the scheduler

The booking office gives a banquet all its halls at once. DS303's F5-26 introduced GPUs as scheduler resources and device visibility; a distributed training job adds four needs. All at once: a job of N ranks is useless with N − 1 GPUs, because the first collective blocks (F8-16); the scheduler must allocate all of them together (gang scheduling). Placement: tensor-parallel groups belong inside one machine (F8-13), so a job asks for whole nodes and a fixed number of GPUs per node. Bounded time: every job has a time limit, often shorter than the training run, so long runs are a chain of jobs. A warning: before a time limit or preemption, the scheduler can send a signal a set time ahead, and then kills the job. D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

How often to checkpoint: Young's first-order interval

Let a checkpoint cost C hours, failures of the job arrive independently with mean time between failures M (exponential), and the interval between checkpoints be T hours of work, with C and T much smaller than M.

  1. Time spent checkpointing, per hour of work: C/T.
  2. Work lost per failure: on average half an interval, T/2 (failures fall uniformly within an interval when T is much smaller than M).
  3. Failures per hour: 1/M, so lost work per hour: T/(2M). (Restart time R adds R/M per hour, independent of T.)
  4. Overhead: f(T) = C/T + T/(2M). Its derivative −C/T² + 1/(2M) is zero at T² = 2CM.
  5. T* = √(2CM); at T*, both terms equal √(C/(2M)), and the overhead is √(2C/M) + R/M.

This is Young's approximation; Daly's paper gives a higher-order estimate that matters when C is not small next to M. D2 D3 The derivation's assumptions are exactly the ones that break at scale: for a job on n nodes that each fail independently with mean Mnode, the job's M is Mnode/n. More nodes, shorter M, shorter T*.

The simulation agrees, and shows where the formula stops

Compare with the formula. Small job: M = 500 h, T* = 10 h, predicted overhead √(2 × 0.1/500) + 0.2/500 = 0.0204, efficiency 1/1.0204 = 0.980; simulated 0.981. Large job: M = 15.6 h, T* = 1.77 h, predicted overhead 0.113 + 0.013 = 0.126, efficiency 0.888; simulated 0.881. R1 The first-order formula gets the best interval right and becomes optimistic about the efficiency as C/M grows: failures during checkpoints and restarts, ignored in the derivation, start to count. And "never checkpoint" on 128 nodes: efficiency 0.010, a mean wall time of 9,714 h for 100 h of work. R1

Warning

Every input in Listing 1 is invented. Real node MTBF depends on the hardware, its age and the site; measure it from your cluster's failure records (DS304's MTBF and MTTR) before using the formula, and remember that failures of nodes in the same rack or on the same switch are not independent.

Job shape, queues and time limits

The scheduler starts a job only when all its resources are free together, so a larger job usually waits longer in the queue; schedulers fill gaps with smaller jobs that fit before the large one's start (backfill, F5-26 and DS303), which works well only when time limits are realistic. A long training run is therefore planned as a chain of jobs, each shorter than the site limit, each ending in a checkpoint (by the warning signal or by its own clock), each requeued or submitted as a dependency of the previous one. Placement matters too: request whole nodes for tensor-parallel groups, and ask the site how to request nodes close together in the network if the scheduler supports topology-aware placement (DG402). D1

Node health and the restart path

A requeued job may land on the node that failed it. Two defences: a health check before training starts (every rank checks its GPU, prints its identity and runs a small all-reduce, so a broken node fails in seconds, not after an hour of waiting), and excluding a node that failed from the next run (schedulers provide an option to exclude named nodes; the job script can record the failing node from the logs). Both are cheap compared with a hung job of hundreds of GPUs (F8-16).

How the hardware actually does it

The scheduler's controller runs on a management node and talks to an agent on every compute node; the agent starts the job's processes, confines them to the allocated CPUs, memory and GPUs (through operating-system control groups and device rules on Linux), delivers signals and reports exits. D1 GPU memory is not shared between jobs in the usual configuration; a GPU is given to one job at a time, which is why the node-local mapping must be exactly right. Storage comes in two kinds: node-local disks (fast, private to one node, often cleared when the job ends) and a shared parallel file system (visible from every node, slower per node, shared by everyone). Checkpoints that a requeued job must read belong on the shared one, or must be copied there before the job ends. No speeds of either are quoted here: measure them on your cluster with the checkpoint you actually write, because C in the formula is that time.

Worked example

A job of 64 nodes, node MTBF 4,000 h (an invented value), checkpoint 6 minutes, restart 15 minutes. Choose T and estimate the efficiency.

  1. M = 4,000/64 = 62.5 h. C = 0.1 h, R = 0.25 h.
  2. T* = √(2 × 0.1 × 62.5) = √12.5 = 3.54 h of work between checkpoints.
  3. Overhead ≈ √(2 × 0.1/62.5) + 0.25/62.5 = √0.0032 + 0.004 = 0.0566 + 0.004 = 0.0606; efficiency about 1/1.0606 = 0.943.
  4. Check: C/M = 0.0016 is small, so the first-order formula should be close (compare R1's small-job case, C/M = 0.0002, error 0.001).
  5. The site's time limit is 24 h: about 6 checkpoints per job; set the warning signal so that a checkpoint (6 minutes) fits with a margin.
  6. Verify: add the line example 100 0.1 0.25 4000 64 to ckpt_interval.in and run Listing 1.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The booking office knows what a banquet is. A scheduler knows resources and time, nothing about steps or checkpoints; whether a requeued job resumes is entirely the job's business.
    • 

  • A knock always comes. A warning signal comes before time limits and (where configured) preemption; a node failure gives no warning at all, so periodic checkpoints are still needed.
    • 

  • Halls are interchangeable. Nodes are not: different GPU models, network positions, and local storage contents.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Worker in the foreground. The script's trap runs only after the worker ends, i.e. after the kill. Fix: background plus wait (Listing 3).
    • 

  • Reading 143 instead of 99. The first wait returned because of the trap. Fix: wait again.
    • 

  • Warning shorter than the checkpoint. The job is killed mid-write. Fix: measure C; set the warning to several C.
    • 

  • Checkpoint on node-local storage. A requeued job on another node starts from zero (forensic lab).
    • 

  • Two ranks on one GPU. Wrong job shape or launcher settings; every rank should print its node, local rank and GPU at start (Listing 5), and the program should refuse (Listing 6).
    • 

  • Checkpoint interval by habit. "Every hour" on 4 nodes and on 512 nodes are very different choices (R1).
