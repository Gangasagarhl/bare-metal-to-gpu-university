Story hook

Analogy

Four halls cook a banquet in a line. Hall 1 prepares the starter, hall 2 the soup, hall 3 the main course, hall 4 the dessert, and each tray travels down the line. At the end, the guests' comments travel back up the line, hall by hall, so that each hall can adjust its recipe for tomorrow.

On the first evening, Mateo sends one enormous tray for all two hundred guests. Hall 1 works while the others wait; then hall 2 works while the others wait; and so on, down and back. Each hall is busy for a quarter of the evening at most. On the second evening, Ayasha cuts the banquet into eight smaller trays. While hall 2 works on tray 1, hall 1 already starts tray 2. The halls still wait at the beginning and at the end of the evening, but much less. How small should the trays be, and what does each hall have to keep on its shelves while the comments travel back?

What you will be able to do

  • Explain pipeline parallelism: stages, micro-batches, activations sent forward and gradients sent back.
  • Derive the bubble fraction of the GPipe schedule, (S − 1)/(M + S − 1), and use it to choose the number of micro-batches.
  • Compare GPipe and 1F1B schedules: same bubble, different activation memory, shown by a schedule simulator.
  • Build a real pipeline with MPI, check its gradients against the single-process model and measure its bubble within 20 % of the formula (milestone F7, part 2).
  • Diagnose a slow pipeline from per-stage busy and idle times.
Jargon box
Pipeline parallelism
Simple words: putting different layers of the model on different GPUs and passing results from one to the next. Analogy: each hall does one course and passes the tray on. Precise: inter-layer model parallelism: the layers are partitioned into S consecutive stages, each on its own device (or group of devices); in the forward pass each stage receives activations from the previous stage and sends its outputs to the next; in the backward pass gradients flow the opposite way. D1
Micro-batch
Simple words: a small piece of the batch that travels through the pipeline on its own. Analogy: one of Ayasha's eight smaller trays. Precise: one of M equal parts of a mini-batch; micro-batches enter the pipeline one after another so that different stages work on different micro-batches at the same time; their gradients are accumulated and the optimizer step is taken once, after all M (synchronous pipeline). D1
Pipeline bubble
Simple words: the time GPUs in a pipeline sit idle at the start and end of each step. Analogy: halls waiting while the first trays travel down the line and the last comments travel back. Precise: the idle time of the stages during the fill and drain phases of a synchronous pipeline schedule; for S equal stages and M micro-batches under the GPipe schedule, the bubble is the fraction (S − 1)/(M + S − 1) of the step time. D1 R1
GPipe schedule
Simple words: every stage does all its forward passes first, then all its backward passes. Analogy: all trays go down the line before any comment comes back. Precise: the synchronous schedule of the GPipe paper: all M forward micro-batch steps, then all M backward steps, then one optimizer step; each stage holds the activations of all M micro-batches until their backward steps (unless it recomputes them). D1
1F1B schedule
Simple words: after a short warm-up, every stage alternates one forward and one backward pass. Analogy: a hall sends a comment back up the line as soon as it can, instead of waiting for all trays to pass. Precise: "one forward, one backward": stage s (counting from 0) first runs S − 1 − s forward steps (warm-up), then alternates forward and backward steps, then finishes the remaining backward steps; with equal stages it has the same bubble as GPipe but holds at most S − s micro-batches of activations instead of M. D2 R1
Activation memory (stash)
Simple words: the results of the forward pass that a stage must keep until it does the backward pass. Analogy: trays kept on the hall's shelves until the guests' comments come back. Precise: the intermediate tensors saved during the forward pass of a micro-batch and needed by its backward pass; its size grows with the number of micro-batches in flight on the stage and with the micro-batch size, and it can be traded for computation by recomputing activations during backward. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

If a model has too many layers for one GPU, you can put the first layers on one GPU, the next layers on another, and so on, like stations on an assembly line. The trouble is that a single big batch makes every station wait for the one before it. So you cut the batch into small pieces. While the second station works on piece 1, the first station starts piece 2. The line still starts empty and ends empty, and those empty moments are wasted. More pieces make the waste smaller, but every station must remember more pieces until the corrections come back.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Stages and messages

The halls pass trays forward and comments back. With S stages, stage s owns a consecutive group of layers. In the forward pass of one micro-batch, stage s receives the activation tensor from stage s − 1, runs its layers, and sends its output to stage s + 1. The last stage computes the loss. In the backward pass, stage s receives the gradient with respect to its output from stage s + 1, runs backpropagation through its layers, accumulates the gradients of its own weights, and sends the gradient with respect to its input to stage s − 1. Only the boundary tensors travel: no weights, no all-reduce inside the pipeline. D1

Listing 2 does exactly this with MPI: rank s is stage s and owns one layer, y = tanh(Ws x + bs) of width 16; micro-batches of 2 samples travel with MPI_Send and MPI_Recv; the message tag is the micro-batch number (plus 1,000 for gradients), so a stage always receives the micro-batch it expects. D3 L1

Correctness: the same gradient

Because the optimizer step is taken once after all M micro-batches, and every stage sums its weight gradients over the micro-batches, the pipeline computes exactly the gradient of the whole mini-batch: the same mathematics as one GPU, split differently. Milestone F7 requires: "Outputs match the single-GPU model within tolerance". M1 In Listing 2 each stage recomputes the whole model on its own and compares its weight gradient with the pipeline's; R2 shows a largest difference of 0 on every stage, for M = 1, 4, 8 and 16. Here the difference is exactly 0, not merely small, because every stage performs the same floating-point operations in the same order as the reference: the pipeline changes where the work runs, not how the sums are formed. R2

The bubble, derived

Take S stages, each needing tf for a forward step and tb for a backward step of one micro-batch, and ignore communication time. Under the GPipe schedule:

  1. The last stage can start micro-batch 0's forward step only after S − 1 forward steps upstream: (S − 1) tf of fill.
  2. The last forward step on the last stage ends at (M + S − 1) tf.
  3. Symmetrically, the backward steps take (M + S − 1) tb from the first backward step on the last stage to the last backward step on the first stage.
  4. Step time: T = (M + S − 1)(tf + tb). Useful work per stage: M (tf + tb).
  5. Bubble fraction: 1 − M / (M + S − 1) = (S − 1)/(M + S − 1).

The GPipe paper gives this overhead in the form O((K − 1)/(M + K − 1)) with K partitions. D1 The schedule simulator computes it cell by cell; R1 prints 0.750 for M = 1, 0.429 for M = 4, 0.273 for M = 8 and 0.086 for M = 32 with S = 4, each equal to the formula. R1 Note that tf and tb cancel: the fraction depends only on S and M.

The measured bubble

Listing 2 measures the bubble on a real (if tiny) pipeline: each forward step sleeps tf = 20 ms and each backward step 40 ms, standing in for GPU kernels, and each stage records how long it was busy. The measured bubble is 1 − (sum of busy times)/(S × step time). The milestone's acceptance test is that "the measured pipeline bubble fraction matches the formula from the GPipe paper within 20 %". M1 R2's four runs printed "within 20 %" for M = 1, 4, 8 and 16; the exact milliseconds change a little from run to run (the container is shared), so read them in the outputs above rather than in this text. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Choosing M: the bubble against everything else

The formula says: more micro-batches, smaller bubble. Three forces push back:

  • Global batch. With a fixed micro-batch size, M micro-batches make the mini-batch M times larger, with the optimisation consequences of F8-12. With a fixed mini-batch, larger M means smaller micro-batches.
  • Efficiency of small micro-batches. A GPU runs a tiny micro-batch less efficiently than a large one (small GEMMs, fixed per-kernel costs), and every micro-batch pays the per-message cost α of its boundary transfers twice per stage boundary. Shrinking micro-batches to shrink the bubble eventually costs more than it saves.
  • Activation memory. Under GPipe, each stage holds the activations of all M micro-batches until their backward steps. R1's "holds at most" column: 8 for M = 8 and 32 for M = 32, on every stage. The GPipe paper reduces this by recomputing activations during backward (re-materialisation), paying extra forward computation. D1 R1
Not verified — check before relying on this

From memory, not opened in this build: the GPipe paper reports that the bubble overhead is negligible when M is at least about four times K (the number of partitions), in its experiments with re-materialisation. Check the GPipe paper (D1), section on performance and the bubble, before using any such rule; this chapter uses only the formula, which it derives.

1F1B: the same bubble with bounded memory

The 1F1B ("one forward, one backward") schedule changes the order of steps on each stage, not the dependencies. Stage s runs S − 1 − s warm-up forwards, then alternates, then drains. In R1, for S = 4 and M = 8, both schedules take 33 units with a bubble of 0.273, but the peak number of micro-batches whose activations a stage holds falls from 8 on every stage (GPipe) to 4, 3, 2, 1 (1F1B, stages 0 to 3). With M = 32 the GPipe stages hold 32, the 1F1B stages still 4, 3, 2, 1. R1 This schedule appears in the PipeDream line of work and in the later Megatron-LM paper on training at scale, which also describes an interleaved variant that gives each device several smaller stages to shrink the bubble at the price of more communication. D2 D4

Note

Asynchronous pipeline schemes (some PipeDream variants) avoid the bubble altogether by not flushing the pipeline between optimizer steps, at the price of computing gradients on slightly stale weights, which changes the training mathematics. This course uses only synchronous schedules, whose result equals the single-GPU computation, because that is what the milestone's acceptance test can check.

Imbalance: the slowest stage sets the pace

The formula assumes equal stages. If one stage takes k times longer than the others, the pipeline's steady state runs at the pace of that stage: every other stage waits for it on every micro-batch. A rough model for GPipe with one stage of time k t and the others t (t = tf + tb): step time ≈ (S − 1) t + M k t. The bubble formula no longer predicts anything; per-stage busy and idle times do. The forensic lab is exactly this case. Balancing stages is a partitioning problem: equal compute per stage (not equal layer counts: embedding and output layers are often heavier), and equal memory per stage, which conflict when 1F1B makes the first stage hold the most activations.

Communication in the pipeline

Per micro-batch, each stage boundary carries one activation tensor forward and one gradient tensor back, of size (micro-batch tokens) × (width) × (bytes per value): point-to-point messages, not collectives. They are small compared with a data-parallel gradient all-reduce, which is why pipeline stages can be placed across machines connected by a slower network, while tensor parallelism stays inside the machine (F8-13). In a combined job, every pipeline stage is itself a tensor-parallel group, and each (stage, tensor rank) position has data-parallel replicas that all-reduce their weight gradients after the pipeline step. D4

How the hardware actually does it

In the halls, a tray waits on a cart between halls. On GPUs, a stage boundary is a send from one GPU's memory to another's: over a GPU-to-GPU link inside a machine, or through the network (with GPUDirect RDMA where available, DG402) between machines. The collective libraries provide point-to-point send and receive on GPU streams, and a stage overlaps them with its computation by using separate streams for communication; MPI can do the same with non-blocking calls. D5 D3 Activation memory lives in GPU memory (HBM on data-centre GPUs), and the "holds at most" column of R1 is, multiplied by the size of one micro-batch's activations, a direct estimate of how much of that memory the schedule needs. No GPU memory size is quoted here; read yours from the vendor's data sheet or from the device query of CU201.

Worked example

Choose M for a pipeline of S = 8 equal stages so that the bubble is at most 10 %.

  1. Require (S − 1)/(M + S − 1) ≤ 0.10, so 7 ≤ 0.10 (M + 7), so M + 7 ≥ 70, so M ≥ 63.
  2. Check M = 63: 7/69 = 0.101, slightly above; M = 64: 7/70 = 0.100. Take M = 64.
  3. Memory under GPipe: each stage holds 64 micro-batches of activations at its peak. Under 1F1B: stage 0 holds 8, stage 7 holds 1 (S − s).
  4. If one micro-batch's activations on a stage take a MiB (measure it on your model), GPipe needs 64 a MiB per stage for activations, 1F1B at most 8 a MiB.
  5. Check with the simulator: R1's last case, S = 8, M = 8, gives 0.467 = 7/15; adding the line 8 64 1 2 1f1b to pipe_sim.in gives 7/70 = 0.100.

The arithmetic in steps 1–2 is exact; the S = 8, M = 8 case is in R1. R1 The engineering conclusion: a deep pipeline needs many micro-batches, and many micro-batches need 1F1B (or recomputation) to fit in memory.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Trays move; activations are copied. A stage sends a copy of its output and keeps the original (the stash) for its backward step. The hall in the story hands the tray away; the GPU cannot.
    • 

  • Guests' comments are not gradients of the whole meal. In backpropagation each stage needs the gradient from the next stage for the same micro-batch, in a strict order; there is no shortcut where a hall adjusts its recipe from its own tasting.
    • 

  • Real halls can speed up when they see a queue. A pipeline stage cannot: its time is fixed by its layers, and an unbalanced split slows every stage to the pace of the slowest one.
    • 

  • The analogy hides that the optimizer waits for all trays. In a synchronous pipeline no weights change until every micro-batch's backward step is done; that wait is what makes the drain bubble.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Deadlock at the boundary. Symptom: stages stop with no error. Cause: both neighbours call a blocking send to each other first, or receive with the wrong tag or source. Find it: log every send and receive with micro-batch and tag; F8-16's watchdog finds the stall. Fix: a strict order (forward sends go down, backward sends go up) or non-blocking calls.
    • 

  • Wrong gradients. Symptom: the per-stage gradient check fails. Cause: gradients averaged over micro-batches on some stages and summed on others, or the stash of one micro-batch used for another. Fix: tag every stash entry with its micro-batch; compare with the single-process model as Listing 2 does.
    • 

  • Out of memory only with more micro-batches. Cause: GPipe holds all M stashes (R1). Fix: 1F1B, recomputation, or fewer micro-batches.
    • 

  • Bubble far worse than the formula. Cause: unbalanced stages (forensic lab) or boundary transfers not overlapped. Find it: per-stage busy and idle times.
    • 

  • Measured bubble that cannot be trusted. Cause: timing on a shared machine. Fix: repeat runs and report the median (MA202, curriculum 13.4), and state the machine.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F8-12 replicated the model and all-reduced gradients; F8-13 split each layer and all-reduced activations inside a group. What carries over: the result must equal the single-GPU computation within a tolerance; communication must be matched exactly on both sides; a model of the step predicts before you measure. M1 What changes: communication becomes point-to-point between neighbours (MPI_Send/MPI_Recv, or the libraries' send and receive), and the main cost is not bytes but idle time: the bubble, and the activation memory of micro-batches in flight. D1 D3 What comes next: F8-15 attacks the memory that every data-parallel replica still duplicates: the optimizer state, the gradients and the parameters.

Summary


    

  • Pipeline parallelism splits the layers into stages; activations go forward, gradients come back, point-to-point.
    • 

  • Micro-batches let stages work at the same time; the step still has a fill and a drain: the bubble.
    • 

  • For equal stages under GPipe, the bubble fraction is (S − 1)/(M + S − 1); R1 reproduces it exactly and R2 measured it within 20 % on a real MPI pipeline.
    • 

  • GPipe holds the activations of all M micro-batches; 1F1B has the same bubble and holds at most S − s.
    • 

  • Gradients equal the single-process computation: the pipeline changes where the work runs, not the mathematics.
    • 

  • An unbalanced stage sets the pace of the whole pipeline; per-stage busy times reveal it.
    •
