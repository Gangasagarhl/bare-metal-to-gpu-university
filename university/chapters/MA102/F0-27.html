Story hook

Analogy

Before bedtime, Hana's mum checks the house. By the front door there is a small green light. It should glow only when everything is shut: "NOT (the door is open OR the window is open)".

Hana says it differently: "The light is green when the door is closed AND the window is closed." Her older brother Kofi says it a third way: "The light is green when the door is closed OR the window is closed." All three sound sensible at bedtime.

But are they the same rule? Two of them are. One is not, and it could leave a window open all night with a green light glowing. In this chapter you will learn the rules for rewriting such sentences safely, and how to prove that two rules really are the same.

What you will be able to do

  • Write logic rules as expressions with AND, OR and NOT, using letters for the inputs.
  • Use the basic laws of Boolean algebra (identity, complement, double negation, commutative, associative, distributive) to rewrite an expression.
  • State and apply De Morgan's two laws.
  • Prove that two expressions are equal with a complete truth table, and show that two are different with one counterexample row.
  • Diagnose a wrong "simplification" of a safety rule (the forensic lab).
Jargon box
Boolean algebra
Simple words: rules for rewriting true/false sentences, like the rules for rewriting sums. Analogy: tidying the house rules on the fridge without changing what they mean. Precise: the algebra of the values 0 and 1 with the operations AND, OR and NOT, and the laws they satisfy; named after the mathematician George Boole. W1 D1 ch. 6
Expression (Boolean)
Simple words: a rule written with letters, AND, OR, NOT and brackets. Analogy: "NOT (door open OR window open)" written short as NOT (D OR W). Precise: a formula built from Boolean variables, the constants 0 and 1, the operators AND, OR, NOT and brackets; it has one output value for each combination of variable values. W1
Equivalent expressions
Simple words: two rules that always give the same answer. Analogy: two ways of saying the same house rule. Precise: two Boolean expressions are equivalent when their outputs agree in every row of their truth table. W1
Counterexample
Simple words: one case that shows a claim is wrong. Analogy: one open window under a green light. Precise: an assignment of input values for which two expressions claimed equal give different outputs; one counterexample is enough to disprove equivalence. W1
De Morgan's laws
Simple words: to push a NOT inside a bracket, flip each part and swap AND with OR. Analogy: "not (open door or open window)" means "closed door and closed window". Precise: NOT (A AND B) = (NOT A) OR (NOT B), and NOT (A OR B) = (NOT A) AND (NOT B); named after the mathematician Augustus De Morgan. W1 R1 D1 ch. 6 D5

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows De Morgan's two laws as a recipe. The NOT outside the bracket moves inside: each letter gets its own NOT, and the AND or OR in the middle swaps.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

In F0-17 you rewrote number sentences without changing their meaning: 3 + 4 is the same as 4 + 3. True/false sentences have rewriting rules too. Together, those rules are called Boolean algebra.

Some rules are easy to believe. "Door shut AND window shut" means the same as "window shut AND door shut". "NOT NOT open" means "open", as "it's not true that the door isn't open" means it is open.

The most useful rules are De Morgan's laws. They tell you what happens when a NOT sits outside a bracket. "It is NOT true that (the door is open OR the window is open)." That means neither is open. So the door is closed AND the window is closed.

See what happened? The NOT went inside and touched each part. And the OR turned into an AND. That is the whole trick: flip each part, and swap AND with OR.

How can you be sure two rules mean the same? Try every case. With two inputs there are only four cases. If the rules agree in all four, they are the same. If they disagree in even one case, they are different. That one case is a counterexample.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A Boolean expression uses letters for inputs (A, B, C), the constants 0 and 1, and AND, OR, NOT. Two expressions are equivalent when they give the same output in every row of the truth table. Because there are only 2n rows for n inputs, you can always check by trying them all. This is a complete proof, not a guess. W1

Each law below can be checked with a truth table of at most 8 rows. W1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The names of the laws in this table (identity, null, idempotent and so on) are the names this course uses. Books do not all use the same names, and the verification pass could not open the textbook named for them, D2 (only its chapter list was readable). The laws themselves do not depend on their names: each one is proved by its own truth table.

Look at the pattern: every law has a twin made by swapping AND with OR and 0 with 1 (double negation is its own twin). The second distributive law has no twin in ordinary arithmetic: 2 + (3 × 4) is not (2 + 3) × (2 + 4). Be careful not to carry number habits across without checking. W1

In C++ the same laws hold for bool values with &&, || and !, and bit by bit for &, | and ~ (F0-25): ~(a | b) equals ~a & ~b in every bit, because these operators work on each bit position separately. D3 §7.6.2.1, §7.6.11, §7.6.14, §7.6.15 W1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Proving by trying every row (some books give this method a name; see the box below) works because the input space is finite; it stops being practical when there are many inputs (each extra input doubles the rows), and then engineers use algebraic steps, or tools that reason about the expressions. Our compiler did some of this reasoning by itself. Listing 3 compares !(a || b) with !a && !b in one expression. With our flags it refused to build: it reported that the comparison "always evaluates to true", which means it had worked out that the two sides are the same. R3 GCC documents this warning, -Wtautological-compare, as a warning for a self-comparison that always evaluates to true or false, switched on by -Wall; -Werror turned it into an error. D6

Not verified — check before relying on this

Two statements about the wider world could not be checked against an opened source: that some textbooks call proof by trying every row "perfect induction", and that chip design tools use Boolean algebra to replace an expression with an equivalent one that needs fewer or cheaper gates. Both point to digital-design textbooks such as D2, whose text was not readable in the verification pass. Check them there before relying on them.

How the hardware actually does it


De Morgan's laws let you build one kind of gate from another: A OR B = NOT ((NOT A) AND (NOT B)), so an OR of inputs can be made from an AND of flipped inputs, followed by a NOT. W1 Hardware is built this way, with complex gates composed from more elementary ones; And, Or and Not can even all be made from Nand gates alone. D4 §1.1.1, §1.1.2 You will use the laws by hand when you build gates in HW102.

Simplify with algebra


Simplify NOT (NOT A OR B) AND B.


    

  1. De Morgan on the bracket: NOT (NOT A OR B) = (NOT NOT A) AND (NOT B).
    2. 

  2. Double negation: = A AND (NOT B).
    3. 

  3. So the whole expression is A AND (NOT B) AND B.
    4. 

  4. Associative and commutative laws let us group (B AND NOT B).
    5. 

  5. Complement: B AND (NOT B) = 0. Null: A AND 0 = 0.
    6. 

  6. Answer: the expression is always 0, whatever A and B are.
    7. 



Disprove with one counterexample


Claim: NOT (A AND B) = (NOT A) AND (NOT B). Try A = 1, B = 0. Left: NOT (1 AND 0) = NOT 0 = 1. Right: (NOT 1) AND (NOT 0) = 0 AND 1 = 0. They differ, so the claim is false. One row is enough. W1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Everyday sentences are fuzzy; Boolean expressions are not. "Shut the door or the window" might mean "one of them" or "at least one". In Boolean algebra, OR always includes the "both" case (F0-26). Translate carefully before you apply a law.
    • 

  • Tidying the fridge rules can change their spirit; equivalent expressions never change their output. Rewriting a house rule in nicer words sometimes changes what people do. A correct algebraic rewrite gives the same output in every row, guaranteed by the truth table. W1
    • 

  • Number habits do not all carry over. In numbers, 1 + 1 = 2; in Boolean algebra, 1 OR 1 = 1. The second distributive law works for Boolean values but not for numbers. W1
