Story hook

Lina's family has moved into a house she has never seen, and the power is out for the evening. She has a torch, a pad of squared paper and a pencil. Her uncle tells her exactly where she is standing each time: "you are on the third tile from the door, facing the window". So Lina only has one job: draw.

She shines the torch in one direction. Where the light lands on something, a wall, a sofa, she shades that square a little. Every square the beam crossed before it landed must be empty floor, so she rubs those a little lighter. She does not press hard: one look could be wrong, a shadow, a reflection. When she looks again from another place and sees the same wall, the square gets darker. A square she has never lit stays plain white paper: unknown, not empty.

After an hour of walking and shining, the page shows the house. Dark squares are walls and furniture, light ones are floor, white ones are corners she never saw. That page is an occupancy grid, and the rule Lina used, "a little darker where the light lands, a little lighter where it passes", is the whole algorithm of this chapter.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what an occupancy grid represents, why each cell holds a probability, and why mapping with known poses is a separate problem from SLAM.
  • Derive the log-odds update of a cell from Bayes' rule and the independence assumption, and compute it by hand for a sequence of observations.
  • Implement an inverse sensor model for a 2D LiDAR with ray tracing (Bresenham), clamping and correct handling of beams with no return.
  • Map the simulated house of this course and measure the map against the true floor plan.
  • Diagnose phantom obstacles in a map from scan data and a map print (forensic lab).
Jargon box
Occupancy grid
Simple words: a map made of small squares, each holding how likely it is that something solid is there. Analogy: Lina's squared paper, shaded darker where the torch landed. Precise: a regular tessellation of the environment into cells, each with a binary random variable "occupied"; the map stores the posterior probability of occupancy of every cell given all measurements and poses, under the assumption that cells are independent. D1 D3
Mapping with known poses
Simple words: drawing a map when someone tells you exactly where you are each time you look. Analogy: the uncle calling out Lina's tile. Precise: estimating the map m from measurements z1:t and poses x1:t that are treated as given; SLAM (F9-53) removes that assumption. D1
Log-odds
Simple words: a way to store a probability as a number that you can add to. Analogy: how many pencil strokes a square has received, darker minus lighter. Precise: l = log(p / (1 − p)); p = 1 − 1/(1 + el). Bayesian updates of a binary variable with independent evidence become additions of log-odds. D1
Inverse sensor model
Simple words: the rule that says what one sensor reading tells you about each square. Analogy: "darker where the light lands, lighter where it passes". Precise: p(mi | zt, xt), the probability that cell i is occupied given only the current measurement and pose; called inverse because it reasons from effect (the reading) to cause (the world). D1
Ray tracing (grid traversal)
Simple words: listing every square that a straight beam passes through. Analogy: the squares the torch light crosses on the paper. Precise: enumerating the cells intersected by a line segment between two cells; this chapter uses Bresenham's integer line algorithm, which visits one cell per step of the major axis. D5
Max-range reading (no return)
Simple words: a beam that did not hit anything the sensor could see. Analogy: the torch light fades out before reaching any wall. Precise: a range value reported at (or beyond) the sensor's maximum because no echo was detected; it is evidence of free space up to the range limit, never evidence of an obstacle. D1 D4
Clamping
Simple words: not letting a square get darker or lighter than a limit. Analogy: Lina never presses so hard that she cannot rub it out again. Precise: bounding each cell's log-odds to an interval [lmin, lmax] so that the map stays able to change when the world changes (a door opens). D1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows one LiDAR beam and the cells it affects. The beam starts in the robot's cell and ends in the cell where it hit a wall. Every cell before the end gets a little evidence for "free"; the end cell gets evidence for "occupied"; cells behind the wall get nothing at all, because the beam never saw them.

Figure 2 is the whole pipeline of the lab: a floor plan, a route, a simulated LiDAR, and the grid that collects the evidence. The robot's pose comes from the simulator's ground truth, which is what "known poses" means.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot that wants to move around a house needs a picture of where things are. The simplest picture is squared paper. Each square says "something is here", "nothing is here", or "I have not looked yet".

The robot's laser works like Lina's torch. It sends light in many directions and measures how far each ray went before it hit something. Where a ray stopped, there is probably something solid. Where it passed, there is probably floor.

The robot never trusts a single look. It adds a little evidence each time, and a square becomes "wall" only after several looks agree. That makes the map calm: one wrong reading does not draw a wall in the middle of the room.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What the grid stores

Divide the floor into square cells of side r (here 0.10 m: an 8 m by 6 m house becomes 80 by 60 cells). Each cell i has an unknown binary state mi: occupied or free. The map we want is the probability p(mi = occupied | z1:t, x1:t) for every cell, given every measurement z and every pose x so far. D1

Computing the joint posterior over all cells at once is impossible: with 4,800 cells there are 24800 maps. The standard occupancy grid makes one strong simplification: each cell is estimated independently of the others. That loses something real (walls are continuous; if one cell of a wall is occupied, its neighbour probably is too) but makes the problem one tiny binary Bayes filter per cell. D1 D3

The update in log-odds

For one binary variable with a static state, Bayes' rule (MA202, and the Bayes filter of F9-31) gives, after some algebra, an update that is a sum when written in log-odds l = log(p/(1−p)):

lt,i = lt−1,i + log( p(mi | zt, xt) / (1 − p(mi | zt, xt)) ) − l0

The middle term is the inverse sensor model in log-odds; l0 is the prior (here 0, meaning p = 0.5: "no idea"). The derivation assumes that measurements are conditionally independent given the map, which is approximately true for well-separated readings and false for, say, a hundred readings of the same reflective spot. D1

This lab's inverse sensor model is the simplest useful one. If the beam ended in the cell, p = 0.7, so locc = log(0.7/0.3) = 0.8473, rounded to 0.85. If the beam passed through the cell, p = 0.4, so lfree = log(0.4/0.6) = −0.4055, rounded to −0.40. Cells the beam did not reach get no update. R2

Which cells a beam touches

The lab traces each beam from the robot's cell to its end cell with Bresenham's line algorithm. It steps one cell at a time along the longer axis and decides with integer arithmetic when to step on the other axis, so it visits a connected chain of cells. Two details matter. First, the end cell is the one containing the measured end point; with range noise, that is sometimes the free cell just in front of the wall, which is why walls in the lab's map are about one cell thicker on the robot's side (you will measure that). Second, a beam that reports the maximum range had no echo: it updates every crossed cell as free, the last one too, and marks nothing occupied. D5 D1

Clamping, and why a map must be able to change its mind

If you add 0.85 a hundred times, a cell's log-odds is 85 and its probability is 1 to the precision of a double. When the door then opens, it would take more than two hundred "free" observations to bring it back below 0.5. The lab clamps every cell to [−4, 4], that is p between 0.018 and 0.982. The worked example shows the price: a door cell observed closed 12 times becomes free again after 12 free observations with clamping and after 28 without. R2 D1

What the lab produces

Driving the 25.75 m route and scanning every 0.25 m (97 scans of 360 beams), the grid ends with 4,079 of 4,800 cells known. Measured against the floor plan, 452 occupied cells are correct, 252 cells are marked occupied although the plan says free, and 1 cell is marked free although it is a wall. All 252 wrong "occupied" cells are next to a true wall: none is farther than one cell. That is the discretisation effect described above, not a mapping failure, and the metric "far from walls" separates the two. R1




Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


The derivation, and what it assumes


Write the posterior of one cell with Bayes' rule, conditioning on the newest measurement: p(m | z1:t) = p(zt | m, z1:t−1) p(m | z1:t−1) / p(zt | z1:t−1). Assume the Markov property (given m, zt is independent of earlier measurements), apply Bayes' rule again to turn p(zt | m) into p(m | zt) p(zt) / p(m), and divide the expression for m = occupied by the one for m = free. The unknown normalisers cancel and the logarithm of the ratio is the sum of the previous log-odds, the inverse sensor model's log-odds, and minus the prior log-odds. Poses are inside every conditional and play no role in the algebra: that is exactly why the poses must be known, or estimated elsewhere. D1


Three assumptions hide in that short derivation, and each one breaks in a recognisable way:


    

  • Static world. A person walking past leaves a trail of occupied cells that only clears as fast as lfree and the clamp allow. Navigation stacks therefore keep a separate obstacle layer for things that move (F9-56).
    • 

  • Independent cells. A beam ending between two cells updates only one; a wide sonar cone would update many with the same evidence and the independence assumption makes them over-confident together. Probabilistic Robotics describes a forward-model alternative that reasons about all cells a measurement touches at once; it is more expensive and rarely used for LiDAR. D1 D3
    • 

  • Independent measurements. A robot standing still and scanning at 10 Hz for a minute adds 600 identical observations; the cells reach the clamp although no new information arrived. The lab only scans after the robot has moved 0.25 m.
    • 



Choosing the inverse sensor model


The two numbers locc and lfree encode how much you trust one reading, and their ratio matters more than their size. With locc = 0.85 and lfree = −0.40, a wall cell that is hit once and passed once by a grazing beam still ends positive. With a model where lfree is larger than locc in magnitude, grazing beams (which pass through the corner of a wall cell because of discretisation) erase thin walls and door frames. A common refinement is to add a small band behind the end point that is also marked occupied, of the size of the range noise, so that the wall's true cell gets the evidence more often; this lab does not do that, and you can see the consequence in the 252 boundary cells. D1 R1


Cost and resolution


Memory grows with area divided by r2: halving the cell size quadruples the cells. Update time grows with the number of beams times the beam length in cells: halving r doubles the work per beam. At 0.10 m and 360 beams of up to 40 cells, one scan touches at most 14,400 cells; the whole lab, including the slow 1 cm ray marching of the simulator, ran in a fraction of a second on the build container. Finer grids also need better poses: a map with 0.05 m cells built from poses that are only good to 0.10 m smears every wall over two cells. That observation is the bridge to F9-53. R1


Beyond two dimensions


The same per-cell log-odds idea extends to 3D voxels. Storing a dense 3D array is expensive, so 3D mapping libraries use sparse trees (octrees) that only store the regions that were observed, with the same clamping idea to keep cells changeable. Robots that drive on a flat floor usually project 3D data into a 2D grid for navigation and keep 3D only for obstacles at different heights (a table top that a 2D LiDAR at knee height cannot see). D6

How the hardware actually does it


A 2D LiDAR (F9-11) measures ranges by timing light or by measuring phase, one direction at a time, while its head spins. Three properties of real devices change the mapping code:


    

  • No-return encoding. When no echo arrives, a device reports something that is not a range: zero, a value above its maximum, or infinity, depending on the driver. The scan message carries the minimum and maximum valid range, and the mapper must treat everything outside that interval as "no return". That is precisely the bug of this chapter's forensic lab. D4
    • 

  • Surfaces that do not return light. Glass, mirrors and very dark materials produce no echo or a wrong one; a glass door is often mapped as free space. Mapping cannot fix this; a second sensor or a manual edit of the saved map does.
    • 

  • Motion during a scan. A spinning head takes time to sweep a full turn, so the first and last beams of a scan are measured from different robot poses. At walking speed this is small; while the robot turns on the spot it is not. F9-53's forensic lab is about a timing error of this kind.
    • 



Not verified — check before relying on this

Worked example


A door cell is seen closed (beam ends in it) 12 times, then the door opens and the cell is crossed by beams again and again. With locc = 0.85, lfree = −0.40, prior 0, the map calls a cell occupied above p = 0.65 and free below p = 0.35. In log-odds those thresholds are log(0.65/0.35) = +0.619 and −0.619.


    

  1. After hit 1: l = 0.85, p = 1 − 1/(1 + e0.85) = 0.701. Already "occupied" by the 0.65 threshold: one reading is enough to draw a wall, which is why locc must not be larger.
    2. 

  2. Unclamped, after 12 hits: l = 12 × 0.85 = 10.20. Clamped: l reaches 3.40 after 4 hits, and 4.00 (p = 0.982) from hit 5 on.
    3. 

  3. Clamped, the free observations bring l from 4.00 down by 0.40 each. Below −0.619 needs 4.00 − 0.40 n < −0.619, so n > 11.5: the 12th free observation (observation 24 overall) gives l = −0.80, p = 0.310: free.
    4. 

  4. Unclamped, 10.20 − 0.40 n < −0.619 needs n > 27.05: the 28th free observation (observation 40) gives l = −1.00, p = 0.269.
    5. 



The program prints the same numbers: R2




The lesson is quantitative: with clamping, the time to forget equals the time to learn the clamp level, whatever happened before. Without it, the time to forget grows with how long the old state was observed. The clamp value is therefore a tuning decision about how dynamic your world is.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lina's uncle knows exactly where she stands. Nobody tells a real robot its pose; odometry drifts (F9-09). Mapping with known poses is a teaching step and a component of SLAM, not something a robot can do on its own. F9-53 removes the uncle.
    • 

  • Lina's eyes see that a wall is continuous and fill gaps. The grid has no such knowledge: each cell is estimated alone, so a wall seen only at a grazing angle can have holes, and nothing in the algorithm closes them.
    • 

  • A torch beam that fades out tells Lina "I could not see". A LiDAR's "no return" arrives as a number in the same array as real ranges. Lina would never mistake darkness for a wall; software that forgets to check the maximum range does exactly that.
    • 

  • Pencil strokes on paper add up linearly. Probabilities do not; only their log-odds do, and only under the independence assumptions of Layer 3.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Phantom arcs of obstacles at the range limit. Symptom: rings or arcs of occupied cells at a fixed distance from where the robot stood, in open space. Cause: no-return readings treated as hits. Find it: print the raw ranges and count how many equal the maximum exactly. Fix: line 35 of Listing 1, plus the driver's documented invalid-range values.
    • 

  • Map mirrored or rotated by 90°. Symptom: the map is a correct house in the wrong orientation. Cause: beam angles counted clockwise instead of counter-clockwise, or x and y swapped when indexing l_[j * kW + i]. Find it: put the robot in a corridor facing +x and check which cells beam 0 marks.
    • 

  • Walls that never appear. Symptom: rooms bounded by unknown cells. Cause: lfree stronger than locc plus grazing beams, or the end cell marked free by an off-by-one in the traversal loop. Find it: log the updates of one wall cell over a run.
    • 

  • A map that never forgets. Symptom: a person who walked by stays in the map for minutes. Cause: no clamping, or a clamp so wide it never matters. Fix: clamp, and keep moving objects in a separate layer.
    • 

  • Out-of-bounds writes. Symptom: AddressSanitizer reports a heap-buffer-overflow in add, or (without sanitizers) random corruption. Cause: a beam end point outside the grid. Fix: check bounds in every cell access (line 54), and build every lab with the sanitizers as run_lab.sh does.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F9-31 and F9-36 estimated the robot's state with a Bayes filter, a Kalman filter and particles; F9-11 read LiDAR data. What carries over: Bayes' rule, the habit of storing belief rather than a single guess, and the noise models of sensors. D1 What changes: the unknown is no longer a few state variables but thousands of cells; the problem is made tractable by assuming cells independent and poses known, both of which are approximations you must keep in mind; log-odds replace probabilities in the update. What comes next: F9-53 drops the known poses: the robot must estimate where it is from the same scans it uses to build the map, which is SLAM.

Summary


    

  • An occupancy grid stores, for every cell, the probability that it is occupied, under the assumptions of independent cells, a static world and known poses.
    • 

  • In log-odds the per-cell Bayes update is a sum: previous value plus the inverse sensor model minus the prior.
    • 

  • The inverse sensor model marks cells a beam crossed as free and the end cell as occupied, and a beam with no return marks nothing occupied.
    • 

  • Clamping keeps the map able to change; with clamping, forgetting takes as long as learning the clamp level.
    • 

  • In the lab, 97 scans gave 4,079 known cells and no wrong cell farther than one cell from a wall; boundary errors come from discretisation and range noise.
    • 

  • Treating "no return" as a hit draws phantom obstacles at the range limit; it stays hidden until the range limit is shorter than the rooms.
    •
