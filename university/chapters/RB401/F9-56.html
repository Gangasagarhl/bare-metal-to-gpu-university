Story hook

Ravi's ride to his grandmother's house has two parts. At home he looks at the map and chooses the streets: that is planning. On the road he does something else entirely: he keeps the bike on the chosen street, swerves around a parked van that no map shows, and slows down at the corner. If a street is closed by road works, he stops, thinks again, and picks another way. If he has gone round the same block twice without getting closer, he gets off, walks back a little and asks again.

A navigation stack is all of that, as separate programs that talk to each other: a map, a planner, a path follower, a layer of "things I can see now" on top of the map, a check that the robot is still making progress, and a list of things to try when it is not. On ROS 2 the usual stack is Nav2. This chapter builds a miniature one, runs it through the simulated house, and then shows what one wrong number does to it.

What you will be able to do

  • Name the parts of a navigation stack and what each one decides: localisation, costmaps and their layers, global planner, controller, goal and progress checkers, recoveries, and the behaviour tree that orders them.
  • Configure a costmap's footprint and inflation for a robot, and justify each number from the robot and the map.
  • Trace a navigation run in an event log: plans, replans, recoveries and the final result.
  • Compute a pure-pursuit command and an inflation cost by hand.
  • Diagnose a robot that touches door frames from its configuration and logs (forensic lab), and rehearse the practical exam "configure navigation for a new map".
Jargon box
Navigation stack
Simple words: the set of programs that take a robot from "go there" to arriving. Analogy: Ravi's map-reading at home plus his steering on the road. Precise: a pipeline of localisation, map representation (costmaps), global planning, local control, goal and progress checking, and recovery, coordinated by a task executive. D2
Costmap
Simple words: a grid that says, for each square, how bad it is to put the robot's centre there. Analogy: the map with dangerous streets shaded darker. Precise: a 2D grid of costs from free to lethal, built by combining layers (static map, obstacles seen by sensors, inflation) and used by planners and controllers. D6
Costmap layer
Simple words: one source of costs on the costmap. Analogy: one transparent sheet on top of the map: road works on one sheet, parked vans on another. Precise: a plugin that writes costs into the costmap: the static layer copies the map, the obstacle layer marks and clears cells from sensor data, the inflation layer adds costs around lethal cells. D6
Footprint and inflation radius
Simple words: how big the robot is, and how far from walls it should prefer to stay. Analogy: the width of the bike, and the extra space you like to keep from parked cars. Precise: the footprint (a radius or polygon) defines lethal cells for the robot's centre; inflation adds a cost that decays with distance up to the inflation radius, so planners prefer paths with clearance. D1
Global planner and controller
Simple words: the part that chooses the whole route, and the part that steers along it moment by moment. Analogy: reading the map at home, and steering on the road. Precise: the global planner computes a path from the current pose to the goal on the costmap (slowly, about once a second here); the controller computes velocity commands many times a second to follow the path while respecting limits and nearby obstacles. D2
Pure pursuit
Simple words: steer towards a point a little way ahead on the path. Analogy: looking a few metres ahead on the road, not at your front wheel. Precise: a path-tracking controller that picks the path point at a look-ahead distance L and commands the arc through it, curvature κ = 2y / L², where y is the point's sideways offset in the robot frame. D3
Progress checker and recovery
Simple words: noticing that the robot is stuck, and trying something to get unstuck. Analogy: going round the same block twice, then walking back and asking. Precise: a progress checker fails the controller when the robot has not moved a minimum distance within a timeout; recovery behaviours (clear costmaps, back up, spin, wait) are then tried, a bounded number of times, before the goal is aborted. D2
Localisation in a known map
Simple words: working out where the robot is on a map that already exists. Analogy: recognising the street you are on. Precise: estimating the pose in the map frame from odometry and scans, for example with a particle filter (Monte Carlo localisation), and publishing the correction as the map-to-odometry transform. D5

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the parts and the data between them. Left: what comes in (the map, odometry, scans, a goal). Middle: localisation and the costmap with its layers. Right: the decision loop: a behaviour tree asks the planner for a path, hands it to the controller, watches progress, and calls recoveries when needed.

Figure 2 is the inflation profile used in the lab: the cost of putting the robot's centre at distance d from the nearest obstacle cell. Inside the robot's radius the cost is lethal; beyond it, the cost decays exponentially and reaches zero at the inflation radius. The planner minimises distance plus cost, so it prefers the middle of a corridor when the detour is small.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

To drive a robot through a house you need several helpers working together. One knows where the robot is. One keeps a map of where it must not go, and adds anything new the robot sees, like a basket on the floor. One chooses a route. One steers along the route many times a second. One watches whether the robot is getting closer, and if not, tells it to back up and think again.

All of them depend on a few numbers that someone typed in, like how big the robot is. If one of those numbers is wrong, the helpers do everything right with the wrong number, and the robot bumps into things.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Localisation first

Navigation needs the robot's pose in the map frame. F9-53 estimated it while building the map; on a finished map, a localiser does the same job without changing the map: it compares scans with the map and corrects odometry drift, publishing the correction as a transform between the map frame and the odometry frame (F9-23). The lab simplifies this step: the simulated robot knows its true pose, so that this chapter isolates planning and control. The forensic lab of F9-53 shows what happens when localisation goes wrong. D5

Costmaps and layers

The mini stack's costmap has three layers (Listing 1, lines 61–113). The static layer is the house map. The obstacle layer marks the end point of each LiDAR beam as an obstacle and clears the cells the beam passed through, so that a basket appears when it is seen and disappears when it is moved. The inflation layer turns every obstacle cell into a lethal disc of the robot's radius plus the decaying cost of Figure 2. The planner of F9-54 runs on the combined grid with step cost = length × (1 + cost). D6

The control loop

Each goal runs this loop at 20 Hz: put down any unmapped box whose time has come (the simulator); every 0.2 s, add a scan to the obstacle layer; every 1 s, replan; if within 0.15 m of the goal, turn to the goal's heading; otherwise run pure pursuit with a 0.5 m look-ahead and slow down near the goal; if the robot has moved less than 0.10 m in 4 s, back up 0.2 m, clear the path and replan; after more than 3 recoveries, abort. The simulated body is a disc of 0.20 m with a bumper that stops it on contact. R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


How Nav2 organises the same parts


Nav2 describes itself as a set of servers, each loading plugins, coordinated by a behaviour-tree navigator: a planner server, a controller server, a behaviour (recovery) server, costmaps with layer plugins, a smoother, and a lifecycle manager that starts them in order. A goal arrives as a ROS 2 action; the behaviour tree (F9-58) decides when to replan, when to call recoveries and when to give up. The mini stack mirrors these parts on purpose, so that each Nav2 parameter you meet has a counterpart you have seen working. D1 D2

Choosing inflation numbers


Three numbers interact. The footprint must be the real robot's size (plus a small margin for localisation error and the controller's tracking error); smaller is never "optimistic", it is wrong. The inflation radius sets how far from walls cost is felt; in a corridor of width w, if the inflation radius exceeds w/2 the robot has no zero-cost cells and will run along the middle, which is usually what you want. The scaling factor sets how strongly the cost pulls away from walls. A door of width 0.9 m with a robot radius of 0.20 m leaves 0.5 m for the centre; with a scaling of 6, the centre of the doorway (0.45 m from each frame) costs 3·exp(−1.5) ≈ 0.67 per metre, a small price, so doors stay usable. D1 R1


Controllers


Pure pursuit is simple and smooth but cuts corners: the look-ahead point is inside the turn, so the robot's track lies inside the path, by more when L is larger. A short L tracks tightly and oscillates. Regulated variants slow down in tight turns and near obstacles. Sampling controllers such as the dynamic window approach simulate many (v, ω) commands a short time ahead, score each against the path, the goal and the costmap, and choose the best; model-predictive controllers optimise a sequence of commands. All of them must respect the base's velocity and acceleration limits, and all of them trust the costmap. D3 D4


Recoveries must be bounded


Recoveries exist for transient trouble: a costmap cluttered by a person who has left, a robot wedged against a corner. They do not fix a wrong configuration. If the same recovery fails in the same place repeatedly, as in the forensic lab, the stack must abort and report, not loop forever. Every abort needs enough context in the log (pose, goal, recovery history) for a person to decide what happened. D2



How the hardware actually does it


On a real robot every number in the configuration is a claim about hardware. The footprint is measured from the chassis, including bumpers and anything mounted on it. Maximum velocities and accelerations come from the motor drivers and from the safety case, not from what the motors can do. The look-ahead and tolerances depend on how well the base tracks commands and how accurate localisation is, which you measure on the robot. The LiDAR's mounting height decides which obstacles it can see at all: a LiDAR above table height does not see the table legs' crossbar or a low basket, and the obstacle layer cannot mark what the sensor cannot see. None of these were measured in this build. D1


Safety
Faculty rule: run every navigation configuration in the simulator first. On a real robot, a hardware emergency stop must be within reach of the person supervising, the test area must be clear of people and fragile objects, the speed limits of the safety case apply (set them lower for first runs), and students below L3 work only under supervision. Treat the bumper as the last line of defence, not as a sensor for navigation.

Worked example


(a) Pure pursuit by hand. Robot at (2.00, 1.00), heading 0 (facing +x), speed limit 0.40 m/s. The look-ahead point on the path is (2.46, 1.20).


    

  1. Offset in the map frame: dx = 0.46, dy = 0.20. With heading 0 the robot frame equals the map frame: xr = 0.46, yr = 0.20.
    2. 

  2. Angle to the point: atan2(0.20, 0.46) = 0.41 rad, less than 1.0 rad, so the robot drives (Listing 1, line 127 would turn on the spot otherwise).
    3. 

  3. L² = 0.46² + 0.20² = 0.2516. Curvature κ = 2 × 0.20 / 0.2516 = 1.59 per metre (a circle of radius 0.63 m).
    4. 

  4. Command: v = 0.40 m/s, ω = v·κ = 0.64 rad/s, below the 1.0 rad/s limit, so not clamped.
    5. 



(b) Inflation cost of a doorway. The door at x = 4 m spans y 1.2–2.1 m (0.9 m). With robot radius 0.20 m, the centre may be at y from 1.4 to 1.9 m. At the middle, y = 1.65 m, the nearest frame cell is about 0.45 m away: cost 3·exp(−6 × 0.25) = 0.67 per metre. At y = 1.45 m, 0.25 m from the lower frame: 3·exp(−0.3) = 2.22 per metre. A path through the middle of the doorway costs (1 + 0.67) per metre of doorway instead of (1 + 2.22), so the planner centres the robot in the door. Cell-centre distances in the grid shift these values slightly; the lab's numbers come from the grid.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Ravi knows how wide his bike is without thinking. A robot knows only the number in its configuration; nothing checks it against the chassis.
    • 

  • Ravi sees a parked van in full. The robot sees only where its LiDAR beams end, at one height; a low basket or a glass door can be invisible.
    • 

  • Ravi's "think again" is creative. A robot's recovery is a fixed list; it cannot fix a wrong number by trying harder.
    • 

  • Ravi's route planning and steering share one brain. In a navigation stack they are separate programs that can disagree, and the costmap is the only common ground.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Footprint smaller than the robot. Symptom: plans through doors look perfect, the robot scrapes frames, recoveries repeat in the same place. Find it: compare the configured radius with the chassis; draw the robot's outline along the plan. Fix: measured footprint plus margin.
    • 

  • Inflation radius not larger than the footprint. Symptom: paths hug walls. Fix: inflation radius well beyond the footprint where space allows.
    • 

  • Obstacles that never clear. Symptom: the robot avoids a place where nothing is any more. Cause: no clearing (no beams through that region, or a sensor that cannot report "no return"). Fix: enable clearing; clear the costmap as a recovery.
    • 

  • Goal never succeeds. Symptom: the robot circles near the goal. Cause: goal tolerances smaller than the controller can achieve. Fix: tolerances from measured tracking error.
    • 

  • Configuration typo kept the default. Symptom: changing a value has no effect. Fix: read back the running configuration; make unknown keys errors (Listing 1, line 56 reports them).
    • 

  • Localisation jump. Symptom: the plan suddenly passes through walls, or the robot is "inside" an obstacle. Cause: the map-to-odometry correction jumped. Find it: plot the pose estimate; see F9-53.
    •

Summary


    

  • A navigation stack is localisation, a layered costmap, a global planner, a controller, goal and progress checkers and bounded recoveries, ordered by a navigator (in Nav2, a behaviour tree).
    • 

  • The costmap combines the static map, sensor obstacles (marked and cleared) and inflation; the planner and controller only know what the costmap says.
    • 

  • In the lab, four goals succeeded and an unmapped basket was handled by a replan at t = 37 s once the LiDAR saw it.
    • 

  • Pure pursuit steers along κ = 2y/L²; inflation numbers decide where in a doorway the robot drives.
    • 

  • A footprint smaller than the robot makes shorter, prettier plans and real contacts; recoveries cannot fix configuration errors.
    • 

  • Every Nav2 name in this chapter must be checked in the documentation of your ROS 2 distribution.
