Story hook

Lina's grandmother asks her to move a small box of tea from the kitchen table onto a low shelf behind the reading lamp. Lina does it without thinking, but a lot happens. She decides where her hand must end up, just above the box with her fingers pointing down. Her shoulder, elbow and wrist find angles that put the hand there. She lowers her hand straight down, so as not to knock the box, closes her fingers, and lifts straight up. Then she carries the box over the lamp, not just her hand: the box hangs below her fingers, and she lifts higher so that it clears the lampshade. She lowers it onto the shelf, opens her fingers, and pulls her hand back up.

A robot arm needs every one of those steps written down. The one people forget is the box: once it is held, it is part of the arm. Forget that, and the plan for the hand is perfect while the box swings into the lamp. That is the forensic lab of this chapter, and MoveIt 2, the usual ROS 2 motion-planning framework, has a name for the fix: an attached collision object.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute forward and inverse kinematics of a planar three-joint arm by hand, and choose between IK solutions using joint limits.
  • Describe the planning scene (robot, fixed objects, attached objects, allowed collisions) and keep it correct through every stage of a pick and place.
  • Build a pick-and-place pipeline from stages: joint-space planning, straight-line (Cartesian) moves, attach and detach, time parameterisation.
  • Map each stage to the MoveIt 2 concept that performs it, knowing which names must still be checked.
  • Diagnose a collision that happens only while an object is carried (forensic lab).
Jargon box
Forward kinematics (FK)
Simple words: from the joint angles, work out where the hand is. Analogy: knowing your shoulder, elbow and wrist angles, you know where your fingertips are. Precise: the map from joint configuration q to the pose of a link frame, built by composing each joint's transform (F9-25); for a planar chain, x = Σ Li cos(q1 + … + qi), z likewise with sin. D2
Inverse kinematics (IK)
Simple words: from where the hand must be, work out the joint angles. Analogy: deciding how to bend your arm to touch a spot. Precise: solving FK(q) = target for q; it may have no solution (out of reach), several (elbow up or down), or infinitely many (redundant arms); solvers are analytic for simple arms and numerical in general. D2
Planning scene
Simple words: the planner's picture of the world: the robot, the furniture, and the things it holds. Analogy: what Lina remembers about the kitchen while her eyes are on the box. Precise: the robot's state and collision geometry, the world's collision objects, objects attached to robot links, and the rules about which pairs may touch; every plan is checked against it. D1
Attached collision object
Simple words: an object the planner treats as part of the robot because the robot is holding it. Analogy: the box in Lina's hand that must also clear the lamp. Precise: a collision object fixed to a robot link; it moves with the link, is checked against the world, and may be allowed to touch the gripper links. D1
Allowed collision matrix (ACM)
Simple words: the list of pairs of things that are allowed to touch. Analogy: fingers are allowed to touch the box they are grasping. Precise: a table of (body, body) pairs whose contacts are ignored by collision checking, such as adjacent links, or the gripper and the object during a grasp. D1
Cartesian path
Simple words: a move in which the hand follows a straight line. Analogy: lowering your hand straight down onto the box. Precise: a sequence of tool poses along a straight line (or other curve) in the workspace, converted to joints by IK at small steps; it fails if any step has no IK solution or a joint jump. D1
Pre-grasp pose
Simple words: a pose just above or in front of the object, from which the hand can move straight in. Analogy: Lina's hand hovering above the box. Precise: a tool pose at a fixed offset from the grasp pose along the approach direction; the free-space planner goes there, a Cartesian move does the last part. D2
Time parameterisation
Simple words: deciding how fast to move along a planned path. Analogy: speeding up gently and slowing down before the shelf. Precise: assigning times to the waypoints of a path so that every joint's velocity and acceleration stay within limits; the path's shape is unchanged. D3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

To move something with a robot arm, you first decide where the hand must go. Then you work out how to bend each joint so the hand gets there. You move the arm without bumping into the table, the shelf or the lamp. You lower the hand straight down, grab, and lift straight up.

Then comes the part people forget. Now the arm is holding a box, so the arm plus box is bigger than before. The robot must plan for the bigger shape, or the box will hit something the hand would have missed.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The arm and its kinematics

The lab's arm is planar: three revolute joints with parallel axes, links of 0.50, 0.40 and 0.15 m, the first joint at (0, 0.40) above the table. Joint angles are relative: q1 from the horizontal, q2 relative to link 1, q3 relative to link 2. FK sums angles along the chain (Listing 1, lines 41–47). IK for a tool-tip position (x, z) with a tool angle φ is analytic: subtract the tool link to get the wrist, solve the two-link triangle with the law of cosines for q2 (two signs: elbow up or down), then q1 from two atan2 terms, then q3 = φ − q1 − q2. Joint limits remove one of the two solutions at every target in this lab. D2 R2

The planning scene

The planner checks every configuration against a scene (Listing 1, lines 75–152): the links as capsules of radius 0.03 m; the table, pedestal, shelf and lamp as boxes; the object either resting (a box) or held (a capsule along the tool axis); and one allowed contact, tool and object, during the grasp. The first link is allowed to start inside the pedestal it stands on, the equivalent of an "adjacent links" entry in an allowed-collision matrix. D1

The task as seven stages

  1. Move to pre-grasp (free space): RRT-Connect in joint space (F9-55), then shortcutting.
  2. Approach: Cartesian move 0.10 m straight down, IK every 1 cm; tool–object contact allowed.
  3. Grasp and lift: attach the object to the tool, then Cartesian 0.10 m up.
  4. Transfer to pre-place: RRT-Connect with the object attached.
  5. Lower: Cartesian 0.10 m down onto the shelf.
  6. Release and retreat: detach (the object is now an obstacle on the shelf), Cartesian 0.10 m up.
  7. Return home: RRT-Connect; tool–object contact no longer allowed.

Each stage's path is then time-parameterised with a trapezoidal velocity profile per segment (joint speed limit 1 rad/s, acceleration 2 rad/s², our model). Finally a simulated execution moves through every stage in steps of 0.01 rad and checks each configuration against the true world, in which the object really travels with the gripper between grasp and release. R1



All seven stages are clear in the world check, and the whole task takes 15.02 s of motion. The transfer stage needed four waypoints: the straight joint-space line from the lift pose to the pre-place pose would have carried the box through the lamp, so the planner went over it. R1



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


IK in general


The lab's arm has three joints for three task coordinates (x, z, φ), so IK has finitely many solutions: two here, one removed by limits. A six-joint arm in 3D usually has up to eight or more for a full pose (6 coordinates); a seven-joint arm has infinitely many, a one-parameter family, which a solver resolves by an extra criterion (closest to the current joints, far from limits). Numerical solvers (Jacobian pseudo-inverse or damped least squares, F9-26 and F9-27) work for any arm but return one solution near their seed, may fail near singularities and may not find a solution that exists. In practice a pick pipeline asks IK for several candidate grasps and keeps those whose solutions are collision-free and reachable. D2


Why Cartesian moves for the last centimetres


Free-space planning in joint space gives a collision-free path but no promise about the tool's route in between: a sampled path might sweep the gripper sideways into the object. The approach and the lift must be straight lines in the workspace, so they are solved as Cartesian paths, IK at small steps, rejected if any step fails or a joint jumps (a sign of switching IK branch). Here the lowering onto the shelf moves joints 0.517 rad for 0.10 m of tool motion, more than twice the approach (0.223 rad): at the pre-place pose the elbow is sharply bent (q2 = −144.4°) and the wrist is only 0.29 m from joint 1, close to the inner edge of the reachable region; near any edge of its workspace an arm needs larger joint motion for the same tool motion, a first sign of approaching a singularity. R1 D2


Time parameterisation


The lab stops at every waypoint and gives each segment a trapezoidal profile on the joint that moves furthest, scaling the others: simple, safe and slow. Each segment takes d/vmax + vmax/amax when d ≥ vmax²/amax = 0.5 rad, else 2√(d/amax). That is why the 11-waypoint Cartesian stages, with only 0.22 rad of motion, take longer (1.89 s) than the move to pre-grasp (1.54 s for 1.04 rad): ten stops. Real pipelines time-parameterise the whole path without stopping, with time-optimal algorithms under velocity and acceleration (and sometimes jerk) limits, and apply velocity scaling factors for safety. D3 R2


How MoveIt 2 organises the same steps


MoveIt 2 describes a central node that holds the planning scene and robot model, plugins for kinematics (IK), planning (by default sampling-based planners from OMPL) and collision checking, request adapters that, among other things, time-parameterise plans, and an execution layer that sends trajectories to the arm's controllers. The robot's geometry comes from its URDF; a semantic description adds planning groups, named poses such as "home", end-effectors and the default allowed collisions. Objects are added to the scene, attached to links on grasp and detached on release, exactly like the flags in Listing 2. D1 D4

How the hardware actually does it


A real arm adds four things the simulation ignores. Its joint controllers track the trajectory with some error, so plans need clearance beyond the collision model's padding. Its gripper does not grasp perfectly: the object may sit in the fingers at an offset, which changes the attached object's pose; good pipelines measure or at least bound it. Its sensors see the scene imperfectly, so objects added from perception carry pose errors. And its limits (speed, acceleration, torque, and the safety limits for working near people) come from the manufacturer's documentation and the safety case, not from the planner's defaults. None of these were measured in this build. D1


Safety
Faculty rule: every manipulation task runs in the simulator first. On a real arm: a hardware emergency stop within reach of the supervisor; the arm's whole reach envelope (plus the length of anything it carries) kept clear of people, with a marked zone; reduced speed scaling for the first runs of any new plan or scene; never reach into the envelope while the arm is powered and enabled; students below L3 only under supervision. Collision checking protects the robot from the scene model, not people from the robot.

Worked example


Pre-grasp: tool tip at (0.62, 0.18), tool pointing down (φ = −90°). Lengths L1 = 0.50, L2 = 0.40, L3 = 0.15; joint 1 at height 0.40.


    

  1. Wrist, relative to joint 1: (0.62 − 0.15 cos(−90°), 0.18 − 0.40 − 0.15 sin(−90°)) = (0.62, −0.07). Distance √(0.3844 + 0.0049) = 0.6239 m, less than L1 + L2 = 0.90: reachable.
    2. 

  2. Law of cosines: cos q2 = (0.6239² − 0.25 − 0.16) / (2 × 0.50 × 0.40) = (0.3893 − 0.41) / 0.40 = −0.0517. So q2 = ±92.97°.
    3. 

  3. Elbow up (q2 = −92.97°): q1 = atan2(−0.07, 0.62) − atan2(0.40 sin q2, 0.50 + 0.40 cos q2) = −6.44° − atan2(−0.3995, 0.4793) = −6.44° + 39.81° = 33.37°.
    4. 

  4. q3 = φ − q1 − q2 = −90 − 33.37 + 92.97 = −30.40°. All three inside the limits (q1 ≥ −0.2 rad = −11.5°).
    5. 

  5. Elbow down (q2 = +92.97°) gives q1 = −6.44° − 39.81° = −46.25°, below the limit of −11.5°: rejected.
    6. 

  6. Time from home (90°, −90°, −90°): joint moves 0.988, 0.052 and 1.040 rad; the largest, 1.040 rad, exceeds 0.5 rad, so it reaches 1 rad/s: 1.040/1 + 1/2 = 1.540 s. A 0.3 rad move never reaches full speed: 2√(0.3/2) = 0.775 s.
    7.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lina feels the box's weight and sees where it is. A planner knows only what the scene says; a forgotten box is invisible to it.
    • 

  • Lina's arm has more joints than needed and she chooses among infinitely many postures without thinking. The lab's arm has exactly two IK solutions for each target, and a seven-joint robot needs an explicit rule to choose.
    • 

  • Lina adjusts continuously while moving. A planned trajectory is fixed before it starts; changes need a new plan, or a separate reactive layer.
    • 

  • Lina would never move a cup at full speed. A robot does whatever speed its time parameterisation and scaling factors allow, so those numbers are a safety decision.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Object not attached after grasp. Symptom: the carried object hits things the gripper clears. Find it: print the scene before planning each stage. Fix: attach on grasp, detach on release, with the right link and touch links.
    • 

  • Grasp "in collision" with the object. Symptom: approach planning fails at the last centimetres. Cause: gripper–object contact not allowed. Fix: allow exactly that pair, exactly for the grasp stages.
    • 

  • IK fails for a reachable pose. Symptom: no solution near the edge of the workspace or for a pose with a different tool angle. Cause: joint limits, the wrong branch, or a numerical solver's seed. Fix: try both branches or several seeds; check the target with FK of a known configuration.
    • 

  • Cartesian path stops part-way. Symptom: the approach covers only part of the distance. Cause: IK fails or jumps branch on the way. Fix: check the fraction achieved and never execute a partial approach without deciding to.
    • 

  • Moves faster than expected. Symptom: jerky, fast motion in the simulator. Cause: scaling factors of 1 or missing time parameterisation. Fix: explicit, low scaling for first runs.
    • 

  • Scene stale after the object was moved. Symptom: plans avoid a ghost object. Fix: update or remove objects when perception or the task changes them.
    •

Summary


    

  • FK maps joints to the tool; IK maps a tool pose to joints and may have none, several or infinitely many solutions; limits choose among them.
    • 

  • The planning scene holds the robot, fixed objects, attached objects and allowed contacts; every plan is only as correct as the scene.
    • 

  • Pick and place is a sequence: free-space plan to pre-grasp, Cartesian approach, attach, lift, plan with the object, lower, detach, retreat, plan home.
    • 

  • Time parameterisation sets speeds under joint limits; in the lab the task took 15.02 s.
    • 

  • Forgetting to attach the object makes perfect plans for the arm that drive the carried box into the lamp.
    • 

  • MoveIt 2 organises the same steps around a planning scene; check its names in its documentation.
