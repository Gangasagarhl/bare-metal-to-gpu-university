Story hook

Ravi wants to ride his bicycle from school to his grandmother's house on the other side of town. He has a paper map. His little sister suggests trying every street in every direction, a little further each time, until one of them reaches Grandma: that will find the shortest way, she says, but it will take all afternoon of looking at streets that go the wrong way.

Ravi does something smarter. For each corner he could ride to next, he adds two numbers: how far he has already ridden to get there, and how far it is from there to Grandma as the crow flies. He always looks next at the corner with the smallest total. Streets that head away from Grandma get a large total and wait; streets towards her get looked at first. He still finds the shortest way, because the crow-flies distance never overestimates the real ride. He just looks at far fewer streets.

That is A*, and it is how a robot that already has the map of F9-52 decides which way to drive through a house.

What you will be able to do

  • Execute A* by hand on a small grid and explain each expansion with g, h and f.
  • Prove why an admissible, consistent heuristic gives an optimal path, and predict what happens when it is not.
  • Compare Dijkstra, A* and weighted A* by cost and number of expansions on the house map.
  • Diagnose a planner that returns longer paths than necessary from its debug trace (forensic lab).
Jargon box
Search graph
Simple words: a list of places and the moves between them, each move with a cost. Analogy: street corners and the streets joining them on Ravi's map. Precise: a graph G = (V, E) with non-negative edge costs; on an occupancy grid, V is the set of free cells and E joins each cell to its 4 or 8 neighbours with cost equal to the step length (times any extra cost of the cell). D1
Configuration space (C-space)
Simple words: the set of all positions the robot could be in, with the ones that would make it touch something removed. Analogy: on the map, shading the pavement too, because a bicycle is wider than a line. Precise: the space of all configurations of the robot; Cfree is the subset where the robot does not intersect obstacles. For a disc robot of radius r translating in the plane, Cfree is the plane minus the obstacles grown by r. D1
Inflation
Simple words: making obstacles fatter on the map so you can plan for the robot's centre as a point. Analogy: shading a bike's width along every wall. Precise: marking every cell within distance r of an occupied cell as blocked (and optionally adding a cost that decreases with distance beyond r, F9-56). D1
Dijkstra's algorithm
Simple words: exploring outwards from the start, always continuing from the nearest place not yet finished. Analogy: Ravi's sister's plan: every street, a little further each time. Precise: a best-first search ordered by g(n), the cost of the best known path from the start to n; with non-negative costs it finds optimal paths to every node it finishes. D3
A* search
Simple words: Dijkstra that also looks at how far each place still is from the goal. Analogy: Ravi's "ridden so far plus crow-flies distance". Precise: best-first search ordered by f(n) = g(n) + h(n), where h estimates the remaining cost; with an admissible heuristic it returns an optimal path, and with a consistent heuristic it never needs to reopen a node. D2
Admissible heuristic
Simple words: a guess of the remaining distance that is never too big. Analogy: the crow-flies distance is never longer than the ride. Precise: h(n) ≤ h*(n) for every node, where h* is the true remaining cost. D2
Consistent (monotone) heuristic
Simple words: a guess that never drops by more than the cost of a step. Analogy: riding one block never brings you closer to Grandma, as the crow flies, by more than one block. Precise: h(n) ≤ c(n, n′) + h(n′) for every edge, and h(goal) = 0; consistency implies admissibility, and f never decreases along a path. D2
Weighted A*
Simple words: A* that trusts its guess more than it should, to finish faster. Analogy: Ravi doubling the crow-flies number to hurry. Precise: best-first search on f = g + w·h with w > 1; with an admissible h, the path cost is at most w times the optimum. D4
Open list and closed set
Simple words: the places you know how to reach but have not finished, and the places you have finished. Analogy: corners pencilled on the map, and corners crossed out. Precise: a priority queue of frontier nodes ordered by f (or g), and the set of nodes already expanded; a node is expanded when it is removed from the open list. D2

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the worked example of this chapter: a 7 × 5 grid with a wall, start S and goal G, 4-connected moves of cost 1 and the Manhattan distance as heuristic. The numbers show the order in which A* expanded the cells. The cells behind the wall in the wrong direction are expanded because they tie on f = 8; the cells in the far left corners and above the wall are never expanded.

Figure 2 shows why the planner works on an inflated map. A disc-shaped robot touches nothing exactly when its centre is at least r away from every obstacle. Growing every obstacle by r turns "a disc among walls" into "a point among fatter walls", and the point is what graph search plans for.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

To find the shortest way through a house, imagine the floor as squares. From each square you may step to its neighbours. You want the cheapest chain of steps from start to goal.

You could try everything, nearest first. That works but is slow. A* is quicker: for each square it adds the distance walked so far and a fair guess of the distance still to go. It always continues from the square with the smallest sum. If the guess is never too big, A* still finds the very shortest way.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From the map to a graph

Start from the occupancy grid of F9-52 (in this lab, the true floor plan, so that planning errors cannot be confused with mapping errors). The robot is modelled as a disc of radius 0.20 m. Every cell whose centre lies within 0.20 m of an occupied cell's centre becomes blocked: the inflated grid. Each remaining free cell is a node; it is joined to its 8 neighbours. A straight step costs 0.1 m, a diagonal step 0.1·√2 m. A diagonal step is only allowed when both cells beside it are free, so that the robot never cuts the corner of an obstacle. Unknown cells must be decided too: a cautious robot treats them as blocked, an exploring robot as free with a cost. D1 R1

Dijkstra, then A*

Dijkstra's algorithm keeps an open list ordered by g, the cost of the best path found so far from the start. It repeatedly removes the node with the smallest g, marks it closed, and relaxes its edges: if reaching a neighbour through it is cheaper than any path known, it records the new cost and parent and puts the neighbour on the open list. When the goal is removed, its g is optimal, and following the parents back gives the path. D3

A* changes one thing: the open list is ordered by f = g + h. On an 8-connected grid with costs as above, the octile distance (diagonal steps for the shorter axis difference, straight steps for the rest) is exactly the shortest path length on an empty grid, so it never overestimates on a grid with walls: it is admissible. It is also consistent. Extra cell costs (F9-56 adds them near walls) only make true paths longer, so the heuristic stays admissible. D2

What the lab measured

On the house, from the living room (1.0, 1.9) to the hall (6.8, 4.8), Dijkstra and A* return the same cost, 7.177 m, but Dijkstra expands 2,457 cells and A* 440. Weighted A* with w = 2 expands 147 cells and returns 8.254 m, 15 % longer; with w = 5, 112 cells and 8.840 m. On the second query (bedroom to the far kitchen corner), w = 2 happens to find the optimal 8.715 m and w = 5 is only 1.9 % longer: weighted A* is not always worse, but it carries no guarantee beyond the factor w. Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


Why A* with an admissible heuristic is optimal


Suppose A* removes the goal from the open list with cost g(goal) = C, but an optimal path of cost C* < C exists. Some node n on that optimal path is on the open list at that moment (the start was, and every node of the path before n was closed with its optimal g). For that n, g(n) is optimal, so f(n) = g*(n) + h(n) ≤ g*(n) + h*(n) = C* < C = f(goal). A* would have removed n before the goal: a contradiction. Admissibility is the only property used. D2


Consistency adds efficiency. If h(n) ≤ c(n, n′) + h(n′), then f is non-decreasing along any path, the first time a node is removed its g is already optimal, and the closed set never needs to be reopened, which is what Listing 1 assumes (a removed node is never put back). With an admissible but inconsistent heuristic, this simple implementation can return a suboptimal path; a correct one must allow reopening. D2


Better heuristics expand fewer nodes


Among admissible heuristics, a larger one (closer to h*) never expands more nodes, apart from ties. h = 0 gives Dijkstra; the Euclidean distance is admissible on an 8-connected grid but smaller than octile; octile is exact on an empty grid. Ties matter too: on grids, many nodes share the same f, and breaking ties towards larger g (nodes closer to the goal), as the worked example does, expands far fewer nodes in open areas. D2 R2


Weighted A*: the bound and the price


With f = g + w·h and admissible h, the returned cost is at most w·C*. The proof follows the same pattern as above with f(n) ≤ g*(n) + w·h*(n) ≤ w·C*. The lab's numbers respect the bound by a wide margin (8.254 ≤ 2 × 7.177 = 14.354). When the heuristic is badly scaled, for example computed in different units from the step costs, the planner silently becomes weighted A* with a huge w, which is exactly the forensic lab of this chapter. D4 R1


Complexity and memory


With a binary heap, A* on a graph with V nodes and E edges costs O((V + E) log V) in the worst case. On the house grid V ≤ 4,800 and E ≤ 8V, trivial. On a 100 m × 100 m building at 0.05 m resolution, V is four million: memory for g, parents and the closed flag (here 13 bytes per cell) is about 50 MB, and the search becomes noticeable. Listing 1 uses lazy deletion (it pushes a node again when its cost improves and skips stale entries when popped), which is simpler than a decrease-key heap and is the usual choice. D3


Beyond grids


A grid path is a chain of cell centres with 45° turns; a differential-drive robot can follow it, but a car-like robot cannot turn on the spot. State-lattice and hybrid A* planners search over (x, y, θ) with motion primitives the vehicle can actually drive. Any-angle planners allow straight segments between any visible cells, giving shorter, smoother paths. Incremental planners such as D* Lite reuse the previous search when the map changes, which matters when a robot replans many times a second. And when the configuration space has many dimensions (an arm), grids become impossible: F9-55. D1 D5

How the hardware actually does it


Planning is pure computation, but three hardware facts shape it. First, the robot's real footprint: a disc is the easy case; a rectangular robot needs either a circumscribed disc (safe but too wide for doors) or a footprint check that depends on heading, which turns 2D planning into 3D (x, y, θ). Second, the computer: the planner shares a CPU with SLAM, perception and control (F9-49); a search that occasionally takes 300 ms is acceptable for a global planner that runs once a second, not for a 20 Hz controller. Measure your planner's worst case on the largest map you will use, not its average. Third, the map's resolution must be finer than the narrowest passage minus twice the radius: in the house, a 0.9 m door minus 0.4 m leaves 0.5 m, five cells; at 0.25 m resolution the same door would be one or two cells, and rounding could close it. D1 R1

Grid of Figure 1: columns x = 0 to 6, rows y = 0 (top) to 4. Wall at x = 3, y = 1 to 3. S = (0, 2), G = (6, 2). 4-connected, step cost 1, h = |x − 6| + |y − 2|. Ties on f are broken by larger g, then by insertion order.


    

  1. Expand S: g = 0, h = 6, f = 6. Neighbours (1,2) f = 1 + 5 = 6, (0,3) f = 1 + 7 = 8, (0,1) f = 8.
    2. 

  2. Expand (1,2): g = 1, h = 5, f = 6. Adds (2,2) f = 6, (1,3) and (1,1) f = 8.
    3. 

  3. Expand (2,2): f = 6. Its right neighbour (3,2) is wall. Adds (2,3) and (2,1) with g = 3, h = 5, f = 8. No node with f = 6 is left: the wall has made the heuristic's promise impossible.
    4. 

  4. Steps 4–9 expand the f = 8 nodes, larger g first: (2,3), (2,1) (g = 3), then (1,3), (1,1) (g = 2), then (0,3), (0,1) (g = 1). (2,4) appears with g = 4, h = 6, f = 10.
    5. 

  5. Steps 10–16 follow the bottom row: every node there has f = 10, and larger g wins each tie, so the search runs straight to the goal: (2,4), (3,4), (4,4), (5,4), (6,4), (6,3), (6,2).
    6. 

  6. Result: 10 steps, 16 expansions out of 32 free cells. Dijkstra (h = 0) would expand every cell with g ≤ 10, which here is all 32.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Ravi's crow-flies distance is a heuristic he can trust because roads are never shorter than a straight line. On a robot, the heuristic is code: it can be in the wrong units, scaled wrongly, or ignore a cost that the edges include. Nothing guarantees admissibility except checking it.
    • 

  • Ravi's map has streets; a robot's grid has cells, and the "streets" (8 neighbours) allow only 45° directions. The shortest grid path is not the shortest path in the house: RRT* in F9-55 finds 7.088–7.154 m where the grid optimum is 7.177 m.
    • 

  • Ravi rides a bicycle of fixed width on roads made for it. A robot must plan in configuration space; a door that looks open on the map may be closed to the robot's centre once the walls are inflated.
    • 

  • Shortest is not best. Ravi might prefer a quiet street; a robot prefers a path away from walls and people. Optimality is always relative to the cost you wrote down.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Paths longer than they should be. Symptom: cost above a Dijkstra reference; very few expansions. Cause: inadmissible heuristic (units, scale, a heuristic in metres with costs in cells). Find it: print h(start) next to the final cost; h(start) must not exceed it. Fix: same units; unit test h ≤ Dijkstra cost on random queries.
    • 

  • Paths through walls at corners. Symptom: diagonal steps between two blocked cells. Cause: no corner-cutting check. Fix: lines 96–99 of Listing 1.
    • 

  • No path through a door that is clearly open. Symptom: "no path" between rooms. Cause: inflation radius larger than half the door width minus a cell, or the map's resolution too coarse. Find it: draw the inflated grid around the door.
    • 

  • Planner never terminates or is very slow. Symptom: CPU at 100 %. Cause: closed set not used, or nodes re-pushed forever because a negative cost slipped in. Fix: assert costs ≥ 0; count expansions and abort above V.
    • 

  • Start inside an obstacle. Symptom: no path although the robot is clearly in a room. Cause: the robot is within the inflated band (after a bump or a pose jump). Fix: plan from the nearest free cell, or clear a small region around the start, and log it.

Summary


    

  • A grid map becomes a graph of free cells with 8 neighbours; inflating obstacles by the robot's radius turns a disc robot into a point.
    • 

  • Dijkstra orders by g; A* by g + h. With an admissible heuristic A* is optimal; with a consistent one it never reopens nodes.
    • 

  • On the house, A* found the same 7.177 m path as Dijkstra with 440 instead of 2,457 expansions.
    • 

  • Weighted A* trades a bounded loss (at most w times the optimum) for fewer expansions.
    • 

  • A heuristic in the wrong units makes the planner a heavily weighted A*: fast, and up to 33 % longer in the forensic lab.
    • 

  • Shortest grid paths hug obstacles and turn in 45° steps; navigation adds costs and smoothing.
