Story hook

The power is out again, but this time Lina's uncle has gone to bed. Lina still has the torch, the squared paper and the pencil. Now nobody tells her where she stands. She counts her steps and guesses: "four steps forward, a quarter turn left". Her steps are not all the same length, and her quarter turns are a little more than a quarter. After ten minutes her drawing of the hallway bends gently to the left, and the kitchen on her page overlaps the living room.

Then she notices something. When the torch lights the edge of the sofa she drew five minutes ago, she can correct herself: "the sofa is there on my page, and I see it here, so I must be standing here, not where my step count says". Each time she lines up what she sees with what she has drawn, her position gets better, and so does the next part of the drawing.

Best of all is coming back to the front door, which she drew at the very start. Her step count says the door is a metre to the right. The torch says it is right in front of her. Now she knows how much her whole walk drifted, and she can bend the entire drawing back into shape. Drawing the map and knowing where you are, together, in the dark: that is SLAM.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the SLAM problem, why it is harder than mapping with known poses or localisation with a known map, and the difference between online and full SLAM.
  • Implement a scan-to-map front end: predict with odometry, correct with a correlative scan matcher, and measure its drift against ground truth.
  • Build a pose graph with odometry edges and loop closures, optimise it with Gauss–Newton, and explain why one false loop closure is catastrophic.
  • Compare the main families of SLAM (EKF, particle filter, graph-based) and their trade-offs.
  • Diagnose a smeared map caused by sensor time stamps that disagree with odometry, and measure the offset from the data (course forensic "The robot that is lost").
Jargon box
SLAM (simultaneous localisation and mapping)
Simple words: building a map of an unknown place while working out where you are in it, using that same map. Analogy: drawing the map of a dark house while walking through it with a torch. Precise: estimating the joint posterior over the robot's poses (the last one in online SLAM, all of them in full SLAM) and the map, given controls or odometry and measurements, starting from an unknown map. D1 D2
Dead reckoning
Simple words: working out where you are only by adding up your own movements. Analogy: counting steps and turns in the dark. Precise: integrating odometry (wheel encoders, inertial data) from a known start; the error grows without bound because every step's error is added and none is ever removed. D1
Scan matching
Simple words: sliding and turning a new laser scan until it fits what you have already seen. Analogy: lining up the sofa you see with the sofa on your page. Precise: estimating the relative pose between a scan and a reference (another scan or a map) by optimising an alignment score; correlative matching evaluates the score on a grid of candidate poses. D4
Front end and back end
Simple words: the part that turns sensor data into "I moved this much" and "I have been here before", and the part that combines all of those into the best overall guess. Analogy: Lina's eyes and step counting, and Lina redrawing the whole page. Precise: in graph-based SLAM, the front end produces constraints (odometry, scan-matching and loop-closure edges) from raw data; the back end solves the resulting non-linear least-squares problem over all poses. D3
Pose graph
Simple words: a list of places the robot has been, with notes saying how far apart pairs of them are. Analogy: the footprints on Lina's page, joined by "four steps" notes. Precise: a graph whose nodes are robot poses and whose edges are relative-pose measurements with information (inverse covariance) matrices; SLAM finds the node poses that minimise the sum of squared, information-weighted edge errors. D3 D5
Loop closure
Simple words: noticing that you are back at a place you have already been. Analogy: recognising the front door at the end of the walk. Precise: a constraint between two poses that are far apart in time but close in space, obtained by place recognition and verified by matching; it lets the back end remove accumulated drift along the whole loop. D2 D3
Data association
Simple words: deciding which thing you see now is which thing you saw before. Analogy: is this the sofa from the living room, or the bed from the bedroom? Precise: the correspondence problem between current observations and map elements or past poses; wrong associations (false loop closures) corrupt the estimate. D1
Time stamp (sensor time)
Simple words: the time written on a measurement saying when it was taken. Analogy: the time Lina wrote next to each torch look. Precise: the acquisition time carried in a message header; any algorithm that combines two sensors looks up the other sensor's state at this time, so a stamp that is late or early by Δt makes the fusion use the wrong pose. D6

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows a pose graph. The circles are poses where the robot took scans. Solid edges join consecutive poses through odometry (or scan matching); they are short and many, and their small errors add up. The dashed edge is a loop closure: a measurement between the last pose and the first, taken because the robot recognised the start. The back end bends the whole chain to satisfy all edges at once.

Figure 2 is the timing picture behind the forensic lab. Odometry arrives every 20 ms and is stamped at measurement time. A scan is measured at time t, but arrives later; if the driver stamps it on arrival, every lookup of "the pose when this scan was taken" returns a pose from the future. While the robot drives straight that hardly matters; while it turns on the spot, the whole scan is rotated.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot in a new house has the same problem as Lina without her uncle. To draw the map, it must know where it is. To know where it is, it needs the map. It solves both at once, a little at a time.

It guesses its movement from its wheels, then checks the guess by comparing what the laser sees with what it has drawn. When it comes back to a place it already knows, it fixes the whole drawing, not only the last step. The one thing it must never do is mistake one place for another, because then it bends the whole drawing the wrong way.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The problem, and why it is hard

In F9-52 the poses were given and the map was estimated. In localisation (F9-34, F9-36) the map is given and the pose is estimated. SLAM estimates both. The difficulty is that the errors are coupled: a heading error at one moment rotates every part of the map drawn afterwards, and those wrongly drawn parts then mislead the next pose estimate. Online SLAM estimates the current pose and the map; full SLAM estimates the whole trajectory and the map. Graph-based methods solve full SLAM; filters solve online SLAM. D1 D2

Dead reckoning drifts

The lab's simulated wheels have a 2 % distance error (a wrong wheel-diameter parameter), a turn bias of 0.02 rad per metre (unequal wheels) and small random noise. These are this simulator's settings, chosen to make drift visible. Driving the 25.6 m route of the house, the odometry-only pose is 0.095 m off after 3 m, 0.619 m after 15.8 m and 1.464 m at the end; the RMS error over all scans is 0.634 m. The map drawn at those poses has 282 occupied cells far from any real wall and 401 wall cells marked free: it is unusable. R1

Correcting with scan matching

The lab's second run predicts each pose with odometry, then searches a window around the prediction (±0.15 m, ±0.08 rad, then a finer search around the best candidate) for the pose at which the scan's end points land on the most strongly occupied cells of the map built so far. This is correlative scan-to-map matching, a simple form of the front end of many 2D LiDAR SLAM systems. The RMS error drops to 0.071 m with a worst case of 0.086 m, and the map has no wrong cell far from a wall. R1 D4



Look at the error column of the scan-matching run: it is not zero, but it does not grow. It settles near 0.07 m early and stays there. Matching against its own map keeps the robot consistent with the map, and the map consistent with itself; if the first scans placed the map 7 cm off, everything stays 7 cm off. Local consistency is not global accuracy, and a long corridor or a large loop will still drift slowly. That residual drift is what loop closure removes. R1


Loop closure and the back end


The second program builds a pose graph from the same odometry: 97 nodes, 96 odometry edges, and loop-closure edges wherever the route passes within 0.3 m of a place it visited at least 15 nodes earlier. The route through the house revisits doors and corridors, so there are 62 such closures. In the lab they are taken from the simulator's true poses plus noise, standing in for a matcher that recognises the place; in a real system they must be found, and that is the hard part. Gauss–Newton reduces the weighted squared error from 151,988 to 46.9 in two steps (further steps change nothing), and the RMS position error from 0.634 m to 0.038 m. R2 D3




The last lines are the most important of the chapter. One false closure, claiming that a pose in the kitchen (5.91, 2.57) is the same place as a pose near the living-room door (4.20, 1.65), is enough to raise the RMS error from 0.038 m to 2.775 m and tear the trajectory by up to 6 m. Least squares trusts every edge according to its stated information; a confident wrong edge wins. Real back ends therefore verify candidates geometrically before accepting them, and many use robust cost functions that reduce the weight of edges with large errors. R2 D3



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


Three families of SLAM


EKF SLAM puts the robot pose and the positions of all landmarks in one state vector and runs the extended Kalman filter of F9-34. It is exact for linear-Gaussian problems and gives a full covariance, but the covariance matrix is quadratic in the number of landmarks and every update touches all of it; it needs landmarks (features) and good data association, and linearisation errors make it over-confident. D1


Particle-filter SLAM (the Rao–Blackwellised family) samples trajectories with particles, as in F9-36, and gives every particle its own map, which is easy because, given a trajectory, the map is the known-poses problem of F9-52. It handles non-Gaussian pose uncertainty and implicit data association; its weakness is particle depletion: after a long loop, the particle that would close it correctly may have been discarded. Grid-based variants with occupancy maps per particle were the standard 2D LiDAR approach for years. D1


Graph-based SLAM separates a front end that produces constraints from a back end that solves non-linear least squares over all poses. The structure of the problem is sparse (each edge touches two nodes), so sparse solvers handle tens of thousands of poses; the map is rebuilt from the optimised poses. Modern 2D and 3D LiDAR and visual SLAM systems are mostly of this kind, with scan matching against local submaps in the front end and loop closures between submaps. D3 D7


Gauss–Newton on a pose graph


Each edge e between nodes i and j carries a measured relative pose zij and an information matrix Ωij. Its error is eij(x) = (xj ⊖ xi) − zij, the predicted relative pose minus the measured one, with the angle wrapped. The back end minimises F(x) = Σ eijT Ωij eij. Linearising each error around the current guess, e(x + Δx) ≈ e + J Δx, gives the normal equations H Δx = −b with H = Σ JT Ω J and b = Σ JT Ω e. The lab builds H densely with numerical derivatives (fine for 97 nodes), holds node 0 fixed by adding a large number to its diagonal (the gauge: without it, the whole graph could slide), and solves with a Cholesky factorisation. Real solvers use analytic Jacobians, sparse factorisation and a damping term (Levenberg–Marquardt) for robustness far from the solution. D3 R2


The weights are the uncertainty model. The lab gives odometry edges σ = 0.05 m and 0.02 rad, and loop closures σ = 0.02 m and 0.005 rad. Make loop closures too weak and they barely move the graph; make odometry too weak and noise in the closures bends straight corridors. The final value of F (46.9) is itself a check: for correctly weighted Gaussian errors, its expected value is about the number of error components minus the number of unknowns, here 3 × (96 + 62) − 3 × 96 = 186. A value much larger than that means the weights are too optimistic or an edge is wrong; this one, being smaller, means the stated σ values are pessimistic for this simulated data. D3


What the scan matcher really optimises


The lab's matcher scores a candidate pose by the sum, over beams with a return, of the positive log-odds of the cell under the beam's end point. It is a brute-force search on a pose grid: 9 × 7 × 7 = 441 coarse candidates with every second beam, then 125 fine candidates with every beam. Its resolution limits its accuracy (the fine step is 0.0125 m and 0.005 rad), its window limits the odometry error it can recover from (±0.15 m between scans), and in a long featureless corridor its score is flat along the corridor: the match is unconstrained in that direction. Production front ends smooth the map score so it can be optimised continuously, search multi-resolution grids for speed, and report the shape of the score around the optimum as a covariance. D4 R1


Time is part of the state


Fusion code asks "where was the robot when this scan was measured?". The answer comes from interpolating odometry (or the transform tree of ROS 2) at the scan's time stamp. Any error in that stamp becomes a pose error equal to the robot's velocity times the stamp error. At 0.4 m/s, 0.15 s is 6 cm, below the grid's resolution; at 1 rad/s while turning, 0.15 s is 0.15 rad, which at 3 m range moves an end point by 0.45 m. That is why timing faults show up as maps that are sharp in corridors and smeared in rooms where the robot turned. Spinning LiDARs add a second, related effect: the beams of one scan are measured over a sweep, and drivers or SLAM front ends correct each beam for the motion during the sweep ("de-skewing"). D6 R3

How the hardware actually does it


Every sensor has its own clock or none at all. A LiDAR may stamp scans with its internal clock, which drifts against the computer's clock; a driver may stamp them when the last byte arrives over USB or Ethernet; an IMU on a microcontroller may stamp samples with a counter that the host must convert. Wheel encoders are read by a motor controller and forwarded with their own latency. Getting all of them onto one time base is a hardware and driver problem before it is a SLAM problem: hardware synchronisation (a trigger line or a pulse-per-second signal), network time protocols with hardware time stamping on the Ethernet interface, or, at least, a measured constant latency subtracted in the driver. Without one of these, the forensic lab's method (estimate the offset from the data) is the fallback, and it only works if the offset is constant. D6

Worked example


How much does a 0.15 s stamp error move a wall? Take the forensic lab's turn: the robot turns on the spot at ω = 1.0 rad/s. The pose looked up is 0.15 s late, so the heading used is wrong by Δθ = ω Δt = 1.0 × 0.15 = 0.15 rad = 8.6°. A beam that hits a wall at range r has its end point moved sideways by about r Δθ: 0.15 m at r = 1 m, 0.45 m at r = 3 m, more than four cells of the grid. When the robot drives straight at v = 0.4 m/s, the same error moves the pose by v Δt = 0.06 m along the motion: less than one cell. Prediction: the map is fine along corridors and smeared after turns.


The evidence agrees. The map built from all scans has 21 occupied cells far from any wall; the map built only from scans taken while odometry reported no turn (turn rate below 0.1 rad/s) has 0. R3


Now the loop-closure arithmetic. In the pose graph, the check value of Layer 3 uses 158 edges of 3 components (474 numbers) and 96 free nodes of 3 unknowns (288): 474 − 288 = 186. The optimiser's final 46.9 is about a quarter of that, consistent with real noise smaller than the stated σ (the simulator's odometry steps have σ = 0.002 per 5 cm step, far below the 0.05 m stated for a 0.25 m edge). R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lina recognises the front door instantly and is never fooled by a second, identical door. Robots are: two doorways of the same width, two identical office corridors, look the same to a 2D LiDAR. Place recognition is the weakest link, and the lab's 2.775 m RMS error from one false closure shows what happens when it fails.
    • 

  • Lina can redraw her page with an eraser in a minute. A SLAM back end re-optimising thousands of poses takes real computing time, and the map must be rebuilt from the corrected poses; systems therefore optimise in the background and correct the map in jumps that the navigation must tolerate.
    • 

  • Lina's sense of time is shared by her eyes and legs. A robot's sensors each have their own delays and clocks; if the time written on a scan is wrong, the robot combines "what I saw" with "where I was" at different moments. Nothing in the torch analogy prepares you for that, which is why the forensic lab is about it.
    • 

  • Lina draws one map. Particle-filter SLAM keeps hundreds of hypotheses, each with its own map, and discards the unlikely ones; a person would rarely work that way.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Map doubled walls after a loop. Symptom: two copies of a room, slightly rotated. Cause: no loop closure, or closures rejected because the matcher's window is smaller than the accumulated drift. Find it: plot the trajectory and the point where it should close. Fix: larger search window for loop-closure candidates (not for the incremental matcher), or a better odometry model.
    • 

  • Map torn apart suddenly. Symptom: a good map becomes nonsense after one optimisation. Cause: a false loop closure. Find it: list the edges with the largest weighted error after optimisation; the false one dominates. Fix: geometric verification, robust kernels, a minimum match score.
    • 

  • Smear only after turns. Symptom: crisp corridors, fuzzy rooms. Cause: time offset between scans and odometry, or no de-skewing of a spinning LiDAR. Find it: build maps from straight and turning segments separately, as in the forensic lab. Fix: correct stamps in the driver; measure the offset.
    • 

  • Scan matcher "slides" in a corridor. Symptom: position error grows along a featureless corridor only. Cause: the score is flat in that direction. Fix: trust odometry more along the corridor (use the match covariance), or add features.
    • 

  • Odometry frame and map frame confused. Symptom: the robot jumps by the accumulated correction each time a new pose arrives. Cause: publishing the corrected pose as if it were odometry. Fix: publish the correction as a separate transform between the map frame and the odometry frame, so odometry stays smooth.
    •

Summary


    

  • SLAM estimates poses and the map together; their errors are coupled, which makes it harder than either alone.
    • 

  • Dead reckoning drifts without bound: 1.464 m after 25.6 m with this lab's odometry model.
    • 

  • Scan-to-map matching keeps the robot consistent with its own map (RMS 0.071 m) but cannot remove slow global drift.
    • 

  • A pose graph with loop closures, solved by Gauss–Newton, removes drift along loops (RMS 0.038 m in the lab).
    • 

  • One false loop closure can ruin the whole estimate (RMS 2.775 m): closures must be verified.
    • 

  • EKF, particle-filter and graph-based SLAM trade covariance, robustness and scale differently.
    • 

  • Time stamps are part of correctness: a 0.15 s late scan stamp smears a map after every turn; the offset can be measured from the data by comparing turn rates.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Mapping with known poses: poses given, estimate the map. Localisation: map given, estimate the pose. Online SLAM: estimate the current pose and the map. Full SLAM: estimate the whole trajectory and the map.
    2. 

  2. Dead reckoning adds every step's error and never subtracts any. Scan matching measures the pose against the map each time, so the error between scans is corrected; what remains is the error of the map itself, which was fixed early (about 7 cm) and drifts only slowly.
    3. 

  3. Good answers say: scan matching only relates nearby poses, so errors still accumulate slowly along the trajectory; a loop closure relates poses far apart in time, and the back end spreads the discovered discrepancy over the whole loop, correcting poses (and map parts) that no local match could fix.
    4. 

  4. Least squares minimises the sum of weighted squared errors; an edge stated with high confidence has a large weight, and satisfying it costs less than violating it, even if it bends many weak odometry edges. Defences: geometric verification of candidates (and a high match-score threshold), robust kernels that cap the influence of large errors, consistency checks between several closures, and switchable constraints.
    5. 

  5. (a) A grid-based particle filter or a lightweight graph SLAM with submaps: both are used with 2D LiDAR; the filter is simple, the graph scales better. (b) Graph-based: sparse solvers scale to many poses; filters would need too many particles or too large a covariance. (c) EKF SLAM with ten landmarks is cheap and well-conditioned, and data association with distinctive beacons is easy.
    6. 

  6. Across the corridor the walls constrain the match well; along it the score is flat, so the matcher picks a position near the odometry prediction (or anywhere in its window): drift along the corridor equals odometry drift.
    7. 

  7. Δθ = 0.5 × 0.05 = 0.025 rad; displacement ≈ 4 × 0.025 = 0.10 m, one grid cell.
    8. 

  8. Expect the odometry-only error to shrink a lot (the bias bends the trajectory) and the scan-matching error to change little. Record your numbers; the build ran only the default settings.
    9. 

  9. The error function depends only on relative poses, so moving every pose by the same transform leaves it unchanged: H is singular. Fixing node 0 removes that freedom; without it, Cholesky would fail (a zero or negative pivot) or the solution would wander.

Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. /odom arrives 0.002 s after its stamp; /scan arrives only 0.010 s after its stamp although the documentation says delivery takes 0.16 s after measurement. So the stamp is about 0.15 s after the measurement: the new driver stamps (almost) at reception.
    2. 

  2. A late stamp makes the mapper use the pose 0.15 s later. Driving straight at 0.4 m/s that is 6 cm, less than a cell; turning at 1 rad/s it is 0.15 rad, which moves walls 3 m away by 0.45 m. Straight-only map: 0 wrong cells far from walls; all scans: 21.
    3. 

  3. Odometry wrong while turning: then the straight-only map would also suffer at the corners, and the turn rates from scans would disagree with odometry by a scale, not by a delay. LiDAR angles wrong (a fixed rotation): would rotate every scan, also when driving straight. Late stamps: explains the turn-only smear and the receive − stamp numbers.
    4. 

  4. Measure the rotation between consecutive scans by shifting one range array against the other (with a parabola through the best three shifts for sub-beam precision), divide by the stamp difference to get a LiDAR turn rate, and compare it with the odometry turn rate looked up at each scan's mid-stamp minus an assumed offset. The offset with the smallest mismatch is +0.15 s. R3
