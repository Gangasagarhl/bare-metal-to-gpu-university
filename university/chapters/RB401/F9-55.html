Story hook

Ravi's cousin Mira arrives in a city park she has never seen. There is no map, only bushes, ponds and paths. She wants to reach the ice-cream stand on the far side. Her trick: she picks a random spot she can see, perhaps a tall tree. From all the places she has already ridden to, she picks the one nearest to that tree. She rides a short way from there towards it, unless a bush is in the way. Then she does the same again.

Her trail branches out across the park like the roots of a plant. It reaches into every open area quickly, because the big empty areas attract most random picks. Soon one branch passes near the stand, and she has a route. The route wiggles, so on the way home she cuts the corners she can see are clear. Mira never drew a grid of the park. That is lucky: for a robot arm with six joints, a grid would have more squares than there are grains of sand on a beach.

Mira's trick is the rapidly-exploring random tree, RRT, the family of planners behind most robot-arm motion planning.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why grids fail in high-dimensional configuration spaces and what sampling buys.
  • Implement RRT and RRT* (choose-parent and rewire), and path shortcutting, and trace one extension by hand.
  • Compute whether a configuration of a two-link arm is in collision, and draw its C-space obstacle.
  • Compare RRT, RRT with shortcutting, RRT* and grid A* by path length and effort, over several random seeds.
  • Diagnose paths that pass through obstacles because the edge check is too coarse (forensic lab).
Jargon box
Configuration space (C-space)
Simple words: every way the robot could be placed, as one point per placement. Analogy: for an arm, the list of all angles the elbow and shoulder can take, drawn as a map. Precise: the space of all configurations q; its dimension is the number of degrees of freedom; Cobs is the set of q in which the robot touches an obstacle or itself, Cfree the rest. D1
Sampling-based planner
Simple words: a planner that tries random positions and connects the good ones. Analogy: Mira's random tall trees. Precise: a planner that builds a graph or tree in C-space from randomly sampled configurations, using only a collision checker (is q free? is the segment q₁q₂ free?) and never an explicit model of Cobs. D1
RRT (rapidly-exploring random tree)
Simple words: a tree that grows from the start towards random points. Analogy: Mira's branching trail. Precise: repeat: sample qrand; find the nearest tree node qnear; move from qnear towards qrand by at most a step to qnew; if the segment is free, add qnew with parent qnear. D2
RRT* (optimal RRT)
Simple words: RRT that keeps improving its route. Analogy: Mira, each time she reaches a new spot, checking whether nearby spots could be reached more quickly through it. Precise: RRT plus two steps per new node: choose the parent among nearby nodes that minimises cost-from-start, then rewire nearby nodes through the new node when that lowers their cost; asymptotically optimal under conditions on the neighbourhood size. D4
Probabilistic roadmap (PRM)
Simple words: scatter many random free positions, join neighbours, and reuse that network for many trips. Analogy: marking good spots and safe links in the park once, then using them every day. Precise: a multi-query planner: a learning phase samples free configurations and connects each to nearby ones with free segments; a query connects start and goal to the roadmap and runs graph search. D5
Probabilistic completeness
Simple words: if a way exists, the chance of finding it grows to certainty as you keep trying. Analogy: Mira will eventually find the stand if there is any way. Precise: the probability that the planner returns a solution, when one exists (with some clearance), tends to 1 as the number of samples tends to infinity; it says nothing about how long that takes, and it cannot prove that no path exists. D2
Asymptotic optimality
Simple words: given more and more time, the route gets as short as possible. Analogy: Mira's route home improving each day. Precise: the cost of the best solution found converges (almost surely) to the optimal cost as the number of samples tends to infinity; RRT* has it, plain RRT does not. D4
Collision checking (edge resolution)
Simple words: testing points along a move to see whether any of them hits something; the resolution is the spacing of those points. Analogy: looking for bushes every few metres along a path. Precise: a segment is declared free if each of its sampled points is free; it can miss any obstacle thinner than the spacing. Exact (continuous) checks exist for simple shapes. D1
Path shortcutting
Simple words: straightening a wiggly path by joining far-apart points when the straight line is clear. Analogy: Mira cutting corners on the way home. Precise: repeatedly pick two points of the path at random and replace the part between them by the straight segment, when that segment passes the collision check. D1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows one RRT extension and the two extra steps of RRT*. The tree (lines and dots) has grown from the start. A random sample qrand is drawn; the nearest node qnear is found; a new node qnew is placed one step towards the sample. RRT* then looks at all nodes inside a circle around qnew: it connects qnew to the one that gives the shortest route from the start, and redirects neighbours through qnew when that shortens their routes.

Figure 2 shows what "configuration space" means for an arm. On the left, a two-link arm and a box in the workspace. On the right, every pair of joint angles (θ1, θ2) is one point; the shaded band is the set of angle pairs in which some part of the arm is inside the box. A move that looks harmless as "change both joints smoothly from A to B" is a straight line in the right picture, and it crosses the band.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Some robots have many joints. Drawing every possible pose on squared paper would need more squares than anyone could ever count. So instead the robot tries random poses. It grows a "tree" of poses it can reach, always from the nearest pose it already knows, a small step at a time, and never through anything solid.

When a branch reaches the goal, there is a route. It is wiggly, so the robot straightens it wherever the straight line is clear. One rule matters more than any other: when you check whether a straight move is clear, you must look closely enough. A thin thing hidden between two checks will be missed.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why not a grid?

A* on the house grid of F9-54 had 4,800 cells. A grid over the joint angles of a 6-joint arm at 1° resolution has 3606, about 2.2 × 1015 cells: no computer stores that. And the obstacles in C-space have no simple shape (Figure 2): you can only test one configuration at a time, by placing the robot's model there and checking for intersections. Sampling-based planners accept this. They need only two functions: "is q free?" and "is the straight segment from q₁ to q₂ free?". D1

RRT in five lines

Start the tree with qstart. Repeat: draw qrand uniformly in the space (with a small probability, the goal itself: the goal bias); find the nearest tree node; take a step of at most 0.3 m towards qrand; if the new point and the segment are free, add it. Stop when a new node lies within 0.3 m of the goal and can be joined to it. The tree spreads quickly into large empty regions because a node on the edge of a big empty region is the nearest node to many samples (its Voronoi region is large): this is the "rapidly exploring" property. D2

What the lab measured

On the same query as F9-54 (living room to hall, disc robot of radius 0.20 m on the inflated map), over 10 random seeds:

  • RRT solved every run; the median run needed 170 iterations, and the median path was 9.584 m, a third longer than the grid optimum of 7.177 m.
  • RRT followed by 200 shortcutting attempts: median 7.440 m.
  • RRT* with 3,000 iterations: median 7.122 m, range 7.088–7.154 m. That is shorter than A*, because A* on an 8-connected grid can only move in multiples of 45°, while RRT* draws straight lines in any direction.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


What the guarantees say, and do not say


RRT is probabilistically complete: if a path with some positive clearance exists, the probability of failing to find it decays towards zero as samples increase. Three limits follow. It cannot tell you that no path exists (a run that fails might just need more samples). The rate depends on the geometry: narrow passages, like a door only slightly wider than the robot, are hit by few samples, and the expected time can grow sharply as the passage narrows. And plain RRT is not asymptotically optimal: its solution converges to a suboptimal one with probability one, because nodes never change their parents. D2 D4


RRT* and its neighbourhood


RRT* adds choose-parent and rewire inside a neighbourhood of radius r. Karaman and Frazzoli showed that asymptotic optimality holds if r shrinks no faster than a constant times (log n / n)1/d, with n nodes in d dimensions; a larger r costs more collision checks per node. Listing 1 uses a fixed r = 0.8 m, which is at least as large as the schedule requires in this small map, at the cost of more checks; for large maps, use the shrinking schedule (or the k-nearest version). One implementation detail is easy to miss: when a node is rewired, the costs of all its descendants must fall by the same amount (Listing 1, lines 111–117); forgetting this leaves the tree with stale costs and wrong choices later. D4 R1


The family


    

  • RRT-Connect grows two trees, one from the start and one from the goal, and tries greedily to connect them after each extension. For single queries in C-spaces with few narrow passages it is often much faster than RRT, and it is a common default for arm planning. D3
    • 

  • PRM spends effort once, building a roadmap of free samples and free edges, then answers many queries with graph search; it suits a fixed environment, such as a work cell where the arm repeats similar moves. D5
    • 

  • Lazy variants postpone edge checks until an edge is on a candidate path, which matters when collision checks dominate the time, as they usually do.
    • 

  • Informed variants (sampling only in the ellipse that could improve the current solution) and bidirectional optimal variants speed up convergence. D4
    • 



Collision checking is the real planner


Almost all the time of a sampling-based planner is spent checking collisions, and almost all its silent bugs are there. Sampled edge checks with spacing δ can miss any obstacle region thinner than δ along the segment. The rule of thumb is to choose δ smaller than the thinnest obstacle or clearance band that matters, measured in the same units as the C-space, and remember that for an arm, a small joint-angle step can move the far end of a long link a long way. The arm in Listing 3 has a reach of 1.8 m, so a 1° step moves its tip about 3 cm. Exact segment checks (swept volumes, or conservative advancement with distance queries) avoid the problem for shapes that support them. The forensic lab of this chapter is this bug. D1 R3


After planning


A planner's path is a list of configurations, not a motion: it must be shortcut or smoothed, then time-parameterised under velocity and acceleration limits (F9-57), and each modification must be collision-checked again. Randomness also has an operational cost: two runs on the same request give different paths. Log the seed with each plan, so that a strange path can be reproduced.

How the hardware actually does it


Three practical facts. First, the collision model is not the robot: it is meshes or simple shapes (boxes, capsules) with a padding; a planner that is correct for the model can still let the real arm brush a cable that was never modelled. Second, planning time competes with everything else on the robot's computer; budgets for arm planning are usually stated as a timeout per request, after which the request fails and the behaviour layer (F9-58) decides what to do. Third, randomness meets real-time: a planner that solves a query in a fraction of a second on most seeds may take far longer on a few. Measure the distribution over many seeds on your real scenes, not one run, and set the timeout from the tail. No hardware measurements were made in this build. D6

Untested on hardware. Every run in this chapter used the simulated house and a simulated arm. Planning times and success rates on a real robot depend on its collision model, its computer and its scenes, none of which were measured here.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Mira sees bushes with her eyes, continuously. A planner sees only the points it checks; between two checks it is blind.
    • 

  • Mira's park is two-dimensional, and her random tall trees are real places. A robot arm's "park" has one dimension per joint, and a random sample is a pose nobody might ever have chosen, which is the point.
    • 

  • Mira's route home improves because she remembers. Plain RRT never improves; only RRT* (or post-processing) does.
    • 

  • Mira would stop if a path were impossible. A sampling-based planner cannot know that; it stops at a timeout.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Paths through thin obstacles or corners. Symptom: an independent validator or the simulator reports contact on a planned path; long segments are the culprits. Cause: edge resolution δ too coarse. Fix: δ below the thinnest relevant feature; validate every final path densely (or exactly) before execution.
    • 

  • Planner times out in narrow passages. Symptom: easy queries succeed, door-to-door queries fail. Cause: few samples land in the passage. Find it: plot samples. Fix: bidirectional search, goal bias, more time, or a sampler that biases towards obstacle boundaries.
    • 

  • Wrong distance metric. Symptom: odd trees in C-spaces with angles. Cause: Euclidean distance on angles ignores wrap-around (179° and −179° are 2° apart). Fix: a metric that wraps angles and weights joints.
    • 

  • RRT* costs drifting. Symptom: RRT* sometimes returns worse paths than plain RRT. Cause: subtree costs not updated after rewiring. Fix: propagate (Listing 1, lines 111–117); assert, in a debug build, that each node's cost equals its parent's cost plus the edge.
    • 

  • Unreproducible failures. Symptom: a bad path in the log cannot be produced again. Cause: seed not logged. Fix: log the seed and the planner configuration with every request.
    •

Summary


    

  • Configuration space turns "robot among obstacles" into "point among C-obstacles"; for arms its dimension is the number of joints, and grids become impossible.
    • 

  • Sampling-based planners need only point and segment collision checks; RRT grows a tree towards random samples, PRM builds a reusable roadmap, RRT-Connect grows two trees.
    • 

  • RRT is probabilistically complete but not optimal; RRT* rewires and converges towards the optimum; shortcutting removes most of RRT's wiggles cheaply.
    • 

  • On the house: RRT 9.584 m, with shortcutting 7.440 m, RRT* 7.122 m, grid A* 7.177 m (medians of 10 seeds).
    • 

  • The collision checker is the planner's eyes: an edge resolution of 0.60 m let 16 of 25 shortcut paths clip door frames.
    •
