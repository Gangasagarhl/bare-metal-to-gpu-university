Story hook

Analogy

Emeka's bakery in a nearby town has two ovens, three fans in the wall and a water pipe from the street. Every night at two o'clock the big bread batch goes into the ovens. For two nights in a row, the bakery's alarm switched the ovens off at a quarter past two, and they came back on by themselves five minutes later. Nobody was there. In the morning, the bread was half-baked and the notebook by the door said only "too hot, switched off" and "switched on again". One fan in the wall had stopped on Tuesday afternoon. Emeka's town also turns its street ventilation down at night to save money, so the air coming in is warmer then. Each of these three facts alone was harmless.

Servers turn electrical power into heat, all of it, and the heat must be carried away by air (or liquid) at every moment. When power or cooling fail, protection mechanisms switch servers off before they are damaged, and the BMC writes down what happened. This chapter covers the server's power and cooling path, the protection levels that act when temperatures rise, and the arithmetic of reliability: how often things fail, how long repair takes, and what redundancy buys. It ends with the course forensic, "The server that reboots at night", solved from a BMC event log and sensor history.

What you will be able to do

  • Trace the power path from the utility to the processor and the cooling path from the cold aisle to the hot aisle, naming the failure points.
  • Explain the protection levels against overheating: fan speed-up, throttling, BMC thresholds and thermal shutdown, hardware trip.
  • Compute availability from MTBF and MTTR for single, series and k-of-n redundant parts, and expected downtime per year.
  • Use a cooling model to show how inlet temperature, load and a failed fan combine.
  • Diagnose a night-time thermal shutdown from SEL entries, sensor history and OS logs, separating causes from red herrings.
Jargon box
PSU (power supply unit)
Simple words: the box in a server that turns mains electricity into the low voltages the electronics use. Analogy: the water meter and pressure reducer where the house joins the mains. Precise: a converter from AC (or high-voltage DC) input to the server's main DC rail(s), often hot-swappable and duplicated; it reports input and output power, temperatures and faults to the BMC over a management bus (PMBus on SMBus/I2C). D2 D1
Inlet temperature
Simple words: the temperature of the air entering the front of the server. Analogy: how warm the street air is when it comes through the bakery's wall fans. Precise: the temperature at the server's air intake, measured by a front-panel sensor; it is the starting point of every temperature inside, so a warmer inlet raises every component's temperature for the same power and airflow; data-centre operators set it through the room's cooling. D3 D4
Thermal protection shutdown
Simple words: the server switches itself off because it is too hot, to avoid damage. Analogy: the bakery alarm that switches the ovens off. Precise: a protective power-off triggered by firmware (the BMC on an upper-critical threshold, or the OS on an ACPI critical trip point) or by the processor's own hardware trip at its maximum temperature, after the softer measures (fans, throttling) were not enough. D1 D5
Power restore policy
Simple words: the rule for whether a server switches itself back on after its power went away. Analogy: "switch the ovens back on when the power returns". Precise: a BMC or firmware setting with values such as always off, always on, or restore the previous state; it decides what happens after an AC loss or, on some platforms, after a protective shutdown; "always on" can turn a protective shutdown into a cycle. D1
MTBF and MTTR
Simple words: how long a part works on average before it fails, and how long it takes on average to fix it. Analogy: how often a pipe bursts, and how long the plumber takes to come. Precise: mean time between failures and mean time to repair (restore); with constant failure rates, the steady-state availability of a repairable part is MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). An MTBF is a statistical rate over a population, not a lifetime of one part. D6
N+1 redundancy
Simple words: one more part than needed, so that one can fail without harm. Analogy: a second water pipe from another street. Precise: a configuration with N + 1 identical parts where N suffice (for example 1+1 power supplies, 3+1 fans); the system survives any single failure, provided the failure is detected and repaired before a second one, and provided the remaining parts can carry the load in all conditions. D6 D3

Terms from earlier chapters: Availability (DS301), Thermal throttling and Thermal zone and trip point (HW205), SEL (F5-29), Fail-safe fan mode (F5-30), BMC.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A computer working hard gets hot, like a runner. Fans blow cool air through it to take the heat away. If the air coming in is warm, or a fan stops, or the computer works extra hard, it gets hotter. When it gets far too hot, a small helper switches it off so nothing melts.

Things break now and then. That is why servers have spare parts built in: two power boxes instead of one, an extra fan. The spare keeps things running while someone replaces the broken part, as long as someone notices.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The power path

Analogy first: water reaches a tap through the mains, a meter and the house pipes, and a leak anywhere stops it. Electricity reaches a processor through the utility feed, an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) with batteries and often a generator behind it, a rack power distribution unit (PDU), the server's power supply units, its main DC rail and the voltage regulator modules (VRMs) next to each processor and memory channel. D3 D2 Every stage can fail, and every stage converts some power into heat. Redundancy is built at several stages: two feeds (A and B) through separate UPSes and PDUs, and 1+1 power supplies in the server so that either can carry the load alone (Figure 1). The BMC reads each PSU's status, input power and faults over PMBus and logs events such as "power supply input lost" in the SEL (F5-29). D1 D2

Power becomes heat

Practically all electrical power a server draws ends up as heat in the room. So cooling capacity must match power, rack by rack. Air enters at the front from the cold aisle, passes the components and leaves at the back into the hot aisle; the room's cooling removes the heat from the hot aisle and supplies cold air again. D3 D4 A component's temperature is the inlet temperature plus a rise that grows with its power and shrinks with airflow. That one sentence explains most thermal incidents: hotter inlet, more power or less airflow, alone or together. Data-centre operators often run the inlet as warm as the equipment allows, to save cooling energy, which reduces the margin for the other two. D3 D4

Protection levels

When temperatures rise, several mechanisms act in order. D1 D5

  1. Fans speed up (the BMC's fan control, F5-30).
  2. Throttling: the processor lowers its clock and voltage to cut power (HW205); the work runs slower.
  3. Thresholds and events: the BMC compares sensors with thresholds (upper non-critical, upper critical, upper non-recoverable) and logs crossings in the SEL. D1
  4. Protective shutdown: on a critical condition the BMC or the OS (ACPI critical trip point, HW205) switches the host off; as a last resort the processor's own thermal trip cuts power. D5

After a protective shutdown, the power restore policy decides whether the server comes back by itself. "Always on" maximises availability after a short power cut, but after a thermal shutdown it brings the server back into the same conditions; if the cause persists, the server cycles. That is the shape of the course forensic.

Reliability in numbers

For a repairable part with constant failure rate, availability A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR) (DS301 introduced availability). D6 Parts that are all needed are in series: multiply their availabilities. Redundant parts where k of n are needed: add the probabilities of all cases with at least k parts up. Expected downtime per year is (1 − A) × 8766 hours. Listing 3 computes these for exercise values (MTBF 100,000 h and MTTR 24 h for a PSU, 50,000 h and 24 h for a fan, 200,000 h and 72 h for the main board); the run (R4) gives:

  • one PSU: 126.2 min/year expected downtime; 1+1 PSUs: 0.0 min/year at this precision;
  • four fans all needed: 1008.6 min/year; N+1 (3 of 4): 0.7 min/year;
  • the server without redundancy: 1323.5 min/year; with 1+1 PSUs and N+1 fans: 190.0 min/year, now dominated by the main board's 189.3, which is not redundant;
  • a service on three such servers that needs two: 0.2 min/year.

Two lessons follow. Redundancy helps only where it is applied: the redundant server is limited by its single board. And the formulas assume independent failures and repair before the next failure; a fan failure that nobody repairs (the forensic case) breaks the second assumption, and a shared cause (a hot room, one feed for both PSUs) breaks the first. D6

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

A lumped cooling model, and what it is good for

Listing 1 is the university's own model of one server's cooling: processor power P = 60 W + 1.6 W per % load; airflow a from the duty of the working fans (0 to 1); a steady processor temperature T* = inlet + 0.318 · P / (0.25 + a); the processor approaches T* with a 4-minute time constant; the fan controller sets duty = 30 % + 3 % per °C above 50 °C, limited to 30–100 %. Every constant was chosen for teaching; none is a property of a real server. The model is good for one thing: showing how the three causes combine. Listing 2's run (R2):

  • With 4 fans, at full load, the processor settles at 78.0 °C with a 22 °C inlet and 82.0 °C with a 26 °C inlet.
  • With 3 fans: 92.0 °C at 22 °C inlet, and 96.0 °C at 26 °C inlet, the only case at or above the model's 95 °C upper-critical threshold.
  • At 60 % load with 3 fans and a 26 °C inlet, the fans already reach 100 % duty, at 75.6 °C: the controller has no reserve left.

In the model, each factor alone raises the temperature by a few degrees; only the combination of a warm inlet, full load and a missing fan crosses the threshold. This is typical of real incidents too: the post-mortem finds several contributing conditions, none of which was alarming on its own. D7

Failure modes that the numbers hide

  • Hidden loss of redundancy. After one fan or PSU fails, the server runs without a spare. If the event is not alerted and repaired, MTTR becomes "until someone notices", and the k-of-n formula no longer applies. D6
  • Redundant parts that cannot carry the full load. N+1 fans help only if N fans can cool the worst case. In the model, 3 fans cannot cool full load with a 26 °C inlet.
  • Common causes. Both PSUs on the same feed, all servers of a rack in the same hot spot, a firmware update rolled out everywhere: these defeat the independence assumption. D3
  • Protection that cycles. A thermal shutdown followed by "always on" can repeat while the cause persists, every night at the same time; the server is reported as "rebooting" rather than "overheating".

Power capping and budgets

Data centres provision power per rack and per row. To fit more servers in a budget, operators can cap a server's power: the BMC or a management system sets a limit, and the platform enforces it through processor power limits (throttling) so that the server never draws more. D2 D3 Capping trades performance under peak load for density and protects upstream breakers; like throttling, it shows up as slower work rather than as an error, so it belongs in the evidence list for performance incidents (F5-32's mistakes table).

How the hardware actually does it

A fan module has a motor driven by a PWM signal from the BMC (duty cycle sets the speed) and a tachometer output with pulses per revolution, which the BMC counts to report rpm; a stopped fan reads 0 rpm and crosses its lower-critical threshold, as in SEL entry 4 of the forensic pack. D1 D2 Temperature sensors are diodes inside the processor (read by the processor and reported to the BMC over a platform interface) and discrete sensors on I2C at the inlet, near memory and in the PSUs. The processor protects itself: above its maximum junction temperature it throttles, and at a higher hardware trip point it signals the platform to cut power, independent of firmware. D5 A PSU contains its own fan, temperature sensors and protection; it reports over PMBus and can shut down on overload or overheating. Redundant PSUs share the load; when one fails, the other takes it all, so each must be sized for the whole server. D2 Untested on hardware in this build.

Safety

Power supplies, PDUs and UPSes carry mains voltage and store energy after disconnection; only qualified people open them. Fans can cut fingers. Never cover air intakes, remove blanking panels from racks, or run a server with its lid off longer than its documentation allows: the airflow path is part of the cooling design. Hot-swap PSUs and fans only as the vendor's documentation describes. D2

Worked example

Compute the availability of "cooling: 3 of 4 fans needed" with the exercise values, and check against R4.

  1. One fan: A = 50,000 / (50,000 + 24) = 0.99952023. Unavailability u = 1 − A = 0.00047977.
  2. All four up: A⁴ = 0.99808230 (this is the "4 of 4" row: 0.9980823, matching R4).
  3. Exactly three up: 4 · A³ · u = 4 · 0.99856 · 0.00047977 ≈ 0.00191632.
  4. 3 of 4: A⁴ + 4 · A³ · u ≈ 0.99808230 + 0.00191632 = 0.99999862 (R4 prints 0.9999986).
  5. Downtime: (1 − 0.99999862) · 8766 h · 60 ≈ 0.73 min/year (R4: 0.7).

Now the uncomfortable part: the MTTR of 24 h assumes the failed fan is replaced within a day. In the forensic case, fan 3 failed on Tuesday at 15:12 and was still dead on Thursday; without alerting, MTTR is "until something else breaks".

Where the analogy breaks


    

  1. A burst pipe is obvious; a failed redundant part is silent. The server keeps working, which is the point of redundancy and also why failures go unrepaired.
    2. 

  2. A second pipe from another street is truly independent; a second PSU on the same feed is not. The analogy hides common causes.
    3. 

  3. Bread can be baked again tomorrow; a backup that died with the host is missing. In the forensic case, the nightly job never completed after the first shutdown, a data-protection consequence of a thermal problem.

Summary


    

  • Power path: feed → UPS → PDU → PSUs → DC rail → VRMs; redundancy at feeds and PSUs. All power becomes heat.
    • 

  • Component temperature = inlet + a rise that grows with power and falls with airflow; incidents combine warmer inlet, higher load and lost airflow.
    • 

  • Protection: fans, throttling, BMC thresholds and SEL events, protective shutdown, hardware trip; the power restore policy decides what happens next.
    • 

  • A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR); series multiply; k of n sum. In the exercise: 1323.5 → 190.0 min/year with 1+1 PSUs and N+1 fans, limited by the single board.
    • 

  • Redundancy works only with detection and repair, independent causes, and spares that can carry the full load.
    • 

  • The course forensic: a dead fan, a warm night inlet and a full-load backup together caused thermal shutdowns and "always on" restarts.
