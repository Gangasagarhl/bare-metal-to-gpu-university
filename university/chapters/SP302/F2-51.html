Story hook

Tomás runs the kitchen of a small restaurant and has bought a new frying pan. He wants to know whether the head chef, Ama, makes an omelette faster with it. On Monday morning he starts his stopwatch, Ama cooks one omelette with the old pan, and it takes a long time. On Monday evening, at the busiest hour, she uses the new pan and it takes even longer. On Tuesday Tomás times the new pan again, but Ama is only showing an apprentice the movements: no eggs are cracked, and the stopwatch says almost nothing. "The new pan is wonderful," Tomás writes.

Every one of those numbers was real, and none of them answered the question. The first omelette of the morning was slow because the stove was cold. The evening omelette was slow because three other cooks were using the same stove. The Tuesday omelette was fast because no omelette was made. The pan was never measured at all.

This chapter is about becoming the opposite of Tomás. Before you make a program faster, you learn to make a measurement that really answers "is this faster, and by how much?" Everything in the rest of SP302 (caches, SIMD, branches, profilers, the roofline) stands on that.

What you will be able to do

  • Design a timing experiment with warm-up runs, many repetitions and a monotonic clock, and report minimum, median and maximum instead of one number.
  • Explain why the median of repeated runs is a better summary of a noisy benchmark than the mean or a single run, and compute median and percentiles by hand.
  • Detect a benchmark that measures nothing because the optimiser removed the work, using the disassembly as evidence.
  • Measure how much run-to-run variation your own machine has, alone and with competing work, and say what that means for the smallest speed-up you can trust.
  • Write a measurement record (machine, toolchain, flags, date, method) that another engineer can repeat.
Jargon box
Benchmark
Simple words: a test that measures how fast something runs, done the same way every time. Analogy: Tomás's stopwatch evenings, if he ran them properly. Precise: a repeatable experiment that applies a defined workload to a system and records a performance metric (time, throughput, latency) together with the conditions of the run. D1
Microbenchmark
Simple words: a benchmark of one tiny piece of code, such as one loop. Analogy: timing only the omelette, not the whole dinner service. Precise: a benchmark of a small, isolated operation; it is easy to run and easy to get wrong, because the surrounding program, the compiler and the machine's state influence it strongly. D1
Warm-up run
Simple words: a run you do first and do not count. Analogy: preheating the stove before the timed omelette. Precise: an untimed execution of the workload before measurement, so that one-time costs (first touch of memory pages, filling caches, loading code, the processor changing its clock speed) do not land in the timed runs. D1 R4
Steady (monotonic) clock
Simple words: a clock that only ever moves forward at a constant rate, even if someone changes the time of day. Analogy: a stopwatch, not the wall clock that is set forward in spring. Precise: in C++, std::chrono::steady_clock, whose time points never decrease as physical time advances; is_steady is true for it. system_clock follows the wall-clock time and may jump. D2 R1
Clock resolution
Simple words: the smallest time step a clock can show you. Analogy: a stopwatch that shows tenths of a second cannot time a 3-millisecond event. Precise: the smallest observable difference between two readings; it is not the same as the clock's declared tick (period), and must be measured. R1
Median and percentile
Simple words: the median is the middle value after sorting; the 90th percentile is the value that 90 % of results are at or below. Analogy: line up all of Tomás's omelette times from fastest to slowest and point at the middle one. Precise: for n sorted values, the median is the value at position (n+1)/2 for odd n (the mean of the two middle values for even n); the nearest-rank p-th percentile is the value at rank ⌈p/100 × n⌉. D4 R5
Run-to-run variation
Simple words: the same program, run again on the same machine, takes a different time. Analogy: the omelette takes longer when other cooks share the stove. Precise: the spread of repeated measurements of an unchanged workload, caused by everything the program does not control: other processes, interrupts, frequency changes, cache and memory state, timer resolution. In this chapter it is reported as (max − min) / median. D1 R4
Optimisation barrier
Simple words: a tiny piece of code that tells the compiler "this value is needed, do not throw away the work that made it". Analogy: Tomás asking to taste the omelette, so the chef must really cook it. Precise: here, bench::keep: a GNU extended-asm statement with no instructions whose input is the value and whose clobber list says "memory"; the compiler must materialise the value and may not delete the computation (dead code elimination, the term of F1-31). D3 R3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Measuring a program is like timing the head chef. Preheat the stove first (warm-up). Time many omelettes, not one, and do it at a quiet hour or at least know how busy the kitchen was (noise). Line the times up and take the middle one (median), because one omelette interrupted by a delivery should not decide the answer. And make sure an omelette was really cooked each time: if the chef only mimes the movements, the stopwatch measures nothing (the compiler deleted the work). Mapping: chef = CPU core, other cooks = other threads and processes, the translator rewriting the recipe = the compiler, stopwatch = steady_clock.

A computer is much more regular than a kitchen, which is why people are surprised by how much its timings wobble. The rest of this chapter shows you the wobble on a real machine, explains where it comes from, and gives you a small tool that handles it.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Start from a question, not a number

A benchmark answers a question such as "does loop B take less time than loop A for 220 doubles on this machine, built with -O2?" Writing the question down forces three decisions that most broken benchmarks skip: what exactly is the workload (sizes, data, which function), under which conditions (machine, compiler, flags, what else is running) and which statistic will decide (here: the median of 21 timed runs after 3 warm-ups). Gregg's book frames benchmarking as an activity with a method, a stated purpose and a check that the benchmark measures what you think it measures D1; this chapter turns that into a harness and a record.

Choose the right clock and know its resolution

C++ offers three clocks in <chrono>. Only steady_clock is guaranteed never to go backwards D2; system_clock follows the wall time, which can be adjusted while your benchmark runs. Listing 3 prints what the three clocks declare on the build machine and measures the smallest step it could actually observe between two readings. In the recorded run all three declared a tick of one nanosecond, only steady_clock reported is_steady=1, and the smallest observed step was a few tens of nanoseconds R1. That observed step, not the declared tick, is the resolution you have. A rule that follows from arithmetic: if one run of your workload lasts only a few times the resolution, quantisation alone changes the result by tens of percent; make each timed run last at least a thousand times the observed step (in the build, that means at least tens of microseconds), by repeating the work inside one timed run if needed.

Warm up

The first run of a workload pays costs that later runs do not: memory pages touched for the first time, caches and the branch predictor filled with this program's data and code, and possibly a processor that is still raising its clock speed D1. Listing 2 (the "quiet" run) times the same 16 MiB summation 21 times without any warm-up. In the recorded output the first five or six runs are visibly slower than the rest; the later runs settle around the median R4. You see the effect even though the array had already been written once before timing started, which tells you it is not only page faults. The lab asks you to check which cause dominates on your machine.

Repeat, sort, take the median

Run times are not symmetric around a "true" value: disturbances only ever add time. An interrupt, a context switch or another process can make a run slower; nothing makes a run faster than the work allows. That is why a few slow outliers pull the mean upwards while the median stays with the bulk of the runs (Figure 2). The minimum is useful as well (it approximates the undisturbed cost), but it is a single extreme value and therefore fragile; this course reports minimum, median and maximum, decides with the median, and uses at least 20 runs, as the curriculum's measurement protocol requires C1 D4. Twenty-one is used because an odd count has a single middle value.

Know how noisy your machine is

Listing 2 can also start busy "neighbour" threads that spin on every CPU while the measured loop runs. The recorded outputs show two things R4. First, the median with four busy neighbours on a four-CPU machine is several times the quiet median: the measured thread is sharing CPU time. Second, even the quiet run has a large spread, because the build container is a shared cloud virtual machine on which other jobs ran at the same time. The consequence is arithmetic: if your machine's spread for an unchanged workload is, say, 10 %, a measured "5 % faster" is not evidence of anything until you repeat the comparison and the difference persists. Report the spread together with the median.

Make sure the work happens

The C++ standard requires a compiler to preserve the program's observable behaviour, and the elapsed time is not part of it D2. A loop whose result is never used can therefore be removed completely; HW202 (F1-31) called this dead code elimination. Listing 1's bench::keep is the countermeasure: an empty GNU extended-asm statement that takes the value as an input and declares that it may touch memory D3. The compiler cannot see inside the asm, so it must compute the value. Listing 2 shows both cases side by side, and the disassembly in the forensic lab proves what happened.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What exactly a timed run contains

The harness reads steady_clock::now() before and after the call. Everything between the two reads is in the measurement: the function, its call overhead, and whatever the operating system did on that CPU in that interval (interrupt handlers, a context switch to another thread). The two clock reads themselves cost time too; that is one reason to keep each timed run long relative to the observed clock step. The compiler may also move independent work across the clock reads unless something orders it; in the harness the work is a call to a non-inlined function ([[gnu::noinline]]), and the clock reads are calls into the library, which in practice keeps the order. If you write your own harness with inlined work, check the disassembly (forensic lab) rather than trusting it. D3 R3

Measure the build you ship

Listing 2 is built twice, with -O0 and with -O2. At -O0 both functions cost several nanoseconds per element and the "discarded" one is not removed; at -O2 the kept loop costs well under one nanosecond per element and the discarded one costs nothing R2. Unoptimised timings therefore say very little about optimised code: the ranking of two versions can even reverse. Benchmarks in this course use the flags of the program you will ship, record them in the run record, and never run under a sanitizer (sanitizers add checks to every memory access; SP202's F2-29 explains why). That is why the lab's timing programs are .cc files built by run.sh, while correctness listings (.cpp) run under AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer.

How many runs, and when is a difference real?

The curriculum fixes the minimum (at least 20 timed runs, median) C1. Two cheap checks make a comparison of versions A and B more trustworthy without heavier statistics (which MA202 covers D4): first, interleave the measurements (A, B, A, B…) or repeat the whole comparison at another time, so that a slow period of the machine does not hit only one version; second, compare the difference of medians with the spread you measured for an unchanged workload. A difference smaller than the spread is "not shown", not "zero". Report both medians, both spreads, and the number of runs.

The run record

A number without its conditions cannot be repeated or compared. Every lab step in this university writes a .log next to its output with the listing, the compiler's own version line, the exact command, the date, the machine and the exit code (Figure 1) R2. Measured numbers are labelled as measurements of that machine on that date, never as properties of a processor model (guide AH-23).

How the hardware actually does it

Three hardware mechanisms explain most of the variation you will see. First, the caches (F1-32, F1-33): a warm run finds its data in cache, a cold run fetches it from DRAM. Second, the CPU is shared: the operating system schedules several threads on each CPU in turn, so a busy neighbour takes time slices from your measured thread (F2-34) D1. Third, processors change their clock frequency with load and temperature (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, HW205's DVFS), so the same instruction sequence can take a different time per instruction in different seconds. On a cloud virtual machine, a fourth layer is added: the virtual CPUs are themselves scheduled by the host. The build container's CPU model string reads only "Intel(R) Xeon(R) Processor @ 2.10GHz" R4; no datasheet was consulted, and the 2.10 GHz in the name must not be read as the speed the code ran at.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Whether and how the build machine changed its clock frequency during the runs could not be observed: the hardware performance counters that would show cycles are not available in the container (see F2-55), and no frequency-control interface was examined. The warm-up effect in the quiet run may come from frequency changes, from cache and TLB state, or from the host scheduling the virtual CPU; the build did not separate these causes. On your own machine, the Linux kernel's CPU frequency documentation (cpufreq) and your processor vendor's documentation of frequency scaling are the documents to check.

Worked example

A colleague timed a function nine times and wrote the results in milliseconds, in the order measured: 10.2, 10.4, 10.1, 10.3, 14.9, 10.2, 10.5, 10.2, 31.0 (file stats.in). Summarise them.

  1. Sort: 10.1, 10.2, 10.2, 10.2, 10.3, 10.4, 10.5, 14.9, 31.0.
  2. Minimum and maximum: 10.1 and 31.0.
  3. Median: n = 9 is odd, so the median is the value at position (9 + 1) / 2 = 5: 10.3.
  4. Mean: the sum is 10.1 + 3 × 10.2 + 10.3 + 10.4 + 10.5 + 14.9 + 31.0 = 117.8; divided by 9 that is 13.09 (rounded). The mean is above eight of the nine values.
  5. 90th percentile (nearest rank): rank ⌈0.9 × 9⌉ = ⌈8.1⌉ = 9, the largest value, 31.0. With only nine runs, the 90th percentile is the worst run: a reason for more runs.
  6. Spread: (31.0 − 10.1) / 10.3 ≈ 2.03, so about 203 %: this machine was disturbed during at least two runs, and the report should say so.

Listing 4 computes the same summary; its real output (below) agrees with every value above and adds the sample standard deviation R5.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A cook who is interrupted notices and tells you; a program that loses its CPU for a millisecond does not know it happened. The only evidence is in the distribution of times, which is why you keep all 21.
    • 

  • Tomás could see whether eggs were cracked. You cannot see whether the compiler kept your loop without reading the generated instructions; "it took some time" does not prove that the work you meant was done (the work might be only partly removed).
    • 

  • A preheated stove stays hot. Caches, the branch predictor and the clock frequency can "cool down" between runs whenever other work runs in between, so warm-up helps only if the timed runs follow it closely.

Summary


    

  • A benchmark is an experiment: a question, a workload, conditions and a statistic, decided before you run it.
    • 

  • Use steady_clock; measure its real resolution and keep timed runs much longer than it.
    • 

  • Warm up, time at least 21 runs, sort, and report minimum, median and maximum; decide with the median.
    • 

  • Disturbances only add time, so outliers pull the mean upwards; the spread of an unchanged workload is the smallest difference you can claim.
    • 

  • The optimiser may delete work whose result is unused; use the result, and check the disassembly.
    • 

  • Measure the build you ship, never a sanitizer or -O0 build, and record machine, toolchain, flags, date and method.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. sumDiscarded consists of endbr64 and ret: no load, no multiply, no add, no backward jump. sumKept contains the loop: movsd (%rax),%xmm0, mulsd %xmm0,%xmm0, addsd %xmm0,%xmm1, then cmp and a jne back to the load.


Hypotheses. (1) The new code is genuinely faster (for example vectorised). (2) The compiler removed the new code's work. Hypothesis 1 predicts SIMD instructions and a loop in sumDiscarded; hypothesis 2 predicts no loop. The disassembly shows no loop, so (2).


Root cause. Line 13, (void)s;: the sum is computed but never used, so under the as-if rule the optimiser deletes the whole loop; the "time" is two clock reads around an empty call.


Fix and verification. Use the result (bench::keep(s) or return it to a caller that uses it). The new function then costs the same as sumKept, within the spread, because the two loops are identical: the claimed speed-up disappears.


Prevention. Review rule: every benchmark keeps its results; any speed-up larger than about the SIMD width times the core count triggers a disassembly check. How the evidence was produced: Listing 2 was built with -O2 by run.sh and disassembled with objdump -d -C --no-show-raw-insn; nothing was edited.
