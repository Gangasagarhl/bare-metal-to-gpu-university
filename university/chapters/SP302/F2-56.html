Story hook

Kofi runs a kitchen with a fast stove and a slow pantry door: ingredients arrive through the door at a fixed number of crates per hour, and the stove can perform a fixed number of cooking steps per hour. He serves two dishes. The salad needs one crate of vegetables per plate and almost no cooking: however fast his cooks chop, the kitchen never makes more salads per hour than the door delivers crates. The slow-braised stew needs many hours of stove work for each crate: there, the door is idle most of the time and only the stove matters.

A consultant arrives and buys a second stove "to make everything faster". Stew output rises. Salad output does not move at all. Kofi could have predicted both without spending anything, if he had known two numbers about his kitchen and one number about each dish: how much cooking each crate of ingredients receives.

That is the roofline model. This chapter measures the two machine numbers for the build machine (peak floating-point rate and memory bandwidth), computes the third for each kernel (arithmetic intensity), draws the roofline, and uses it to judge the course's matrix multiply as we optimise it step by step, from about 2 GFLOP/s to about 49 GFLOP/s in the recorded run.

What you will be able to do

  • Measure a compute roof (peak FLOP/s) and a memory roof (bandwidth) on your own machine with microbenchmarks.
  • Compute the arithmetic intensity of a kernel from its operation and byte counts, and state the assumptions behind the byte count.
  • Draw a roofline and place kernels on it; decide whether each is memory-bound or compute-bound and how far it is from its roof.
  • Optimise a CPU matrix multiply step by step (loop order, cache blocking, register tiling, threads), verifying correctness at every step and recording a speed table.
  • Predict which optimisations can help a kernel and which cannot, before trying them.
Jargon box
Arithmetic intensity
Simple words: how much calculation a program does for each byte it moves to or from memory. Analogy: how many cooking steps each crate of ingredients receives. Precise: the ratio of floating-point operations to bytes of memory traffic, in FLOP/byte; which traffic is counted (DRAM, a cache level; compulsory only or measured) must be stated. D1
Roofline model
Simple words: a chart that shows the fastest a kernel can possibly run on a machine, given how much calculation it does per byte. Analogy: the kitchen's limit: the stove's or the pantry door's, whichever is lower for that dish. Precise: attainable performance = min(peak FLOP/s, arithmetic intensity × peak bandwidth), drawn on log–log axes as a slanted line (memory roof) meeting a flat line (compute roof). D1
Ridge point
Simple words: the intensity where the two limits meet. Analogy: the dish for which the stove and the door are equally busy. Precise: peak FLOP/s divided by peak bandwidth (FLOP/byte); kernels to its left are memory-bound, to its right compute-bound. D1
Memory-bound and compute-bound
Simple words: limited by moving data, or limited by calculating. Analogy: the salad (door-limited) and the stew (stove-limited). Precise: a kernel is memory-bound if intensity × bandwidth is below peak FLOP/s, compute-bound otherwise; the label describes the roof above it, not how close it runs to that roof. D1
Attainable performance
Simple words: the roof's height above a kernel: the best it could do. Analogy: the most plates per hour this dish could reach in this kitchen. Precise: min(peak, AI × bandwidth) for that kernel's intensity; "percent of roof" = measured / attainable. D1
SGEMM
Simple words: multiplying two matrices of single-precision numbers. Analogy: the stew: lots of cooking per ingredient. Precise: single-precision general matrix multiply, C = αAB + βC in the BLAS interface; here α = 1, β = 0, row-major, M × K times K × N, 2MNK floating-point operations. D4
Register tiling (micro-kernel)
Simple words: computing a small block of the answer entirely in registers, reusing every loaded number many times. Analogy: keeping a few pans on the stove and adding to all of them from each crate before fetching the next. Precise: an innermost kernel that keeps an MR × NR block of C in vector registers and, for each k, loads NR values of B and MR values of A to perform MR × NR fused multiply-adds; here MR = 6, NR = 16 with AVX2. D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

A kitchen has two speed limits: how fast the stove can cook and how fast the pantry door can deliver. A dish that needs little cooking per crate (a salad) is limited by the door; a dish that needs a lot of cooking per crate (a stew) is limited by the stove. Mapping: stove = the core's floating-point units (peak FLOP/s); pantry door = memory bandwidth (bytes per second); cooking per crate = arithmetic intensity (FLOP per byte).

If you know which limit your dish hits, you know what to buy and what to change. A faster stove does nothing for salads. A wider door does nothing for stews. And if your dish runs far below both limits, the problem is neither the stove nor the door: it is how the cooks work, which is what the matrix-multiply lab improves.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Measuring the two roofs

Compute roof. Listing 1 (peak.cc) runs 12 independent chains of fused multiply-adds (each FMA counts as two floating-point operations) that never touch memory. Independent chains are needed because one chain must wait for its previous result (the FMA's latency); with enough chains in flight, the units are kept busy every cycle (Little's law, HW301). In the recorded run one core reached 100.5 GFLOP/s with 8-lane AVX2 and 220.7 GFLOP/s with 16-lane AVX-512; four threads reached 168.8 with AVX2 R2.

Memory roof. Listing 2 (stream.cc) runs the triad a[i] = b[i] + s·c[i], counting 12 bytes per element (two 4-byte loads and one 4-byte store) D3. With 128 MiB arrays (far larger than any cache), one core moved 12.4 GB/s with AVX2 and four threads 12.6 GB/s R3. With 16 KiB arrays that stay in cache, the same AVX2 code moved 168.7 GB/s: the memory roof depends on where the data lives, and the DRAM roof is the one that matters for large data.

Ridge point. 100.5 / 12.4 ≈ 8.1 FLOP/byte for one core; 168.8 / 12.6 ≈ 13.4 for four cores R5. A kernel must do at least about 8 floating-point operations per byte from DRAM before the stove, rather than the door, can limit it on one core.

Arithmetic intensity of two kernels

Triad: 2 FLOP (one multiply, one add) per 12 bytes: 0.17 FLOP/byte. Far left of the ridge: memory-bound. Its attainable rate is 0.17 × 12.4 ≈ 2.1 GFLOP/s, and the recorded AVX2 run reached 100 % of it R5 (by construction: this run also defined the roof).

SGEMM, n × n: 2n³ FLOP. Compulsory traffic, if each matrix crosses the memory bus exactly once: three matrices of n² floats, 12n² bytes. Intensity 2n³ / 12n² = n/6: 85.3 at n = 512, 170.7 at n = 1024. Far right of the ridge: compute-bound, attainable 100.5 GFLOP/s on one core R5. That intensity assumes perfect reuse; the naive loop moves far more bytes than that (it rereads B for every row of A), so its real intensity is much lower. Layer 3 returns to this.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What the roofline does and does not say

The roofline gives an upper bound from two machine numbers and one kernel number. It says nothing about why a kernel runs below its roof. Step 1's point sits at 2 % of the compute roof, but it is not compute-bound in any useful sense: its real traffic is much larger than the compulsory 12n² bytes, so its real intensity is far to the left, and on a roofline drawn with measured traffic it would sit near the slanted memory roof of whichever cache level feeds it. The model's original paper discusses both kinds of intensity and adds lower "ceilings" (no SIMD, no FMA, poor locality) below the roofs to explain such gaps (D1, pending verification D1). Our chart uses compulsory traffic only, which places every SGEMM step at the same intensity: honest as an upper bound, misleading as a diagnosis. On real hardware, counting DRAM bytes with hardware counters (F2-55, untested here) would give measured intensity.

Which roof is "the" roof

  • SIMD width. The AVX-512 peak (220.7) is about twice the AVX2 peak (100.5) in our run R2. Our SGEMM uses AVX2, so we drew the AVX2 roof; an AVX-512 kernel would have a higher roof. State which instructions the roof assumes.
  • Cache levels. Each memory level has its own bandwidth roof: 168.7 GB/s for the in-cache triad versus 12.4 GB/s from DRAM R3. A kernel whose working set fits in cache should be judged against the cache's roof.
  • Write-allocate. A store to a line that is not in cache usually first reads that line (F2-52). Counting it, the triad moves 16 bytes per element, not 12, and the measured bandwidth would be reported a third higher. We counted 12 and say so in the output's header R3; vendor and benchmark conventions differ.
  • Threads. Single-core and all-core roofs differ; compare a threaded kernel with the all-core roof (the tool does: step 5 is judged against 168.8).

Why the micro-kernel works

For each k, the 6 × 16 micro-kernel loads 16 floats of B (two AVX2 registers) and broadcasts 6 floats of A, then performs 6 × 2 = 12 vector FMAs, that is 96 lane FMAs or 192 FLOP, while the 12 accumulators of C stay in registers for the whole k-panel. That is 192 FLOP per 88 bytes loaded from cache, an intensity of about 2.2 FLOP/byte against the L1 cache, high enough that the L1 roof does not limit it. Twelve independent accumulators also cover the FMA latency, as in peak.cc D4. The remaining gap to the roof (39 % of it) comes from what we left out, compared with production libraries: packing A and B into contiguous buffers, prefetching, tuning MR, NR and the block sizes to this cache hierarchy, and using AVX-512 D4. Those are good next steps for the project, each one measured.

Multi-core roofs on a shared machine

Four threads of the FMA microbenchmark reached 1.68 times one thread, not 4 R2; the DRAM triad hardly scaled (12.4 to 12.6 GB/s) R3. Possible causes include virtual CPUs that share physical cores (hyper-threads), other tenants on the host, and a per-VM bandwidth limit; we did not establish which. The lesson for method: measure the roofs on the machine you report, with the same thread count, and say how they were measured.

How the hardware actually does it

The compute roof is set by the number of floating-point units that can start an FMA each cycle, the vector width, and the clock frequency: peak = cores × FMA units × lanes × 2 × frequency D5. The memory roof is set by the memory channels, their transfer rate and how many outstanding misses a core can keep in flight (Little's law again: bandwidth ≈ bytes in flight / latency, F1-39, Little's law) D5. Our numbers are measurements, not this formula: we could not observe the frequency (F2-51) or the core's unit count.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The number of FMA units per core, the clock frequency during the runs, whether AVX-512 lowers the frequency on this processor, the memory configuration and whether the 4 virtual CPUs are separate physical cores are not known from any document in this build; the CPU model string ("Intel(R) Xeon(R) Processor @ 2.10GHz") does not identify a product. Therefore the theoretical peak cannot be computed and compared with the measured 100.5 GFLOP/s, and the poor 4-thread scaling (1.68× for FMA, 1.01× for DRAM bandwidth) is unexplained. The roofs in this chapter are measurements on a shared cloud virtual machine in one session (AH-23), not properties of a processor. The arithmetic intensities assume compulsory traffic (each matrix moved once; triad with no write-allocate). To check on a real machine: the vendor's optimization reference manual (execution units, AVX frequency behaviour), the platform's memory specification, and measured DRAM traffic from hardware counters.

Worked example

Place the threaded SGEMM at n = 1024 on the roofline and say how much better it could get. Numbers from roofline.out and sgemm.out R4 R5:

  1. FLOP: 2 × 1024³ = 2,147,483,648.
  2. Compulsory bytes: 3 matrices × 1024² floats × 4 bytes = 12,582,912.
  3. Intensity: 2,147,483,648 / 12,582,912 ≈ 170.7 FLOP/byte (= 1024 / 6).
  4. All-core roofs: 168.8 GFLOP/s and 12.6 GB/s; ridge 168.8 / 12.6 ≈ 13.4. 170.7 > 13.4: compute-bound.
  5. Attainable: min(168.8, 170.7 × 12.6 ≈ 2,150) = 168.8 GFLOP/s.
  6. Measured: 39.0 GFLOP/s, so 39.0 / 168.8 ≈ 23 % of the roof.
  7. Interpretation: memory bandwidth is not the limit even if real traffic were ten times the compulsory amount (intensity 17, still right of the ridge). The gap is inside the core: how well the micro-kernel feeds the FMA units, and how well the threads scale. The next optimisations to try are those of Layer 3 (packing, block sizes, AVX-512), not anything about DRAM.

Contrast, the triad at 128 MiB: intensity 0.17, attainable 0.17 × 12.4 ≈ 2.1 GFLOP/s, measured 2.07: at the roof. No compute optimisation can help it; only moving fewer bytes can (fusing it with the next loop that reads a, or using smaller data types).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A kitchen's door and stove work in parallel only if someone organises it. A CPU overlaps memory transfers and arithmetic automatically to a large degree, which is why min(), not a sum, is a reasonable model.
    • 

  • The kitchen has one door. A CPU has a hierarchy of "doors" (registers, L1, L2, L3, DRAM), each with its own bandwidth: there are several slanted roofs.
    • 

  • The amount of cooking per crate is fixed by the recipe. A kernel's real intensity depends on how the code is written: blocking raises reuse and so raises the intensity measured at DRAM, without changing the mathematics.
    • 

  • Kofi's stove has one speed. A CPU's peak changes with the instructions (scalar, AVX2, AVX-512), the number of cores and the frequency.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Speed-ups. 16 KiB: 168.7 / 31.1 ≈ 5.4×. 128 MiB: 12.4 / 10.7 ≈ 1.16×, the "about 15 %" seen in production.


Benchmark and build. Neither was wrong. The benchmark used a 16 KiB array, which stays in cache, where the scalar loop is limited by instruction throughput and AVX2 removes that limit. Production arrays come from DRAM. The 128 MiB AVX2 row shows the AVX2 code running and reaching the DRAM roof.


On the roofline. Intensity 2 FLOP / 12 bytes ≈ 0.17. Attainable from DRAM: 0.17 × 12.4 ≈ 2.1 GFLOP/s. Scalar measured 1.8 (86 % of the roof), AVX2 2.07 (100 %; rows a and b of roofline.out). The scalar version was already close to the memory roof, so at most about 15 % was available to any compute optimisation.


The warning sign. Computing the intensity (0.17, far left of the ridge at about 8) and benchmarking at production size. A five-minute roofline estimate would have predicted at most about 15 %.


What would help. Moving fewer bytes: fuse the triad with the loop that consumes a so a is not written to and reread from DRAM; use 16-bit data where precision allows; or restructure so the data is processed in cache-sized chunks through several steps. Then measure. How the evidence was produced: stream.cc built with -O2 -pthread, scalar version compiled with no-tree-vectorize, median of 21 runs, 12 bytes counted per element; output unedited.
