Story hook

The book club now has a lock: only one friend at a time may hold the club's only key to the storeroom. On Friday the phone lines between Amara's town and the other two go down. Amara cannot reach anyone, so she decides she is in charge now and gives herself the key. In the other two towns, Bao also cannot reach Amara, so he decides he is in charge and gives the key to Chidi. Both "leaders" tell their friend "yes, the key is yours". On Monday the lines come back, Amara's notebook overwrites Bao's, and Chidi learns that the key he was promised was never his. Nobody did anything wrong by their own rules; the rules were wrong.

That is split brain, and it is the most expensive failure a cluster service layer can have, because every part of the system works and still breaks a promise. This chapter builds the layer that prevents it in your own kernel: a small Raft for agreement, a replicated state machine holding a lock table and a job table, and a scheduler on top that places jobs and restarts them when a node leaves. Then it runs the same partition against the friends' rule above and shows the broken promise in the logs.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why membership views must not decide who may write, and why a majority must.
  • Implement Raft leader election and log replication in a freestanding kernel over raw Ethernet, and read terms, votes, commits and truncations from three nodes' logs.
  • Build a replicated state machine (compare-and-set lock and a job table) and a scheduler that submits, places and restarts jobs through the log.
  • Demonstrate under a network partition that only the majority side commits, that the minority's client gets "outcome unknown", and that a lowest-id-leader design breaks safety.
  • Defend a cluster-layer design in a design review against the MP2 rubric.
Jargon box
Cluster service layer
Simple words: the shared services that make many machines act as one cluster: who is in, what is agreed, what runs where. Analogy: the club's list of members, its notebook of decisions, and its organiser. Precise: in this course, membership (F5-44), a consensus-replicated state machine holding locks and the job table, and a scheduler driven by that state machine, running in every node's kernel. G1
Log index (replicated log)
Simple words: the position of a command in the shared list of commands. Analogy: the line number in the club notebook. Precise: a position, counted from 1, in the log that Raft replicates; an entry is identified by its index and the term in which a leader created it, and two logs that hold an entry with the same index and term are identical up to that index. D1
Log truncation (conflict)
Simple words: a follower throwing away entries that the leader does not have. Analogy: crossing out lines you wrote in your copy of the notebook while you could not reach the friend who keeps the real one. Precise: when an append from the current leader carries an entry whose term differs from the follower's entry at the same index, the follower deletes that entry and all after it; only uncommitted entries can be deleted this way. D1
Outcome unknown
Simple words: the honest answer when a request timed out: it may or may not have happened. Analogy: "I posted the letter; I never heard back." Precise: the result a client must report when it received no commit confirmation in time; the command may later be committed (by this or a later leader) or discarded, so the client must not assume either and must check or retry idempotently. D2
Cluster scheduler (replicated)
Simple words: the part that decides which node runs each job, with its decisions written in the shared log. Analogy: the organiser assigning chores, writing each assignment in the notebook. Precise: a leader-side loop that proposes JOB_ASSIGN entries for submitted jobs and for jobs whose node left the view; workers start a job only when the assignment is applied, and report completion through the log, so that every node agrees on each job's state. D3
Naive leader rule (lowest live id)
Simple words: "whoever has the lowest number among the nodes I can see is in charge." Analogy: "the friend with the oldest membership card who answers the phone decides." Precise: a leader-selection rule computed from each node's own membership view, with no majority requirement; under a partition every side elects a leader and accepts writes. R4
Design review
Simple words: a meeting where engineers examine a design before (or while) it is built, looking for what can go wrong. Analogy: the club reading the new rules aloud and asking "what if the phones are down?" Precise: a structured review of a written design against requirements and failure scenarios, with named reviewers, recorded findings and a decision; in this university, the R1–R4 gates of mega projects and the P exam of DS403. G1

Terms from earlier chapters used here without redefinition: consensus, term (Raft), follower, candidate, leader, election timeout, committed entry, replicated state machine, majority quorum, split brain, compare-and-swap, fencing token, scheduler, membership view, cluster.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

The club fixes its rules. Decisions are written in a shared notebook, and a line counts only when more than half of the friends have copied it into their own notebooks. One friend writes the order for everyone (the leader), but only after more than half voted for them in a numbered round (the term). If the phone lines are down, the friends who are cut off can still write in their own notebook, but the line never counts, because they cannot reach more than half. When the lines come back, they cross out what never counted. The organiser who assigns chores writes each chore in the same notebook, so everyone agrees who does what. Mapping: pizza vote = election; friend who writes the order = leader; shared notebook = replicated log; "more than half copied it" = committed; round number = term; lines down = partition; organiser = scheduler.

Amara, alone in her town, can still try to give herself the key. She writes it in her notebook and waits. Nobody else copies it, so she has to say "I don't know if this worked". That honest "I don't know" is what keeps the club from promising one key to two people.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The rule that matters. A view says "whom I can hear". A majority says "enough nodes that no other group can also have enough". Two groups that each hold more than half of the nodes would share a node, and that node only votes once per term. That is the whole reason a majority, not a view, must decide: in quorum.cpp every way of cutting three, four or five nodes into two groups lets at most one side decide under the majority rule, and lets both sides decide under the lowest-id rule (R1). D1 D2

Raft in the kernel. The lab's raft.cc follows the algorithm you simulated in DS302 (F5-18 to F5-21), now in a freestanding kernel over raw Ethernet:

  • A follower that hears no leader within its election timeout (100 + 30 × node id + up to 39 ticks, randomised) starts an election: new term, votes for itself, broadcasts a vote request with its last log index and term.
  • A node grants at most one vote per term, and only to a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own.
  • A candidate with votes from a majority becomes leader, appends a NOOP entry, and sends append messages every 20 ticks to every follower.
  • A follower accepts an append only if its log matches the leader's at the previous index; otherwise it reports where it stands and the leader backs up.
  • The leader commits the highest index stored on a majority, but only if that entry is from its own term; every node applies committed entries in order.

D1

The state machine and its clients. Committed entries are applied to a small state machine (sm.cc): a key-value table with compare-and-set, used as a lock, and a job table. A node with a command broadcasts it; only the leader appends it. The client waits up to 300 ticks for the commit; if none arrives it says "outcome UNKNOWN" and does not pretend.

The scheduler. Jobs are entries too. Node 2 submits four jobs; the leader proposes a JOB_ASSIGN for each, choosing the least-loaded node in its view; a worker starts a job only when its JOB_ASSIGN is applied on that worker; when it finishes, it sends the result to the leader, which appends JOB_DONE. When the leader's view loses a node that holds an assigned job, it proposes a new JOB_ASSIGN for that job. In the run, node 3 halts at its tick 700, before its job 3 finishes; the leader (node 1) notices at its tick 724, re-assigns job 3 to node 2 at index 12, and node 2 completes it: all four results agree with Python on the host (R2).

Membership is still useful. It decides hints: which node to place a job on, which jobs look orphaned. It never decides who may write: that is the log's job. A wrong hint costs a job run twice; a wrong write decision costs a broken promise.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Reading the partition run. Every claim of Raft's safety argument shows up in the logs (R3):

  • Election safety. Node 1 is leader of term 1, node 2 of term 2: never two leaders in one term. Two leaders at once are allowed and happen (node 1 still believes it leads during the partition).
  • Commit needs a majority. Node 1 appends CAS lock ''->n1 at index 2 in term 1 and waits; its append messages are dropped by the switch ("DROPPED for n2", "DROPPED for n3"), so match_idx never reaches 2 and nothing commits. Its client reports UNKNOWN at tick 1200.
  • Leader completeness, log matching. On healing, node 1 receives term 2 appends, steps down, finds that its index 2 has term 1 where the leader's has term 2, truncates one entry, and applies index 2 (NOOP) and index 3 (CAS lock ''->n3). All three final states: lock=n3.
  • Exactly one OK. Of the two clients, only node 3 was told OK. The acceptance check counts it (R5).

D1

Why the NOOP. A new leader cannot conclude that an entry from an earlier term is committed by counting replicas: the Raft paper shows a scenario where such an entry, stored on a majority, is later overwritten. The rule is therefore to commit only entries of the current term, which commits earlier ones by the log-matching property; appending a NOOP at the start of each term makes that happen at once instead of waiting for the next client command. D1

D1 D2

The scheduler's real problem: zombie workers. The leader re-placed job 3 because node 3 left its view. In the lab node 3 had halted, so the job ran once. If node 3 had only been partitioned, it would have kept running job 3 while node 2 ran it again: at-least-once execution. For a job that counts primes that is harmless; for a job that writes to storage it is not. Two defences exist. Make jobs idempotent (write results under the job id, last writer identical). Or fence: every JOB_ASSIGN carries its log index as a fencing token, and storage rejects writes with a token lower than one it has seen (fencing token). The assigned_at_index field in sm.h's job record is there for exactly that extension. D2 D3

Timeouts and the lab's numbers. Heartbeats every 20 ticks; election timeouts 130–169 ticks for node 1, 160–199 for node 2, 190–229 for node 3; suspicion after 50 ticks in the membership layer. The per-node offset is a deliberate bias that makes the first election predictable in the lab (node 1 timed out at its tick 163 and won within one tick in both runs); production code randomises without bias. The election timeout must be well above the heartbeat period and the message delay, or followers will start elections against a healthy leader. D1

Note

Impossibility, in one sentence. No deterministic protocol can guarantee that consensus is always reached in an asynchronous system in which even one process may crash (the FLP result); Raft and Paxos keep safety always and get liveness only when timing is good enough, which is why the lab's minority client hears UNKNOWN rather than a wrong OK. D4 D5

Cluster managers in practice. Large cluster managers keep their state in a small consensus-replicated store and run scheduling as a separate service that reads and writes it, much as this lab does at toy scale; they add admission control, priorities, resource vectors instead of "load", and preemption. D3 Treat this sentence as a pointer to the Borg paper, not as a description of any system; the Source Researcher must confirm it.

How the hardware actually does it

The layer runs on the same hardware path as F5-44: three emulated PCs, a polled e1000, the polled PIT clock and the lab switch. Two hardware facts shape it. First, every node's clock is separate and counts from its own boot, so all Raft timing is local (timeouts measured on one node's clock); Raft's safety does not depend on clocks at all, only its liveness does. Second, the lab has no stable storage: the kernel has no disk driver in this course, so term, vote and log live in RAM and a restarted node would be a new node. On real machines the persistence step (write and flush to an SSD before answering a vote or an append) usually dominates the latency of a commit. D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The statement that persistence dominates commit latency on real machines is from general experience, not from a measurement in this build. MP2's performance evidence must measure it on the learner's own system (for example with an NVMe flush in the learner's OS from MP1, or fsync on a Linux virtual machine) before any number is claimed.

The partition is made by labswitch: between 6,000 and 16,000 ms on the host's clock it drops every frame between group {1} and group {2,3} and logs each kind of message dropped, rate-limited to one line per 2 s per kind (R3). Real partitions are messier: one-way loss, partial connectivity (node 2 hears node 1 but node 3 does not), and long delays rather than clean cuts. A layer that only passes clean-cut tests has not been tested.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Worked example

Question. For a cluster of n nodes cut into two groups, in how many cuts can each side decide, under the majority rule and under the lowest-id rule? What does that say about choosing n?

Step 1: count the cuts. Put node 1 in group A to count each cut once. Group A is node 1 plus any subset of the other n − 1 nodes, except all of them (then B would be empty): 2n−1 − 1 cuts. For n = 3: 3; for 4: 7; for 5: 15.

Step 2: the majority rule. A side decides if it has more than n/2 nodes. Two sides cannot both have more than half, so "both decide" is 0 for every n. For odd n one side always has a majority; for even n an equal split leaves nobody able to decide: for n = 4 the cuts {1,2}|{3,4}, {1,3}|{2,4} and {1,4}|{2,3}.

Step 3: the lowest-id rule. Every non-empty side elects its own lowest id: both sides decide in every cut.

Step 4: run it.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Friends know when the phones are down; nodes do not. Node 1 kept believing it led term 1 for the whole partition. Nothing in Raft tells a deposed leader it is deposed until it hears a higher term.
    • 

  • Crossing out a line in a notebook is visible; truncating a log entry is not. The client who sent the truncated command is not told; it only knows "outcome unknown". The truncation shows in one log line on one node.
    • 

  • Friends vote once per pizza; nodes vote once per term, and terms are only numbers. A node that comes back with a higher term can force an election although nothing was wrong (the missing pre-vote of Layer 3).
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Elections forever after a heal. Cause: a deposed leader or candidate that does not restart its timer when it becomes a follower; it times out at once and starts another term. Found while building the lab; fixed in become_follower (line 48).
    • 

  • Two leaders in the same term. Cause: votes not reset on a new term, or granted to two candidates. Find it: grep "LEADER for term" across all node logs; each term must appear at most once.
    • 

  • Committed entries lost. Cause: committing an earlier term's entry by counting replicas. Find it: a scenario test that crashes leaders in sequence. Fix: the current-term condition (line 197) and the NOOP.
    • 

  • Jobs start twice. Cause: starting on proposal rather than on apply, or re-placing a job from a stale view while the original worker is alive. Fix: start on apply; fencing tokens or idempotent jobs.
    • 

  • Client says "failed" and retries. Cause: treating a timeout as failure. Fix: report UNKNOWN; make retries idempotent with client sessions.
    • 

  • The test passes because the race never happened. Fault-injection runs must check that the fault occurred (the forensic step's log says "the race did not happen this time" and fails the run if so).
    •

Summary


    

  • Views are opinions; only a majority can decide, because two majorities always share a node, and a node votes once per term.
    • 

  • Raft: randomised election timeouts, one vote per term for an up-to-date log, append with a consistency check, commit by majority from the current term, apply in order.
    • 

  • Under the lab's partition the minority leader kept its title but could not commit; its client heard UNKNOWN, the majority committed, and the minority's uncommitted entry was truncated on healing.
    • 

  • A scheduler whose decisions are log entries keeps every node in agreement on each job; re-placing on view changes gives at-least-once execution, so jobs need idempotence or fencing.
    • 

  • The lowest-id leader rule lets both sides decide in every cut; in the forensic run two clients were told OK and one acknowledged write was erased.
    • 

  • The lab's Raft lacks persistence, membership changes, snapshots, client sessions and linearizable reads; MP2 adds them with deterministic fault-injection tests.
    •
