Story hook

Analogy

Priya's arms are the "motors" that hold her tray up. When the tray is light she barely notices the effort. When the tray is full she can hold it, but she tires much faster than the extra glasses alone would suggest. And when the head waiter suddenly hands her a second full jug, the tray dips for a moment before her arms catch up: muscles need a moment to push harder.

A drone's motors and propellers behave in the same three ways. More thrust costs much more than proportionally more power. A motor cannot change its speed instantly. And the relation between "how hard I command" and "how much thrust I get" is not a straight line.

So far the simulator has said simply "thrust = kT ω², with a 30 ms lag". This chapter opens that box: a motor model with voltage, current and back-EMF, a propeller model with thrust and drag torque, the time the rotor needs to change speed, and how to measure kT from a thrust-stand table without fooling yourself.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the model of a motor driving a propeller: V = R i + Ke ω, τ = Kt i, Jr dω/dt = Kt i − kQ ω², T = kT ω².
  • Compute the steady speed for a voltage, the voltage and current for hover, and the electrical power.
  • Derive the motor time constant near an operating point and check it against a simulated step.
  • Fit kT to a thrust-stand table by least squares and judge the fit by its residuals.
  • Diagnose a simulator built on the wrong form of thrust model.
Jargon box
Back-EMF
Simple words: the voltage a spinning motor makes by itself, which pushes against the supply. Analogy: the faster Priya's arms already push, the less extra effort a new push adds. Precise: the electromotive force induced in the windings by rotation, e = Ke ω, opposing the applied voltage (same definition as HW302 F1-69). U1B1
Motor constants (Kt and Ke)
Simple words: push per amp, and voltage per unit of speed. Analogy: how strong Priya's arms are per unit of effort. Precise: torque constant Kt (N·m per A) and back-EMF constant Ke (V per rad/s); in consistent SI units they are numerically equal for an ideal motor (HW302 F1-69). U1
Thrust coefficient kT and torque coefficient kQ
Simple words: two numbers that say how much push and how much drag-twist a propeller gives at a given speed. Analogy: how much the tray rises, and how much Priya's wrist is twisted, per effort. Precise: the constants of the static propeller model T = kT ω² (N) and Q = kQ ω² (N·m), valid for a given propeller in still air; found by measurement. B1D1
Operating point and linearisation
Simple words: a steady condition, and a straight-line description of small changes around it. Analogy: how Priya's arms respond to one more glass when the tray is already full. Precise: an equilibrium (here a steady rotor speed ω0); replacing the non-linear model by its first-order Taylor expansion around ω0 gives a first-order linear system with time constant τ (RB202, MA301). D1U1
Motor time constant
Simple words: how long the rotor takes to cover most of a change in speed. Analogy: the moment the tray dips before Priya's arms catch up. Precise: for small steps around ω0, τ = Jr / (KtKe/R + 2 kQ ω0); after τ the speed has covered 63.2 % of the step. D1
Thrust stand
Simple words: a rig that holds a motor and propeller still and measures the push and the speed. Analogy: weighing how hard Priya can push up on a bathroom scale. Precise: a fixture with a load cell (and often a torque sensor) that records thrust, torque, speed, voltage and current at a series of commands, from which kT, kQ and efficiency are fitted. B1
Least-squares fit and residual
Simple words: choosing a model's number so that it misses the measurements as little as possible, and the misses themselves. Analogy: drawing the best smooth line through bumpy marks. Precise: the parameter that minimises the sum of squared differences between data and model; for T = k ω², k = Σ Tj ωj² / Σ ωj⁴; residual = measured − model. D1U1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the model of one motor with its propeller. Voltage pushes current through the windings against the back-EMF; current makes torque; torque minus the propeller's drag torque speeds the rotor up; the speed makes thrust. The speed feeds back twice, through the back-EMF and through the drag torque, and both feedbacks slow the rotor down until it settles.

Figure 2 is the table a thrust stand gives, here made by Listing 2 from the model plus small errors, together with the fitted curve T = kT ω². The curve bends upwards: doubling the speed gives four times the thrust.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A drone motor is an electric motor that spins a propeller. The battery pushes electricity through the motor's coils, and that makes the motor turn. A spinning motor also makes its own small voltage that pushes back against the battery. The faster it spins, the stronger the push-back. That is why a motor settles at a speed instead of spinning faster forever.

The propeller pushes air down and makes thrust. Spin it twice as fast and it pushes four times as hard. But it also gets much harder to turn, because the air drags on it. So holding a lot of thrust costs a lot of battery.

Engineers measure all this on a thrust stand: a rig that holds the motor still and measures how hard it pushes at each speed. Then they find the numbers that make the model match the measurements.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The motor

Multirotors use brushless DC motors driven by ESCs (HW302 F1-69, F1-70). For flight dynamics, a common simplification treats the motor and its ESC as an ideal DC motor fed with an average voltage V. Two equations describe it: the windings, V = R i + Ke ω, where R is the winding resistance and Ke ω the back-EMF; and the torque, τm = Kt i. In SI units Kt = Ke for an ideal motor. The course quad uses R = 0.1 Ω and Kt = Ke = 0.01 (exercise values). U1B1

The propeller

A propeller in still air is modelled by two quadratic laws: thrust T = kT ω² and drag torque Q = kQ ω², with kT = 1.0 × 10−5 N/(rad/s)² and kQ = 1.5 × 10−7 N·m/(rad/s)² for the course quad. These are the coefficients the simulator and the mixer of F10-02 already use. B1D1

The rotor equation and its steady state

The rotor and propeller together have inertia Jr = 2 × 10−5 kg·m². Torque from the motor minus drag torque from the air changes their speed: D1

Jr dω/dt = Kt (V − Ke ω) / R − kQ ω²

In the steady state the right side is zero, a quadratic in ω: kQ R ω² + KtKe ω − Kt V = 0. Its positive root is the steady speed (Listing 1, steadySpeed). Going the other way, the hover speed of 495.2 rad/s needs V = (kQ R ω² + KtKe ω)/Kt = 5.320 V and a current of kQ ω²/Kt = 3.679 A per motor (runs R1, R4). R1R4

Power: why thrust is expensive

The mechanical power the propeller absorbs is Q ω = kQ ω³. Since T = kT ω², power grows as T3/2: doubling the thrust multiplies the speed by √2 = 1.414 and the mechanical power by 23/2 = 2.828. In this model the steady current is kQ ω²/Kt, so the thrust per watt of electrical power is kT Kt / (kQ V) = 0.6667/V: the harder the motor is driven, the less thrust each watt buys. Listing 1's last column shows exactly 0.3333 N/W at 2 V and 0.0556 N/W at 12 V. D1R1R4

How fast the rotor responds

Around an operating point ω0, a small change δω obeys Jr dδω/dt = −(KtKe/R + 2 kQ ω0) δω + (Kt/R) δV. That is a first-order system like RB202's motor (F9-16), with time constant D1U1

τ = Jr / (KtKe/R + 2 kQ ω0)

At hover τ = 17.41 ms. Listing 1 checks it by simulating a +1 V step from hover with the full non-linear model: the speed covers 63.2 % of the change in 17.14 ms. The two differ slightly because a 1 V step is not "small": the drag term grows during the step and makes the rotor settle a little faster. Note that both feedbacks shorten τ: without the propeller's drag it would be JrR/(KtKe) = 20.0 ms. R1R4

Note

The simulator of F10-04 uses a motor lag of 30 ms, an exercise value set before this chapter's model existed. This chapter's model gives 17.41 ms at hover for the same rotor. The lab asks you to replace the fixed lag with the full rotor equation, or at least with the derived τ, and to see what changes.

Measuring kT: a least-squares fit

A thrust stand gives a table of speeds ωj and thrusts Tj. To find kT, choose the k that makes the sum of squared misses Σ (Tj − k ωj²)² smallest. Setting its derivative with respect to k to zero gives one line: D1

k = Σ Tj ωj² / Σ ωj⁴

Listing 2 makes a table from kT = 1.0 × 10−5 plus small pseudo-random errors (at most 0.05 N, fixed seed), fits it and gets 1.0035 × 10−5: 0.35 % off, because of the errors. The residuals (measured − model) are small and change sign without a pattern: 0.025, 0.014, 0.017, −0.044, −0.001 … That lack of pattern is what tells you the model's form is right; the forensic lab shows what a wrong form looks like. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What the ideal DC model hides. A brushless motor is a three-phase machine; the ESC switches its phases electronically, and the "voltage" of the model is an average set by the ESC's switching (HW302 F1-70). The model leaves out the winding inductance (fast, electrical time constant), iron and friction losses, the ESC's own losses and current limits, and the drop of the battery voltage under load. It is still the standard first model for flight dynamics because it captures the two things that matter: steady speed rises with voltage, and the rotor needs time to change speed. B1U1

Propellers in moving air. T = kT ω² is a static model: it assumes still air. A propeller moving through air — in climb, descent or forward flight — sees a different inflow, and its thrust and torque change. Beard and McLain give propeller models that include the airspeed; DN201 keeps the static form. B1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The dimensionless forms of the propeller laws (thrust and power coefficients scaled with air density, rotational speed and diameter), the way they change with airspeed, and the definitions used in manufacturers' propeller data were not checked in this build. Check the propulsion chapter of Beard and McLain (B1) and the data sheet of the actual propeller before converting any published propeller data into kT and kQ.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Hobby motor data sheets often give a "Kv" rating. If, as is often assumed, it means the no-load speed in revolutions per minute per volt, then Ke = 60 / (2π Kv) in V per rad/s, and the course motor's Ke = 0.01 would correspond to about 955 rpm per volt (run R4). Whether a given data sheet uses exactly this definition, and how close real motors come to Kt = Ke, must be checked in the data sheet of the named motor (AH-21) and by measurement (DN202).

Fitting honestly. A fit always returns numbers; only the residuals tell you whether the model deserves them. Look for patterns (all positive at the ends and negative in the middle means a missing curvature), compare the residual size with the measurement error you expect, and never extrapolate far outside the measured range. MA202 treats measurement noise; F10-05's forensic lab is a fit gone wrong. D1U1

How the hardware actually does it

The flight controller sends each ESC a command (a pulse width or a digital value, DN202 F10-10). The ESC turns it into a switching pattern for the motor's three phases, which sets an average voltage or, in some ESCs, a speed loop of its own. The rotor speed then follows the dynamics of this chapter. Whether the command maps to voltage, to duty cycle or to speed depends on the ESC and its firmware; this is one reason why the same airframe can behave differently with different ESCs. U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Which ESC protocols report the motor speed back to the flight controller, and in what units, was not checked in this build. Check the ESC and motor-protocol notes in the PX4 and ArduPilot documentation (registry 4.8) and the ESC's own manual (DN202 F10-10).

Safety

A thrust stand runs a motor with its propeller fitted: this is not part of DN201. When you meet it in DN202, it is done only with the stand clamped down, a guard around the propeller, eye protection, nobody in line with the propeller disc, a current-limited supply or a battery handled by the faculty's LiPo rules, and adult supervision for learners below L3.

Worked example

Problem. Find, for one motor of the course quad at hover: the voltage, the current, the electrical power, the mechanical power and the copper loss.

  1. Speed. ω0 = √(2.4525 / 10−5) = 495.2 rad/s (F10-01).
  2. Current. In the steady state Kt i = kQ ω²: i = 1.5 × 10−7 × 495.2² / 0.01 = 3.679 A.
  3. Voltage. V = R i + Ke ω = 0.368 + 4.952 = 5.320 V.
  4. Power. Electrical V i = 19.57 W per motor (78.3 W for four). Mechanical Q ω = kQ ω³ = 18.22 W. Copper loss i² R = 1.353 W. In this ideal model the difference is exactly the copper loss, and 93.1 % of the electrical power reaches the propeller.
  5. Check. Run R4 prints every value; Listing 1 prints the hover voltage.

A real motor and ESC lose more (switching, iron and friction losses), so a real hover needs more electrical power than this model says; DN202 measures it. D1R4B1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Priya's arms get tired: their strength changes during the evening. The motor model has constant R, Kt and Ke; real windings warm up and their resistance changes, and the battery voltage falls as it discharges, neither of which the model includes. B1
    • 

  • Priya feels the tray directly. The flight controller usually knows only its command, not the actual speed or thrust; the model of this chapter is how it (and the simulator) predicts them. U1
    • 

  • Arms push against a tray; a propeller pushes against air that moves. The thrust model assumes still air, and changes when the air through the rotor moves (climb, wind, forward flight). B1
    •

Method hints


A good model leaves residuals that look like noise: small, both signs, no shape. Read the residual column as a curve. Then test the model against something it must get right, such as zero thrust at zero speed.

Summary


    

  • Motor: V = R i + Ke ω and τ = Kt i; propeller: T = kT ω² and Q = kQ ω² in still air.
    • 

  • Rotor: Jr dω/dt = Kt(V − Keω)/R − kQω²; the steady speed solves a quadratic.
    • 

  • The course motor hovers at 5.320 V and 3.679 A; mechanical power grows as T3/2, so thrust per watt falls as the motor works harder.
    • 

  • Near an operating point the rotor is a first-order system with τ = Jr/(KtKe/R + 2kQω0), 17.41 ms at hover.
    • 

  • Fit kT by least squares, k = ΣTω²/Σω⁴, and judge the fit by its residuals, not by the number it returns.
