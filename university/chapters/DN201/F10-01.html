Story hook

Analogy

Amara is helping at a family party. She carries a tray of full glasses from the kitchen to the garden. Standing still, she holds the tray at chest height. Her arms push up exactly as hard as the glasses pull down, and the tray neither rises nor sinks. When her aunt adds two more glasses, Amara feels the tray sink a little until she pushes harder.

Then she starts to walk. To get the tray moving forward, she leans her push forward a little, and to stop she leans it back. Outside, a breeze pushes against the tray, and she has to push into the wind to keep going. Nobody taught her equations for this. Her arms simply balance "up" against "down" and "forward" against "back".

A multirotor drone does the same job with nothing to hold on to. Its propellers push air down, and the air pushes the drone up. This chapter names the four forces in Amara's story — thrust, weight, drag and, for wings, lift — writes them as vectors, and puts them into the first and simplest simulator of the course: a drone that can only go up and down.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain thrust, weight, drag and lift, and how a propeller or a wing makes a force by pushing air.
  • Write Newton's second law for a multirotor moving up and down, and derive the hover thrust condition T = m g, including the tilted case T = m g / cos a.
  • Calculate the thrust-to-weight ratio, the hover thrust per rotor and the steepest tilt that still keeps the height.
  • Simulate vertical flight in C++ and check the simulator against a value you derived (the terminal climb speed).
  • Diagnose from a flight log why a vehicle hovers below its height setpoint after a mass change.
Jargon box
Force
Simple words: a push or a pull, with a size and a direction. Analogy: Amara's arms pushing the tray. Precise: a vector quantity that changes the motion of a body; measured in newtons (N), where 1 N accelerates 1 kg by 1 m/s². Forces on one body add as vectors (MA201 F0-47). B1U1
Newton's second law
Simple words: the total push on a thing decides how quickly its speed changes. Analogy: push the tray harder than the glasses weigh and it speeds up upwards. Precise: for a body of constant mass m, the vector sum of the forces equals m times the acceleration: ΣF = m a. B1
Weight
Simple words: the pull of the Earth on the vehicle. Analogy: the glasses pulling the tray down. Precise: the gravitational force m g, pointing down; this course uses g = 9.81 m/s². B1V1
Thrust (propeller force)
Simple words: the push a spinning propeller makes by throwing air backwards (for a drone: downwards). Analogy: Amara's arms pushing the tray up. Precise: the force a rotor exerts on the vehicle along its spin axis, the reaction to the momentum the rotor gives to the air; on a multirotor each rotor's thrust points along the body's up axis. Not the C++ library of the same name (CU401). B1
Drag
Simple words: the push of the air against anything that moves through it. Analogy: the breeze pushing against the tray. Precise: the aerodynamic force component opposite to the velocity relative to the air; this course models the body's drag as k v|v| with an exercise value k. B1D1
Lift
Simple words: the upward push a wing gets from moving through the air. Analogy: none in the tray story: Amara's tray has no wings (F10-06 adds one). Precise: the aerodynamic force component perpendicular to the oncoming air; for a wing L = ½ ρ V² S CL (F10-06). B1
Hover thrust condition
Simple words: the total thrust needed to stay at the same height. Analogy: pushing up exactly as hard as the glasses weigh. Precise: with no vertical acceleration and no vertical drag, the vertical part of the thrust equals the weight: T cos a = m g for a vehicle tilted by a (T = m g when level). D1
Thrust-to-weight ratio
Simple words: how many times the vehicle's weight its rotors can push at most. Analogy: how much more Amara could lift than the full tray weighs. Precise: Tmax / (m g); below 1 the vehicle cannot hover; the margin above 1 is what is left for climbing, tilting and control. D1
Terminal climb speed
Simple words: the top speed at which a vehicle climbs with a fixed extra thrust, because drag grows until it uses all of the extra. Analogy: walking into the wind: the faster you go, the harder the wind pushes back. Precise: the speed at which T − m g − k v² = 0, so v = √((T − m g)/k) in this chapter's model. D1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the same multirotor three times from the side. Hovering, two forces balance: thrust up, weight down. Climbing, thrust must also beat drag, which acts against the motion. Tilted, the thrust leans with the body; only its upward part holds the weight, and its sideways part pushes the vehicle sideways. That last picture is how every multirotor moves around, and F10-02 explains how it tilts.

Figure 2 is drawn from the real output of Listing 2, the vertical simulator. Thrust below the weight does nothing on the ground; thrust equal to the weight still does nothing; only thrust above the weight lifts off, and then drag slowly eats the extra push until the speed levels off.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Anything that flies has to push something down to be pushed up. A bird pushes air down with its wings. A drone pushes air down with its propellers. The air pushes back up just as hard. That push is called thrust.

The Earth pulls the drone down. That pull is its weight. If the thrust is as big as the weight, the drone stays where it is: it hovers. More thrust and it rises; less and it sinks. Air also pushes against anything that moves through it. That push is drag, and it always works against the motion.

A drone's propellers can only push "up" as the drone sees it. So to fly forwards, the drone tips forwards a little. Then part of the push goes forwards and part still goes up. If it tips too far, the "up" part is too small, and it sinks unless the propellers spin faster.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Forces are vectors, and they add

From MA201 (F0-47) you know vectors: arrows with a length and a direction, written as lists of numbers. A force is a vector. Several forces on the same body add up to one total force, and Newton's second law says that this total decides the acceleration: ΣF = m a. In this course the world frame has x forward, y to the left and z up, like the frames of MA201 (F0-53). B1U1

Three forces act on a multirotor in still air. The weight is (0, 0, −m g). The thrust of the rotors points along the body's up axis; when the body is level, that is (0, 0, T), where T is the sum of the four rotors' thrusts. The drag on the body points against the velocity. Wings add a fourth force, lift, which is the subject of F10-06. B1

Where thrust comes from

A propeller blade is a small spinning wing. As it turns, it pushes air down through the rotor disc, giving that air downward momentum every second. By Newton's third law the air pushes the rotor up with an equal and opposite force: that is thrust. Turning faster moves more air, faster, so thrust grows with rotor speed. F10-05 gives the model this course uses, Ti = kT ωi², and shows how to measure kT. In this chapter you only need the total T and its upper limit. B1U1

Note

The course quad. All numbers in DN201 describe one simulated vehicle, "the course quad": mass 1.0 kg, four rotors, each able to push at most 10 N. These are exercise values chosen for round arithmetic, not the data of any real product. A real vehicle's numbers come from its datasheets and from measurements (DN202).

Moving only up and down

Start with the simplest case: the vehicle stays level and moves only along z. With v the vertical speed (up positive), the three forces are T up, m g down, and drag k v|v| against the motion. Newton's second law along z gives D1

m dv/dt = T − m g − k v|v|

The product v|v| is v² with the sign of v, so drag always opposes the motion. Three results follow by algebra alone: D1

  • Hover. Staying still (v = 0, dv/dt = 0) needs T = m g. For the course quad that is 9.81 N in total, 2.4525 N per rotor. This is the hover thrust condition. R4
  • Climbing starts. With T = 1.2 m g and v = 0, the acceleration is 0.2 g = 1.962 m/s². Figure 2's first climbing point shows exactly that value (run R2). R2
  • Terminal climb speed. As v grows, drag grows, until T − m g − k v² = 0. Then v = √((T − m g)/k). For T = 1.2 m g and k = 0.05 N per (m/s)², v = 6.264 m/s; the simulation is at 5.979 m/s after 6 s and still creeping up. R2D1

The thrust-to-weight ratio Tmax/(m g) summarises how much margin a vehicle has. The course quad has 40 N for 9.81 N of weight: 4.08. Add mass and the margin shrinks; at 4.5 kg it falls below 1 and the vehicle cannot hover at all (Listing 1). R1

Tilting: the hover thrust condition, part two

Now tilt the vehicle by an angle a and keep the thrust along the body's up axis (Figure 1, right). The thrust vector has an upward part T cos a and a sideways part T sin a. To keep the height, the upward part must still equal the weight: D1

T cos a = m g  ⇒  T = m g / cos a,    sideways acceleration = T sin a / m = g tan a

Tilting costs thrust. At 30° the course quad needs 11.328 N instead of 9.81 N and accelerates sideways at 5.664 m/s². The steepest tilt that still keeps the height is where m g / cos a reaches Tmax: cos a = 9.81/40, a = 75.8°. Listing 1 shows 80° asking for more than the rotors can give. F10-03 extends this to roll and pitch together: T = m g / (cos φ cos θ). R1R4

Lift in one paragraph

A wing makes force from its own motion through the air instead of from spinning blades. Its lift grows with the square of the airspeed, L = ½ ρ V² S CL, where ρ is the air density, S the wing area and CL a number that depends on the wing's angle to the air. A wing therefore needs speed to fly; a rotor makes its own airspeed by spinning, which is why a multirotor can hover. F10-06 works through the wing case. B1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What the simple model leaves out. The model T − m g − k v|v| treats thrust as something the controller chooses and drag as one number. Real rotors behave less simply: the thrust a rotor makes at a given speed depends on the air flowing through it, which changes when the vehicle climbs, descends or flies forward, and near the ground or a wall. Beard and McLain develop propeller and aerodynamic models in more detail. DN201 keeps the simple model on purpose, so that every number in the simulator can be checked by hand, and states the limits openly. B1D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

How much a real rotor's thrust changes in a climb, in a fast descent, in forward flight and close to the ground, and the drag coefficient of a real airframe, are not given here: no source was opened during this build. Check the propulsion and aerodynamics chapters of Beard and McLain (B1, sections to be named in the dossier), and measure your own vehicle (DN202 bench labs) before using any number.

Mass, not weight, resists acceleration. Newton's law has m on both sides of the hover condition: weight is m g, and the inertia that resists acceleration is m. That is why adding a camera both raises the hover thrust and makes the vehicle slower to respond to the same extra thrust. B1D1

Units and numerics. The simulator uses SI units everywhere (kg, m, s, N) and a time step of 1 ms with the semi-implicit Euler rule of MA302 (F0-76): first update the speed from the acceleration, then the height from the new speed. The terminal speed is a good test of the integration: halving the step must not change it, because it does not depend on the step at all. U1R2

The ground is a constraint, not a force you choose. Lines 21–24 of Listing 2 stop the vehicle from falling through the ground: while it sits there, the ground pushes up just enough to cancel the net downward force. This is why thrust below the weight shows a = 0 in the first second of Figure 2, not a negative acceleration. R2

How the hardware actually does it

A flight controller cannot measure thrust. It commands each motor through an electronic speed controller (ESC, HW302 F1-70, DN202), which sets the motor's speed or power; the thrust follows from the propeller (F10-05). The controller then judges the result from its sensors: the accelerometers of the IMU (HW302 F1-65) and, for height, a barometer or other height sensor (DN202). That is why the hover thrust is a number the controller must learn or be told, as this chapter's forensic lab shows. U1

An accelerometer does not measure acceleration alone: it measures the specific force, the non-gravitational force per unit mass. In a hover it reads about +g along the body's up axis (the thrust per kilogram), and in free fall it reads zero. DN301 builds attitude estimation on this fact. B1U1

Worked example

Problem. A 0.25 kg camera is fitted to the course quad (new mass 1.25 kg). Find (a) the hover thrust per rotor, (b) the rotor speed for it with kT = 1.0 × 10−5 N per (rad/s)² (F10-05), (c) the thrust-to-weight ratio, and (d) the steepest tilt that still keeps the height.

  1. Hover thrust. Weight m g = 1.25 × 9.81 = 12.2625 N; per rotor 12.2625 / 4 = 3.0656 N.
  2. Rotor speed. ω = √(3.0656 / 10−5) = 553.7 rad/s, against 495.2 rad/s without the camera.
  3. Thrust-to-weight. 40 / 12.2625 = 3.262 (it was 4.077).
  4. Steepest tilt. cos a = 12.2625 / 40 = 0.3066, so a = 72.1° (it was 75.8°).
  5. Check. Run R4 (checks.cpp) prints all four values; they were not typed by hand.

The camera costs margin everywhere: more speed to hover, less left for climbing and tilting. R4D1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Amara's hands touch the tray and can pull as well as push. A rotor only pushes air one way: its thrust on the vehicle is never negative, and it always points along the body's up axis. A multirotor cannot "push itself down" faster than gravity pulls; it can only reduce thrust. B1
    • 

  • Amara feels the weight of the tray directly. A flight controller feels no weight: its accelerometer reads the thrust per kilogram, not gravity, and reads zero in free fall. It must infer "down" and the hover thrust from sensors and models (DN301). B1
    • 

  • Amara can move the tray forward with her arms while it stays level. A multirotor has no sideways push at all: it must tilt its whole body, and while it tilts, the upward part of the thrust shrinks unless the rotors speed up. D1
    •

Expected observations


The formula gives 4.429 m/s for 1.1 and 9.905 m/s for 1.5 (run R4); with 1.1 the speed at 8 s is still only 3.849 m/s in this build's scratch run, so the climb must be lengthened. With the 40 N clamp and ratio 5.0, the thrust is limited to 40 N, so the first climbing acceleration is (40 − 9.81)/1.0 = 30.19 m/s² instead of 4 g. Halving dt changes a few printed values in the last decimal (for example 3.481 becomes 3.480 m/s at 4 s); the terminal speed does not change. These are values from the formulas, run R4 and scratch runs of the modified program during this build, not published listings; your own run is the check.

Summary


    

  • Thrust, weight, drag and lift are forces: vectors that add, and their sum equals m a.
    • 

  • Rotors make thrust by pushing air down; on a multirotor the thrust always points along the body's up axis and is never negative.
    • 

  • Hover thrust condition: T = m g when level, T = m g / cos a when tilted by a; the sideways acceleration is then g tan a.
    • 

  • The thrust-to-weight ratio is the margin for climbing, tilting and control; payload eats it.
    • 

  • With quadratic drag, a constant extra thrust gives a terminal climb speed √((T − m g)/k).
    • 

  • The vertical simulator of Listing 2 reproduces these formulas; it is the first step towards the 3D simulator of F10-04.
