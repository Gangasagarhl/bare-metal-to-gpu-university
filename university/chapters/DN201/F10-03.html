Story hook

Analogy

Mei is on the phone with her cousin Ravi, who wants to take a photo of a tray of drinks from exactly the same angle as Mei did. "Hold the tray level," she says. "Turn yourself to face the window. Now tip the front of the tray down a little. Then tip it to the right a bit." Ravi does exactly that, and the photo matches.

The next day Ravi tries again from memory, but in another order: first he tips the tray to the right, then tips the front down, then turns to the window. The tray ends up at a slightly different tilt. Same three amounts, different order, different result.

Three numbers in a fixed order are how most people — and most ground-station screens — describe which way a drone is tilted and turned: yaw, pitch and roll. They are easy to read and a trap to compute with. This chapter defines them exactly, shows how they connect to what a gyroscope measures, and shows the one place where they stop working.

What you will be able to do

  • Define roll, pitch and yaw in DN201's axes and build the rotation matrix R = Rz(ψ) Ry(θ) Rx(φ) from them.
  • Compute the direction of the thrust in the world from the attitude, and the hover thrust T = m g / (cos φ cos θ).
  • Convert body rates (what a gyroscope measures) into Euler-angle rates, and explain why the conversion fails at pitch ±90°.
  • Choose a representation (Euler angles, rotation matrix, quaternion) for display, for computation and for a simulator.
  • Diagnose a ground-station display whose roll and yaw "jump" during a loop.
Jargon box
Attitude
Simple words: which way a drone or robot is tilted and turned, ignoring where it is. Analogy: how the tray is tipped and turned, not where in the room it is. Precise: the orientation of the body frame relative to the world frame, RWB (MA201 F0-53), represented by a rotation matrix, a unit quaternion or a set of angles. (Same definition as MA201 F0-55.) B1U1
Euler angles (yaw–pitch–roll)
Simple words: three angles — turn left-right, tip nose up-down, roll side to side — that describe an attitude. Analogy: Mei's "turn to the window, tip the front, tip to the right". Precise: in DN201, as in MA201 F0-55, R = Rz(yaw ψ) Ry(pitch θ) Rx(roll φ); other orders and axis choices exist and must be stated. B1U1
Body rates (p, q, r)
Simple words: how fast the drone is turning about its own three axes, right now. Analogy: how fast Mei's wrist is twisting, felt in her own hand. Precise: the components of the angular velocity vector ω in the body frame: p about body x, q about body y, r about body z, in rad/s; a three-axis gyroscope measures them (plus errors). B1
Euler-angle rates
Simple words: how fast the three angles on the screen are changing. Analogy: how fast Mei's three instructions would have to change to follow her wrist. Precise: the time derivatives (φ̇, θ̇, ψ̇); they equal a matrix W(φ, θ) times the body rates (p, q, r), and W contains tan θ and 1/cos θ. B1D1
Gimbal lock
Simple words: at nose straight up or straight down, the three angles stop describing the attitude uniquely. Analogy: with the tray held vertically, "turn" and "roll" become the same movement. Precise: for yaw–pitch–roll at pitch ±90°, the matrix depends only on yaw ∓ roll; the angles cannot be recovered uniquely and small attitude changes can need large angle changes (same definition as MA201). U1B1
Small-angle approximation
Simple words: for small tilts, the angle rates are about the same as the body rates. Analogy: while the tray is nearly level, twisting your wrist changes the tilt by the same amount. Precise: for |φ|, |θ| ≪ 1 rad, W(φ, θ) ≈ I, so (φ̇, θ̇, ψ̇) ≈ (p, q, r), and sin a ≈ a, cos a ≈ 1; the basis of the simple attitude controllers of DN301. B1D1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 fixes the signs used everywhere in DN201. With x forward, y left and z up, the right-hand rule makes positive roll lower the right side, positive pitch lower the nose, and positive yaw turn the nose left. The positive-pitch sign surprises many people: in a "z up" frame, nose down is positive. Beard and McLain use x forward, y right, z down, where positive pitch lifts the nose; both are correct once stated.

Figure 2 is the forensic lab's evidence drawn as a graph: a vehicle doing a loop, nose up first, turning about one axis only, while the displayed roll jumps by 180°. Keep it in mind while you read Layer 2; the explanation is gimbal lock.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

To say which way a drone faces and tips, people use three angles. Yaw says which way the nose points, like a compass direction. Pitch says how far the nose is tipped down or up. Roll says how far it is tipped to one side.

The order matters. "Turn, then tip forward, then tip sideways" ends somewhere different from "tip sideways, then tip forward, then turn". So every drone program writes down which order it uses, and sticks to it.

The angles are made for people to read. Inside, the drone's computer keeps the attitude in a different form, because the three angles have a weak spot: when the nose points straight up or straight down, "turn" and "roll" become the same movement, and the numbers can jump without the drone moving strangely at all.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From three angles to one rotation matrix

MA201 (F0-52, F0-55) built rotations about single axes. The Euler angles of DN201 combine them in the order yaw ψ, pitch θ, roll φ: U1B1

R = Rz(ψ) Ry(θ) Rx(φ)

R turns a vector written in body coordinates into the same vector in world coordinates. Its columns are the body's x, y and z axes seen from the world (MA201 F0-52). Listing 1 builds R for φ = 10°, θ = −20°, ψ = 30° and gets the angles back with the inverse formulas θ = asin(−R31), φ = atan2(R32, R33), ψ = atan2(R21, R11). The round trip is exact in run R1. R1D1

Where the thrust points

The thrust of every rotor is along body z (F10-02). In the world, body z is the third column of R: D1

R e3 = ( cos φ sin θ cos ψ + sin φ sin ψ,  cos φ sin θ sin ψ − sin φ cos ψ,  cos φ cos θ )

The vertical part is cos φ cos θ, whatever the yaw. So the hover thrust condition of F10-01 becomes D1

T cos φ cos θ = m g  ⇒  T = m g / (cos φ cos θ)

For φ = 10°, θ = −20°, cos φ cos θ = 0.92542 and T = 10.6006 N for the course quad (runs R1, R4). F10-04's simulator test T3 holds exactly this attitude with exactly this thrust and checks that the vertical speed stays zero. R1R4

The horizontal part decides where the vehicle accelerates. With roll zero, the thrust direction is (sin θ cos ψ, sin θ sin ψ, cos θ): the vehicle accelerates in the direction its nose points, and yaw only rotates that direction. That is why a multirotor can fly forward in any compass direction: yaw chooses the direction, pitch the amount. D1R4

What the gyroscope measures

A three-axis gyroscope fixed to the body measures the body rates p, q, r: the angular velocity about the body's own axes (HW302 F1-65). They are not the rates of change of the displayed angles. Because yaw is applied first and roll last, a turn about body z changes yaw, pitch and roll together once the vehicle is tilted. The relation, derived from R = RzRyRx, is B1D1

φ̇ = p + sin φ tan θ · q + cos φ tan θ · r
θ̇ = cos φ · q − sin φ · r
ψ̇ = (sin φ · q + cos φ · r) / cos θ

Listing 1 does not trust this formula: it turns the attitude by a tiny step with the body rates (0.3, −0.2, 0.5) rad/s, recomputes the angles, and differences them. Formula and difference agree to six decimals: 0.133420, −0.283786 and 0.487047 rad/s (run R1). R1

Gimbal lock

Two of the three formulas divide by cos θ (tan θ = sin θ / cos θ). At θ = ±90° they divide by zero; near it, tiny body rates give huge angle rates. Listing 1 prints the factor 1/cos θ: 5.76 at 80°, 57.30 at 89°, 572.96 at 89.9°. At exactly ±90° the matrix R depends only on ψ ∓ φ (MA201's gimbal lock), so roll and yaw cannot be told apart, and the inverse formulas pick one answer. In Figure 2, that answer flips from (roll 0, yaw 0) to (roll 180°, yaw 180°) as the pitch passes −90°: it is the same attitude written differently, because pitch is only defined between −90° and +90°. Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Small angles. Near hover, φ and θ are small, tan θ ≈ 0 and cos θ ≈ 1, so (φ̇, θ̇, ψ̇) ≈ (p, q, r). Many simple attitude controllers rely on this small-angle approximation, controlling each Euler angle with the matching body torque, as the controller in this course's forensic programs does. It is accurate when tilts stay small and wrong for aerobatics; DN301 shows controllers that work on the rotation directly. B1D1

Conventions are a frequent source of bugs. Three choices must agree between every piece of software that shares an attitude: the axes (x forward, y left, z up here; x forward, y right, z down in Beard and McLain), the order of the rotations, and whether R maps body to world or world to body. A mismatch in any one of them gives attitudes that look plausible but are wrong. Write all three at the top of every file that touches attitude, as quadsim.hpp and Listing 1 do. B1D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Which axes, rotation order and quaternion convention PX4, ArduPilot and MAVLink use for attitude in their messages and logs was not checked in this build. Check the PX4 Autopilot User Guide, the ArduPilot documentation and the MAVLink Developer Guide (registry 4.8) for the releases recorded in the DN302/DN303 dossiers before converting their attitudes into DN201's axes.

Angle wrapping. Yaw jumps from +180° to −180° as the nose passes south in a "yaw between −180° and +180°" display. A yaw controller that subtracts angles must wrap the difference into (−180°, 180°], or it will turn the long way round. The forensic log shows ±180° as both signs for the same direction. R3D1

How the hardware actually does it

No sensor measures attitude directly. The gyroscopes of the IMU measure body rates; the accelerometers measure specific force, which points along "up" only when the vehicle is not accelerating; a magnetometer gives a rough heading (HW302 F1-65; DN202 F10-08). An estimator running on the flight controller combines them into an attitude, usually kept as a quaternion, many times a second (DN301 F10-13 to F10-15). The angles you see on a ground station are converted from that estimate for display. U1B1

The IMU's axes are those of its chip, which may be mounted turned relative to the vehicle's body axes. The flight controller applies a fixed mounting rotation before anything else; getting it wrong is equivalent to a wrong convention (DN202 F10-09). U1

Worked example

Problem. The course quad must accelerate forward at 2 m/s² while holding its height, with roll zero. Find the pitch angle and the thrust. Then the vehicle turns to yaw 90° and repeats the manoeuvre: in which world direction does it accelerate?

  1. Thrust direction. With φ = 0, the horizontal part of the thrust is T sin θ along the nose, the vertical part T cos θ.
  2. Hold height. T cos θ = m g. Accelerate. T sin θ = m × 2. Divide: tan θ = 2 / 9.81, θ = 11.52° (nose down, positive in DN201).
  3. Thrust. T = m g / cos θ = 10.012 N.
  4. Direction. The thrust direction is (sin θ cos ψ, sin θ sin ψ, cos θ): (0.1998, 0, 0.9798) for ψ = 0, (0, 0.1998, 0.9798) for ψ = 90°. With yaw 90° the nose points along world +y, and the vehicle accelerates to the world's left.
  5. Check. Run R4 prints all of these numbers.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Mei's instructions are about her own hands; she does not need numbers, and she never meets gimbal lock because she would simply turn the tray another way. Euler angles are a fixed recipe of three numbers, and the recipe has a singular point that the vehicle can reach in a loop. B1
    • 

  • Mei feels the tilt of the tray directly. A drone never measures its attitude; it estimates it from gyroscopes, accelerometers and a magnetometer, and the estimate can be wrong (DN301). U1
    • 

  • In the story the three movements happen one after the other. A drone rotates about all three axes at once; the Euler angles only describe where it ends up, and their rates are not the body rates the gyroscopes measure. D1

Summary


    

  • Attitude is the orientation of the body frame in the world; DN201 uses x forward, y left, z up and R = Rz(ψ) Ry(θ) Rx(φ).
    • 

  • Positive roll lowers the right side, positive pitch lowers the nose, positive yaw turns the nose left in these axes.
    • 

  • The thrust points along the third column of R; its vertical part is cos φ cos θ, so T = m g / (cos φ cos θ) holds the height.
    • 

  • Gyroscopes measure body rates (p, q, r); Euler-angle rates follow from W(φ, θ), which contains tan θ and 1/cos θ.
    • 

  • At pitch ±90° Euler angles lose a degree of freedom (gimbal lock); displayed roll and yaw can jump without any physical cause.
    • 

  • Use Euler angles for display, rotation matrices for turning vectors, quaternions for integration.
    •
