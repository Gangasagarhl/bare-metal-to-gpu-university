Story hook

Analogy

Kofi has carried trays for years, and he knows things he could not write down. A full tray is harder to start tipping than an empty one, and once it starts to tip it wants to keep going. A long, narrow tray tips easily along its short side and hardly at all along its long side. And when he turns on the spot with a tray, the glasses do not turn by themselves: he has to turn the tray with them.

All of this is mass and inertia: how hard it is to start or stop a movement, in a straight line and in rotation. Kofi learned it with his hands. A simulator has to learn it from equations — the same few equations for a tray, a drone, a satellite or a spinning book thrown into the air.

This chapter writes those equations for the course quad, builds the university's multirotor simulator from them (first flat, in 2D, then in 3D), and — most important — tests the simulator against answers you can derive by hand, so that you can trust it before DN301 flies controllers in it.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the Newton–Euler equations of a multirotor: translation in the world frame, rotation in the body frame, and the kinematics that link them.
  • Explain the inertia matrix and the gyroscopic term ω × Jω, and what happens to a spinning body without it.
  • Build a planar (2D) and a full 3D multirotor simulator in C++ with a fixed-step RK4 integrator and a quaternion attitude.
  • Validate the simulator with tests derived by hand: hover, free fall, the tilted hover thrust condition, torque signs, conservation laws and step-size convergence.
  • Diagnose a simulator that violates conservation of angular momentum.
Jargon box
Rigid body
Simple words: an object that does not bend or stretch, so one position and one attitude describe it completely. Analogy: the tray, if the glasses were glued on. Precise: a body in which the distance between any two points is constant; its configuration has six degrees of freedom (three of position, three of attitude). B2B1
Inertia matrix (inertia tensor)
Simple words: how hard it is to start the body turning about each axis. Analogy: the long tray tips easily one way and hardly the other. Precise: the symmetric 3 × 3 matrix J relating angular velocity to angular momentum, L = J ω; in principal axes it is diagonal, J = diag(Jx, Jy, Jz), in kg·m². B2
Angular momentum
Simple words: the "amount of spin" a body carries. Analogy: a spinning tray that keeps turning after you let go. Precise: L = J ω (body frame for a rigid body about its centre of mass); with no external torque, L expressed in the world frame stays constant. B2
Newton–Euler equations
Simple words: the two laws that move a rigid body: forces change its velocity, torques change its spin. Analogy: pushing the tray along, and twisting it. Precise: m dv/dt = ΣF (world frame) and J dω/dt + ω × Jω = Στ (body frame, about the centre of mass). B2B1
Gyroscopic term
Simple words: the part of the rotation law that makes a spinning body wobble and precess even without any torque. Analogy: none in the tray story; think of a spinning book thrown into the air. Precise: the term ω × Jω in J dω/dt + ω × Jω = τ, which appears because J is constant in the body frame while the body frame rotates; it vanishes when ω is along a principal axis. B2
State (of a simulation)
Simple words: the list of numbers that says everything about the vehicle at one instant. Analogy: where the tray is, how fast it moves, how it is tilted and how fast it turns. Precise: here x = (p, v, q, ω, ω1..4): position and velocity (world), unit quaternion, body rates and the four rotor speeds — 17 numbers. D1
RK4 (classical Runge–Kutta)
Simple words: a way to step a simulation forward that samples the slope four times per step. Analogy: checking the path at the start, twice in the middle and at the end before taking a step. Precise: xn+1 = xn + h (k1 + 2k2 + 2k3 + k4)/6 with the four slope evaluations of MA302 F0-76; a fourth-order method. U1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the whole simulator on one page. Commands go through the motors to the rotor speeds; the forward mixer of F10-02 turns rotor speeds into a thrust and a torque; the thrust drives translation, the torque drives rotation; the kinematics turn velocities into position and attitude; one RK4 step produces the next state. The dashed lines are why this is not just two separate problems: the attitude decides where the thrust points, and the spin feeds back into the rotation through the gyroscopic term.

Figure 2 shows the 2D simulator (Listing 1) answering a question you can also answer by hand: give the vehicle a short roll pulse, then hold the tilted hover thrust. The roll settles at 13.75°, the height stays almost constant, and the vehicle accelerates sideways at g tan 13.75° = 2.401 m/s².

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Two rules move a drone. The first rule is about pushes: the harder you push something, the faster its speed changes, and heavy things change speed more slowly. The second rule is about twists: the harder you twist something, the faster its turning changes, and things with their weight far from the middle change their turning more slowly.

A computer simulation uses these rules in tiny time steps. At each step it asks: where is the drone, how fast is it going, how is it tilted, how fast is it turning? It works out the pushes and twists from the propellers, and moves everything forward a tiny bit. Then it does it again, a thousand times for each second.

A simulation can be wrong and still look fine. So engineers test it with questions they can answer without it: "If the propellers stop, how far does it fall in two seconds?" If the simulation gives a different answer, it has a bug.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The state

A rigid body has six degrees of freedom. The simulator describes it by its position p and velocity v in the world frame, its attitude as a unit quaternion q (body to world, MA201 F0-55; F10-03), and its body rates ω = (p, q, r). The four rotor speeds are part of the state too, because motors take time to change speed (F10-05). B1D1

Translation: Newton in the world frame

The forces are the thrust T along body z, the weight and the drag (F10-01). Written in the world frame, with R the rotation matrix of q: B1D1

dp/dt = v,    m dv/dt = R (0, 0, T) − m g e3 − k |v| v

with e3 = (0, 0, 1). This is F10-01's vertical equation with the thrust turned by the attitude: the third column of R (F10-03) times T.

Rotation: Euler's equation in the body frame

For rotation, the analogue of m is the inertia matrix J. For the course quad, which is symmetric about its body axes, J is diagonal: Jx = Jy = 0.010 kg·m² and Jz = 0.018 kg·m² (exercise values). The rotation law, written in the body frame where J is constant, is B2B1

J dω/dt = τ − ω × (J ω)

τ is the torque from the mixer (F10-02). The extra term ω × Jω is the gyroscopic term. It appears because the body frame itself rotates: the angular momentum L = Jω is constant in the world when no torque acts, but seen from the turning body frame its components change. When the body turns about a single principal axis (ω along x, y or z), ω × Jω = 0 and the law reduces to J dω/dt = τ, the twist version of F = m a. B2D1

Kinematics: from body rates to attitude

The attitude changes with the body rates. With a quaternion this is one line, from MA201 F0-55: U1B1

dq/dt = ½ q ⊗ (0, ω)

The product is on the right because ω is measured in the body frame (the same reason Listing 1 of F10-03 multiplied the small turn on the right). After each step the quaternion is renormalised to length 1. Together, translation, rotation and kinematics are the Newton–Euler equations of the vehicle. Add the motor lag of F10-05, dωi/dt = (ωcmd,i − ωi)/τm, and the model of Figure 1 is complete.

2D first

Restrict the vehicle to the y–z plane, with roll φ as the only rotation. Then R (0, 0, T) = (0, −T sin φ, T cos φ), ω has only the x component p, ω × Jω = 0, and the equations shrink to six numbers: D1

m dvy/dt = −T sin φ,   m dvz/dt = T cos φ − m g,   Jx dp/dt = τx,   dφ/dt = p

Listing 1 integrates them with semi-implicit Euler steps of 1 ms. The two left rotors act as one, the two right rotors as another, and τx = a (fleft − fright) as in F10-02. A planar model is small enough to check every number by hand, which is why the course card asks for 2D first.

3D: the university's simulator

Listing 2, quadsim.hpp, implements Figure 1 for all six degrees of freedom: vectors and quaternions, the course quad's parameters, the forward mixer and the clamped inverse mixer, the derivative of the state, and a fixed-step RK4 integrator (MA302 F0-76). Every later DN201 lab and both DN201 forensic logs of F10-02 and F10-03 use it. D1U1

Testing a simulator

A simulator is a model of physics; it is trustworthy only where it reproduces physics you already know. Listing 3 runs six tests, each with an answer derived without the simulator: R3D1

  • T1 hover: four rotors at √(m g / 4kT) = 495.23 rad/s, level: nothing may move in 5 s.
  • T2 free fall: rotors stopped, no drag: z(2 s) = −g t²/2 = −19.62 m.
  • T3 tilted hover: roll 20°, pitch −10°, thrust m g / (cos φ cos θ) = 10.6006 N: the vertical speed stays zero and the horizontal acceleration equals the horizontal part of the thrust over m. This is the course's exam P item, "derive the hover thrust condition and test it in simulation".
  • T4 signs: left motors faster gives positive roll rate; clockwise motors faster gives positive yaw rate (F10-02).
  • T5 conservation: a torque-free body keeps its kinetic energy and its world-frame angular momentum, and a spin about the intermediate principal axis is unstable (it flips).
  • T6 convergence: halving the step changes a 2 s manoeuvre by less than 1 mm.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The intermediate-axis flip. For a body with three different principal inertias, steady spin about the largest or smallest axis is stable, and spin about the middle one is not: any small disturbance grows, and the body flips over and back periodically. It is a consequence of the gyroscopic term alone. Test T5 starts a spin of 10 rad/s about body y (Jy = 0.014 kg·m² between 0.010 and 0.018) with a 0.001 rad/s disturbance, and the body rate about y reaches −9.978 rad/s within 5 s: the body turned over, while energy and angular momentum stayed constant to 10−12–10−14 (run R3). A simulator that misses ω × Jω cannot do this, which makes T5 a sharp test. B2R3

What the model leaves out. The rotors are themselves spinning bodies; when the vehicle rotates, their angular momentum adds gyroscopic torques that the model ignores. The rotor thrust depends on the airflow through the rotor (F10-01), the drag acts at the centre of mass only, the inertia matrix is assumed diagonal, and the motors are first-order lags. Beard and McLain and other texts discuss richer models; DN201 starts with this one because every part can be tested. B1D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

How large the left-out effects (rotor gyroscopic torques, airflow effects on thrust, off-diagonal inertia terms) are for a real vehicle was not checked in this build. Check Beard and McLain (B1) and "Modern Robotics" (B2) for the models, and measure the real vehicle's mass, centre of mass and inertia (DN202) before relying on the simulator for it.

Numerics. RK4 with a fixed 1 ms step is accurate for this model (T6 shows a difference of 2 × 10−9 m between 2 ms and 1 ms steps after 2 s) and reproduces polynomial motion such as free fall to rounding error (T2: 1.1 × 10−12 m). Fixed steps matter: a controller in DN301 runs at fixed periods, and a variable-step integrator would make the timing of commands irregular. The quaternion is renormalised after each step; without that, rounding errors would slowly make it a non-rotation. The fastest dynamics in the model, the motor lag of 30 ms, is thirty times the step, comfortably inside RK4's range. R3U1

Frames, again. Translation is integrated in the world frame because gravity is constant there; rotation in the body frame because J is constant there. The quaternion is the bridge. Mixing the frames up (adding a world-frame vector to a body-frame one) is the most common error in hand-written simulators, and test T3 catches it. D1

How the hardware actually does it

A real vehicle does not run this model; it simply obeys physics. The model's parameters must be found from the hardware: the mass with a scale, the motor and propeller constants on a thrust stand (F10-05), the inertia with a dedicated measurement, and the drag from flight logs. Until then, the simulator describes the course quad and nothing else. B1D1

The same equations run inside flight software as models for estimators and controllers (DN301), and inside the simulators used for software-in-the-loop testing (DN302, DN303, DN401). There, the simulator replaces the vehicle and the sensors, and the flight code cannot tell the difference — which is exactly why such a simulator must be tested as carefully as the flight code. U1

Worked example

Problem. In the 2D simulator (Listing 1), each side gives m g/2 at hover. From 1.0 s to 1.2 s the left side gets +0.2 N and the right −0.2 N; from 1.2 s to 1.4 s the opposite. Predict the final roll angle and the sideways acceleration once the thrust is set to m g / cos φ.

  1. Torque. τx = a (fleft − fright) = 0.15 × 0.4 = 0.06 N·m. Angular acceleration 0.06 / 0.010 = 6.0 rad/s².
  2. Spin up and down. After 0.2 s the roll rate is 6.0 × 0.2 = 1.2 rad/s and the angle 0.5 × 6.0 × 0.2² = 0.12 rad. The opposite pulse brakes the rate back to zero in another 0.2 s and adds another 0.12 rad: φ = 0.24 rad = 13.75°.
  3. Sideways acceleration. With T = m g / cos φ, the sideways part is −T sin φ / m = −g tan φ = −2.401 m/s² (towards the vehicle's right, −y).
  4. Compare with the simulation. R1 shows roll 13.75° from 1.5 s on, and vy changing by −0.600 m/s every 0.25 s, that is −2.40 m/s²; the program also prints −2.401 m/s². Run R4 recomputes the prediction.
  5. The small sink. R1 shows z falling slowly (−0.043 m/s): during the pulse the thrust was m g while the body was already tilted, so the vertical part was a little short. That is the tilted hover condition of F10-01, seen as an error.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Kofi's tray is held: his hands supply forces and torques whenever they are needed. A flying vehicle has only its rotors' thrust and torques, limited as in F10-02, plus gravity and drag. D1
    • 

  • A tray with glasses is not rigid: drinks slosh and change how it responds. The simulator assumes a perfectly rigid body with fixed mass and inertia; a real vehicle has flexible arms, a battery that can shift, and a payload that may swing. B1
    • 

  • Kofi never sees ω × Jω, because he turns trays slowly about one axis at a time. A tumbling body shows it strongly (the flip of test T5); intuition from carrying trays does not predict it. B2

Hardware and software needed


No hardware. A computer with a C++20 compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0 through university/labs/run_lab.sh.


Safety


None beyond normal computer use: this lab is simulation only.

Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. Constant: the body rates (2, 10, 1) rad/s and the energy 0.729000 J. Changing: all three world-frame components of the angular momentum (for example Lz moves between 0.00528 and 0.02265).
    2. 

  2. Without external torque, the angular momentum vector in the world frame is constant (direction and size). Here its size stays 0.14256 but its direction swings: the law is violated.
    3. 

  3. ω × Jω = (0.0400, −0.0160, 0.0800) N·m (run R4): not zero. The body rates of a correct torque-free body would therefore change (dω/dt = −J−1(ω × Jω) ≠ 0); "perfectly steady" body rates are the symptom, not a virtue.
    4. 

  4. Energy is ½ ωᵀJω; with ω held constant it cannot change. The missing term is exactly the one that redistributes rotation between the axes while keeping energy and |L| constant, so leaving it out keeps the energy right and breaks the direction of L.
    5. 

  5. Bug: the rotational equation omits the gyroscopic term; no_gyro_bug.cpp computes dω/dt from the (zero) torque only. Fix: dω/dt = J−1(τ − ω × Jω), as in quadsim.hpp lines 154–155.
