Story hook

Analogy

Nadia, a new waiter, is crossing the garden terrace on a windy evening with an empty tray. She holds it flat, walks fast, and tips its front edge up a little. The moving air pushes the tray up against her hand: it suddenly feels almost weightless. Tip it a little more and it pushes up harder. Tip it too far and the push suddenly goes away and the tray flaps.

Later, carrying full glasses round the tight corner by the fountain, she leans the tray into the turn. The glasses do not slide outwards, but they press into the tray harder than when she walked straight. The tighter the corner, the heavier they feel.

An aeroplane's wing is Nadia's tilted tray: moving air pushes it up. And a turning aeroplane, like the tray at the fountain, must carry more than its own weight. This chapter explains both, and why aeroplanes fly far on little thrust while multirotors hover anywhere — and how VTOL aircraft try to do both.

What you will be able to do

  • Use the lift and drag equations to find the speed needed for level flight and the stall speed.
  • Explain the lift-to-drag ratio and compute the best one from a simple drag polar.
  • Compute the load factor, stall speed and radius of a level turn at a given bank angle.
  • Simulate an unpowered glide and compare the steady state with the formula.
  • Compare the thrust a multirotor and an aeroplane of the same weight need, and describe the three common VTOL designs qualitatively.
Jargon box
Lift coefficient
Simple words: the upward push of moving air on a wing, and a number saying how good the wing is at it at that moment. Analogy: the breeze pushing Nadia's tilted tray up. Precise: L = ½ ρ V² S CL, with air density ρ, airspeed V, wing area S and lift coefficient CL, which grows with the angle of attack up to a maximum CL,max. Lift itself was defined in F10-01. B1U1
Angle of attack
Simple words: how much the wing is tipped up against the air coming at it. Analogy: how far Nadia raises the tray's front edge. Precise: the angle between the wing's reference line (chord) and the direction of the oncoming air; CL rises roughly in proportion to it until the stall. B1
Stall (aerodynamic)
Simple words: when the wing is tipped too far, the air stops following it and the lift drops. Analogy: the tray tipped too far, flapping. Precise: flow separation above a critical angle of attack, at which CL reaches CL,max and then falls; the stall speed in level flight is Vs = √(2W/(ρ S CL,max)). B1D1
Drag polar
Simple words: a formula saying how much drag comes with each amount of lift. Analogy: the harder the breeze lifts the tray, the harder it also pushes it back. Precise: CD = CD0 + k CL², a parasite part CD0 and an induced part k CL² that comes with making lift. B1
Lift-to-drag ratio (L/D)
Simple words: how many newtons of lift a wing gives per newton of drag. Analogy: how much the breeze lifts the tray for each bit it pushes back. Precise: L/D = CL/CD; with the drag polar its maximum is 1/(2√(CD0k)) at CL = √(CD0/k). D1B1
Flight-path angle and glide ratio
Simple words: how steeply the aircraft climbs or sinks, and how far a glider travels forwards per metre it sinks. Analogy: the slope of the garden path Nadia walks down. Precise: the flight-path angle γ is the angle of the velocity above the horizontal (negative when descending); in a steady glide tan(−γ) = D/L, so the glide ratio equals L/D. D1B1
Bank angle and load factor
Simple words: how far the wings are tilted in a turn, and how many times its weight the wing must lift. Analogy: the tray leaned into the corner, and the glasses pressing in harder. Precise: in a level turn with bank φ, L cos φ = W, so the load factor n = L/W = 1/cos φ; the turn radius is V²/(g tan φ). D1B1
VTOL (vertical take-off and landing)
Simple words: an aircraft that can take off straight up like a multirotor and then fly on wings like an aeroplane. Analogy: Nadia lifting the tray straight up at the bar, then walking fast so the breeze helps. Precise: a class of aircraft that combine powered lift for take-off, hover and landing with wing-borne cruise; the change between the two is the transition. Common layouts: tailsitter, tiltrotor, quadplane (separate lift and cruise propulsion). B1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A multirotor holds itself up by blowing air down with its propellers, all the time. An aeroplane does it differently: it moves forward fast, and its wings, tipped up a little, push the moving air down. The air pushes the wings up. That push is called lift.

The faster the aeroplane goes, the more lift its wings make. Too slow, and the wings cannot hold it up. Tip the wings too far to get more lift, and the air stops flowing smoothly over them, and the lift suddenly drops. That is a stall.

The aeroplane's propeller only has to push it forward against the air, which is much easier than holding the whole aeroplane up. That is why aeroplanes can fly far on a small battery. But they cannot stand still in the air. VTOL aircraft are clever mixtures that can do both.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Lift and drag

The standard model of a wing is B1

L = ½ ρ V² S CL D = ½ ρ V² S CD, CD = CD0 + k CL²

Both grow with the square of the airspeed V. The pilot or autopilot changes CL by changing the angle of attack, up to the stall at CL,max. The course aeroplane (exercise values) has mass 1.5 kg, wing area S = 0.30 m², CL,max = 1.2, CD0 = 0.03 and k = 0.05, and the chapter uses ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ as a round sea-level-like air density. D1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The value ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ is used here as an exercise value. That it is the sea-level density of the International Standard Atmosphere, and how density falls with altitude and rises in cold air, were not checked in this build. Check the standard-atmosphere table cited by Beard and McLain (B1) or the dossier's atmosphere source before using it for a real aircraft.

Level flight and stall speed

In level flight L = W, so the speed needed for a chosen CL is V = √(2W/(ρ S CL)). The slowest possible level flight uses CL,max: the stall speed. For the course aeroplane (W = 14.715 N) it is 8.17 m/s. Listing 1's first table shows the trade: flying slowly needs a large CL, and so more induced drag; flying fast needs little CL but has more parasite drag. D1R1

The lift-to-drag ratio

In level flight the propeller must provide T = D = W/(L/D). Maximising L/D = CL/(CD0 + kCL²) gives CL = √(CD0/k) = 0.7746, where the induced and parasite parts are equal (0.030 each), and L/D = 1/(2√(CD0k)) = 12.91. So the course aeroplane flies level at 10.17 m/s on 1.140 N of thrust. A multirotor of the same weight needs 14.715 N just to hover: 12.91 times more. That ratio is the main reason fixed-wing drones fly longer and further on the same battery. D1R1R4

Note

Less thrust does not automatically mean less power, because power is thrust times speed and a propeller's efficiency depends on how it is used (F10-05 Layer 3). The comparison of thrust is exact for this model; a comparison of battery energy needs a propeller model in forward flight, which DN201 does not build.

Gliding

With the motor off, the weight is the only "engine": the aircraft descends along a path at angle γ. In a steady glide the forces balance along and across the path, and tan(−γ) = D/L. At best L/D the glide angle is −4.429°, the speed 10.153 m/s and the sink rate 0.784 m/s: from 100 m the course aeroplane can glide 1291 m in still air. Listing 2 simulates the point-mass equations D1B1R2R4

m dV/dt = −D − m g sin γ m V dγ/dt = L − m g cos γ

starting level at 15 m/s. The aircraft first dives (γ reaches about −22°), trades speed for height and back, and settles at −4.42° and 10.159 m/s after 60 s, close to the formula (Figure 3). This slow swap of speed and height is called the phugoid in aeronautics. R2B1

Turns and load factor

To turn, an aeroplane banks: it rolls by an angle φ so that part of the lift points to the side. In a level turn the vertical part must still carry the weight: L cos φ = W, so D1

n = L/W = 1/cos φ Vstall,turn = Vs √n r = V²/(g tan φ)

At 60° bank n = 2.00: the wing carries twice the weight, and the stall speed rises from 8.17 to 11.55 m/s. This is the same condition as F10-01's tilted multirotor, T = mg/cos(tilt): whatever does the lifting must grow as it tilts. At 12 m/s and 60° the radius is 8.5 m (Listing 1). R1U1

VTOL: three ways to do both

  • Tailsitter: the whole aircraft stands on its tail for take-off and pitches over by about 90° to fly on its wing. Few parts; the attitude change is large, which is exactly where F10-03's Euler angles fail and quaternions are needed.
  • Tiltrotor: the rotors tilt from pointing up (hover) to pointing forward (cruise). One set of motors does both jobs; the tilt mechanism adds weight and failure modes.
  • Quadplane (separate lift and cruise): a normal aeroplane with an extra set of vertical lift rotors and a separate forward motor. Simple to control, but the lift rotors are dead weight and drag in cruise.

In every design the transition is the hard part: at low speed the wing cannot carry the weight, so the rotors must make up the difference W − L(V) until the speed is safely above the stall speed. B1D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What the point-mass model leaves out. Listings 1 and 2 treat the aeroplane as a point with lift and drag. A real aircraft has six degrees of freedom like F10-04's quad, with aerodynamic moments from the wing, tail and control surfaces (ailerons, elevator, rudder) that depend on angle of attack, sideslip, rates and surface deflections. Beard and McLain develop that full model and its linearisations; this chapter only needs the forces. B1

Stall is not a number but an event. CL,max = 1.2 is a model value. A real wing's maximum lift depends on its shape, its surface, the airspeed and size (Reynolds number), and on whether one wing tip stalls before the other — which can drop a wing and start a spin. A turning aircraft stalls at a higher speed than a straight one (Layer 2), so "keep above the stall speed" must always say "at this load factor". B1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The statements about Reynolds-number effects, tip stall and spins are general aeronautics recalled during authoring, not taken from an opened source in this build. Check the aerodynamics chapter of Beard and McLain (B1) and, for any real airframe, its manufacturer's documentation.

Numerical method. Listing 2 uses semi-implicit Euler (MA302): the speed is updated first and the new speed is used for the angle, which keeps the oscillation from growing artificially. With 1 ms steps against a motion that takes tens of seconds, the step is far smaller than needed; the lab asks you to measure that. U1R2

Multirotors in forward flight also get some lift from their tilted body and a change in rotor thrust with airspeed, but they have no wing to carry their weight; the thrust-versus-hover comparison of Layer 2 is why long-endurance missions use wings. B1

How the hardware actually does it

An aeroplane's flight controller uses the same kinds of sensors as a multirotor's (DN202), plus an airspeed sensor in many designs, because lift depends on airspeed, not on speed over the ground. Wind makes the two differ: an aeroplane flying at 10 m/s airspeed into a 5 m/s headwind moves at only 5 m/s over the ground, yet its stall speed is about airspeed. Control is by surfaces — ailerons for roll, elevator for pitch, rudder for yaw — driven by servos, while the motor sets thrust. A VTOL flight controller runs both a multirotor mixer (F10-02) and a fixed-wing one and blends them during the transition. B1U1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Which airspeed sensor types are common on small fixed-wing drones and how flight software uses them were not checked in this build. Check the airspeed-sensor and fixed-wing sections of the PX4 and ArduPilot documentation (registry 4.8).

Safety

Fixed-wing drones fly fast and far: a loss of control covers ground quickly, and a stall near the ground leaves no height to recover. Outdoor flying belongs to later courses (DN304 and the faculty's flight rules), under local aviation law, with an experienced pilot in charge. Nothing in this chapter is flown.

Worked example

Problem. The course aeroplane flies at 12 m/s. What is the steepest level turn it can fly without stalling, and how tight is it?

  1. Lift coefficient in straight flight. CL = W/(½ρV²S) = 14.715 / (0.18375 × 144) = 0.5561.
  2. Largest load factor. The wing can give at most CL,max = 1.2, so nmax = 1.2/0.5561 = 2.158.
  3. Bank angle. cos φ = 1/nmax, so φmax = 62.39°.
  4. Radius and turn rate. r = V²/(g tan φ) = 144/(9.81 × 1.913) = 7.68 m; turn rate g tan φ / V = 89.6 °/s.
  5. Check. Listing 1's turn table gives 8.5 m at 60° and 5.3 m at 70° (where it would already stall); 7.68 m at 62.4° fits between them. Run R4 prints every number above.

In practice an autopilot keeps a margin well below nmax, because gusts and manoeuvres add load. D1R4R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nadia's tray gets lift from the wind and her hand; a wing has only the air and must keep moving to get any lift at all. Stop, and the lift is gone — the one thing a multirotor never has to worry about. B1
    • 

  • The tray's "stall" is a flap; a real stall can drop one wing and start a spin, a much more serious event (Layer 3). B1
    • 

  • Nadia leans the tray into the corner to keep the glasses from sliding; an aeroplane banks to make the turn, because tilted lift is what pulls it round. D1

How the evidence was produced: turn_stall.cpp computes, for each bank angle, the lift coefficient a level turn at a constant 11 m/s needs, n × W/(½ρV²S), and compares it with CL,max = 1.2 (run R3). It does not simulate the fall itself. Wrong answers and why: "the simulator's turn code is buggy" — the numbers follow n = 1/cos φ exactly; "the speed dropped" — the speed is held at 11 m/s in every row; "the stall speed is 8.17 m/s, so 11 m/s is safe" — that is the straight-flight value, true only for n = 1.
