Story hook

The cellar water wheel from F1-69 now has four valves around it. Open the top-left and bottom-right valves, and water from the roof tank flows through the wheel left to right; open the other pair, and it flows right to left and the wheel turns backwards. Olu, who looks after the plumbing, writes a rule on the wall in big letters: never open both valves on the same side. That would let the tank empty straight into the drain without touching the wheel.

Sven wants the wheel to turn slowly. He cannot half-open the valves, so he opens and closes them quickly, on for a moment, off for a moment. The heavy wheel does not notice the flicker; it turns at a speed set by the fraction of time the water flows.

One day Sven slams a valve shut while water is rushing. There is a loud bang in the pipes: water hammer. Olu adds a small bypass loop so that the moving water has somewhere to go. These are the three ideas of this chapter: the H-bridge, PWM, and the flyback path.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the four switches of an H-bridge, which combinations drive, brake, coast, and which are forbidden.
  • Use PWM to set a motor's average voltage, and predict the current ripple from the PWM frequency.
  • Explain why switching off an inductive load needs a freewheeling path, and what happens without one.
  • Calculate the dead time needed to prevent shoot-through from switch delays.
  • Describe what a gate driver and an ESC add to a bridge.
Jargon box
PWM (pulse-width modulation)
Simple words: switching a supply on and off very fast, so that on average the motor gets a lower voltage. Analogy: Sven flicking the valves open and shut. Precise: a rectangular switching waveform of fixed frequency whose duty cycle D sets the average output voltage D·Vsupply; the load's inductance and inertia average the pulses. D2 D1
Shoot-through
Simple words: both switches on one side of the bridge on at once, shorting the supply. Analogy: both valves on one side open: the tank empties straight into the drain. Precise: simultaneous conduction of the high-side and low-side switches of one bridge leg, giving a low-resistance path from supply to ground; even brief overlaps waste power and heat the switches, and long ones destroy them. D1 D3
Dead time
Simple words: a short pause between switching one side off and the other on. Analogy: Olu waiting until one valve is fully shut before opening the other. Precise: a deliberate delay inserted between the turn-off command of one switch in a leg and the turn-on command of the other, longer than the difference between the switches' turn-off and turn-on delays. D3 R4
Freewheeling (flyback) diode
Simple words: a one-way path that lets a motor's current keep flowing gently when the switch opens. Analogy: Olu's bypass loop that stops the water hammer. Precise: a diode placed so that an inductive load's current can continue when its switch turns off, clamping the switch voltage to about the supply plus one diode drop; in a MOSFET bridge, the body diodes or the opposite switch provide this path. D1 R3
Current ripple
Simple words: how much the motor current wobbles up and down with every PWM pulse. Analogy: how much the water flow surges each time a valve flicks. Precise: the peak-to-peak variation of load current within a PWM period; for a period much shorter than L/R it is about V·D(1−D)/(L·fPWM). D2 R2
Decay modes (coast and brake)
Simple words: what the bridge does with the motor current when it is not driving: let it fade, or short the motor to slow it. Analogy: closing all valves (the wheel spins on) or connecting both sides of the wheel together (the wheel's own push-back slows it). Precise: in coast (fast decay) the current returns through the diodes against the supply and decays quickly, and the motor freewheels; in brake (both low or both high switches on) the motor's back-EMF drives current through the short and produces a braking torque. D2 D3
Gate driver
Simple words: a helper chip that switches the big transistors quickly and safely. Analogy: a strong assistant who turns the stiff valves fast when Sven gives the word. Precise: a circuit that converts logic-level commands into the gate voltages and currents a power MOSFET needs, including a raised supply for high-side switches, often with built-in dead time and fault protection. D1 D3
ESC (electronic speed controller)
Simple words: a ready-made board that drives a brushless motor from a speed command. Analogy: a valve-room helper who reads the wheel's marks and works all the valves by herself. Precise: a three-phase bridge, gate drivers and a microcontroller running commutation (F1-69) and speed control; it accepts a command such as a servo-style pulse or a digital protocol defined in its documentation. D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A microcontroller pin cannot power a motor; it is far too weak. So it controls switches, and the switches connect the motor to the battery.

Four switches arranged like the letter H let the motor go forwards or backwards: close one diagonal pair, the current flows one way; close the other pair, it flows the other way. Closing the two switches on the same side is the one thing you must never do: it connects the battery straight to itself, and things get very hot.

To go slowly, the switches turn on and off thousands of times per second. On half the time means about half speed. The motor is too heavy to notice the flicker.

A motor is a coil of wire, and coils hate sudden changes. If you switch the current off suddenly, the coil makes a big voltage spike. A diode gives the current a safe path to fade away.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The H-bridge

An H-bridge (see the glossary) has two legs, each with a high-side switch to the supply and a low-side switch to ground; the motor joins the two midpoints (Figure 1). With four on/off switches there are 16 combinations. Listing 1 classifies them all: one forward (AH + BL), one reverse (BH + AL), two brakes (both low or both high), six coasting states, and 7 of 16 forbidden because they turn on both switches of one leg. R1 Driver firmware should make the forbidden states impossible by construction, for example by commanding each leg as a single "high or low" signal and letting hardware produce the complementary pair. D3

PWM and current ripple

The switches are either on or off; the motor sees either the supply or (nearly) zero. Switching with a duty cycle D gives an average voltage D·Vsupply, and the motor's inductance and inertia smooth the pulses. D2 Listing 2 drives the F1-69 pretend motor through a bridge at 25, 50 and 75% duty: the mean speeds are 1395, 2791 and 4186 rpm, the same as a steady 1.5, 3.0 and 4.5 V would give, at both PWM frequencies. R2 What differs is the current ripple. At a pretend 500 Hz (period 2 ms, longer than L/R = 0.5 ms) the current swings from −1.105 to +1.182 A at 50% duty, reversing every cycle; at 20 kHz (period 50 µs, much shorter than L/R) it stays between 0.000 and 0.078 A. R2 When the period is much shorter than L/R, the ripple is about V·D(1 − D)/(L·f): 6 × 0.25 / (0.001 × 20000) = 0.075 A at 50%, close to the 0.078 A measured. D2 Large ripple means extra i²R heating for no extra torque, audible noise, and torque pulsing; very high frequency means more switching loss. Many small-motor drives use frequencies above human hearing; the right range is in the driver's datasheet. D3

Why does the current reverse at 500 Hz? During the off time, Listing 2 turns both low switches on (brake-style). The motor's back-EMF then drives current backwards through the short, braking it a little every cycle. R2 D2

Switching off an inductor

An inductor's voltage is L·di/dt. If a switch tries to stop its current instantly, di/dt becomes huge and so does the voltage across the switch. D1 Listing 3 switches off a winding carrying 1 A. With a freewheeling diode, the current circulates through the diode and decays over about a millisecond while the switch sees 6.7 V (supply plus diode drop). Without it, the model forces the current through the open switch, modelled as a pretend 10 kΩ, and the switch voltage starts at 10,000 V. R3 In reality the switch breaks down (avalanches) at its rating long before that and may be destroyed; the model's number depends entirely on the 10 kΩ chosen, so read it as "far above any rating", not as a prediction. D1 In an H-bridge built from MOSFETs, each switch's body diode, or the opposite switch turned on, provides the freewheeling path. D3

Dead time

Real switches do not turn on and off instantly, and turning off is often slower than turning on. If the controller turns H off and L on at the same instant, both conduct for the difference: shoot-through (Figure 3). The fix is dead time: wait before turning the other switch on. In the forensic model (turn-on 20 ns, turn-off 60 ns, pretend), dead time 0 gives 80 ns of overlap per PWM period (two edges), 20 ns gives 40 ns, and 40 ns or more gives none. R4 Too much dead time also costs: during it, the current flows through body diodes, which waste more power, and the effective duty changes slightly. D3

Gate drivers, driver ICs and ESCs

Power MOSFETs need their gate charged quickly to a voltage well above their source, which for a high-side switch is above the supply; a gate driver provides this (often with a bootstrap capacitor) and usually inserts dead time and watches for faults. D1 D3 Small robots usually use a motor-driver IC that contains the bridge, gate drivers, current sensing and protection. A brushless motor uses three legs (a three-phase bridge) and an ESC, which adds a microcontroller running commutation and speed control. D4

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Losses in a switch. Conduction loss is i²·RDS(on) while on; switching loss grows with frequency, voltage, current and switching time; body-diode conduction during dead time adds Vf·i. The total sets the heat sink. D1
  • Decay modes. Slow decay (current recirculates through a shorted motor) keeps ripple low and brakes the motor; fast decay (current returns to the supply through the diodes) drops current quickly, useful for current control and steppers, but regenerates energy into the supply. D2 D3
  • Regeneration. A braking or back-driven motor returns energy to the supply rail. A battery can usually absorb it; a bench supply may not, and the rail voltage can rise — one reason drivers have overvoltage protection. D2
  • Current sensing. A shunt resistor in the low side, or sensing inside the driver, lets firmware limit current, detect stalls (F1-69) and do torque control. D3
  • Layout. The loop from the supply capacitor through the two switches of a leg carries fast-changing current; keeping it small reduces voltage spikes and noise coupled into sensors (F1-72). D1

How the hardware actually does it

The microcontroller's timer generates PWM in hardware: a counter runs at a fixed clock, a compare register sets the duty, and complementary outputs with a programmable dead-time generator are a common feature of motor-control timers. D5 These outputs feed a gate driver or a driver IC, which switches power MOSFETs; a bulk capacitor near the bridge supplies the fast current pulses so the battery wiring does not have to (F1-71). D1 Driver ICs typically add undervoltage lockout, overcurrent and overtemperature shutdown, and a fault pin the firmware should read. D3

Safety

Use only the kit's low-voltage driver board and motor, with a current-limited supply, the motor clamped and no propellers. Never connect or disconnect a motor while the bridge is powered: the inductive spike can damage the driver. Driver chips and MOSFETs can become hot enough to burn: do not touch them during or just after a test. L2 learners work with a supervising adult. S1

Worked example

Task. A bridge drives a motor with L = 2 mH (pretend) from 12 V at 50% duty. (a) The average voltage? (b) What PWM frequency keeps the ripple at or below 0.1 A? (c) The switches' turn-on delay is 30 ns and turn-off delay 110 ns (pretend). What is the minimum dead time, and what would you set?

  1. Average. 0.5 × 12 = 6 V.
  2. Frequency. Ripple ≈ V·D(1−D)/(L·f) ≤ 0.1 → f ≥ 12 × 0.25 / (0.002 × 0.1) = 15,000 Hz. Check the assumption: the period 67 µs must be much shorter than L/R (for R = 2 Ω, L/R = 1 ms): yes. A choice above hearing, such as 20 kHz, gives 0.075 A.
  3. Dead time. Overlap = turn-off delay − turn-on delay − dead time; zero overlap needs dead time ≥ 110 − 30 = 80 ns. Delays vary with temperature and between parts, so add margin, for example 150–200 ns, then check on an oscilloscope.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Valves are slow; MOSFETs switch in nanoseconds. The speed is why PWM works, and also why nanosecond delays matter (dead time). R4
    • 

  • Water hammer is a pressure wave; inductive kick is stored magnetic energy. The energy ½·L·i² must go somewhere: into the diode loop, or into the switch's breakdown. D1
    • 

  • Opening both valves on one side wastes water; shoot-through can destroy parts in microseconds. The supply's current is limited only by wiring and battery resistance. D1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the driver gets hot even when the motor is idle. Cause: shoot-through from too little dead time, or PWM running at zero command. How to find it: supply current with the motor disconnected; forensic lab. Fix: dead time from the datasheet delays plus margin.
    • 

  • Symptom: whining noise and a hot motor at low speed. Cause: PWM frequency in the audible range with large ripple. Fix: higher PWM frequency within the driver's limits.
    • 

  • Symptom: driver dies when the motor is unplugged while running. Cause: inductive spike with no current path. Fix: never hot-plug; check freewheeling paths and TVS/clamp parts if the datasheet recommends them.
    • 

  • Symptom: the microcontroller resets at the instant of reversing. Cause: going straight from full forward to full reverse doubles the voltage across the winding and draws a very high current. Fix: ramp through zero; current limit (F1-71).
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. deadtime.cpp models one leg at 20 kHz with complementary commands; each switch conducts from its command rise + 20 ns to its command fall + 60 ns (pretend delays), and counts the time both conduct.


Root cause. Shoot-through from zero dead time. At t = 25.00 µs, H's command falls and L's rises together. L conducts from 25.02 µs (turn-on delay 20 ns); H keeps conducting until 25.06 µs (turn-off delay 60 ns): 40 ns of overlap at this edge, and another 40 ns at the opposite edge, 80 ns per period. At 20 kHz that is 80 ns × 20,000 = 1.6 ms of supply short per second, independent of the motor command — so the driver heats while the motor (which sees an average of zero) stays cold.


Fixes. Dead time at least 60 − 20 = 40 ns (Evidence 2 shows zero overlap from 40 ns), with margin for part and temperature variation (for example 100 ns); use the timer's or gate driver's dead-time generator; also stop PWM (all switches off, or a brake state) when the command is zero. Verify: supply current at standstill drops to the board's quiescent current; overlap 0 in the model; on hardware, scope both gates.


Wrong answers. "Motor stalled": a stalled motor would be hot and draw V/R; the motor is cold. "Faulty driver": every part behaves as its delays say; the timing is wrong, not the part.


Prevention. Derive dead time from the datasheets in a design review; measure idle supply current in bring-up; an automated check that the configured dead time exceeds the computed minimum.
