Story hook

The restaurant's old cellar has no light switch. Kwame needs to know how far the back wall is before he carries a crate down. He claps once and listens. The echo comes back almost at once: the wall is close. In the long storeroom next to it, the echo takes longer. He has never measured anything with a ruler down there, only with time.

Ines tries a different trick at the pass. She closes one eye, then the other, and watches a pepper grinder on the counter jump sideways against the wall behind it. The grinder jumps a lot; the far wall hardly at all. Her two eyes are a little apart, and the size of the jump tells her how near the grinder is.

Echo timing and two-eyed jumping are the two big ideas behind a robot's depth sensors. A LiDAR times echoes of light; a stereo camera measures the jump. This chapter computes both, builds a scanning range sensor in software, and then catches it inventing a wall.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute range from time of flight (d = c·t/2) and the range step of a timer of given resolution.
  • Explain how a scanning LiDAR builds a point set from (angle, range) pairs, and what occlusion, maximum range and invalid returns look like in the data.
  • Compute depth from stereo disparity (Z = f·B/d) and show why its error grows with the square of the distance.
  • Compare pulsed and phase-based time of flight, stereo and structured light, by what each measures and where each fails.
  • Diagnose a "phantom obstacle" caused by treating invalid returns as measurements.
Jargon box
Time of flight (ToF)
Simple words: measuring distance by timing how long a signal takes to go out and come back. Analogy: Kwame's clap and its echo in the cellar. Precise: a ranging method in which range d = v·t/2, where t is the round-trip time and v the propagation speed; for light in vacuum v = c. D1 D2
LiDAR
Simple words: a sensor that measures distances with laser light, often in many directions. Analogy: clapping into the dark in every direction, one after another, and timing each echo. Precise: light detection and ranging: an active sensor that emits light (usually laser pulses or modulated light) and measures range from the returned light; a scanning LiDAR sweeps the beam to produce many (angle, range) measurements. D1
Scan and point cloud
Simple words: one sweep of measurements, and the dots they make when drawn in space. Analogy: a sketch of the storeroom made only from echo times in each direction. Precise: a scan is a set of (bearing, range) readings from one sweep; converting each to Cartesian coordinates (x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ in the sensor frame) gives a 2D point set, or a 3D point cloud for multi-beam sensors. D1
Maximum range and invalid return
Simple words: the furthest the sensor can measure, and the special value it reports when no echo came back. Analogy: clapping into an open field: no echo at all. Precise: beyond its maximum range, or when the surface returns too little light, a range sensor reports no valid measurement; drivers encode this as a special value (zero, the maximum, infinity, or a flag) defined in the sensor's documentation. D1 D4
Occlusion
Simple words: things hidden behind other things. Analogy: the crate in front of the back wall: the clap's echo comes from the crate, and the wall behind it is not heard. Precise: a range sensor measures only the first surface along each beam; regions behind it produce no measurements (a "shadow" in the point set). D1 R2
Stereo disparity
Simple words: how far a point jumps sideways between the left and right camera images. Analogy: the pepper grinder jumping when Ines switches eyes. Precise: for two rectified cameras with focal length f (pixels) and baseline B, a point at depth Z appears shifted by disparity d = f·B/Z pixels, so Z = f·B/d. D3
Structured light
Simple words: a depth camera that projects a known pattern and looks at how it bends over objects. Analogy: shining a striped tablecloth pattern on the counter and seeing the stripes shift over the bowls. Precise: active triangulation: a projector casts a known pattern and a camera at a known offset observes it; depth follows from the pattern's displacement, by the same geometry as stereo. D3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A LiDAR sends out a very short flash of invisible light and waits for it to bounce back. Light is so fast that the wait is tiny: for a wall one metre away, it is about seven billionths of a second. A clock inside the sensor measures that wait, and the sensor turns it into a distance.

A spinning LiDAR does this in many directions, one after another. Each direction gives one dot where the light hit something. Draw all the dots and you get the outline of the room, seen from the robot. Places hidden behind a box have no dots.

A stereo camera works like your two eyes. Each camera sees the scene from a slightly different place. Near things move a lot between the two pictures, far things barely move. The amount of movement tells the distance.

Both can fail. A flash that hits nothing never comes back. Then the sensor must say "no answer", and the program must not treat that as "something right here".

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Pulsed time of flight

Like Kwame's clap, a pulsed ToF sensor emits a short light pulse and measures the round-trip time t to the echo; the range is d = c·t/2. D1 The speed of light in vacuum, c = 299,792,458 m/s, is exact by the definition of the metre; in air light is slightly slower, a correction that matters only at high precision. D2 §2.2, §2.3.1 The numbers are small: Listing 1 prints 6.671 ns for 1 m and 66.713 ns for 10 m. A timer that counts in ticks of Δt resolves range in steps of c·Δt/2: 149.90 mm for 1 ns ticks, 14.99 mm for 100 ps ticks. R1 Pulsed sensors therefore need very fine timing electronics, or they average many pulses. D5

Phase-based (indirect) time of flight

Many depth cameras do not time single pulses. They modulate their light at a frequency fm and measure the phase shift φ of the returned light; the range is d = c·φ / (4π fm). Because phase repeats every 2π, ranges repeat every c / (2 fm): a target just beyond that distance looks very close (range ambiguity). D5 This follows from the definitions: a full cycle of phase is one modulation period, and the light travels out and back. The modulation frequencies and how a product resolves the ambiguity are in its datasheet. D4

Scans and points

A 2D scanning LiDAR rotates one beam (or a mirror) and reports ranges at regular bearings; one revolution is a scan. In the sensor's frame, a reading (θ, r) is the point (r cos θ, r sin θ). D1 Listing 2 is the university's scanning simulator: a sensor at (2.0, 1.5) m in a 6 m × 4 m room with a 0.6 m box, one beam per degree, ray casting to the nearest wall. Its text map shows the room's outline and the occlusion shadow on the right wall behind the box: those wall cells have no points because every beam aimed there ends on the box. R2 A scan is not taken at one instant: if the robot moves during the sweep, points from the start and end of the sweep are in different robot positions (motion distortion), which software must correct. D1

How range readings go wrong

The beam model of "Probabilistic Robotics" lists four ways a single range reading is produced: a correct hit with small noise; an unexpected nearer object (a person walking past); a maximum-range reading when nothing returned; and random readings from unexplained failures. D1 Dark, shiny, transparent and very oblique surfaces return too little light or reflect it away; glass may be seen through; a beam that touches the edge of a near object and a far wall may report a "mixed pixel" between them. D1 The point for a programmer is that a driver must say which readings are invalid, and every consumer must check it: the forensic lab shows what happens otherwise.

Stereo and structured light

Ines's two eyes are a stereo pair. For two parallel cameras with focal length f (in pixels) and baseline B, a point at depth Z has disparity d = f·B/Z, so Z = f·B/d. D3 With a pretend f = 700 px and B = 0.12 m, Listing 3 gives 1.00 m at 84 px and 8.00 m at 10.5 px. R3 Because Z is inversely proportional to d, a fixed disparity error δ produces a depth error of about Z²·δ/(f·B): a half-pixel error spreads the estimate over 0.012 m at 1 m but 0.764 m at 8 m, 64 times more for 8 times the distance. R3 D3 Stereo also needs texture: a blank wall gives nothing to match. Structured light fixes that by projecting its own pattern, and uses the same triangulation. D3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Range resolution versus accuracy. The tick of Listing 1 limits resolution; accuracy also depends on the pulse shape, the detector's timing walk with signal strength, temperature and calibration. Datasheets specify accuracy over a stated range of target reflectivities; compare like with like. D5 D4
  • Angular resolution and spacing. Points on a surface at range r are spaced about r·Δθ apart; at 1° spacing (Listing 2) that is 17 mm at 1 m and 70 mm at 4 m, so thin objects (a chair leg) can fall between beams at long range. The beam itself also has a width that grows with distance. D1
  • Measurement models. Probabilistic robotics treats each reading as a draw from a mixture of the four cases above, with parameters learned from data; the max-range case is a spike at rmax and must be handled explicitly, not as a real obstacle at rmax or at 0. D1 RB302 and RB401 build on this.
  • Stereo matching and calibration. Z = f·B/d assumes rectified images (rows aligned) and calibrated f, B and principal points; matching chooses d per pixel by comparing small windows, and fails on repeated patterns and occluded regions. D3 Camera models are in F1-68.
  • Interference. Two active sensors of the same kind can see each other's light; products use coding, timing or modulation schemes to reduce this, documented per product. D4
  • Data volume. A 3D LiDAR or depth camera produces large data streams over Ethernet or USB (F1-46, F1-51); small single-point ToF sensors often use I2C (F1-47). The interface and packet format are in the datasheet. D4

How the hardware actually does it

In a pulsed sensor, a laser diode driver fires a short current pulse; a photodiode (often an avalanche photodiode or an array of single-photon detectors) and an amplifier detect the echo; a comparator marks its arrival; and a time-to-digital converter measures the interval between start and stop, often by combining a fast counter with finer interpolation. D5 In a scanning unit, a motor turns the optics and an encoder (F1-66) reports the angle of each measurement. In a stereo or structured-light camera, two image sensors (F1-68) and often a projector are synchronised, and a processor computes disparities on board. D3 Your program usually receives finished ranges or depth images; the invalid-value convention, units and frame are in the driver documentation.

Safety

LiDARs and many depth cameras contain lasers. Use only a sensor whose documentation states its laser safety class under the applicable laser-safety standard (D6) and that class's conditions of use; never look into the emitter, never point it at people's eyes, and never open the housing or modify the optics. L2 learners use one only with a supervising adult. D6 S1

Worked example

Task. (a) A pulsed sensor's timer reads 400 ticks of 100 ps for an echo. What is the range? (b) A phase-based depth camera modulates at a pretend 20 MHz. What is its unambiguous range, and what range does a phase of π/2 mean? (c) A stereo camera (f = 700 px, B = 0.12 m) measures d = 30 px with ±0.5 px uncertainty. Give the depth and its range.

  1. Pulsed. t = 400 × 100 ps = 40 ns. d = 299,792,458 × 40 × 10−9 / 2 = 5.996 m. (Listing 1's conversion with 400 ticks gives the same.)
  2. Phase ambiguity. c / (2 fm) = 299,792,458 / (2 × 20 × 106) = 7.49 m. Phase π/2 is a quarter of a cycle, so d = 7.49 / 4 = 1.87 m, or 1.87 + 7.49 = 9.37 m, or 16.86 m: the sensor alone cannot tell.
  3. Stereo. Z = 700 × 0.12 / 30 = 2.80 m. With d = 30.5 px: 2.754 m; with 29.5 px: 2.847 m. The half-pixel uncertainty spans about 9 cm; the approximation Z²·δ/(f·B) = 2.8² × 0.5 / 84 = 0.047 m on each side agrees.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A clap goes everywhere; a laser beam goes one way. Kwame hears the nearest echo from any direction. A LiDAR beam is narrow and measures only along its line; a thin object between beams is missed entirely. D1
    • 

  • Sound is slow; light is not. Kwame could time an echo by ear. Light covers a metre and back in under 7 ns (Listing 1), so the sensor needs electronics that time in picoseconds, not a stopwatch. R1
    • 

  • Silence is obvious to Kwame; to a program it is a number. When no echo returns, Kwame knows he heard nothing. A driver must encode "nothing" as some value, and a program that forgets to check treats it as a distance (forensic lab). R4
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot stops for an obstacle that is not there. Cause: invalid returns (0 or max) used as ranges. How to find it: histogram of raw ranges; spikes at exactly 0 or at the maximum. Fix: filter invalid readings by the driver's documented convention before any use.
    • 

  • Symptom: the map is mirrored or rotated. Cause: wrong sign or zero of the bearing (clockwise versus anticlockwise, where 0° points). How to find it: put one object to the robot's left and check where it appears. Fix: use the frame convention from the driver documentation.
    • 

  • Symptom: walls look bent when the robot drives. Cause: motion during the sweep not corrected. Fix: de-skew each point with the robot's motion during the scan (RB401).
    • 

  • Symptom: stereo depth noisy at range and holes on plain walls. Cause: disparity error grows as Z²; textureless surfaces cannot be matched. Fix: use stereo where it is precise, add texture (structured light) or another sensor.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. phantom.cpp computes ranges in an ideal corridor of half-width 1 m (side walls at y = ±1 m, open ahead) with a pretend maximum range of 8 m; beams that hit nothing within 8 m are reported as 0.0, as this pretend driver's "no echo" convention. The safety check stops if any reading in ±30° is below 0.5 m, without checking validity.


Root cause. Along bearing θ the side wall is 1/|sin θ| metres away: 5.759 m at ±10°, 11.5 m at ±5°. Beyond 8 m (|θ| below about 7.2°) there is no echo, so the driver reports 0.000 for −7° … +7°: the 15 flagged beams. The check treats 0.000 as a real obstacle at the sensor and stops. The neighbours' ranges grow smoothly towards the centre (2.000, 2.366, 2.924, 3.864, 5.759) and then jump to 0: physically impossible for a real object, a clear sign of an encoding.
