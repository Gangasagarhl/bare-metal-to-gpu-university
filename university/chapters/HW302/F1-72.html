Story hook

In the restaurant, Kenji measures the level of soup in the big pot with a dipstick, and writes it in the log book. Every reading is a little different: the soup sloshes, his hand shakes. He learns a trick: take several readings and write down the average. The wobble shrinks.

But one day the readings are all too high, and only while the dishwasher is running. Averaging does not help at all. Ama, the plumber, finds the reason in the cellar: the pot's drain and the dishwasher's drain share the last stretch of pipe. When the dishwasher empties, the water backs up into the shared pipe and pushes up the level under the pot. The soup did not change. The reference did.

These are the two kinds of trouble in this chapter. Random noise, which averaging and filtering reduce. And interference from other parts of the robot, coupled through shared wires, which no amount of averaging removes: it has to be found and wired out. The chapter ends with the course project: a calibrated sensor logger that brings together everything from F1-64 onwards.

What you will be able to do

  • Predict how much averaging N readings reduces random noise, and recognise errors that averaging cannot remove.
  • Design a first-order digital low-pass filter and state its cut-off frequency and delay.
  • Explain common-impedance (shared ground) coupling and why a star ground cures it.
  • Use decoupling, separate supply paths, twisted pairs and shielding where they help.
  • Diagnose a sensor offset that correlates with motor current, using a regression on logged data.
  • Build the course project: a calibrated sensor logger that meets stated acceptance tests.
Jargon box
Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
Simple words: how big the thing you want to measure is compared with the wobble on top of it. Analogy: the soup level compared with the sloshing. Precise: the ratio of signal power to noise power, often given in decibels as 10·log10(Ps/Pn), or as a ratio of amplitudes (20·log10). D1
Averaging (σ/√N)
Simple words: taking many readings and using their average makes random wobble smaller, but slowly. Analogy: Kenji's several dipstick readings. Precise: the mean of N independent readings, each with standard deviation σ, has standard deviation σ/√N; averaging does not reduce offsets or noise that is correlated between readings. D4 R1
Low-pass filter (exponential moving average)
Simple words: a calculation that keeps slow changes and smooths away fast wobbles. Analogy: Kenji writing down a value that only moves a tenth of the way towards each new reading. Precise: the first-order recursive filter y[n] = y[n−1] + a·(x[n] − y[n−1]), 0 < a ≤ 1; its gain falls with frequency, and it delays the signal. D3 R2
Cut-off frequency (−3 dB)
Simple words: the frequency at which a filter starts to cut a signal noticeably. Analogy: how quickly the soup can really change before Kenji's slow log starts missing it. Precise: the frequency at which the filter's amplitude gain is 1/√2 (−3 dB) of its pass-band gain. D3
Common-impedance (shared ground) coupling
Simple words: when two circuits share a wire, the current of one changes the voltage the other sees. Analogy: the shared drain pipe backing up under the soup pot. Precise: interference caused by a current I1 flowing through an impedance Z also in circuit 2's path, adding I1·Z to circuit 2's signal; common in ground returns shared by motors and sensors. D2 D1
Star ground
Simple words: every part gets its own ground wire back to one common point. Analogy: every drain gets its own pipe to the main sewer. Precise: a grounding layout in which separate return paths meet at a single point, so that one circuit's return current does not flow through another's reference; at high frequency, ground planes and short return loops matter more. D2
Decoupling capacitor
Simple words: a small capacitor next to a chip that supplies quick bursts of current so the supply voltage stays steady. Analogy: a small bucket of water beside each sink for sudden needs. Precise: a capacitor placed close to a device's supply pins, providing a low-impedance local source for fast current changes and keeping supply noise from spreading; values and placement are in the device's datasheet. D1 D5
Shielding and twisted pairs
Simple words: ways of running wires so that other wires and motors disturb them less. Analogy: covering a pipe and keeping its supply and return pipes side by side. Precise: twisting a signal and its return wire together keeps the loop area small and cancels much magnetic pickup; a shield (a conductive cover) intercepts electric-field coupling and must be connected at the end the design specifies. D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every measurement wobbles a little. If you measure many times and take the average, the wobble gets smaller. But it gets smaller slowly: to make it half as big, you need four times as many readings.

A filter is a smart kind of averaging. It keeps the slow changes you care about and throws away fast wobbles. The price is that it reacts a bit late.

Some errors are not wobbles. If your ruler is bent, measuring a hundred times gives the same wrong answer a hundred times. In a robot, a common "bent ruler" is a ground wire shared by a sensor and a motor. When the motor pulls current, the sensor's ground moves, and every reading shifts. The fix is not maths; it is wiring: give the sensor its own ground wire.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Averaging and its limits

If readings x1…xN are independent with standard deviation σ, their mean has standard deviation σ/√N. D4 Listing 1 checks this with a synthetic 1.000 V signal and 10 mV of noise: measured spreads 10.207, 5.121, 2.445, 1.247 and 0.624 mV for N = 1, 4, 16, 64, 256, against predictions 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25 and 0.625 mV. R1 Two limits matter. First, cost: each halving of the noise needs four times the readings, so four times the time. Second, an offset is not noise: with a 25 mV offset, the 256-reading average is 1.0249 V, still about 25 mV high. R1 Averaging also assumes independence; noise that changes slowly (drift) or follows another signal (interference) is correlated and averages away much less. D4

Low-pass filtering

A running average throws away nothing new and smooths continuously. The simplest recursive filter is the exponential moving average: y[n] = y[n−1] + a·(x[n] − y[n−1]). D3 Listing 2 measures its gain for sine waves at 1000 samples/s with a = 0.1 and compares with the formula |H| = a / |1 − (1 − a)·e−j2πf/fs|: 0.9982 at 1 Hz, 0.7026 at 17 Hz (−3.07 dB, so the cut-off is near 17 Hz), 0.1916 at 87 Hz, 0.1009 at 174 Hz. R2 At 50 Hz the measured peak is 0.3157 against 0.3193: at 1000 samples/s, the 50 Hz wave's samples do not land exactly on its peaks, so the largest sample is a little below the true amplitude. R2 Applied to the jumpy IMU of F1-65, this filter would shrink the 0.30 g vibration at 87 Hz to about 0.06 g. R2 The price is delay: after a step, the output passes 63.2% in 10 samples, 10 ms. R2 A filter cannot remove what aliasing has already folded into the pass band; that needs an analog anti-alias filter before the ADC (F1-64). D3

Grounds and interference

"Ground" is only a reference: the voltage every other voltage is measured against. A ground wire has resistance (and inductance), so a current through it makes a voltage along it. D1 When a motor's return current flows through a piece of wire that is also part of a sensor's reference, the sensor's ground rises by I·R and the ADC sees the sensor voltage plus I·R: common-impedance coupling. D2 In the forensic log, a 0.05 Ω shared segment and 3 A of motor current add 0.15 V to a 1.000 V sensor. R3 Because it follows the motor current, the error is not random and averaging does nothing; the cure is to separate the return paths (a star ground, Figure 1), and in the model the error disappears completely. R4

Other coupling paths: switching currents in loops create magnetic fields that induce voltages in nearby loops (reduce loop areas; twist signal and return together); fast voltage edges couple through stray capacitance (keep sensitive wires away from motor and PWM wires; shield). D2 Supply noise spreads through shared supply wiring; decoupling capacitors close to each chip and separate supply paths for motors and logic (F1-71) keep it local. D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Filter design beyond one pole. Higher-order IIR filters (for example Butterworth) and FIR filters give steeper roll-off; the trade-off is always between attenuation, delay and phase distortion. Notch filters remove one known frequency (such as a motor's vibration) with little effect elsewhere. D3
  • Cut-off of the EMA. For small a, the −3 dB frequency is about a·fs/(2π) ≈ 16 Hz here; Listing 2's 17 Hz is close. The equivalent time constant is about 1/a samples. D3 R2
  • Correlation analysis. Logging a suspected source (motor current, PWM duty) alongside the disturbed signal and fitting a line (Listing in the key) turns "it seems worse when the motors run" into a measured coupling coefficient, here 0.0500 V/A — the shared resistance itself. R4
  • Differential measurement. Measuring a sensor's output against its own ground pin (a differential ADC input) rejects most of the ground shift, up to the ADC's common-mode limits. D1 D5
  • Digital buses are not immune. I2C (F1-65) uses ground-referenced logic levels and open-drain lines with pull-ups; ground shifts and coupled edges cause bit errors and stuck buses, which a logic analyser shows directly. D1
  • EMC rules. Products sold in many markets must meet electromagnetic compatibility regulations; the reasons are the same mechanisms, at larger scale. D2

How the hardware actually does it

A printed circuit board for mixed motor and sensor electronics typically separates the high-current motor section from the sensitive analog section, routes the motor return directly to the battery connector, and ties the grounds at one point near the supply; a solid ground plane gives every signal a low-inductance return path directly beneath it. D2 D1 Each chip has decoupling capacitors next to its supply pins; motors often have small capacitors across their terminals to absorb brush noise (F1-69). D1 Sensor modules' datasheets give their supply-noise tolerance and recommended decoupling, and an ADC's datasheet its input type (single-ended or differential) and reference. D5

The reference kit's parts for this chapter are the Adafruit LSM6DSOX breakout, the Raspberry Pi Pico 2 and the Pololu 5190 gearmotor with DRV8833 driver (build/KIT.md, ruling D1), but no interference was measured on them in this build. Real coupling depends on the actual layout and wiring and must be measured; the sensor's and ADC's datasheets (D5) give their noise and input specifications.

Worked example

Task. A distance sensor gives 2.00 V at 0.50 m and its output falls by 1.00 V per metre near that distance (pretend). Its noise is 20 mV (standard deviation) per reading at 1000 readings/s (pretend). (a) The distance noise per reading? (b) How many readings must be averaged to get 2 mm? How long does that take? (c) With 2.5 A of motor current through a shared 0.04 Ω ground, how large is the distance error, and does averaging help?

  1. Per reading. 20 mV ÷ 1.00 V/m = 0.020 m = 20 mm.
  2. Averaging. 20 mm / √N = 2 mm → √N = 10 → N = 100 readings = 0.1 s. A low-pass filter with a similar noise reduction would have a comparable delay.
  3. Ground shift. 2.5 × 0.04 = 0.10 V → 0.10 m = 100 mm of error, in one direction, as long as the motors run. Averaging does not help: the error is not random. Fix the wiring (separate return, or measure differentially).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A backed-up drain is slow; ground shifts follow current in microseconds. PWM edges (F1-70) produce ground shifts at the switching frequency and above, where wire inductance matters as much as resistance. D2
    • 

  • Water needs pipes; electric and magnetic fields do not. Coupling through the air (capacitive and inductive) has no plumbing equivalent. D2
    • 

  • Kenji's readings wobble for many reasons; Listing 1's noise is ideal. It is Gaussian and independent; real noise often is not. R1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a sensor reading changes when motors run, even with the robot still. Cause: shared ground or supply. How to find it: log the sensor and the motor current together and fit a line (forensic lab). Fix: separate returns; differential measurement.
    • 

  • Symptom: filtered readings lag behind reality and a control loop oscillates. Cause: too much filtering (filter delay). Fix: raise the cut-off; filter only as much as the noise requires; account for delay in control (RB201).
    • 

  • Symptom: averaging more readings stops improving the result. Cause: offset, drift or interference, which are not independent random noise. Fix: calibrate the offset; find the interference source.
    • 

  • Symptom: I2C errors only when the motors start. Cause: ground shift or coupled edges. How to find it: logic analyser and scope on SDA/SCL during a motor start. Fix: wiring, pull-up strength per the bus specification, routing away from motor wires.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. ground.cpp models a sensor whose output is a steady 1.000 V relative to its own ground pin; its ground wire joins the motor's return, and the last 0.05 Ω (pretend) back to the battery is shared; the ADC measures against the battery ground. Logged voltage = 1.000 + Imotor × 0.05 + 2 mV noise; motor current 3 A × command, with 0.15 A noise.


Root cause. Common-impedance ground coupling. Motors off: about 1.000 V. Command 0.5 (about 1.5 A): about 1.075 V. Command 1.0 (about 3 A): about 1.15 V. The step is about 0.075 V per 1.5 A = 0.05 V/A: a resistance of 0.05 Ω. ground_check fits the whole log: slope 0.0500 V/A, correlation 0.999; with the shared segment removed (star ground), slope 0.0000 and correlation −0.023. R4 The wall did not move (the note in the log), and random noise or vibration would not give a voltage proportional to motor current.


Fixes. Wiring: run the sensor's ground directly to the ADC's ground at the star point (verify: new log, slope near 0). Measurement: measure the sensor differentially against its own ground pin, if the ADC supports it (verify the same way). A software correction (subtracting 0.05 × I) is a fallback only: it depends on a resistance that changes with connectors and temperature.


Wrong answers. "More averaging": shown not to help. "Noisy sensor": the off-state readings are steady around 1.000 V.


Prevention. A wiring review that traces every high-current return path; a bring-up test that logs every analog sensor with motors off and pushing against a clamp, and fails if the readings change by more than a set tolerance.
