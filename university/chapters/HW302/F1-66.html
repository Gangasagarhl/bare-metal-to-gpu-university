Story hook

The restaurant makes its own pasta. Ade turns the crank of the pasta machine, and with every notch of the crank a little spring clicks. Ravi, who has to roll out exactly two metres of dough, stands beside him and counts clicks: twenty clicks per turn, so many turns per metre. He never looks at the dough.

One afternoon Ade turns the crank backwards to free a stuck sheet. Ravi keeps counting up. His count is now wrong, and he cannot know it from the clicks alone, because a click sounds the same in both directions. Then the machine is fitted with a second spring, set a little behind the first. Now the two clicks come in an order: first-then-second when the crank goes forwards, second-then-first when it goes backwards. Ravi can count up and down.

That pair of springs is a quadrature encoder. On a robot it sits on a motor shaft or a wheel, and the program that counts its clicks must keep up with it, or it will believe the robot drove backwards.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how incremental (quadrature) and absolute encoders report rotation, and why quadrature needs two channels.
  • Decode a quadrature signal with a state table, counting up, down, and detecting invalid transitions.
  • Convert counts to angle, distance and speed, and choose between the "count in a window" and "time between edges" speed methods.
  • Predict the highest speed a polling decoder can follow, and explain why hardware counters or interrupts are used.
  • Diagnose a decoder that loses counts from a log of true and decoded counts.
Jargon box
Incremental encoder
Simple words: a sensor that gives a pulse for every small step a shaft turns. Analogy: the click of the pasta crank at every notch. Precise: a rotary sensor that outputs pulses as the shaft moves; position is known only relative to where counting started. D1 The robotics courses define Encoder for counting wheel turns (RB101).
Quadrature (A/B channels)
Simple words: two pulse signals a quarter of a step apart, so their order tells the direction. Analogy: the second spring set a little behind the first. Precise: two square waves A and B, 90 electrical degrees out of phase; each cycle passes through four states (00, 01, 11, 10 in one direction), and the order of the states gives the direction. D1
Counts per revolution (CPR) and x4 decoding
Simple words: how many counts one full turn gives. Analogy: twenty clicks per turn of the crank. Precise: an encoder with L lines (pulses per channel per revolution) gives 4L counts per revolution when every edge of both channels is counted (x4 decoding), 2L or L with x2 or x1 decoding. Whether a datasheet quotes lines or counts must be checked. D1 D2
Index pulse
Simple words: one extra pulse once per turn, to find a known position. Analogy: a painted mark on the crank wheel. Precise: a third channel (often called Z or I) that pulses once per revolution and is used to reset or check the count at a reference angle. D1
Absolute encoder
Simple words: a sensor that tells the exact angle at any moment, even just after power-on. Analogy: a crank wheel with the angle printed at every notch. Precise: a rotary sensor that outputs a code for the current angle; optical ones often use a Gray code so that only one bit changes between neighbouring positions. D1 D4
Gray code
Simple words: a way of numbering in which the next number always differs in exactly one bit. Analogy: a row of switches where you only ever flip one at a time to count. Precise: a binary code in which successive values differ in one bit; binary b converts to Gray g = b XOR (b >> 1). D4
Odometry
Simple words: working out how far you went by counting wheel turns. Analogy: Ravi measuring dough by counting clicks. Precise: estimation of displacement from wheel or joint rotation measurements; for a wheel of radius r, distance = 2πr × counts / CPR, valid only while the wheel does not slip. D3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the two channels while the shaft turns forwards and then backwards, with the four states written between the edges. Figure 2 is the same information as a state machine: four states in a ring, one step clockwise counts up, one step anticlockwise counts down, and a jump across the ring cannot be explained.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

An encoder counts how much a shaft turns. Inside, a disk with many slots or many little magnets turns with the shaft. A sensor next to the disk sees a slot, then no slot, then a slot: on, off, on, off. Each change is one count.

One sensor cannot tell forwards from backwards. So encoders have two sensors, A and B, placed a little apart. Going forwards, B changes first and then A. Going backwards, A changes first. The order tells the direction.

The computer must look at A and B often enough. If it looks too rarely, several changes happen while it is not looking. Then it may count the wrong way, or not at all. That is the mistake in this chapter's forensic lab.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

How encoders sense rotation

Like the clicking spring on the crank, an incremental encoder produces a pulse per small step. Optical encoders shine light through a slotted disk onto photodetectors; magnetic encoders sense a magnetised ring with Hall-effect or magnetoresistive sensors. Both produce square waves after a comparator. D1 The number of lines per revolution, the output type (push-pull, open-collector, differential) and the supply voltage come from the named encoder's datasheet. D2

Quadrature decoding

With two channels 90° apart (quadrature), each cycle has four states and four edges. If you sample A and B and compare each new state with the previous one, there are 16 combinations: four "no change", four "+1", four "−1" and four "both changed" (Figure 2's diagonals). D1 Listing 1 stores the answer for each combination in a 16-entry table indexed by (previous << 2) | current. Fed the input 00 01 11 10 00 01 11 10 00 it counts to 8; fed the reverse order it counts down; and the jump 01 → 10 is reported as ERROR: both channels changed, so the decoder cannot know whether two steps went forwards or two went backwards. R1 Counting every edge of both channels is x4 decoding: an encoder with L lines gives 4L counts per revolution. D1

From counts to angle, distance and speed

Angle = 360° × count / CPR. On a wheel of radius r, distance = 2πr × count / CPR: this is odometry, and it is only as good as the assumption that the wheel does not slip. D3 Speed can be estimated in two ways, which Listing 2 compares with a pretend 400 CPR encoder: R2

  • Count in a window (the frequency method): count edges in a fixed time T; speed = Δcount / (CPR × T). Its resolution is one count per window, 1/(400 × 0.010 s) = 0.25 rev/s here, so at 0.1 rev/s the three windows read 0.25, 0, 0: useless at low speed, excellent at high speed. R2
  • Time between edges (the period method): a timer measures the time between two edges; speed = 1 / (CPR × Δt). With a pretend 1 MHz timer it reads 0.1000 rev/s exactly at 0.1 rev/s, but at 150 rev/s only 16 ticks separate the edges and the estimate is 156.25 rev/s, 4 % high. R2

Practical speed estimators switch between the two or combine them. D1

Absolute encoders

An incremental encoder only knows how far it moved since counting began; after power-on, a robot arm with incremental encoders must find a reference (an index pulse or a limit switch). An absolute encoder outputs the angle itself, as a code read in parallel or over a serial bus such as SPI (F1-47). D1 Optical absolute disks often use a Gray code, in which neighbouring positions differ in one bit: if the disk stops on a boundary, a misread bit gives one of the two neighbouring positions, never a far-away one. With plain binary, 011 → 100 changes three bits at once, and a misread could give 111. D4

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Sampling limit. A sampling decoder must see every state, so it must sample at least once per edge: with x4 decoding, the edge rate is 4 × lines × rev/s. If two edges happen between samples, the decoder sees a diagonal (invalid); if three happen, the states look like one step backwards; if four, like no movement. The forensic lab shows all three. D1 R3 This is aliasing (F1-64) on a four-state signal.
  • Hardware decoding. Many microcontroller timers can count quadrature edges in hardware, so no edge is missed regardless of what the software is doing; otherwise, edge interrupts on both channels (F1-42) must be fast enough for the highest edge rate. Whether the kit's microcontroller has such a timer mode is in its reference manual (unverified box below). D5
  • Noise and glitches. Long cables and motor switching put spikes on encoder lines (F1-72). Differential outputs, digital glitch filters on the inputs and the "invalid transition" counter are the usual defences; a non-zero invalid count in normal running means something is wrong. D1
  • Counter width. A 16-bit hardware counter wraps after 65,536 counts; compute differences with unsigned arithmetic and cast the difference to a signed 16-bit value, which is correct as long as fewer than 32,768 counts pass between reads (two's complement, MA102). D4
  • Gear ratios. An encoder on the motor shaft behind a gearbox of ratio G gives G × CPR counts per wheel revolution, and its readings include gearbox backlash; an encoder on the wheel does not see backlash but has fewer counts. The ratio and backlash come from the gearmotor's datasheet. D2

How the hardware actually does it

An optical encoder's photodetectors produce small analog currents; a comparator inside the encoder squares them into logic levels, sometimes with an interpolator that creates more edges per slot. D1 On the microcontroller side the A and B lines enter digital input pins; in a timer's encoder mode, the timer's counter register is incremented or decremented by a small hardware state machine like Figure 2 on every edge, and the software only reads the counter. D5 This is the same division of labour as the IMU's internal converter in F1-65: hardware does the fast, regular part; software reads results.

Worked example

Task. A wheel of diameter 65 mm carries an encoder giving 400 counts per wheel revolution with x4 decoding (pretend values). (a) What distance does one count represent? (b) The counter reads 1234: how far has the wheel rolled, and through what angle? (c) The robot's top speed is 1.5 m/s. What edge rate must the decoder handle, and is polling at 2000 Hz enough? (d) An absolute encoder reports the 3-bit Gray code 110: which position is that?

  1. Circumference. π × 0.065 m = 0.2042 m. One count = 0.2042 / 400 = 0.511 mm.
  2. Distance and angle. 1234 × 0.2042 / 400 = 0.630 m; angle = 1234 / 400 × 360° = 1110.6°, that is 3 full turns plus 30.6°.
  3. Edge rate. 1.5 m/s ÷ 0.2042 m per revolution = 7.35 rev/s; × 400 counts = 2,938 edges per second. A decoder polling at 2000 Hz sees fewer samples than edges: it will miss states (the forensic lab shows what happens). At least 2,938 samples per second are needed in the ideal case, with margin for uneven timing; in practice a hardware counter or edge interrupts.
  4. Gray to binary. The first binary bit equals the first Gray bit: 1. Each next binary bit = previous binary bit XOR next Gray bit: 1 XOR 1 = 0, then 0 XOR 0 = 0. Binary 100 = position 4. Check with g = b XOR (b >> 1): 100 XOR 010 = 110. ✓

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Ravi hears every click; a polling program may not. A person listening hears each click when it happens. A program that looks at the lines every 0.5 ms only sees the state at those moments; edges in between are lost or misread. R3
    • 

  • The two springs click; the encoder's channels have levels. Decoding uses the levels of both channels at each moment, not just "a click happened", which is why the table needs the previous and current state of both. R1
    • 

  • Dough does not slip on the crank; wheels slip on floors. The count measures the wheel's rotation exactly, but the distance the robot moved only while the wheel grips. D3
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the count goes down when the wheel goes forwards. Cause: A and B swapped, or the motor mounted the other way. How to find it: turn the wheel slowly by hand and print the states. Fix: swap the channels in software (a sign), not by rewiring in a hurry.
    • 

  • Symptom: the count is right at low speed, wrong at high speed. Cause: sampling slower than the edge rate. How to find it: an invalid-transition counter that rises with speed (forensic lab). Fix: hardware counter or edge interrupts; check the edge rate against the sample rate.
    • 

  • Symptom: distance is a factor of 4 (or 2) off. Cause: confusion between lines and counts (x1/x2/x4). How to find it: turn the wheel exactly one revolution by hand and read the count. Fix: use the measured CPR.
    • 

  • Symptom: a sudden jump of about 65,536 counts. Cause: the hardware counter wrapped and the difference was computed without wrap-around handling. Fix: signed 16-bit difference of unsigned readings (Layer 3).
    • 

  • Symptom: odometry drifts on a smooth floor while the counts look clean. Cause: wheel slip, or a wrong wheel diameter. Fix: calibrate the diameter by driving a measured distance; combine with other sensors (RB302).
    •

Summary


    

  • Incremental encoders give pulses per step; two channels in quadrature give direction; an index pulse gives a reference.
    • 

  • A quadrature decoder is a four-state machine: one step clockwise +1, anticlockwise −1, a diagonal is invalid.
    • 

  • x4 decoding counts every edge: CPR = 4 × lines; check which one a datasheet quotes.
    • 

  • Counts → angle, distance (odometry, valid without slip) and speed.
    • 

  • Window method: good at high speed; period method: good at low speed.
    • 

  • Absolute encoders report the angle directly, often in Gray code.
    • 

  • A decoder must see every edge: missed edges give invalid jumps or even reversed counts; hardware counters prevent this.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. polling.cpp moves a perfect encoder with constant acceleration (true count = ½ × 4000 × t², so the count rate rises by 4000 counts/s every second) and polls the A/B state every 0.5 ms (2000 Hz), decoding with the table of Listing 1. No noise was added; the wiring is correct.


Root cause. Polling at 2000 Hz cannot follow more than 2000 edges per second. The log agrees exactly up to 0.50 s (2000 counts/s) and diverges from 0.60 s (2400 counts/s), when the invalid count starts to rise: two edges between polls produce diagonal states. Between 0.5 and 1.0 s the decoded count still rises but more slowly (one +1 for each single step, nothing for each double step). Above 4000 counts/s (after 1.0 s), three edges between polls are common: the table reads them as −1, so the decoded count falls (1000 at 1.0 s, 500 at 1.5 s, 0 at 2.0 s), and from 1.5 s the invalid count stops changing because four edges between polls look like "no change". At 2.0 s the true rate is 8000 counts/s, exactly four edges per poll, and the decoder sees nothing at all.


Wrong answers. "Channels swapped": then the count would be negative from the start, not correct up to 0.5 s. "Electrical noise": noise would not produce a clean, speed-dependent pattern on a log without any noise in it, and it would not be zero below a sharp threshold.


Fixes. Use the microcontroller's hardware quadrature counter, or edge interrupts on both channels fast enough for the maximum edge rate; or (worse) poll much faster than the maximum edge rate with margin. Verify by re-running the same acceleration log and requiring decoded = true and invalid = 0 to the top speed.


Prevention. The design states the maximum edge rate (from top speed, CPR and gear ratio) next to the decoder's capability; the firmware reports the invalid-transition counter and raises a fault if it is ever non-zero.
