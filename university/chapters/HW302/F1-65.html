Story hook

Lucía carries a full bowl of soup from the kitchen to table nine. She does not look at the soup, yet she knows when the bowl tilts, because she feels the weight shift in her wrist. She knows when she turns a corner, because she feels herself rotate. And on the long walk past the windows she knows which way she is facing, because she can see the sunlit courtyard on her left.

Three senses, three different questions: which way is down, how fast am I turning, which way am I facing. Each one alone is not enough. Her wrist cannot tell a tilt from a sudden push. Her sense of turning drifts if she spins slowly for a long time. The courtyard helps only where she can see it.

A robot's IMU is a chip with the same three senses: an accelerometer, a gyroscope and often a magnetometer. This chapter opens that chip, reads it over a bus, calibrates it, and then uses a spectrum to catch it lying.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what an accelerometer, a gyroscope and a magnetometer each measure, in which units, and the main error of each (bias, noise, drift, disturbance).
  • Write a driver that probes an IMU over I2C, configures its range, reads raw two's-complement registers and converts them to physical units.
  • Calibrate a gyroscope's bias and compute tilt (roll and pitch) from a resting accelerometer.
  • Compute and read an amplitude spectrum with a discrete Fourier transform, and check it against an independent tool.
  • Diagnose vibration-induced noise in a logged IMU signal from its spectrum.
Jargon box
IMU (inertial measurement unit)
Simple words: a small chip that feels tilting, pushing and turning. Analogy: Lucía's wrist and her sense of turning while she carries the soup. Precise: a sensor package that measures specific force with accelerometers and angular rate with gyroscopes, usually along three perpendicular axes each; a magnetometer is often added. D1 (The F9 courses use the registered analogy "the balance sense in your inner ear", analogy registry.)
Accelerometer
Simple words: a sensor that feels pushes, and also feels gravity pulling down. Analogy: the weight of the bowl shifting in Lucía's wrist. Precise: a sensor whose output is the specific force along its axis: acceleration minus gravitational acceleration, so at rest it reads 1 g directed opposite to gravity (upwards). D1
Specific force
Simple words: what an accelerometer really feels: every push except gravity's own pull. Analogy: the table pushing up on the bowl when it rests. Precise: the non-gravitational force per unit mass, f = a − g (vectors), in m/s² or in units of g. D1
Gyroscope (rate gyro)
Simple words: a sensor that measures how fast it is turning. Analogy: Lucía feeling herself turn the corner. Precise: a sensor whose output is the angular rate about its axis, in degrees or radians per second; MEMS gyros used in IMUs measure rate, not angle. D1
Magnetometer
Simple words: a compass sensor: it measures the magnetic field around it. Analogy: seeing the sunlit courtyard and knowing which way you face. Precise: a sensor that measures the local magnetic field vector; heading can be estimated from its horizontal components once tilt is known, and nearby iron and electric currents disturb it. D1
Bias
Simple words: a sensor's reading when the true value is zero. Analogy: a kitchen scale that shows 3 grams with nothing on it. Precise: a constant or slowly varying offset added to a sensor's output; it is estimated (calibrated) and subtracted. D1
Drift (of an integrated gyro)
Simple words: the angle error that keeps growing when you add up turning rates. Analogy: after spinning slowly for a long time, Lucía no longer knows which way she faces. Precise: the growth of error in an angle obtained by integrating rate measurements; a constant bias b produces an error b·t that grows linearly with time, and noise adds a random walk. D1 R2
Full-scale range
Simple words: the largest value the sensor can report before it saturates. Analogy: the highest mark on the scale's dial. Precise: the configured measurement range (for example ±4 g); with a 16-bit signed output, one count is range / 32768. D4 R1
DFT (discrete Fourier transform)
Simple words: a calculation that tells how much of each frequency a list of samples contains. Analogy: sorting the kitchen's noise into the hum of the fridge, the whirr of the mixer and the clatter of plates. Precise: for N samples x[n], X[k] = Σ x[n]·e−i2πkn/N, k = 0 … N−1; bin k corresponds to k·fs/N hertz; the FFT is a fast algorithm that computes the same values. D2
Amplitude spectrum
Simple words: a chart of "how big is each frequency" in a signal. Analogy: a list of each kitchen machine and how loud it is. Precise: for a real signal, A[k] = 2|X[k]|/N for 0 < k < N/2 gives the amplitude of a sinusoid at bin frequency k·fs/N. D2 R4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

An IMU is a chip about the size of a fingernail. Inside are tiny parts that bend a little when the chip is pushed or turned. Circuits measure how much they bend and turn that into numbers.

The accelerometer feels pushes. When the chip sits still on a table, it still feels something: the table pushing up against gravity. So a resting accelerometer tells you which way is up, and from that, how much the chip is tilted.

The gyroscope feels turning speed. To know how far you turned, you add up the speeds over time. But every gyroscope has a small error, even when still. Add up a small error long enough and it becomes a big one. That is called drift.

The magnetometer feels the Earth's magnetic field, like a compass. Nearby iron and motors confuse it. Because every sense has a weakness, robots combine them, which is a story for later courses.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What the accelerometer measures

Lucía's wrist feels the bowl's weight, and also every push she gives it. An accelerometer measures specific force: the vector f = a − g, where a is the sensor's acceleration and g is gravitational acceleration (pointing down). At rest, a = 0, so f = −g: a vector of size 1 g pointing up. D1 If the sensor's z axis is up when it lies level, a level resting sensor reads (0, 0, +1) g. Tilted by roll φ about x and pitch θ about y, it reads (−sin θ, sin φ cos θ, cos φ cos θ) g, the formula used inside the university's pretend IMU (line 103 of uimu_model.h). D1 Inverting it gives tilt from a resting reading:

roll = atan2(fy, fz), pitch = atan2(−fx, √(fy² + fz²))

Listing 1 recovers roll 30.07° and pitch −9.99° for a model tilted by 30° and −10°, and the length of the averaged vector is 0.9984 g. R1 The formula holds only when a = 0. A robot that accelerates, bumps or vibrates adds a to f, and the "tilt" computed this way is wrong while that lasts. D1

What the gyroscope measures

The gyroscope outputs angular rate ω about each axis, plus a bias b and noise. D1 An angle is obtained by integrating: θ(t) = θ(0) + ∫ω dt, in a program θ += ω·Δt every sample. A bias b adds b·t to the angle, without limit. Listing 2 integrates a gyro that is not turning at all, with a pretend bias of 0.10 °/s: after 60 s the angle reads 5.964°, close to 0.10 × 60 = 6°. With the bias subtracted, the error after 60 s is −0.036°: the noise's random walk, much smaller but still growing. R2 Calibration estimates b by averaging while the sensor is known to be still; Listing 1's 200-sample average gives +0.803, −0.349, +0.098 °/s for a model bias of +0.80, −0.35, +0.10. R1 Real biases change with temperature and time, which is why estimators keep re-estimating them (RB302, DN301). D1

What the magnetometer measures

The magnetometer measures the local magnetic field vector. Once tilt is known from the accelerometer, the field can be projected onto the horizontal plane and a heading computed. D1 The field near a robot is distorted by magnetised parts and by iron that bends the field, and by the currents in motor wires; calibration (and placing the sensor away from motors) reduces but does not remove this. D1 No magnetometer is modelled in this chapter's programs; its calibration is practised in DN202.

From registers to units

Most IMU chips contain the whole chain of F1-64 for each axis and present results in registers read over I2C or SPI (F1-47). D4 A driver therefore does four things: probe (read an identity register and compare with the documented value), configure (write the full-scale range and data rate), read (a burst of data registers) and convert (bytes → signed counts → units). The university's pretend part, U-IMU6, stores each axis as a 16-bit two's-complement number, high byte first; with range ±R, one count is R/32768. R1 In the run, the z bytes 1B 6D form 0x1B6D = 7021 counts = 7021 × 4 / 32768 = 0.8571 g at ±4 g. Swapping the two bytes by mistake gives 27931 counts and an impossible 3.41 g. R1 Which byte comes first, whether the values are signed, the scale factors and the axis directions are defined only by the real part's datasheet. D4

Seeing vibration with a spectrum

Motors and wheels shake the robot, and the accelerometer feels every shake. In a time plot this looks like "noise". A DFT splits a block of N samples into frequency bins k·fs/N; the amplitude spectrum 2|X[k]|/N shows how big a sinusoid at each bin is. D2 Random sensor noise spreads thinly over all bins; vibration from a rotating part piles up at its rotation frequency and its multiples (harmonics). D2 Listing 3 computes the DFT directly from its definition; the forensic lab applies it.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • MEMS mechanics. MEMS accelerometers measure the displacement of a small proof mass on springs, usually by capacitance; MEMS gyros measure the Coriolis effect on a vibrating mass. Both have resonances, and vibration near those or above the output data rate can alias into the band you use (F1-64). D1 Datasheets specify internal digital low-pass filters and output data rates; choose them together. D4
  • Error model. A standard per-axis model is measurement = scale × true + bias + noise, plus cross-axis coupling and misalignment; full calibration estimates scale, bias and alignment from several known orientations (for an accelerometer, the six faces of a cube against gravity). D1 Datasheet noise densities and bias stability figures come from the named part (D4).
  • Clipping. Large vibration can exceed the full-scale range; clipped samples cannot be averaged back. A clipped sine's average is no longer zero, so clipping also shifts the mean and corrupts tilt. Choose a range with headroom for the vibration you measure. R1
  • DFT details. The bin width is fs/N, so 1000 samples at 1000 Hz give 1 Hz bins (Listing 3). A frequency between two bins spreads into neighbours ("leakage"); a window function reduces that at the cost of wider peaks. The direct DFT costs N² operations; an FFT gets the same numbers in about N log N. D2 numpy's FFT on the same file gives the same four peaks to four decimals (R5), which is the cross-check of Listing 3. R5
  • I2C details. I2C transfers are framed by start and stop conditions; each byte is acknowledged by the receiver; the controller reading data acknowledges each byte except the last; a device that does not acknowledge its address signals absence. D3 A driver must turn a missing acknowledge into an error, not wait forever (Listing 1, step 6; and curriculum milestone C14's acceptance test, C1).

How the hardware actually does it

On the microcontroller side, the I2C peripheral generates start and stop conditions, shifts bytes out and in on the data line while it drives the clock line, and reports events (address acknowledged, byte received, no acknowledge) through status flags and interrupts; your driver feeds it bytes and reacts to those events (F1-47, F1-42). D3 On the IMU side, the converter samples each axis at the configured output data rate and latches results into the data registers; many parts guarantee that a burst read returns all axes from the same sample. D4 The pretend U-IMU6 does this explicitly: reading the first data register latches a fresh sample (line 78 of the model). R1

A real IMU's address options, identity register, register map, byte order, range settings, scale factors, axis directions, data rates, filters and start-up time come only from its datasheet (D4, dossier part F). Whether a real part latches all axes together is also a datasheet question.

Worked example

Task. A U-IMU6 is configured to ±4 g and ±500 °/s. A burst read returns the twelve bytes of Listing 1's step 3: 07 55 0F F8 1B 6D 00 32 FF E5 00 06. Convert every axis.

  1. Scale. Accelerometer: 4 / 32768 g per count = 0.00012207 g. Gyro: 500 / 32768 = 0.015259 °/s per count.
  2. AX = 0x0755 = 1877 → 0.2291 g. AY = 0x0FF8 = 4088 → 0.4990 g. AZ = 0x1B6D = 7021 → 0.8571 g.
  3. GX = 0x0032 = 50 → 0.763 °/s. GY = 0xFFE5: the top bit is 1, so it is negative: 0xFFE5 − 0x10000 = −27 → −0.412 °/s. GZ = 0x0006 = 6 → 0.092 °/s.
  4. Sanity check. |a| = √(0.2291² + 0.4990² + 0.8571²) = √(0.0525 + 0.2490 + 0.7346) = √1.0361 = 1.018 g: one noisy sample of a resting sensor, close to 1 g. The 200-sample average in step 5 of the output is 0.9984 g. R1
  5. Tilt from this one sample. roll = atan2(0.4990, 0.8571) = 30.2°; pitch = atan2(−0.2291, √(0.4990² + 0.8571²)) = atan2(−0.2291, 0.9918) = −13.0°. The single sample is 3° off in pitch because of noise; the average gives −9.99°.

The gyro values (0.76, −0.41, 0.09 °/s) are bias plus noise: the model is not turning. Integrating them without calibration would make the robot "turn" by itself.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lucía knows when she is pushing; the accelerometer does not. She can tell her own push from a tilt. The accelerometer measures their sum, f = a − g, and cannot separate them without other information. D1
    • 

  • Lucía's drift is vague; a gyro's is arithmetic. A bias of b degrees per second produces exactly b·t degrees of error, as Listing 2 shows (5.964° after 60 s for 0.10 °/s). R2
    • 

  • The courtyard is always where it is; the magnetic field near a robot is not. Motor currents and steel parts change the field the magnetometer sees, sometimes more than the Earth's field itself; seeing the courtyard is never disturbed by the soup. D1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: impossible values such as 3.4 g at rest. Cause: bytes combined in the wrong order. How to find it: print raw bytes and compare both orders (Listing 1, step 3). Fix: follow the datasheet's byte order.
    • 

  • Symptom: small negative values appear as large positive ones (65,509 instead of −27). Cause: the 16-bit value was not interpreted as signed. How to find it: values near 65,536 in a quantity that should hover around zero. Fix: cast to std::int16_t as be16 does.
    • 

  • Symptom: readings exactly twice or half of what you expect. Cause: the scale used in software does not match the range written to the chip. How to find it: read the configuration register back. Fix: derive the scale from the value actually written.
    • 

  • Symptom: the heading turns slowly while the robot is still. Cause: gyro bias not removed, or estimated while the robot moved. How to find it: integrate a still recording (Listing 2). Fix: calibrate at rest; re-estimate when temperature changes.
    • 

  • Symptom: the program hangs when the sensor is unplugged. Cause: the driver waits forever for an acknowledge. How to find it: unplug it on purpose (simulated in step 6). Fix: treat no-acknowledge and timeouts as errors.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F1-64 built the measurement chain; F1-47 introduced I2C and SPI; MA201 gave vectors and rotations. What carries over: codes, step sizes and sampling rules; register reads over a bus; two's-complement numbers (MA102). D3 What changes: the chain is now inside the sensor, configured by registers you write; measurements are three-dimensional vectors in the sensor's own axes; gravity is always in the accelerometer's reading; integrating a rate turns small biases into growing errors; and vibration must be looked at in the frequency domain. D1 What comes next: F1-66 measures rotation a different way, by counting the steps of a wheel, which never drifts while the wheel does not slip.

Forensic lab


Scenario: "The jumpy IMU". Hana's robot estimates its tilt from the accelerometer. Standing still with the drive motors off, the tilt estimate is steady. With the motors running (wheels lifted, robot clamped), the estimate jumps by many degrees from sample to sample. Hana suspects a broken sensor. She records one second of the z accelerometer with motors off and one with motors on, at 1000 samples per second.


Evidence pack. The logger's summary and the start of jumpy_imu.csv (R3), the spectrum computed by Listing 3 (R4), and the same peaks computed independently by numpy (R5). The data are synthetic: generated by the university's program jumpy_log.cpp, not recorded on a robot; how it was made is in the key.

Method hints. A faulty sensor and electrical noise usually spread energy over many frequencies or produce steps and spikes; a rotating machine produces lines at its rotation frequency and multiples. Summary


    

  • An IMU combines accelerometers (specific force), gyroscopes (angular rate) and often a magnetometer (magnetic field).
    • 

  • At rest the accelerometer reads 1 g upwards; tilt follows from atan2 of its components, but only while the robot is not accelerating.
    • 

  • Integrating a gyro turns a bias b into an error b·t: 0.10 °/s gave 5.96° in 60 s; calibration at rest removes most of it.
    • 

  • Magnetometers give heading but are disturbed by iron and motor currents.
    • 

  • A driver probes, configures, burst-reads and converts: byte order, sign and scale come from the datasheet.
    • 

  • I2C reads are framed by start, address, acknowledges, repeated start, not-acknowledge and stop; a missing acknowledge must become an error.
    • 

  • A DFT exposes vibration as peaks at a rotation frequency and its harmonics; random noise spreads thinly across all bins.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. jumpy_log.cpp wrote 1000 samples at 1000 Hz of 1.0 g plus Gaussian noise (standard deviation 0.01 g) for the motors-off column, and the same plus 0.30 g at 87 Hz and 0.08 g at 174 Hz (phase 1 rad) for the motors-on column. Nothing else was changed.


Root cause. Mechanical vibration from the drive train reaching the IMU. Evidence: the mean is unchanged (1.0002 g and 1.0000 g) while the standard deviation grows from 0.0102 g to 0.2194 g; the motors-on spectrum has exactly two lines, 87 Hz (0.2995 g) and 174 Hz (0.0795 g), while every other bin stays at the noise floor (about 0.001–0.0015 g, as in the motors-off spectrum). A second harmonic at exactly 2 × 87 Hz is the signature of a periodic mechanical source (an imbalance or a once-per-revolution impact), not of a random sensor fault. The C++ DFT and numpy agree on all peaks (R4, R5).


Why the tilt jumps. Each sample's f includes up to ±0.38 g of vibration, so atan2 of single samples swings by many degrees; the average of the vibration over whole cycles is near zero, which is why the mean is unchanged.


Fixes. Mechanical: balance the rotating part, stiffen the frame, or soft-mount the IMU; verify with a new log whose 87 Hz peak is much smaller. Signal processing: a low-pass filter well below 87 Hz before computing tilt (the exponential filter of F1-72, Listing 2, attenuates 87 Hz to 0.19 of its size at a = 0.1) or the IMU's own digital low-pass filter; verify by recomputing the spectrum of the filtered signal. Choose the IMU's data rate and filter so that vibration is not aliased.


Wrong answers. "Broken sensor": the motors-off data are clean and the noise floor is the same in both. "Electrical noise": possible in general, but it would not normally produce one clean line and its exact harmonic that track a mechanical rotation; measuring with the motor powered but mechanically decoupled would separate the two.


Prevention. A bring-up test that logs the IMU at a high rate with motors at several speeds and checks the spectrum for peaks above a threshold.
