Story hook

On a bright afternoon, Nadia closes the storeroom door and the room goes dark, except for a small round hole in the shutter of the window. On the opposite wall a picture appears: the sunny courtyard, the herb pots, the delivery bicycle, all upside down. Nobody painted it. Light from each point outside travels in a straight line through the hole and lands on one spot of the wall.

Farid, curious, holds a sheet of squared paper against the wall and starts copying the picture square by square, from the top row down. By the time he reaches the bottom, the bicycle has moved, and his drawing shows a bicycle that leans as if it were falling over.

The hole in the shutter is a pinhole camera. Farid's squared paper, filled row by row, is an image sensor with a rolling shutter. This chapter computes where a point lands, how light becomes a number in each square, how colour is guessed, and why a moving pole can lean.

What you will be able to do

  • Project a 3D point to a pixel with the pinhole model and its intrinsic parameters, and back-project a pixel to a ray.
  • Explain how a pixel turns light into a number: exposure, full well, gain, quantisation and photon shot noise.
  • Describe how a colour filter mosaic and demosaicing produce a colour image, and where they create false colours.
  • Compare rolling and global shutters, and predict the skew of a moving object.
  • Diagnose leaning objects in images as rolling-shutter distortion from the row timing.
Jargon box
Pinhole camera model
Simple words: the simplest description of a camera: every ray of light goes through one point and hits a flat image. Analogy: the hole in Nadia's shutter and the picture on the wall. Precise: a projection in which a point (X, Y, Z) in the camera frame maps to image coordinates u = fx·X/Z + cx, v = fy·Y/Z + cy; real lenses add distortion that is calibrated separately. D2 D1
Intrinsic parameters
Simple words: the numbers that describe one camera's own geometry: how strongly it magnifies and where its centre is in the image. Analogy: how far the wall is from the hole, and where on the wall the hole's straight-ahead line lands. Precise: the focal lengths fx, fy in pixels and the principal point (cx, cy), often written as the calibration matrix K; obtained by calibration. D2
Image sensor (CMOS)
Simple words: a chip covered in tiny light meters, one per pixel. Analogy: Farid's squared paper, one square per pixel. Precise: an array of photodiodes that convert light into charge during the exposure; in a CMOS sensor, each pixel has its own readout transistors and rows are read through column circuits and ADCs. D1
Exposure and full well
Simple words: how long each pixel collects light, and the most light it can hold. Analogy: how long a bucket stands in the rain, and how much it holds before it overflows. Precise: the exposure (integration) time is the interval during which a pixel accumulates photo-generated charge; the full-well capacity is the maximum charge before the pixel saturates. D1
Shot noise
Simple words: the natural randomness of light: even steady light arrives as uneven raindrops. Analogy: two buckets in the same steady rain never collect exactly the same number of drops. Precise: photon arrivals (and the electrons they produce) follow Poisson statistics, so a mean of N electrons has standard deviation √N and signal-to-noise ratio √N. D3 D5
Colour filter array (Bayer mosaic)
Simple words: tiny coloured filters over the pixels, so each pixel measures only red, green or blue. Analogy: Farid colouring each square with only one of three crayons, in a fixed pattern. Precise: a mosaic of colour filters over the sensor; the common Bayer pattern repeats a 2 × 2 tile with one red, two green and one blue filter; full colour is estimated by demosaicing. D1
Demosaicing
Simple words: guessing the two missing colours at every pixel from its neighbours. Analogy: filling in each square's missing crayons by looking at the squares around it. Precise: interpolation of a full RGB image from a colour-filter-array image; simple methods produce false colours along sharp edges. D1 R3
Rolling shutter and global shutter
Simple words: reading the picture one row after another, or all at once. Analogy: Farid copying the wall row by row, versus taking a photograph of the whole wall at once. Precise: with a rolling shutter, each row's exposure starts and ends a fixed row time after the previous row's, so moving objects are skewed; with a global shutter, all pixels expose during the same interval. D1 D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A camera is a dark box with a small opening. Light from every point of the scene goes straight through the opening and lands on one spot inside. Near things make big pictures; far things make small ones. That is why a pole twice as far away looks half as tall.

Inside a digital camera, the light lands on a chip covered with millions of tiny light buckets, the pixels. Each bucket collects light for a short time and then is emptied and measured. More light, or a longer time, gives a bigger number. If a bucket overflows, it just says "full", and the details are lost.

The buckets cannot see colour. So each one gets a tiny coloured filter: red, green or blue. The camera then guesses the full colour of each pixel from its neighbours. Usually the guess is good. At sharp edges it can invent colours that were never there.

Many cameras read their buckets one row at a time. If something moves fast, the top rows see it in one place and the bottom rows in another. A straight pole then looks as if it leans.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Projection: the pinhole model

Nadia's hole sends each point's light along one straight line. With the camera frame's Z axis pointing forward (X right, Y down, a common computer-vision convention), similar triangles give the pinhole model: u = fx·X/Z + cx, v = fy·Y/Z + cy. D2 The intrinsic parameters fx, fy (focal lengths in pixels) and (cx, cy) (the principal point) are found by calibrating each camera; real lenses also bend straight lines (radial and tangential distortion), which calibration models and removes. D2 D1 Listing 1 uses pretend values f = 600 px and principal point (320, 240) for a 640 × 480 image: a 1 m pole at 2 m is 300 px tall, at 4 m 150 px, at 8 m 75 px. R1 At 1 m its top lands at v = −60, outside the image: the formula does not know about image borders. A point behind the camera (Z ≤ 0) has no image at all. R1

Projection loses depth: every point on the ray X/Z = 0.2, Y/Z = 0 lands on pixel (440, 240). Going back from a pixel gives a ray, not a point; depth needs a second view (stereo, F1-67), a known size, or a depth sensor. D2 R1

From light to a pixel value

Each pixel's photodiode turns arriving photons into electrons during the exposure time; the charge is converted to a voltage, amplified (the gain) and digitised by an ADC (F1-64). D1 A pixel holds at most its full-well capacity; more light saturates it at the top code. D1 Light itself is noisy: photon arrivals follow Poisson statistics, so a pixel that collects N electrons on average varies with standard deviation √N, and its signal-to-noise ratio is N/√N = √N. D3 D5 Listing 2 tabulates this for a pretend pixel (full well 10,000 electrons, 10-bit output): 2 electrons per ms for 1 ms gives code 0 and SNR 1.4; 50 per ms for 40 ms gives code 204 and SNR 44.7; 1000 per ms for 40 ms saturates at 1023. R2 Longer exposure helps dark scenes but blurs motion and saturates bright parts; gain raises the numbers but not the SNR of the light itself.

Colour: the Bayer mosaic

Photodiodes measure intensity, not colour. A colour filter array puts one filter over each pixel; the Bayer pattern repeats R G / G B, with two greens per tile. D1 Demosaicing estimates the missing two colours per pixel. Listing 3 uses the crudest method, one colour per 2 × 2 tile, on a scene with a red-white edge in the middle of a tile: the tile across the edge becomes (9, 4.5, 9), a magenta-like colour that is neither red nor white. R3 Better algorithms use larger neighbourhoods and edge directions, but every method guesses, and sharp, high-contrast edges are where guesses fail. D1 For robot vision, a monochrome sensor (no filters) gives full resolution and more light per pixel when colour is not needed.

Rolling and global shutters

Farid filled his paper row by row. Many CMOS sensors do the same: each row's exposure is shifted by a fixed row time from the previous row's (Figure 3), so the image is not one instant. D1 If an object moves horizontally at v pixels per second and the row time is tr, row r sees it shifted by v·tr·r pixels: vertical edges lean. A global shutter exposes all pixels during the same interval and has no such skew. D1 The row time and shutter type of a sensor are in its datasheet. D4 The forensic lab measures this skew.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • The camera matrix. With homogeneous coordinates, projection is x ≃ K [R | t] X, where K holds the intrinsics and R, t the camera's pose (extrinsics). Calibration estimates K, distortion and, for multiple cameras, the relative poses; reprojection error in pixels is its quality measure. D2 MA201's rotations and matrices are exactly what is used here.
  • Noise budget. Besides shot noise, sensors have read noise (added at readout, independent of light), dark current (charge that accumulates without light, growing with temperature and exposure), fixed-pattern noise and quantisation. At low light, read noise dominates; at high light, shot noise does. Values are per sensor and come from its datasheet or from measuring a dark and a flat frame. D1 D4
  • Dynamic range and HDR. The ratio between full well and the noise floor limits the range of brightness one exposure can capture; some sensors combine several exposures (HDR) at the cost of motion artefacts. D1
  • Rolling shutter in geometry. For a rolling-shutter camera on a moving robot, each row has its own pose; visual odometry and SLAM either model this or prefer global-shutter sensors. D1
  • Interfaces and data rates. Width × height × bits per pixel × frames per second sets the data rate; camera modules connect over parallel buses, serial camera interfaces (the MIPI Alliance's CSI-2 is common on SoCs) or USB. The curriculum notes that camera sensors are identified by a chip-ID register read over I2C, the control channel next to the image data path. C1 D4
  • Timestamps. For fusion with an IMU (F1-65), you need the time at which each row (or the middle of the exposure) was captured, not when the frame arrived in memory; sensors and drivers differ in what they report. D4

How the hardware actually does it

In a CMOS sensor, each pixel contains a photodiode and a few transistors: reset, transfer, a source-follower amplifier and a row-select switch. To read a row, its select line is enabled; every column's amplifier passes that row's pixel voltages to column circuits, which sample them (often twice, once after reset and once after transfer, and subtract: correlated double sampling) and convert them with column ADCs. D1 Rows are selected one after another, which is the physical origin of the rolling shutter; a global-shutter pixel adds a storage element so that all pixels can stop exposing at once and wait to be read. D1 The control side (exposure time, gain, region of interest, frame rate) is a set of registers written over I2C; the image data leave over a separate high-speed interface. D4

Worked example

Task. A camera has fx = fy = 600 px, principal point (320, 240) (pretend values). (a) A doorway 0.9 m wide, centred straight ahead, appears 135 px wide. How far away is it? (b) Where does the point (−0.3, 0.2, 1.5) m project? (c) The camera has a rolling shutter with a row time of 20 µs and 480 rows (pretend), and the robot turns so that the scene moves at 1500 px/s across the image. How far does a vertical edge lean between the top and bottom rows?

  1. Distance from size. width in pixels = f·W/Z, so Z = f·W/width = 600 × 0.9 / 135 = 4.0 m.
  2. Projection. u = 600 × (−0.3)/1.5 + 320 = −120 + 320 = 200; v = 600 × 0.2/1.5 + 240 = 80 + 240 = 320. Pixel (200, 320): left of centre and below it, as expected for negative X and positive (downward) Y.
  3. Skew. Time from top row to bottom row = 479 × 20 µs = 9.58 ms. Shift = 1500 px/s × 0.00958 s = 14.4 px. A vertical pole leans by about 14 px over the image height: small but enough to bias a line detector.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A pinhole is sharp at all distances; a lens is not. The tiny hole lets very little light in; cameras use lenses to collect more, which brings focus, depth of field and distortion that the pinhole model leaves out. D1
    • 

  • Farid looks at the colours; the pixel cannot. A pixel counts electrons only; colour exists only because a filter was placed over it, and the other two colours at that pixel are guesses. R3
    • 

  • Farid can be slow or quick; a sensor's row time is fixed. The skew of a rolling shutter is predictable from the row time and the motion, which is what makes it diagnosable (forensic lab). R4
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: distances computed from images are consistently off by a factor. Cause: focal length in millimetres used where pixels are needed, or intrinsics from a different resolution. How to find it: measure an object of known size at a known distance. Fix: calibrate at the resolution you use; f in pixels = f in mm / pixel pitch (both from the datasheet).
    • 

  • Symptom: bright regions are flat white with no detail. Cause: saturation. How to find it: count pixels at the top code. Fix: shorter exposure or lower gain; HDR if available.
    • 

  • Symptom: coloured fringes along sharp edges. Cause: demosaicing (Listing 3) or the wrong Bayer order (RGGB versus GRBG). How to find it: a red object appearing blue indicates the wrong order. Fix: set the order from the datasheet; use monochrome when colour is not needed.
    • 

  • Symptom: straight objects lean or wobble when the robot turns or vibrates. Cause: rolling shutter. Fix: global shutter sensor, shorter readout, or model the row timing.
    •

Summary


    

  • Pinhole model: u = fxX/Z + cx, v = fyY/Z + cy; size in pixels falls as 1/Z.
    • 

  • A pixel is a ray; depth needs another view, a known size, or a depth sensor.

  • Pixels count electrons during exposure; they saturate at full well and are quantised by an ADC.

  • Shot noise: √N; SNR = √N; four times the light doubles the SNR.

  • A Bayer mosaic measures one colour per pixel; demosaicing guesses the rest and can make false colours at edges.
    • 

  • Rolling shutters expose rows at different times: moving objects lean by v·tr per row; global shutters do not.

  • Sensor parameters (row time, full well, noise, Bayer order) come only from the datasheet.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. rolling.cpp renders a one-column pole at column 10 + v·t, where t = row × 1.0 ms is the row's capture time; v = 0 for Image A and 0.8 columns/ms for Image B. The lens is ideal: no distortion was modelled.


Root cause. Rolling-shutter skew. Evidence: Image A is straight (column 10 in every row), so the lens and the pole are straight. In Image B the pole is at column 10 in row 0 (t = 0) and column 22 in row 15 (t = 15 ms): 12 columns in 15 ms = 0.8 columns per ms, exactly the image motion during the turn. The lean grows linearly with row number, as rolling-shutter timing predicts.


Fixes. Hardware: a global-shutter camera (verify: Image B straight while turning). Software: correct each row's position with the robot's rotation rate from the IMU and the row time, or let the line detector accept lines whose lean matches the predicted skew; verify on the same turning sequence. Operational: slow the turn while detecting.


Wrong answers. "Lens distortion": it would bend lines in Image A too and would not depend on motion. "The pole leans": it is straight in Image A.


Prevention. Read the shutter type and row (line) time in the sensor datasheet when choosing the camera; add a bring-up test that images a vertical target while the robot turns at its maximum rate.
