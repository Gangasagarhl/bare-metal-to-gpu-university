Story hook

The restaurant's water comes from a tank on the roof. When nobody uses water, the pressure at every tap is good. Hamid starts the big dishwasher in the basement, and at that moment Grace, at the sink upstairs, sees her tap sputter and nearly stop. The tank is not empty. The trouble is that the tank's outlet is narrow and the dishwasher and the sink share the same pipe for most of the way down. The more the dishwasher pulls, the less pressure is left for everyone else.

Worse: the coffee machine at the end of the same pipe has a safety switch. When the pressure drops too low, it shuts down and restarts its long warm-up. Every time the dishwasher starts, the coffee machine resets.

A robot has the same plumbing. The battery is the tank with its narrow outlet (internal resistance), the motors are the dishwasher, and the microcontroller is the coffee machine with its brown-out switch. This chapter computes packs, run time and voltage sag, and then diagnoses a robot that reboots every time it drives off. It also sets out the safety rules for lithium-polymer batteries, which store a lot of energy in a small, easily damaged package.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute a pack's voltage, capacity and energy from its series and parallel layout.
  • Use C-rates and a power budget to estimate run time with a margin.
  • Model a battery as an open-circuit voltage and an internal resistance, and predict voltage sag under load.
  • Diagnose brown-out resets from a voltage and current log, and choose a fix.
  • Follow the course's LiPo safety rules and know which numbers must come from the battery maker.
Jargon box
Cell, series and parallel (S and P)
Simple words: a battery pack is several cells joined end to end (more volts) and side by side (more capacity). Analogy: stacking tanks on top of each other raises the pressure; putting them side by side holds more water. Precise: in an nSmP pack, n cells in series multiply the voltage by n, and m strings in parallel multiply the capacity by m; energy scales with both. D4 R1
Capacity (Ah) and energy (Wh)
Simple words: how much charge a battery can give, and how much work that can do. Analogy: how many litres the tank holds, and how much those litres can do at the tank's pressure. Precise: capacity in ampere-hours is the charge delivered under stated test conditions; energy in watt-hours is approximately capacity × average voltage. Both are rated by the maker under specific conditions. D4 D1
C-rate
Simple words: a way to say how fast you charge or discharge, compared with the battery's size. Analogy: "1C" empties the tank in about an hour; "2C" in about half an hour. Precise: current divided by the rated capacity in Ah (per hour); the maker states maximum continuous and burst discharge C-rates and the maximum charge rate. D1 D4
Internal resistance and voltage sag
Simple words: a battery's voltage drops when you take a lot of current, because the battery itself resists it. Analogy: the narrow tank outlet: the faster the flow, the more pressure is lost there. Precise: to first order, terminal voltage = open-circuit voltage − I·Rint; Rint varies with temperature, state of charge and age, and the drop also heats the cell (I²Rint). D4 D3
State of charge and cutoff voltage
Simple words: how full the battery is, and the voltage at which you must stop using it. Analogy: the tank's level gauge, and the mark below which the pump must stop. Precise: state of charge (SoC) is the remaining fraction of capacity; the open-circuit voltage depends on it in a chemistry-specific way; the discharge cutoff is the minimum voltage the maker allows, below which the cell is damaged. D1 D4
Power budget
Simple words: a list of everything that uses power, how much, and for how long. Analogy: a list of every tap, machine and toilet in the restaurant with how much water each uses per hour. Precise: a table of loads with their average and peak currents (or powers) at each supply rail, summed to size the battery, regulators, wiring and fuses, with an explicit margin. D3
Brown-out
Simple words: the supply voltage dips too low for the electronics, so they reset or misbehave. Analogy: the coffee machine shutting down when the pressure drops. Precise: a supply voltage below a device's minimum operating level; microcontrollers usually include a brown-out detector that holds or puts the chip in reset below a configured threshold, given in its datasheet. D5 R3
Soft start
Simple words: starting a motor gently instead of all at once. Analogy: Hamid opening the dishwasher's valve slowly. Precise: ramping a motor's command (or a supply's output) over a set time to limit the inrush current and the resulting voltage drop. R4 D3
Safety — LiPo and other lithium batteries

Lithium-polymer (LiPo) and other lithium cells store a lot of energy; a damaged, overcharged, over-discharged or short-circuited cell can overheat, vent and catch fire. D1 Course rules: S1

  • Follow the battery maker's instructions for charging, discharging, storage and disposal. Every voltage limit, current limit and temperature limit comes from the maker (D1), never from this chapter, a forum or memory.
  • Charge only with a charger made for that chemistry and cell count, set as its manual says (D2), on a non-flammable surface, never unattended, and away from anything that can burn.
  • Never use a pack that is swollen ("puffy"), dented, punctured or with damaged wires; take it out of use and follow the maker's and local disposal rules.
  • Never short the terminals: cover them, use connectors that cannot be plugged in backwards, and put a fuse sized from the power budget close to the pack.
  • L2 learners handle, charge and connect batteries only with a supervising adult. No mains-voltage work in this course.
  • The reference kit's own rules (build/KIT.md, "Safety notes": charge in LiPo mode inside a LiPo bag or metal box, at or below the rated charge current, never unattended; store at the maker's storage voltage; kids' kits use AA cells, never LiPo) apply on top of these, and the maker's instructions override both.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A battery is like a tank of water up high. The higher it is, the harder the water pushes: that is the voltage. The bigger the tank, the longer it lasts: that is the capacity.

Join batteries in a line and the push adds up. Join them side by side and they last longer.

A battery cannot give all its water at once. Its "outlet" is narrow, so when something big, like a motor, pulls hard, the push left for everything else drops. If the push drops too much, the robot's small computer stops and starts again. That is called a brown-out.

The batteries in many robots are LiPo batteries. They are powerful and light, but they can catch fire if they are damaged, charged the wrong way, or short-circuited. A grown-up must help, and the rules from the battery's maker must always be followed.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Packs, capacity and C-rate

Listing 1 uses a pretend cell (3.6 V nominal, 2.0 Ah — not the numbers of any real chemistry). In series, voltages add: 3S gives 10.8 V. In parallel, capacities add: 3S2P gives 10.8 V, 4.0 Ah and 43.2 Wh. R1 The C-rate expresses current relative to capacity: for the 4.0 Ah pack, 1C = 4 A and 5C = 20 A. D4 R1 A robot drawing 2.5 A on average would ideally run 1.6 h; using only 80% of the capacity (our own design margin, not a battery rule) gives 77 minutes. R1 The maker's datasheet states the capacity, the nominal voltage, the maximum continuous and burst discharge currents, the charge limits and the safe voltage window. D1

Internal resistance and sag

A real cell is not an ideal voltage source. To first order it is an open-circuit voltage (OCV), which falls as the charge is used, in series with an internal resistance (Figure 1). D4 Listing 2 discharges a pretend cell (OCV table 4.00 V full to 3.00 V empty, Rint = 0.05 Ω, cutoff 3.20 V) at four currents. At 1 A the terminal starts at 3.95 V and the cell gives 1.83 Ah over 110 min; at 10 A it starts at 3.50 V and reaches the cutoff after 4.0 min having given only 0.66 Ah; at 20 A it starts at 3.00 V, already below the cutoff. R2 The charge did not vanish: the I·R drop made the terminal voltage reach the cutoff while charge was still inside. Heat in the cell grows as I²R: 0.05 W at 1 A, 5 W at 10 A. R2 Real cells show more effects (temperature, ageing, slower recovery), and their usable capacity at high current is in the maker's discharge curves. D1 D4

Power budgets

A power budget lists every load with its supply rail, average current and peak current: motors (running, starting and stalled, F1-69), the microcontroller, sensors, radio, camera. Sum the averages for run time and the peaks for wiring, fuses, regulators and the battery's maximum current, with a margin. D3 A regulator that steps the battery down for the electronics needs its input to stay above its own minimum (dropout) under the worst sag; that minimum is in its datasheet. D3 D5

Brown-out

Motor start-up current is close to stall current (F1-69). It flows through the pack's internal resistance and every shared wire, and the voltage at every other load drops by I × Rshared. If the microcontroller's input falls below its minimum, its brown-out detector resets it; the motors stop, the voltage recovers, the MCU boots and starts the motors again. D5 In the forensic model (Figure 2) this loop repeats every 102 ms: 11 resets in 1.2 s. R3 Two independent fixes each remove all resets in the model: feeding the MCU from the pack terminals (so it no longer shares the 0.10 Ω wire), or a 300 ms soft start of the motors. R4

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Better battery models. Adding one or more RC pairs to the OCV-plus-R model captures the slower voltage recovery after a load step; parameters are fitted from pulse tests. State-of-charge estimation combines current counting (coulomb counting) with OCV corrections. D4
  • Cell balance and protection. In a series pack, cells drift apart; chargers made for multi-cell lithium packs balance each cell through a separate connector, and a battery management system (BMS) or protection circuit disconnects on over- or under-voltage, over-current or temperature. Which protection a given pack has is in its documentation. D1 D2
  • Distribution. Star distribution from the pack, separate wires for motors and logic, a bulk capacitor at the driver and local decoupling at each chip reduce the voltage that one load's current imposes on another (F1-72). D3
  • Regulators. A linear regulator wastes (Vin − Vout) × I as heat; a switching regulator is more efficient but adds ripple and noise. Both have a minimum input voltage. D3
  • Brown-out detector settings. The threshold should be set so the chip resets cleanly before its voltage is too low to run correctly; a chip that runs on below its minimum can corrupt memory or flash writes. D5

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a lithium cell, lithium ions move between the electrodes through an electrolyte while electrons travel through the external circuit; the cell voltage depends on the electrode chemistry and the state of charge. A pouch ("LiPo") cell has a soft case that swells when the cell is damaged or abused, which is why a puffy pack is a warning sign. D4 D1 On the electronics side, a microcontroller's supply supervisor compares its supply against a reference and holds the core in reset while the supply is below the threshold; the threshold options and the minimum operating voltage are in the MCU datasheet. D5

The kit's real pack and charger. The reference kit (build/KIT.md, ruling D1) uses the TurtleBot3 Burger's 11.1 V (3S) 1800 mAh LiPo (19.98 Wh, 5C) and the SkyRC B6neo balance charger (1–6S, balance current up to 500 mA, charge current up to 10 A); the 10.8 V three-cell pack of this chapter's model stands for that pack (ruling A2). Worked example

Task. A robot has a pretend 2S1P pack built from Listing 1's pretend cell (7.2 V, 2.0 Ah) with a total internal resistance of 0.1 Ω (pretend). Loads: two motors averaging 0.6 A each and peaking at 4 A each at start; electronics 0.3 A. (a) Average current and ideal run time. (b) Run time with an 80% margin. (c) The pack voltage at a simultaneous start of both motors at full charge. (d) If the electronics' regulator needs at least 6.0 V input (pretend), is there a problem?

  1. Average. 0.6 + 0.6 + 0.3 = 1.5 A. Ideal time = 2.0 / 1.5 = 1.33 h = 80 min.
  2. Margin. 0.8 × 80 = 64 min.
  3. Peak. 4 + 4 + 0.3 = 8.3 A; drop = 8.3 × 0.1 = 0.83 V; terminal ≈ 7.2 − 0.83 = 6.37 V (at the nominal voltage; less when the pack is partly empty).
  4. Check. 6.37 V is above 6.0 V when full, but with only 0.37 V margin; as the OCV falls with use, the same start will dip below 6.0 V. Fixes: soft start or staggered motor starts, separate wiring for the electronics, or a regulator with a lower minimum input.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A water tank's pressure depends on height; a cell's voltage depends on chemistry and state of charge. The OCV curve is not linear and differs between chemistries. D4
    • 

  • A narrow outlet only limits flow; internal resistance also heats the cell. High current warms a battery from inside, which matters for safety. R2
    • 

  • Water does not catch fire. A damaged or abused lithium cell can. No part of the analogy covers that: the safety box does. D1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot resets when the motors start or reverse. Cause: brown-out from sag on shared wiring. How to find it: log or scope the MCU supply during a start; read the MCU's reset-cause register if it has one (D5). Fix: separate supply paths, soft start, bigger capacitance at the electronics, lower-resistance wiring.
    • 

  • Symptom: run time far below the estimate. Cause: peak currents forgotten, high-current sag reaching the cutoff early, or an aged pack. Fix: measure average current; budget peaks; check the pack's internal resistance against new.
    • 

  • Symptom: pack warm after a short run. Cause: current above what the pack is made for, or a stalled motor. Fix: check the maker's current limits; stall detection (F1-69).
    • 

  • Symptom: a swollen pack. Cause: damage or abuse. Fix: none: stop using it and follow the safety box.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. brownout.cpp runs power_model.h with the MCU fed after the shared wire and the motors started at full duty. Pack 10.8 V and 0.15 Ω, shared wire 0.10 Ω, MCU reset below 7.0 V, boot 100 ms, motors as an equivalent F1-69 motor (all pretend).


Root cause. Brown-out from motor inrush current through the shared resistance. At 100.0 ms the MCU starts the motors at 100%; current rises to 8.09 A (100.5 ms), 12.18 A (101.0 ms) and 14.30 A (101.5 ms), and the MCU voltage falls 8.81 → 7.77 → 7.23 V. At 101.90 ms, with 15.21 A flowing, the MCU input reaches 6.998 V: 10.8 − 15.21 × (0.15 + 0.10) ≈ 6.998 V. The MCU resets, the motors stop, the voltage recovers, and after the 100 ms boot the cycle repeats: resets at 101.90, 203.82, 305.74 … ms, every 101.92 ms, 11 in 1.2 s. The bench supply worked because (in this story) its output resistance and wiring were much lower than the pack's.


Fixes. (1) Feed the MCU from the pack terminals: its voltage then sees only the 0.15 Ω drop, about 8.5 V at 15 A — 0 resets in Listing 4. (2) Soft start over 300 ms: the motor speeds up while the current stays lower — 0 resets. Both together give the most margin. Verify on the real robot by logging the MCU supply minimum during repeated drive-offs at a low state of charge.


Wrong answers. "Software crash": a crash would not occur exactly when the supply crosses 7.0 V, nor would it be cured by changing a wire. "Battery empty": the open-circuit voltage returns to 10.8 V between resets.


Prevention. Put peak (start-up) currents in the power budget and compute the voltage at every load at that peak; bring-up test: drive-off at the lowest allowed state of charge while logging the MCU supply and the reset cause.
