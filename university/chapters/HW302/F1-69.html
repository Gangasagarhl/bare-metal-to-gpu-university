Story hook

Below the restaurant, in the old cellar, there is a water wheel that once drove the flour mill. Tomás opens the valve from the roof tank. At first the wheel stands still and water rushes hard through the channel. Then the wheel starts turning, faster and faster, and something odd happens: the water in the channel slows down. The spinning wheel pushes back against the water that drives it.

Yuki hangs a heavy sack of grain on the wheel's rope. The wheel slows, and the water rushes again. She hangs a second sack, and the wheel stops completely; the water roars through, doing no useful work, and the channel's walls start to wear.

That wheel is a DC motor: the water pressure is the voltage, the water flow is the current, the push-back of the spinning wheel is the back-EMF, and the stopped wheel with roaring water is a stalled motor drawing its largest current. This chapter builds a working model of all of it, then adds brushless motors and servos.

What you will be able to do

  • Model a brushed DC motor with its electrical and mechanical equations, and simulate its start-up.
  • Read a torque–speed line: no-load speed, stall torque, stall current and maximum power.
  • Explain how a brushless motor is commutated from Hall sensors, and what a broken sensor does.
  • Describe what is inside a hobby servo and how its pulse command maps to an angle.
  • Diagnose a stalled motor from a log of voltage, speed and current.
Jargon box
Brushed DC motor
Simple words: a motor that spins when you connect it to a battery, and spins the other way if you swap the wires. Analogy: the cellar water wheel, turned by water pressure. Precise: a motor whose rotating winding (armature) is fed through brushes and a commutator that reverse the current in each coil as the rotor turns, producing torque in a fixed direction with a permanent-magnet or wound field. D1
Back-EMF
Simple words: the voltage a spinning motor makes by itself, which pushes against the supply. Analogy: the turning wheel pushing back on the water. Precise: the electromotive force induced in the windings by rotation, e = Ke·ω, opposing the applied voltage; it is why current falls as the motor speeds up. D1
Motor constants (Kt and Ke)
Simple words: two numbers saying how much push you get per amp, and how much voltage you get per unit of speed. Analogy: how hard the wheel turns per bucket of water, and how hard it pushes back per turn. Precise: torque constant Kt (N·m per A) and back-EMF constant Ke (V per rad/s); in consistent SI units they are numerically equal for an ideal motor. D1
Stall current and stall torque
Simple words: the current and push a motor has when it is held still. Analogy: the wheel stopped by sacks while the water roars through. Precise: at zero speed there is no back-EMF, so the current is V/R and the torque Kt·V/R, the largest values on the torque–speed line; the winding then dissipates V²/R as heat. D1 R1
Torque–speed curve
Simple words: a graph showing how a motor slows down as you load it more. Analogy: the wheel's speed for each number of sacks. Precise: for a DC motor at fixed voltage, speed falls nearly linearly from the no-load speed to zero at stall torque; mechanical power, torque × speed, peaks near half the stall torque. D1 R1
Brushless DC motor (BLDC)
Simple words: a motor with magnets on the spinning part and coils that stay still, switched by electronics instead of brushes. Analogy: a wheel turned by three water jets that a helper opens and closes in turn as the wheel moves. Precise: a permanent-magnet synchronous motor with stator windings (usually three phases) commutated electronically from rotor-position information (Hall sensors, back-EMF sensing, or an encoder). D1
Commutation (six-step, Hall sensors)
Simple words: choosing which coils get current, depending on where the magnets are. Analogy: the helper deciding which jet to open by looking at marks on the wheel. Precise: in six-step (trapezoidal) commutation, three Hall sensors spaced 120 electrical degrees apart give six valid 3-bit patterns; each selects one phase driven high, one low and one floating. D1 D5
Hobby servo
Simple words: a small box that turns its arm to the angle you ask for. Analogy: a wheel with a helper who keeps adjusting the valve until a pointer matches the order slip. Precise: a motor, gearbox, position sensor (often a potentiometer) and controller in one housing, commanded by the width of a repeating pulse; the pulse-to-angle mapping and timing are given by its datasheet. D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A motor turns electricity into turning. Inside, electricity flows through coils of wire next to magnets, and the magnets push the coils around.

When a motor spins, it also acts a little like a generator: it makes its own voltage that pushes back. So a fast, free-spinning motor takes only a little current. If you grab the shaft and hold it still, the push-back disappears and the motor takes a lot of current. That current makes the wire hot. A motor that hums but does not turn is getting hot.

A heavier load makes the motor slower. With no load it is fastest; held still it pushes hardest.

A brushless motor has its magnets on the part that spins and its coils on the outside. A small computer switches the coils on in turn, using sensors that tell it where the magnets are. A servo is a motor, a gearbox, a sensor and a little brain in one box: you tell it an angle, and it goes there.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The brushed DC motor model

The winding has a resistance R and an inductance L; turning induces a back-EMF Ke·ω. Kirchhoff's voltage law around the loop (Figure 1) gives V = R·i + L·di/dt + Ke·ω. The current makes a torque Kt·i, which accelerates the rotor's inertia J against friction b·ω and the load: J·dω/dt = Kt·i − b·ω − Tload. D1 Listing 1 integrates these two equations with small time steps for a pretend motor (R = 2 Ω, L = 1 mH, K = 0.01 in SI units, J = 10−5 kg·m², b = 10−6 N·m·s). R1

At a 6 V step, the current jumps to nearly 3 A within the first milliseconds (2.868 A at 10 ms), because the motor is still almost stopped. As speed rises, back-EMF rises and the current falls: 1.122 A at 200 ms, 0.077 A after 1 s at 5583 rpm. R1 Two time scales appear: the electrical one, L/R = 0.5 ms, and the mechanical one, about J·R/(KtKe) = 0.2 s. D1 No-load speed is proportional to voltage: 1861, 3722 and 5583 rpm at 2, 4 and 6 V. R1

The torque–speed line

In steady state di/dt = 0 and dω/dt = 0. Eliminating i gives a straight line: speed falls from the no-load speed to zero at the stall torque Kt·V/R. For the pretend motor at 6 V, stall current is 3.00 A and stall torque 0.0300 N·m. D1 R1 Listing 1's loaded runs fall on that line (Figure 2): 4187 rpm at 0.0075 N·m, 2792 at 0.015, 1396 at 0.0225. Mechanical power, torque × speed, is largest at half the stall torque (4.385 W here) and zero at both ends. R1 A gearbox trades speed for torque by its ratio, minus its own losses and friction. D1

Current tracks torque: the more load, the more current. That is a useful sensor: a rising motor current with falling speed means the robot is pushing against something.

Brushless motors

A brushed motor's brushes rub and wear and spark. A brushless motor moves the magnets to the rotor and leaves the three-phase windings on the stator, so the switching has to be done electronically. D1 The simplest scheme reads three Hall sensors spaced 120 electrical degrees apart; together they give six valid patterns per electrical revolution, and each pattern selects which phase is driven high, which low, and which floats (six-step commutation). Listing 3 implements the pretend motor's table; a healthy sequence is 101, 100, 110, 010, 011, 001. R3 Patterns 000 and 111 never occur in a healthy motor, so seeing one means a sensor fault. D5 R3 Sensorless drives estimate position from the back-EMF of the floating phase instead; more advanced drives (field-oriented control) use an encoder or an estimator and smooth sinusoidal currents. D1

Hobby servos

A hobby servo packs a small DC motor, a gearbox, a position sensor and a controller in one box. It is commanded by a repeating pulse whose width sets the target angle; Listing 2 uses the pretend mapping 1.0 ms → −90°, 1.5 ms → 0°, 2.0 ms → +90°. D4 R2 The internal controller drives the motor at a speed proportional to the error, limited to a top speed (300°/s pretend), and stops inside a small deadband: the arm reaches 89.0° for a 90° target and −44.0° for −45°. R2 That 1° gap is the deadband, not a bug: the servo stops trying when it is "close enough". The real mapping, pulse repetition rate, speed and deadband are in the servo's datasheet. D4

Stepper motors, which move in fixed steps without needing a sensor, are a fourth family; this chapter does not model them.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Transfer function. Neglecting L, the voltage-to-speed response is first order with time constant τm = J·R/(KtKe + R·b); with L it is second order, and the electrical pole is much faster here (0.5 ms against 0.2 s). Controllers in RB201 are tuned against this model. D1
  • Efficiency and heat. Copper loss is i²R; at stall all input power becomes heat. Continuous ratings in datasheets are thermal limits; peak torque can be much higher for short times. D1 D3
  • Friction is not linear. The model uses viscous friction b·ω. Real gearboxes add static (breakaway) and Coulomb friction, which create a minimum voltage below which the motor will not start: the forensic lab models a breakaway torque. D1 R4
  • Electrical vs mechanical degrees. A BLDC with p pole pairs completes p electrical revolutions per mechanical one; Hall patterns repeat every electrical revolution. D1
  • Parameter identification. R from a locked-rotor current at low voltage; Ke by spinning the motor and measuring its open-circuit voltage; J and b from a coast-down curve. Each is a measurement you can make on the real motor and compare with the datasheet. D1

How the hardware actually does it

In a brushed motor, carbon or metal brushes press on a segmented copper commutator on the shaft; as the shaft turns, the segments under the brushes change, reversing the current in each coil at the right moment. The brush contact is also an electrical noise source (sparks), which is why small capacitors are often fitted across motor terminals, as the kit's documentation will show if it does. D1 D3 In a BLDC the "commutator" is six transistors in a three-phase bridge (F1-70) and a controller that reads the Hall sensors, which are digital magnetic-field sensors mounted in the stator. D1 D5 A hobby servo's controller reads a potentiometer on the output shaft and drives its motor through a small H-bridge. D4

Safety

Any real motor in this course is a small low-voltage kit motor, clamped to the bench, with fingers, hair and cables clear of the shaft before power is applied; no propellers. A stalled motor gets hot: do not hold a stalled motor at full voltage, and do not touch it after a stall test. L2 learners work with a supervising adult. S1

Worked example

Task. A motor (pretend values) has R = 4 Ω and K = 0.02 V·s/rad = 0.02 N·m/A, with friction neglected. At 12 V: (a) stall current and stall torque; (b) no-load speed in rpm; (c) speed and current with a 0.03 N·m load; (d) the maximum mechanical power.

  1. Stall. ω = 0, so i = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A, torque = K·i = 0.06 N·m.
  2. No load. Torque 0 means i = 0 (no friction), so V = K·ω, ω = 12/0.02 = 600 rad/s = 600 × 60 / 2π ≈ 5730 rpm.
  3. Load 0.03 N·m. i = T/K = 1.5 A. V = R·i + K·ω → ω = (12 − 6)/0.02 = 300 rad/s ≈ 2865 rpm: half the stall torque gives half the no-load speed, as the straight line predicts.
  4. Maximum power. At half stall torque: P = 0.03 × 300 = 9 W. Electrical input there is 12 × 1.5 = 18 W, so efficiency at maximum power is 50% even in this ideal motor: the other 9 W is i²R = 2.25 × 4 heat.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Water wheels have no inductance. A motor's current cannot change instantly (L·di/dt); switching it off suddenly causes a voltage spike, which F1-70 must handle. The water wheel has no such effect. D1
    • 

  • The wheel's push-back is mechanical; back-EMF is electrical. Back-EMF is a voltage that exists even with nothing connected: spin a motor by hand and you can measure it. That is a generator, which the water wheel analogy only hints at. D1
    • 

  • A stalled wheel only wastes water; a stalled motor heats up. The current at stall turns into heat in the winding (Layer 3). R4
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the motor hums at low commands but does not move. Cause: friction above the torque that the low voltage can produce (stall). How to find it: speed zero with current V/R (forensic lab). Fix: minimum duty or a feed-forward term; less friction; current limit.
    • 

  • Symptom: the supply voltage dips or the microcontroller resets when the motor starts. Cause: start-up current near stall current. How to find it: Listing 1, Part A. Fix: ramp the command; separate supply paths (F1-71).
    • 

  • Symptom: a BLDC jerks, runs rough, or turns only in one direction. Cause: wrong Hall-to-phase table or a broken Hall wire. How to find it: log the Hall pattern while turning by hand; look for 000/111 and repeated steps (Listing 3). Fix: the datasheet table; check wiring.
    • 

  • Symptom: a servo jitters around its target or stops a little short. Cause: deadband and pulse noise. Fix: a stable pulse source; accept the deadband or choose a servo with a smaller one.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. stall.cpp runs the Listing 1 motor (6 V supply × duty as average voltage) with a gearbox that needs a breakaway torque of 0.02 N·m (pretend) before it moves, for 1 s per duty.


Root cause. Stall below the breakaway torque. At zero speed the torque available is Kt·V/R: 0.01 × 3.6/2 = 0.018 N·m at 60%, less than 0.02, so the motor stays stalled; at 70%, 0.01 × 4.2/2 = 0.021 N·m > 0.02, so it starts. The stalled currents equal V/R exactly (0.3 A … 1.8 A), proving the winding is healthy, and i²R heats it with up to 6.48 W while nothing moves. Once moving, the current is about 2.0 A, set by the friction torque (0.02/0.01 = 2 A). In this model nothing moves below 4.0 V (Kt·V/R = 0.02 N·m), that is about 67% duty; just above it the motor turns slowly, for example 4.08 V gives (4.08 − 4.0)/0.01 = 8 rad/s ≈ 76 rpm at the motor shaft, so creeping is possible only with commands in a narrow band above the threshold.


Fixes. Reduce gearbox friction (lubrication, alignment), which lowers the starting voltage; set a minimum duty just above the starting threshold instead of commanding values that can only stall; closed-loop speed control with the encoder (F1-66), which raises the voltage until the wheel moves; real gearboxes often have higher static than moving friction, in which case a short kick above breakaway helps too. Verify: the log shows nonzero speed at the target creep command and no stalled rows.


Wrong answers. "The motor is broken": the current equals V/R and the motor runs normally above 70%. "The driver is faulty": the voltage and current are consistent with a working driver.


Prevention. A firmware stall check: commanded voltage above a threshold, speed zero, current near V/R for more than a set time → cut the drive and report. And a bring-up test that finds the minimum starting duty.
