Story hook

Every morning at the restaurant, Amara checks the cold room. A thermometer hangs on the wall: a needle that leans further as the room warms. The needle itself is not a number. It is a position, and it can sit anywhere along the dial. Amara's job is to turn it into a number and write it in the log book.

She does this at fixed times, every half hour, and she writes the nearest whole degree, because the book has no room for more. One day the head chef, Kenji, looks at the book and frowns. "The cold room warms up and cools down every hour, exactly," he says. "That is strange." Amara walks into the cold room and watches the needle for ten minutes. It swings up and down every few minutes, because the cooling machine switches on and off. Her half-hour readings caught the swings at unlucky moments and made a slow wave that was never there.

Three ideas hide in this story: a physical thing that moves a needle, someone who reads it at fixed times, and someone who rounds it to the nearest mark. In electronics they are called transduction, sampling and quantisation. This chapter is about all three, and about the slow wave that was never there.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the measurement chain from a physical quantity to a number in a program: transducer, conditioning, anti-alias filter, sample-and-hold, analog-to-digital converter.
  • Compute the step size (LSB) of an n-bit ADC, the code for a given input voltage, and the worst-case quantisation error.
  • Predict the apparent frequency of a signal sampled too slowly (aliasing), and choose a sample rate and filter that prevent it.
  • Run the university's ADC and sampling models and check their output against your own hand calculation.
  • Diagnose a phantom slow oscillation in a logged signal as aliasing, from two logs taken at different sample rates.
Jargon box
Transducer
Simple words: a part that turns one kind of physical thing (heat, light, pressure, movement) into another, usually an electrical signal. Analogy: the cold-room thermometer, which turns warmth into a needle position. Precise: a device that converts energy or a physical quantity from one form to another; a sensor uses a transducer to produce an electrical quantity (voltage, current, resistance or charge) that depends on the measured quantity. D1
Signal conditioning
Simple words: the circuit that makes a sensor's signal the right size and cleanliness for the converter. Analogy: wiping the thermometer's glass and turning it so the needle can be read from the doorway. Precise: amplification, level shifting, filtering and buffering applied between a transducer and an ADC so that the signal fits the converter's input range and bandwidth. D1
ADC (analog-to-digital converter)
Simple words: the part that turns a voltage into a whole number. Analogy: Amara reading the needle and writing the nearest whole degree. Precise: a circuit that compares an input voltage with a reference and outputs an n-bit code; an ideal n-bit ADC divides its input range into 2n equal steps. D1 Introduced in F1-08 as part of sampling and quantisation.
Reference voltage (Vref)
Simple words: the voltage the ADC treats as "full scale". Analogy: the top mark on the thermometer's dial. Precise: the voltage against which an ADC compares its input; for an ideal unipolar n-bit converter, code = floor(Vin / Vref × 2n), clamped to 0 … 2n − 1. D1 R1
LSB (least significant bit) step
Simple words: the voltage difference between two neighbouring codes. Analogy: the gap between two marks on the dial. Precise: Vref / 2n for an ideal n-bit converter; also called the resolution in volts. D1 R1
Quantisation error
Simple words: the small difference between the true voltage and the voltage the code stands for. Analogy: the part of a degree Amara cannot write down. Precise: the difference between the input and the value represented by its code; for an ideal converter reported at mid-step it is at most half an LSB in magnitude. D1 R1
Sample rate (fs)
Simple words: how many readings are taken per second. Analogy: how often Amara walks to the cold room. Precise: the number of samples per second, in hertz (Hz); the sample period is Ts = 1 / fs. D2
Nyquist frequency
Simple words: half the sample rate: the fastest wobble the readings can show correctly. Analogy: if Amara reads every half hour, she cannot see anything that swings faster than once an hour. Precise: fs / 2; a band-limited signal whose frequencies are all below it can be reconstructed from its samples (the sampling theorem), and components above it fold back to lower frequencies. D2
Aliasing
Simple words: a fast wobble that looks like a slow one because it was read too rarely. Analogy: the slow wave in Amara's log book that was never in the cold room. Precise: after sampling at fs, a sinusoid at f is indistinguishable from one at |f − k·fs| for any integer k; the alias seen is the one in 0 … fs/2. D2 R2
Anti-alias filter
Simple words: a filter in front of the ADC that removes wobbles too fast to be sampled. Analogy: a thick case around the thermometer that makes the needle too slow to follow the quick swings. Precise: an analog low-pass filter placed before sampling that attenuates components above the Nyquist frequency enough that their aliases are negligible. D1 D2
Sample-and-hold
Simple words: a circuit that freezes the voltage for a moment so the ADC can measure it. Analogy: Amara taking a photo of the needle so she can read it calmly. Precise: a switch and capacitor that track the input and then hold its value constant during the conversion. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A computer cannot feel heat or see light. It can only work with numbers. So every sensor is a translator. First, something physical changes something electrical: warmth changes a resistance, light makes a tiny current, a push bends a part and changes a voltage. That is the transducer, like the needle of the thermometer.

Then the electrical signal is made the right size, and cleaned. Then, at regular moments, a circuit freezes the voltage and measures it against a ruler called the reference voltage. The answer is a whole number from a fixed set, like the marks on the dial. Reading at regular moments is called sampling. Rounding to a mark is called quantisation.

Two things can fool you. If the marks are far apart, small changes vanish. And if you read too rarely, a fast wobble can look like a slow wave that never existed. The rule that fixes the second problem is easy to say: read more than twice as often as the fastest wobble, and remove faster wobbles before you read.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Transduction

Kitchen first: the thermometer turns heat into a needle angle, and only then does anyone read it. A transducer does the same for a circuit: it converts the measured quantity into an electrical one. Some change a resistance (a thermistor with temperature, a strain gauge with stretch), some produce a voltage or current directly (a photodiode with light), some change a capacitance or a charge (the tiny moving plates inside the accelerometers of F1-65). D1 A resistance is turned into a voltage by a circuit such as the voltage divider of F1-04. Which quantity a sensor changes, how much per unit, and how linear that relation is are properties of the named part, so they come only from its datasheet (D3). D3

Conditioning and filtering

Next, signal conditioning fits the signal to the converter: an amplifier makes a small signal bigger, a level shifter moves a signal that swings below 0 V into the converter's range, and a buffer stops the converter's input from loading the sensor. D1 Then comes the anti-alias filter, an analog low-pass filter. It must sit before the sampler, because once a signal has been sampled, an alias is just a number like any other and no later program can remove it. D2 The RC circuit of F1-05 is the simplest such filter.

The ADC: codes, steps and errors

The sample-and-hold freezes the input while the converter works. D1 An ideal n-bit ADC then divides the range from 0 to Vref into 2n equal steps of size LSB = Vref / 2n, and outputs the number of the step the input falls in:

code = floor(Vin / Vref × 2n), clamped to 0 … 2n − 1.

This is the model in Listing 1. With a pretend Vref = 3.0 V and n = 3 there are 8 codes and the step is 0.375 V; an input of 0.374 V gives code 0 and 0.376 V gives code 1, because the boundary is at exactly 0.375 V. R1 If your program reports each code as the voltage in the middle of its step, (code + 0.5) × LSB, the quantisation error is never more than half a step: the 10-bit sweep in Listing 1 found a worst error of 0.001465 V, exactly half of its 2.930 mV step. R1 Inputs outside the range are clamped: 3.20 V reads as the top code, 7, and the program cannot know how far above the range the input was. R1

Real converters are not ideal. Their datasheets give offset error, gain error, non-linearity, noise and the exact transfer function (some round instead of truncating, some report signed codes). Those numbers belong to the named part and come only from its datasheet. D1 D3

Sampling and aliasing

Reading at a sample rate fs means taking one sample every Ts = 1/fs seconds. The sampling theorem says that a signal containing only frequencies below fs/2, the Nyquist frequency, is fully described by its samples. D2 A component above the Nyquist frequency is not lost; it is disguised. Its samples are identical to those of a component at |f − k·fs|, and the one you will see is the copy between 0 and fs/2. This is aliasing. D2 Listing 2 checks it: a 9 Hz sine sampled at 100 Hz shows 8.95 Hz by counting zero crossings (the right answer, within the counting method's resolution), but sampled at 10 Hz it shows exactly 1.00 Hz, as the formula |9 − 1 × 10| = 1 predicts. R2 The table at the end of its output shows the folding: at fs = 10 Hz, inputs of 1, 9, 11, 19 and 21 Hz all look like 1 Hz. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Quantisation as noise. When the input moves over many steps between samples, the quantisation error behaves like a random value spread evenly over ±LSB/2. A uniform distribution of width LSB has standard deviation LSB/√12 (a result from probability, MA202), so each extra bit halves the error and its standard deviation. D4 D1 This model fails for a constant input: then the error is the same every time and averaging does not reduce it (F1-72).
  • Resolution is not accuracy. Part B of Listing 1 shows that a 16-bit converter with a 3.0 V reference has steps of 0.046 mV. R1 Whether the bottom bits mean anything depends on the reference's accuracy, the converter's noise and the wiring (F1-72), all of which come from the named parts' datasheets and from measurement. D1
  • How fast is fast enough. The theorem's limit fs/2 assumes a perfect filter. Real analog filters fall off gradually, so designs sample several times faster than the highest frequency of interest and let the filter attenuate everything near and above fs/2. The exact choice is a trade-off between filter order, sample rate and how much aliased energy the application can accept. D2 D1
  • Converter types. Converters are built in several ways (successive approximation, delta-sigma, flash, among others) that trade speed, resolution and power; delta-sigma converters oversample and filter digitally, which moves part of the anti-alias job inside the chip. Which type a microcontroller or sensor uses, and its timing, is in its datasheet. D1 D3
  • Timing jitter. Sampling at uneven moments adds error proportional to how fast the signal is changing. A hardware timer that starts conversions (F1-48) gives evenly spaced samples; starting conversions from a busy software loop does not. D1

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a typical microcontroller ADC, an analog multiplexer selects one input pin, a small capacitor charges to the pin's voltage while the sampling switch is closed (the "hold" begins when it opens), and the conversion logic compares the held voltage against fractions of Vref to find the code; the result lands in a data register that your driver reads over the internal bus, by polling a "done" flag or in an interrupt (F1-41, F1-42). D1 Sensor chips that speak I2C or SPI (F1-47) contain this whole chain and deliver codes in registers, which is exactly how the IMU of F1-65 works. D1

Worked example

Task. A temperature sensor's conditioning circuit outputs 0.50 V at 0 °C and rises by 0.010 V per °C (pretend numbers for this exercise). It feeds a 10-bit ADC with Vref = 3.0 V. (a) What code does 25 °C give? (b) What temperature step does one code represent? (c) The room temperature can change by a few degrees per minute at most, but the wire picks up a 48 Hz interference. What sample rate and filter would you choose?

  1. Voltage. 0.50 + 25 × 0.010 = 0.75 V.
  2. Code. 0.75 / 3.0 × 1024 = 256.0, so code = floor(256.0) = 256. (Exactly on a boundary; a real converter's datasheet says which side it lands on.)
  3. Step. LSB = 3.0 / 1024 = 2.930 mV (Listing 1, Part B). One code is 2.930 mV ÷ 10 mV per °C ≈ 0.29 °C.
  4. Reported value. Reporting at mid-step gives (256 + 0.5) × 2.930 mV = 0.7515 V → 25.15 °C, an error of 0.15 °C.
  5. Sampling. The wanted signal changes slowly, so 10 samples per second would be plenty for it. But 48 Hz interference sampled at 10 Hz appears at |48 − 5 × 10| = 2 Hz: a fake wobble. Either put a low-pass filter before the ADC that makes 48 Hz negligible, or sample fast (for example 1000 Hz, Nyquist 500 Hz) and then filter and average digitally (F1-72). The analog filter is the only option that works at a low sample rate.

Check step 4: 0.7515 − 0.75 = 0.0015 V = 0.15 °C, which is half of the 0.29 °C step. The input sat exactly on a step boundary, the worst place, so the error equals the half-LSB bound and never exceeds it. You can check steps 2–3 by adding these inputs to inputs in Listing 1 with bits = 10.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Amara can look twice; a sampler cannot. When the log looked strange, Amara walked in and watched the needle. A program has only the samples; once aliasing has happened there is nothing left to look at. That is why the filter must come before the sampler. D2
    • 

  • Amara's rounding is to the nearest mark; many ADCs truncate. The ideal model of Listing 1 takes the floor, so the reported value is biased low by half a step unless the program reports mid-step. Which rule a real converter uses is in its datasheet. R1 D3
    • 

  • The thermometer needle has its own slowness; a voltage does not. A heavy needle smooths quick swings by itself, a bit like a filter. An electrical signal on a wire has no such inertia unless you add a filter on purpose.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a slow oscillation in a log that nobody can explain. Cause: aliasing of a faster component. How to find it: log the same signal at a much higher rate for a short time (forensic lab). Fix: analog low-pass filter before the ADC, or a higher sample rate followed by digital filtering.
    • 

  • Symptom: the reading is stuck at the top code (1023 for 10 bits) when the input should be near it. Cause: the input is above Vref and clamps (Listing 1, the 3.20 V row). How to find it: measure the input with a meter (F1-07). Fix: scale the input down with a divider, or use a larger reference if the part allows it.
    • 

  • Symptom: every value is off by about half a step. Cause: converting codes with code × LSB (bottom of step) while expecting rounding. How to find it: compare the reported value for a known input with the ideal model. Fix: use (code + 0.5) × LSB, or the transfer function from the datasheet.
    • 

  • Symptom: readings jump when another pin or the motors change. Cause: wiring and ground problems, not the converter. How to find it and fix: F1-72.
    •

Summary


    

  • A sensor chain is: transducer → conditioning → anti-alias filter → sample-and-hold → ADC → number.
    • 

  • An ideal n-bit ADC has 2n codes and a step of Vref/2n; inputs outside the range clamp.
    • 

  • Reported at mid-step, the quantisation error is at most half a step (measured: 1.465 mV for 10 bits at 3.0 V).
    • 

  • Resolution (step size) is not accuracy; a real part's errors come from its datasheet.
    • 

  • Sample faster than twice the highest frequency present; components above fs/2 appear as aliases at |f − k·fs|.
    • 

  • Aliases cannot be removed after sampling, so the low-pass filter goes before the sampler.
    • 

  • A short, fast log is the standard tool to expose aliasing.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. forensic.cpp generates a constant 1.20 V plus a 0.05 V sine at 48 Hz, converts it with an ideal 10-bit ADC (pretend 3.0 V reference) and samples it at 50 Hz (Log A) and 1000 Hz (Log B). No filter was modelled.


Root cause. A 48 Hz interference on the wire, sampled at 50 Hz without an anti-alias filter, aliases to |48 − 50| = 2 Hz. Evidence: Log A's wave has the same size (codes 392 to 426) as Log B's, but Log B shows about 48 Hz (96 mean-crossings in 1 s), and the alias formula maps 48 Hz at 50 Hz sampling to 2 Hz (about 1.9 Hz from Log A's crossings over 10 s). A real 2 Hz oscillation would appear at 2 Hz in Log B too: in its 50 ms it would barely move, but Log B swings fully every ~21 ms.


Fixes. (1) An analog low-pass filter before the ADC with its corner well below 25 Hz: Log A becomes nearly flat. (2) Sample at 1000 Hz and average groups of 20 samples in software, logging 50 averages per second: Log A becomes nearly flat. (3) Remove the source of interference or shield the wire (F1-72).


Wrong answers. "The sensor oscillates": Log B shows no 2 Hz component. "ADC noise": noise is not periodic, and the wave is smooth and large (34 codes).


Prevention. A design rule that every analog input has a filter matched to its sample rate, and a commissioning test that logs each input at a high rate once.
