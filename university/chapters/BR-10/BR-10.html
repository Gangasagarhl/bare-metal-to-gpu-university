Story hook

Analogy

Tomás has a stamp collection of three hundred thousand stamps and one question: how many of them are rare, and what are they worth together? At his kitchen table he sorts them himself. He knows exactly how long it takes, because he times every evening with the same kitchen clock, and he writes the answer in his notebook. If he drops a box, he sees it. If he gets tired, he knows.

The collection grows, so Tomás asks two friends in other towns to help. He sends each of them a box by post with a letter: "sort these, write back the count". That is where the surprises begin. Ana answers within a day. Kwame never answers, and Tomás waits a whole month before he phones and learns that Kwame's letter box was blocked. Ana's watch runs half an hour fast, so her letter seems to say she finished before Tomás posted the box. And one evening Ana is slow because her cousins are using her table too; Tomás thinks the stamps of that box were harder.

The stamps are the same. The sorting rules are the same. Tomás's habit of checking the answer and timing the work is still right. What changed is everything around the work: other houses, other clocks, letters that may never come back, and a table Tomás cannot see. This bridge is about that change.

What you will be able to do

  • Run the same job on one machine and on a three-node virtual cluster, check that both give the same answer, and explain the measured difference from the logs (launch, messages, shared CPUs, stragglers).
  • Explain what carries over from the laptop (programs, tests, measurement discipline) and what changes: independent failures, remote management through a BMC, schedulers that choose where code runs, shared storage and network bottlenecks, NUMA inside each server, and observability.
  • Demonstrate and fix the three traps of the step: benchmarking on a noisy shared node, assuming clocks agree, and having no timeouts.
  • Diagnose a slow cluster job from a controller log and node logs written with different clocks, and name the straggler.
  • Write a batch script that runs the job under a scheduler and falls back to a local run, and say which of its details are verified and which are not.
Jargon box

Terms you already know and will use again: cluster, node and cluster scheduler (F5-22, which comes right after this bridge), partial failure (F5-08), timeout (F5-09), clock skew (F5-10), heartbeat (F5-11), run-to-run variation and median (F2-51), NUMA (F1-39), BMC and out-of-band management (F3-42), noisy neighbour (F5-32), observability and structured log (F5-41). The new terms of this bridge:

Virtual cluster
Simple words: several pretend computers made inside one real computer, so you can practise cluster work without a cluster. Analogy: three friends who play at living in different towns by sitting in three rooms of one house and passing letters under the doors. Precise: a set of virtual machines, containers or processes on one host that each act as a node, with separate address spaces and network connections between them; it reproduces the software interfaces of a cluster but not its independence: they share the host's CPUs, clock, kernel (for processes) and power. In this bridge each node is a process reached over a loopback TCP connection. R2 D3
Straggler
Simple words: the one helper who is much slower than the others, so everyone waits for them at the end. Analogy: Ana sorting at a table she shares with her cousins. Precise: a node or task that finishes much later than its peers, so that the completion time of a parallel job, which is the maximum over its parts, is set by it; causes include a shared or overloaded node, a slow disk or link, or an uneven share of the work. D2 R5
Fixed and dynamic task assignment
Simple words: either everyone gets their pile at the start, or everyone comes back for the next piece when they finish. Analogy: Tomás posting three equal boxes at once, or posting one small box at a time to whoever has written back. Precise: in fixed (static) assignment, task t goes to worker t mod n before the run starts, as in a block or cyclic decomposition by rank; in dynamic assignment (self-scheduling), an idle worker receives the next pending task, so faster workers do more tasks. Dynamic assignment absorbs stragglers at the cost of one message per task. R5 D3
Unbounded wait
Simple words: waiting for an answer with no point at which you give up. Analogy: Tomás waiting a whole month for Kwame's letter. Precise: a blocking call (a receive, a poll, a collective operation) made without a deadline; if the peer stalls while its connection stays open, nothing ever wakes the caller, and the program hangs without an error. R6 D2
Round-trip offset estimate
Simple words: asking a friend "what time is it on your watch?" and using how long the call took to judge how wrong their watch is. Analogy: phoning Ana, noting the time when you ask and when she answers, and assuming her answer was spoken half-way between. Precise: an estimate of the offset between a remote clock and the local clock: send a request at local time t0, receive the remote reading T at local time t1, and take T − (t0 + t1)/2; the error is at most (t1 − t0)/2, so the probe with the shortest round trip is kept. This is the reasoning behind Cristian's method (F5-10). D3 R8
Node fencing
Simple words: making sure a node you have given up on really stops, so it cannot do harm later. Analogy: asking Kwame's town to close his post office until he has been checked. Precise: forcibly isolating a suspected node (powering it off through its management controller, cutting its storage or network access) before its work is given to others, so that a node that was only slow cannot later write stale results; fencing tokens (F5-11) are the software counterpart at the receiving side. In this bridge's lab a kill signal stands in for a power-off through a BMC. D2 R7

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

On your laptop, one computer does the whole job, and you can see it. On a cluster, many computers each do a part, and you cannot see any of them. You only get messages.

So you keep your good habits: the same program, the same check of the answer, the same careful timing. And you add three new ones. Never wait for a message forever: decide how long is too long. Never trust that two computers' clocks agree. And never trust one timing from a computer that other people are using at the same time.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

In the story Tomás's stamps never changed; only the world around them did. The lab of this bridge is built the same way: one job, written once in job.hpp (count the primes below a limit and add them up), run first on "the laptop" (one process) and then on a three-node virtual cluster (three processes reached over TCP, Listing 2). You will see that the job, its test and its measurement carry over without a single changed line, and that six things change around it.

What carries over: programs, tests, measurement discipline

Programs. The function that does the work, job::countPrimes, is the same code in all four places it runs: the laptop (Listing 1), every node of the virtual cluster (Listing 2), every MPI rank (Listing 4), and the Slurm script's job (Listing 10). Nothing in it knows where it runs. That is the first design rule of cluster code: keep the work a pure function of its inputs, and keep the "where and when" in a separate layer. R1 R9

Tests. The laptop's answer becomes the oracle for every distributed run: 25,997 primes below 300,000, summing to 3,709,507,114. R1, R2, R3, R7, R8, R9 and R11 all print that same pair or say "identical"/"matches", including R7, where one node died and another stalled. A distributed result that you have not compared with a single-machine result is a result you do not know. R1 R7 R11

Measurement discipline. F2-51's harness bench.hpp (warm-up runs, many timed repetitions with a steady clock, minimum, median, maximum) is copied into this lab byte for byte; R15 proves it with cmp. The curriculum's measurement protocol (warm-up, then the median of at least 20 timed runs, with the machine recorded) is the same habit, and it applies to clusters unchanged. What changes is how hard it is to keep: Layer 3 shows a cluster benchmark whose spread is several times the laptop's on the same machine. R15 C2 R3

Change 1: many machines fail independently

A laptop either works or it does not; when it crashes, every program on it stops, and you notice. A cluster has partial failures: one node stops while the others continue, and the others learn about it only through what stops arriving. F5-08 and F5-11 gave the theory; the lab shows the two forms the evidence takes. When node n3 kills itself in R7 (an injected fault standing in for a power loss), the operating system closes its end of the TCP connection, and the controller's next read returns end-of-stream: a clear, fast signal, logged as event=node_lost node=n3 evidence=connection_closed 2.2 ms after the task was sent. When node n2 stalls with its connection still open, nothing arrives at all; only a deadline can turn that silence into a decision. R7 D2

Independent failures also multiply. F5-22 computes the chance that a job on k nodes for T hours sees no failure, (1 − p)k·T, and shows it falling exponentially with node-hours. The consequence for code is that recovery (re-running a lost task, restarting from a checkpoint) is part of the program's design, not an afterthought. Our controller re-queues the lost task; F8-16 does the same for a whole training job. D2

Change 2: remote management through the BMC

On a laptop you press the power button and look at the screen. A server in a data centre is managed from far away through its baseboard management controller (BMC): a separate small computer on the main board, powered whenever the server is plugged in, reachable over its own management network, able to switch the host on and off, read its sensors and event log, and show its console. It speaks IPMI and, on newer machines, Redfish (F3-42, F5-29). D5

This matters for distributed code in one precise place: fencing. When a node is suspected (n2 in R7), the cluster cannot see whether it is dead or only slow, and a slow node that wakes up later may write stale results. Powering it off through its BMC turns "suspected" into "certainly stopped". The lab cannot do that, so the controller's destructor sends SIGKILL to the suspected node's process and says so in the log: event=fence node=n2 how=SIGKILL (stand-in for a power-off through its BMC). R7 D2

Change 3: schedulers decide where code runs

On the laptop, your program runs where you start it. On a cluster you describe what the job needs (nodes, tasks, memory, a time limit) and a scheduler decides when and on which nodes it starts (F5-22, F5-23). Inside a running job there is often a second, smaller decision: which process does which piece. Listing 2's controller makes exactly that decision in two ways. With fixed assignment, task t goes to node t mod 3, the way an MPI program usually divides work by rank (Listing 4 does so). With dynamic assignment, whichever node is idle receives the next task. R3 shows dynamic assignment giving 4, 5 and 3 tasks to the three nodes in one run: the faster nodes came back sooner. D1 R3 R9

Two habits follow. Your code must find out where it is (a rank, a host name, environment variables the launcher sets), not assume it; Listing 4 prints its host, and Listing 10 prints the scheduler's job id or "not set". And your measurements must say where they ran: the same job placed on different nodes is a different experiment. R11 D1

Change 4: shared storage and networks become bottlenecks

On a laptop the disk and the memory bus belong to you. On a cluster, the input often lives on a shared file system that every node reads through the network (F5-22), and all nodes' results travel back the same way. Adding nodes divides the compute but not the shared part. Listing 9 makes that concrete with exercise values: with one shared server, 64 nodes are only 10.95 times faster than one, and no number of nodes can beat 13, because 300 s of reading stay; with the input staged to local disks, the speedup is 64. The shape of that table, not its invented numbers, is the lesson: whenever a resource is shared by all nodes, it is the first suspect when a job does not scale. R13 D2

The network is a shared resource too, and in our lab it is the only one: every task costs a request and an answer. For the small job the tasks take about 1–7 ms each (R2's log), and an answer reaches the controller 0.15–1.1 ms after the node finished (R8, after the clock correction of Trap 2): a visible cost for the small job and a negligible one for the wide job of Layer 3. On a real network messages cost more, and DS201's latency and throughput measurements (F5-07) and DG401's alpha-beta model (F8-03) are how you estimate them before you design the split. R2

Change 5: NUMA inside each server

A cluster node is usually a server with more than one processor socket, each with its own memory channels: a NUMA machine (F1-39, F5-28). A core reaching memory attached to the other socket waits longer and shares the link between the sockets. So "where your code runs" has two scales on a cluster: which node, and which cores and memory inside that node. The container of this build reports one socket and one NUMA node (R12), so the effect cannot be shown here; what the lab does show is the mechanism used to control the inner scale: Listing 5 pins each node process to one CPU with sched_setaffinity. R12 D8 D9

Watch out

Untested on hardware: no measurement in this bridge comes from a multi-socket server. On one, first read its topology (the firmware's SRAT and SLIT tables as the OS reports them, F5-28), then pin processes and their memory to the same NUMA node and compare with an unpinned run, using the same harness. Write your own numbers; do not borrow anyone's.

Change 6: observability is required

On the laptop you can run a debugger or watch a process list. On a cluster, the nodes are far away and many, and by the time you look, the moment has passed. What you have is what the programs wrote down. Listing 2's controller therefore writes a structured log: one line per event, a timestamp, an event name and key-value fields (t= 504.4ms event=timeout node=n2 task=1 waited_ms=500 action=suspect_node,requeue_task). From such lines alone you can draw Figure 2, count tasks per node, and find a straggler (the forensic lab). The controller also records two times for each task: how long the node says it worked (on the node's clock) and how long the controller waited for the answer (on its own clock). The difference between the two is where queueing, network and scheduling delays hide, and Layer 3 shows that this difference, not the node's own report, is what exposes a noisy node. R7 R5 D6

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The comparison the card asks for: laptop against three nodes

R3 and R4 are Listing 3 built with -O2 and run twice: a small job (primes below 300,000, 21 timed runs) and a job ten times wider (below 3,000,000, 11 timed runs). In both, the cluster answers were identical to the laptop's in every run. The medians: small job, laptop 19.87 ms and cluster 11.55 ms, a speedup of 1.72; wide job, laptop 497.39 ms and cluster 287.77 ms, a speedup of 1.73. Three nodes, yet less than two times faster. Four explanations are candidates, and the lab lets you test each: R3 R4

  1. Launch cost. R3 reports 0.73 ms to start three nodes, paid once and excluded from the timed runs. It cannot explain a gap of several milliseconds per run, and none of the 270 ms gap of the wide job.
  2. Messages. Twelve requests and twelve answers per run over loopback. R8 shows an answer arriving 0.15–1.1 ms after the node finished; against R3's 11.55 ms that is a real share, against R4's 287.77 ms it is negligible, yet the two speedups are the same.
  3. Uneven work. Later tasks cover larger numbers, which cost more to test. With dynamic assignment the last task to start sets the end; R4's last run shows busy times of 236.4, 280.0 and 263.9 ms on the three nodes, so the slowest node worked about 18 % longer than the fastest.
  4. The nodes do not have three CPUs to themselves. This is the largest effect, and it is the first trap of the bridge.

The check for explanation 4 is Part A of Listing 5 (R5): start k identical copies of the work at once and see how much work the machine really does in parallel. With k = 3 the slowest copy took 116.68 ms against 110.46 ms alone, so the machine did 2.84 CPUs' worth of work at that moment; with k = 4 the slowest took 231.76 ms, only 1.91 CPUs' worth, although four logical CPUs are visible. The container is a virtual machine on a shared host, and how much CPU it really gets changes from minute to minute: R2, the same comparison built with the sanitizers a minute earlier, measured a "speedup" of 0.91 with a laptop spread of 140 %. The three virtual nodes share those CPUs with each other and with whatever else the host is running. That is the honest explanation of 1.72: the program parallelises; the machine under it did not have three free CPUs. R5 R2

Trap 1: benchmarking on a noisy shared node

F2-51 showed noise on one machine; a cluster adds two new forms of it. First, nodes are shared: other jobs, the scheduler's agents, monitoring and, for virtual machines, other tenants of the host take CPU time, cache and memory bandwidth (F5-32's noisy neighbour). Second, a parallel job finishes when its slowest part finishes, so noise on one node becomes the whole job's time. Part B of Listing 5 shows both at once. The three nodes are pinned to CPUs 0, 1 and 2, the controller to CPU 3, and in the noisy runs three busy "neighbour" processes are pinned to CPU 1, the CPU of node n2. Figure 3 draws the medians of R5. R5 D9

Three lessons sit in those four bars. The first is the trap itself: if you benchmarked "your cluster job" in the noisy configuration, you would report 160 ms and blame your code. The second is the straggler: in the noisy fixed run n1 and n3 were finished long before n2, and the job time is n2's time. The third is the observability lesson from Layer 2: n2's own clock said it was busy 133.8 ms for its four tasks, but the controller waited 163.8 ms for them; the part a node cannot see (time before it was even scheduled to run) shows only from the outside. The forensic lab (R14) shows the extreme case, a node whose own timer reports nothing unusual at all; the comparison of the two numbers is the robust signal. R5

The fix is a protocol, not a line of code: (1) measure the machine's real parallel capacity before you measure your program (Part A); (2) ask the scheduler for whole nodes for benchmarks, so that no other job shares them (Slurm's option for this is in the unverified table of F5-23), and record which nodes you got; (3) pin processes and record the pinning; (4) repeat, report the median and the spread, never one run; (5) report per-node times next to the job time, so a straggler is visible; (6) prefer dynamic assignment or smaller tasks when nodes can differ. C2 D1 R5

Trap 2: assuming clocks agree

Every node has its own clock, set and corrected by its own operating system, and two clocks read at the same instant differ by an amount that changes over time (clock skew and drift, F5-10). The three nodes of the virtual cluster share one machine's clock, so Listing 8 injects the error: n2's clock reads 400 ms ahead and n3's 300 ms behind, which is exactly what a misconfigured real node would look like. Then it does what people do with cluster logs: it subtracts timestamps taken on different machines. D3 R8

The results in R8 are absurd in the instructive way. The "queue delay" from the controller's send to the node's start is +400.229 ms for task 1 on n2 and −299.616 ms for task 2 on n3: a task that started before it was sent. In the merged timeline, n3 finishes task 2 at −297.714 ms, before the controller sent anything, and n2 finishes task 1 at +403.506 ms, long after the controller received its answer at +3.657 ms. Seven of the twelve tasks have a finish time outside the only possible window, between their send and the arrival of their answer. Nothing in the program is wrong. The data are wrong because the clocks are. R8

Three rules fix it. First, measure durations on one clock: a task's duration as end minus start on the same node is correct in R8 regardless of the offset (the last column), and a request's round trip is correct on the controller's steady clock. Second, when you must compare clocks, estimate the offset: ask each node for its time several times, keep the probe with the shortest round trip, and take the node's reading minus the midpoint of that round trip. R8's estimates are −0.022, +399.999 and −300.001 ms with error bounds of ±0.031 ms, and after correction all twelve tasks fall inside their windows. Third, for order, use causality, not clocks: an answer is always after its request, whatever the timestamps say, and logical clocks (F5-10, after Lamport) carry that order explicitly. R8 D3 D4

The estimate has limits you must state when you use it. Its error bound is half the shortest round trip, here tens of microseconds because the "network" is loopback; across a real network it is larger. It is a snapshot: clocks drift, so an offset measured now is wrong later, which is why real clusters run a time-synchronisation service continuously and why logs should carry the node's identity next to each timestamp. And the estimate assumes the request and the answer take about equally long; if the paths are asymmetric, the error is still within the bound but no longer centred. D3 D2

Trap 3: no timeouts

R6 is Listing 6: n2 stalls forever on its first task, with its process alive and its connection open, and the controller has no deadline. The log shows eleven tasks done by n1 and n3 in the first 25.3 ms; after that line, nothing. The controller is blocked in poll with an infinite wait, the program prints no error, and only the lab's 5-second limit ends it, with the exit code 124 that timeout documents for "the command timed out". A hang has no symptom except silence, and silence is easy to mistake for "still working". R6 L1 D9

R10 shows that this is not a property of our toy. Listing 4 with the argument stall makes MPI rank 1 sleep before the reduction. Ranks 0 and 2 finish their share and enter MPI_Reduce, which cannot complete without rank 1; nothing in the program has a deadline, and timeout 8 stops mpirun with exit code 124. This is F8-16's "hung job" in miniature, and the reason the curriculum's milestone F8 asks for "a watchdog with timeouts on collectives". R10 D7 C2

R7 is the fix (Listing 7, Figure 2). Every task has a 500 ms deadline. n3 dies on its second task; the closed connection is noticed at once and the task re-queued. n2 stalls for 1.2 s; at 504.4 ms its deadline expires, n2 is suspected, its task goes to n1, and the job finishes at 505.9 ms with the laptop's answer. At 1205.1 ms n2's answer for task 1 finally arrives; the controller ignores it because the task is already done, and then fences n2. Four engineering rules are visible in that log: R7

  • A timeout is a suspicion, not a proof (F5-09, F5-11). n2 was alive and would have answered. The controller must be ready for the late answer.
  • Tasks must be safe to run twice. Task 1 was computed by n2 and by n1. Because it is a pure function of its range, the duplicate is harmless and can be dropped; a task that appended to a file or charged a bank account would need an idempotency key or fencing at the receiver (F5-09, F5-11).
  • Fence what you suspect. A suspected node gets no new work, and at the end it is stopped by force, which on a real cluster means through its BMC.
  • The deadline costs time. The whole job took 503 ms, almost all of it waiting for the deadline: 11 of 12 tasks were done after 28.7 ms. The deadline must be long enough not to suspect healthy-but-slow nodes (tasks here take 1–7 ms; in R5's noisy runs a node waited over 160 ms for four tasks) and short enough that a real stall does not cost too much. Choosing it from measured task times, with margin, is part of the design; heartbeats (F5-11) shorten detection for long tasks.

From this model to a real scheduler

Listing 2 is a teaching model, and its limits are part of the lesson. A real batch scheduler such as Slurm separates two layers that the model merges: the cluster scheduler, which allocates whole nodes (or CPUs, memory and GPUs) to jobs from a queue, enforces time limits and records accounting (F5-23); and the job's own launcher, which starts the processes inside the allocation and gives each its rank (F5-22, F8-07). Our controller plays the second role with dynamic tasks, which an MPI program normally does not do: MPI ranks usually get fixed shares, which is why the straggler and the hung collective of this bridge matter so much on the DG402 path. How the hardware actually does it

A real cluster node is a server: one or more processor sockets with their memory channels (NUMA domains, F1-39 and F5-28), network adapters on PCI Express, often local disks and GPUs, and a BMC on standby power with its own network port (F5-29). Nodes are connected by switches into a cluster network, and a separate management network reaches the BMCs. Each node runs its own operating system with its own clock, kept close to the others by a time-synchronisation service and drifting between corrections. Each node also has its own power and cooling path, which is what makes failures independent (and why correlated failures appear when nodes share a power feed or a switch). D8 D5 D3 D2

The virtual cluster of this lab is honest about some of that and not about the rest. Real: each node is a separate process with its own address space (fork, OS201), the only way between controller and nodes is TCP through the kernel's network stack on the loopback interface (F5-05), a dead process really closes its connection, and the kernel's scheduler really decides when each process runs on which CPU, which is why R5's noise is real noise. Not real: all three nodes share one kernel, one clock (so skew had to be injected), one network interface that never drops a packet, one power supply, and four virtual CPUs whose real capacity varies (R5 Part A). Worked example

1. What should three nodes give? R3's laptop median for the small job is 19.87 ms. If the work divided perfectly over three nodes with no overhead, the job would take 19.87 / 3 = 6.62 ms. The measured cluster median is 11.55 ms, so the parallel efficiency is 6.62 / 11.55 = 0.57, and the speedup 19.87 / 11.55 = 1.72 (R3 prints 1.72). Now use R5 Part A, measured a few seconds later: with three copies at once the machine delivered 2.84 CPUs' worth of work. The best we could have hoped for at that moment is therefore 19.87 / 2.84 = 7.00 ms, and the remaining gap of about 4.5 ms per run belongs to uneven tasks, messages and the noise visible in R3's 24 % spread. R4 confirms the first-order picture with a job 25 times longer: 497.39 / 287.77 = 1.73. R3 R4 R5

2. The shared file server (R13). Exercise values: 600 GB of input, one shared server delivering 2 GB/s in total, compute 3,600 node-seconds. Reading takes 600 / 2 = 300 s however many nodes read, because they share the server. On k = 8 nodes the compute takes 3,600 / 8 = 450 s, so the job takes 300 + 450 = 750 s against 300 + 3,600 = 3,900 s on one node: a speedup of 3,900 / 750 = 5.20. As k grows the compute term vanishes and the job approaches 300 s, so the speedup can never exceed 3,900 / 300 = 13.0. With the input staged to local disks at 1 GB/s per node, reading takes 600 / (8 × 1) = 75 s and the job 75 + 450 = 525 s; relative to one node's 600 + 3,600 = 4,200 s that is 8.00. R13 prints exactly these rows. This is Amdahl's law (F1-30) with the shared server as the serial part. R13

3. Correcting one timestamp (R8). Task 2 ran on n3, whose estimated offset is −300.001 ms. The naive delay from send to start is −299.616 ms. The corrected delay is the node's start minus the offset minus the send time, which is the naive delay minus the offset: −299.616 − (−300.001) = +0.385 ms, the value R8 prints. A positive delay of a few tenths of a millisecond is what a loopback message and a process wake-up should cost. R8

4. What did the deadline cost? (R7). Without faults, the small job takes about 8–12 ms on three nodes (R3). In R7 the last healthy task finished at 28.7 ms on n1 alone (n3 was dead, n2 stalled), and the job could not finish before task 1's deadline expired: send at 3.0 ms + 500 ms = 503.0 ms, observed at 504.4 ms because poll's wake-up and the millisecond rounding of msSince add a little. Task 1 then took 1.4 ms on n1, so the job ended at 505.9 ms. The deadline, not the work, set the job time: halving it would have saved about 250 ms, at the price of more false suspicions on a noisy node. R7

QA note: the divisions in examples 1 and 2 were checked with Python during authoring (6.623, 0.5734, 6.997; 5.2, 13.0, 8.0); the values printed by the programs are in R3, R5 and R13.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Friends can explain; nodes cannot. When Kwame finally answers the phone, he tells Tomás his letter box was blocked. A suspected node tells you nothing: R7's controller never learns why n2 was slow, and n3 leaves only a closed connection. You get the cause only from logs written before the failure, or from the node's BMC and its event log afterwards.
    • 

  • A wrong watch is easy to spot; a wrong clock is not. Ana's half-hour error is obvious to a human reading a letter. A 300 ms error in a node's log looks like a perfectly normal timestamp; only a check such as R8's window test reveals it, and real offsets also drift.
    • 

  • Posting a box costs days; sending a task costs microseconds. In the story communication is the expensive part. In R3 on loopback the messages are cheap and the shared CPUs dominate; on a real network the balance moves again. Measure; do not reason from the story's proportions.
    • 

  • The analogy hides the extra copy. In the story, once Tomás gives Kwame's box to Ana, Kwame's box is "gone". In R7, n2 also finished task 1, so the same task was done twice. The program must be ready for duplicates, which friends exchanging letters rarely meet.
    •

Summary


    

  • Moving from a laptop to a cluster keeps the program, its tests and the measurement protocol; the single-machine answer becomes the oracle for every distributed run.
    • 

  • Nodes fail independently: a closed connection proves a process is gone; a missing answer only raises a suspicion.
    • 

  • Every wait needs a deadline. Without one, a stalled peer causes a silent hang (R6, R10); with one, the task is re-queued, duplicates are ignored, and the suspected node is fenced, on real servers through its BMC.
    • 

  • Cluster nodes and virtual machines are shared: measure capacity first, use whole nodes and pinning for benchmarks, report medians, spreads and per-node times, because the slowest node sets the job's time.
    • 

  • Clocks of different machines disagree: measure durations on one clock, estimate offsets with round trips when you must compare, and use causality for order.
    • 

  • Schedulers decide where code runs; shared storage and networks limit scaling like a serial part in Amdahl's law; NUMA adds placement inside each server.
    • 

  • Observability is the only window: structured logs from both sides of every exchange let you reconstruct what happened.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. R14 is forensic.cpp (below) with fixed assignment (task t to node t mod 3); node n2 waits 25 ms before starting every task (queueMs = 25, a stand-in for waiting for a CPU on a shared node); node n3's clock is set 300 ms behind (clockOffsetMs = -300). Nothing else was changed.




    

  1. Observation (from R14): n1 has tasks 0, 3, 6, 9; n2 has 1, 4, 7, 10; n3 has 2, 5, 8, 11. Assign-to-done gaps in the controller log are a few milliseconds for n1 and n3 (for example task 0: 3.4 to 6.3 ms; task 11: 15.2 to 27.3 ms), but about 27 ms for every n2 task (task 1: 3.5 to 30.1; task 4: 30.1 to 57.1; task 7: 57.3 to 84.6; task 10: 84.9 to 112.5), while n2's own node_ms is only 1.2–2.3 ms.   2. "n2's tasks are harder" predicts long node_ms on n2 and similar values for neighbouring tasks elsewhere: rejected, n2's work takes 1–2 ms like the others. "The network to n2 is slow" predicts a delay before start and after end; the node log shows the start of each n2 task about 25 ms after its send (task 1 sent at about 0 ms after the first send, n2 start at +25.6) and the end close to the controller's receipt: the delay is before the start. "n2 waits before it starts each task" fits all numbers.

  3. n2 is the straggler. Each of its four tasks loses about 25 ms before it starts, and with fixed assignment its tasks cannot go elsewhere, so they run one after another after n1 and n3 have finished (n1 at 17.6 ms, n3 at 27.3 ms in the controller log): 4 × about 27 ms ≈ 110 ms of job time. The cause is on the node, before the work: on a real cluster a shared or overloaded node, as in R5.
