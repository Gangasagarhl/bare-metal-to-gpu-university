Story hook

Ines's uncle Tomasz has a second sheet of paper. This time he does not draw doors. He draws everything that moves into and out of the flat in a normal week: letters through the letterbox, shopping carried up the stairs, the neighbour who waters the plants with the spare key, the cleaner on Thursday, parcels left with the porter downstairs. Each thing gets an arrow. Then he draws a dashed line wherever the arrow passes from "people we don't control" to "people we trust": at the front door, at the porter's desk, at the letterbox.

"Now," he says, "for each arrow that crosses a dashed line, ask six questions. Could someone pretend to be the neighbour? Could someone change the parcel on the way? If something goes missing, can we tell who had it? Can anyone read the letters? Can anyone block the stairs? Can the cleaner get into rooms she should not?" Ines counts: eleven arrows, five dashed crossings, six questions each. It is a lot of questions, but none of them needed inspiration. That is the point of this chapter.

What you will be able to do

  • Draw a data-flow diagram of a system with external entities, processes, data stores, data flows and trust boundaries, and check it against the drawing rules.
  • Name the six STRIDE categories and the security property each one violates.
  • Enumerate threats with STRIDE per element, and rank them by trust-boundary crossings, with the course's engine (Listing 1).
  • Write a threat table with one decision per threat, and tell a claimed control from a verified one.
  • Diagnose a reviewed diagram whose missing trust boundary hid the threats that mattered.
Jargon box
Data-flow diagram (DFD)
Simple words: a drawing of what moves through a system, from where to where. Analogy: Tomasz's second sheet: letters, shopping and keys as arrows. Precise: a diagram made of four element types (external entities, processes, data stores, data flows) plus trust boundaries; the course uses it as the answer to "what are we working on?" because threats attach to its elements. D1
External entity
Simple words: a person or system outside your control that sends or receives data. Analogy: the postman. Precise: a DFD element representing something the system interacts with but does not implement or control (a user, another company's service, a sensor's physical environment). D1
Process (DFD)
Simple words: a piece of your system that does something with data. Analogy: the porter who sorts parcels. Precise: a DFD element that transforms or forwards data: a program, a service, a firmware task. Not the same as an operating-system process (OS201), although one often maps onto the other. D1
Data store
Simple words: a place where data waits. Analogy: the cupboard where parcels are kept. Precise: a DFD element that holds data at rest: a file, a database, a parameter area in flash, a log. D1
Trust boundary
Simple words: a line where "who is in charge" or "who can be trusted" changes. Analogy: the castle wall and its gate; the front door of the flat. Precise: a line on the DFD between elements that run with different privileges, belong to different owners or are reachable by different attackers; flows that cross it are where most threats concentrate. D1
STRIDE (threat categories)
Simple words: six kinds of thing that can go wrong on purpose, one per letter. Analogy: Tomasz's six questions for each arrow. Precise: Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege; each is the violation of one security property (authentication, integrity, non-repudiation, confidentiality, availability, authorization). Not the "stride" of memory access (HW203). D1
STRIDE per element
Simple words: a rule for which of the six questions to ask about which kind of element. Analogy: you do not ask whether a cupboard can lie about its identity. Precise: a table mapping DFD element types to the STRIDE categories that usually apply to them, used to enumerate threats systematically (Layer 3 gives the course's table). D1
Spoofing · Tampering · Repudiation · Information disclosure · Denial of service · Elevation of privilege
Simple words: pretending to be someone else · changing data or code · denying you did something · reading what you should not · stopping something from working · getting more power than you were given. Analogy: a fake neighbour · a swapped parcel · "I never took the key" · reading the letters · blocking the stairs · the cleaner opening the locked study. Precise: the six STRIDE categories, threats against authentication, integrity, non-repudiation, confidentiality, availability and authorization respectively. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A castle has walls, and the guards stand at the gates, because that is where people and carts come in. To protect a system, first draw what moves in it: who sends what to whom. Then draw the walls: the places where "inside" turns into "outside". Every arrow that passes through a wall is a gate, and at every gate you ask the same six questions: is this really who it claims to be, could the load have been changed, can we prove later who sent it, can strangers read it, can someone block it, and could someone get more power through it than they should? The six questions have a name made of their first letters: STRIDE.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Drawing the system

A data-flow diagram has four kinds of element. External entities are people and systems you do not control. Processes are the parts of your system that do something with data. Data stores hold data at rest. Data flows are arrows from one element to another, labelled with what moves. On top of these you draw trust boundaries: dashed lines around groups of elements that trust each other. D1

A DFD is not a class diagram, a network diagram or a flowchart. It shows data, not control: an arrow says "this data moves this way", not "this code calls that code". Shostack recommends DFDs for threat modelling because they make the places where data crosses from one trust level to another visible, and those are where attacks happen. D1

Drawing rules

The course checks four rules, and the engine checks them for you (Listing 1, lines 131–168). Data does not move by itself, so every flow has a process at one end at least: a store does not send data to another store, and a person does not write to a database without some program in between. Every element has at least one flow; an element with none is either missing its arrows or does not belong in the diagram. Every flow names elements that exist. And there is at least one trust boundary: a diagram with none claims that everything trusts everything, which is almost never true and always worth a written reason. D1 R1

From letters to a list

STRIDE per element turns the categories into a procedure: for every element of the diagram, ask the letters that apply to its type (Figure 2). An external entity can be spoofed and can repudiate; a process can suffer all six; a store can be tampered with, read and made unavailable (and repudiation matters when the store is a log); a flow can be tampered with, read and blocked. D1 The worked example has 6 elements and 7 flows and the procedure produces 48 candidate threats; the course robot of F11-15 produces 90. R1 Most are quick to decide, but none is skipped without a decision.

The per-element table is a heuristic, not a law. It says where threats of each kind usually live, so that a team with limited time asks the questions most likely to find something. It can be wrong for a specific system: a data store that is a hardware security element can be spoofed (a counterfeit part), and an external entity can be the victim of information disclosure. Teams that find a threat outside the table write it down anyway. D1

Where to draw boundaries

A trust boundary goes wherever one of three things changes: privilege (user space and kernel; normal world and secure world, F11-05), ownership (your service and someone else's; your robot and the visitor's phone), or reachability (things only you can reach and things anyone on the network or within radio range can reach). Physical boundaries matter for robots and drones: the case of the robot is a boundary for the attacker "someone who can touch it", and the radio link is a boundary for "anyone within range". The engine models boundaries as zones: each element belongs to exactly one zone, and a flow between two zones crosses a boundary. D1 R1

Zones are a simplification. Real systems nest boundaries (a container on a host on a cloud account), and some boundaries cut through an element (a process that runs partly with privileges). When that matters, split the element: "Relay service, public API" and "Relay service, admin API" as two processes in two zones. The engine's rule "boundary first" then puts the public API's threats at the top.

Levels of detail

A first diagram has one process per program and shows the whole system on one page; when a process hides a boundary or holds most of the threats, draw it again in more detail on a second page. The course keeps diagrams to one page each and links pages by the element they expand. The rule of thumb for "detailed enough": every trust boundary that matters is drawn, and every element's threats can be decided without arguing about what is inside it. D1

From threats to decisions

The threat table is the deliverable: one row per threat with an id, the letter, the element, a sentence describing the threat in this system, the decision (mitigate, remove, transfer, accept), the owner, and for mitigations the test that proves it. The engine prints the first three columns and marks threats for which the diagram claims a control ("claimed: enc"). A claimed control is not a verified one: a flow marked [enc] may be encrypted with a key that every robot shares, or encryption may be on in the design and off in the build. The table's last column, the test, is what turns a claim into evidence; F11-15 writes such tests. R1

Ranking is not scoring. The engine sorts threats on elements and flows that touch a trust boundary first, because a threat there can be exercised by an attacker on the other side without first breaking something else. Within that group, the team ranks by the harm to the assets of F11-13 and by how cheap the attack is (the attack trees). The course avoids formulas that multiply guessed numbers, for the reason given in F11-13. D1

How the hardware actually does it

Many trust boundaries of a DFD are enforced by hardware, and a diagram is only as true as the mechanism behind each dashed line. The boundary between a user program and the kernel is the processor's privilege levels and the page tables the kernel controls (F3 courses, OS303). The boundary between the normal and secure worlds on an Arm system is TrustZone (F11-05). The boundary between a device that does DMA and the rest of memory is the IOMMU: the curriculum's design table tells driver writers to "treat device-provided values (lengths, indices, descriptor contents) as untrusted" and to enable the IOMMU, because external devices can attack through DMA. C2 On a robot, the serial or CAN link between the main computer and the motor microcontroller is a boundary only if the microcontroller checks what it receives; if it obeys anything, the two are one trust zone, and the diagram must say so.

The practical consequence: next to each dashed line, write the mechanism that enforces it ("page tables", "IOMMU on", "message codes, F11-15", "nothing: same zone"). A boundary with "nothing" beside it is a wish. The forensic lab of this chapter is about a team that drew no boundary at all.

Worked example

Task. Model the plant-watering robot of Figure 1: the gardener taps a schedule into a phone app, the app sends it to a relay service on the internet, the relay keeps it in a store and passes it to the robot controller at home, which logs what it waters and reports status back.

  1. Elements. One external entity (Gardener), three processes (Phone app, Relay service, Robot controller), two stores (Schedule store, Watering log, marked [log]).
  2. Zones. user, phone, cloud, home: four owners and four places an attacker could stand.
  3. Flows. Seven, written as in stride.in. The relay claims authentication of its callers ([auth]); three flows claim encryption ([enc]).
  4. Crossings. Taps (user to phone), schedule (phone to cloud), schedule (cloud to home), status (home to cloud): 4. R1
  5. Count. External entity: 2 letters. Three processes: 3 × 6 = 18. Schedule store: 3. Watering log: 4. Seven flows: 7 × 3 = 21. Total 2 + 18 + 3 + 4 + 21 = 48, of which 4 have a claimed control (one S on the relay, three I on flows): 44 open. The engine prints the same numbers. R1
  6. Order. Everything that touches a crossing comes first: the gardener (his taps cross user|phone), the three processes and the four crossing flows, T01–T32. The two stores and the three flows that stay inside one zone come last, T33–T48: they only talk to processes in their own zone. R1
  7. Five decisions. T27 (tampering of the schedule from relay to robot): mitigate with a message code from a key the relay and robot share; test: a changed schedule is rejected (F11-15). T15 (spoofing of the robot controller: someone pretends to be the robot to the relay): mitigate with a per-robot key; test: status from an unknown key is refused. T13 (denial of service of the relay): transfer and accept: the robot keeps its last good schedule if the relay is down, owner: robot team; test: unplug the network, the robot still waters. T06 (information disclosure by the phone app): accept for this version, reason: the schedule on the phone is the gardener's own data. T20 (elevation of privilege in the robot controller via a malformed schedule): mitigate by a bounded parser fuzzed in the SS302 way; test: the fuzzer finds no crash in a fixed time budget.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A castle wall is visible; a trust boundary is a decision. Nobody argues where a stone wall is. A trust boundary exists only if a mechanism enforces it (Hardware section), and the same network cable can be inside one boundary in one design and cross one in another.
    • 

  • A castle has one wall; systems nest them. A container on a host in a cloud account has three boundaries around it, and attackers who are already inside one of them (a malicious tenant, a compromised dependency) are the usual case, not the exception.
    • 

  • Guards stand only at gates; software threats also live inside. STRIDE per element asks about every process, including those with no crossing flow, because a bug inside a trusted zone (elevation of privilege) can turn into a new gate.
    • 

  • Six questions at a castle gate are quick; at a software gate they are not. The letters are prompts, not tests. "Can the load be changed?" for a radio link needs knowledge of the protocol (F11-16), not a glance.
    •
