Story hook

Ines and her uncle Tomasz are going away for a week. Before they leave, Tomasz asks her a strange question: "If you were a burglar, how would you get into our flat?" Ines starts with the front door, then the balcony window. Tomasz writes each answer on a sheet of paper, with a box in the middle labelled "what a burglar wants": the laptop, the bicycle, the box of family photos.

After ten minutes the list looks complete. Then the neighbour's son walks past and says, "You still keep the spare key under the flower pot, right?" Nobody had written that down. It is not a door or a window. It is a way in that the family itself made, for its own convenience, and then forgot.

Ines adds one more line: the robot vacuum cleaner that has a camera and answers a phone app. Can someone else's phone talk to it too? She does not know. That is the moment this chapter is about: you sit down on purpose, think like someone who wants to cause harm, and write down what you find before they do.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a threat model is, which four questions it answers, and why it is written before an attack, not after one.
  • List the assets, entry points and attackers of a small system, and draw them on one page.
  • Build and evaluate an attack tree with AND and OR nodes, and show how a mitigation moves the cheapest attack somewhere else.
  • Inventory entry points from the running system itself, not only from its documentation.
  • Run a threat-modelling workshop and review another team's model with a checklist.
  • Diagnose from logs a threat model that missed an entry point.
Jargon box
Threat model
Simple words: a written answer to "what could go wrong with this thing on purpose, and what will we do about it?" Analogy: thinking like a burglar about your own house. Precise: a structured description of a system, the threats against it and the decisions taken about each threat, produced by the people who build the system and kept up to date with it; the course follows Shostack's framing around four questions (Layer 2). D1
Asset
Simple words: something worth protecting or worth attacking. Analogy: the laptop, the bicycle and the photos in the box in the middle of the sheet. Precise: anything of value in or reachable through the system: data (a camera stream, a map, a key), a capability (making the robot move), or availability (the robot doing its job); some assets are also stepping stones to others. D1
Entry point (attack surface)
Simple words: a place where something from outside can get in. Analogy: a door, a window, a cat flap or a key under a pot. Precise: an interface through which data, commands or physical access enter the system: a network port, a radio, a button, a file the system reads, an update channel, a debug or service connector. Not the entry point of a program, the address where its execution starts (F2-44). D1
Attack surface
Simple words: all the entry points together. Analogy: every door, window and hiding place of the house, counted. Precise: the set of entry points and the code and data reachable through them; reducing it (closing ports, removing features, disabling service connectors in shipped builds) removes threats instead of mitigating them one by one. D1
Threat and vulnerability
Simple words: a threat is something bad that could happen; a vulnerability is a weakness that lets it happen. Analogy: "a burglar comes in through the balcony" is a threat; "the balcony window does not lock" is a vulnerability. Precise: a threat is a possible harmful action against an asset by someone (or something) through an entry point; a vulnerability is a specific flaw in design or implementation that makes a threat achievable. Threat modelling works mostly at the threat level, early, before every vulnerability is known. D1
Attack tree
Simple words: a drawing that breaks one bad goal into the smaller steps an attacker would need. Analogy: "get in" splits into "through the door" OR "through the window"; "through the door" needs "get the key" AND "nobody is home". Precise: a tree whose root is an attacker goal and whose children are alternative (OR) or jointly required (AND) sub-goals; leaves carry attributes such as cost or required skill, and the tree is evaluated bottom-up (Listing 1). D1 R1
Mitigation
Simple words: something you change so that a threat becomes harder or impossible. Analogy: moving the spare key to a trusted neighbour. Precise: a design or implementation change that addresses a threat; the other possible decisions about a threat are to remove the feature that causes it, to transfer it to someone else, or to accept it with a written reason (Layer 2). D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When you lock up a house, you think about doors. A burglar does not care about doors; he cares about getting in, and any way will do. So the trick is to switch sides for an hour. You pretend to be the burglar, walk around your own house, and write down every way in, what you would take, and what would stop you. Then you switch back and fix the easiest ways in first. A computer, a robot or a drone has ways in too: its network, its radio, its buttons, the little connector inside the case. The list is the threat model. You make it before anyone tries, and you redo it whenever the house changes.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why model at all. SS301 built chains of trust (F11-01) and SS302 removed classes of memory bugs (F11-07 to F11-12). Both are mitigations: answers. Threat modelling is how you find the questions those answers belong to, for a whole system, before code exists or before it ships. Shostack's book, the reading for this course, presents it as an engineering activity that anyone on a team can learn, not a talent of security specialists. D1

Four questions

The course organises every threat model around four questions, following Shostack: what are we working on; what can go wrong; what are we going to do about it; did we do a good job? D1

  1. What are we working on? A model of the system: in this chapter a sheet like Figure 1, from F11-14 on a data-flow diagram. Without it, people discuss different systems without noticing.
  2. What can go wrong? Threats, found with a method rather than by inspiration: attack trees (this chapter), STRIDE (F11-14), and lists of known attacks for the technology at hand.
  3. What are we going to do about it? A decision per threat: mitigate it, remove the feature, transfer it to someone better placed, or accept it with a written reason and an owner.
  4. Did we do a good job? A check of the model itself: is the diagram true to the system, does every threat have a decision, was every mitigation tested?

Assets, entry points, attackers

The first sheet of any model has the three parts of Figure 1. Assets are what is worth protecting. For a robot these are not only data: "the robot moves only when its owner wants" is an asset, and so is "the robot keeps working". Entry points are where anything from outside gets in. Attackers are described by what they can reach and what they want, not by stereotypes: "someone on the same Wi-Fi network", "a visitor who can touch the robot for a minute", "someone who has the robot on a bench for a day". D1

Shostack warns against starting from attackers or from assets alone, and prefers starting from the system that you build, because that is the thing you know best. D1 The course follows that advice: the sheet's centre is the system, and assets and attackers are written around it.

Attack trees

An attack tree takes one attacker goal and breaks it down. Each node is OR (any child is enough) or AND (all children are needed). Leaves get an attribute: here an effort in points, elsewhere a skill level, an amount of money, or "needs physical access". Evaluating the tree bottom-up gives the cheapest way to the goal: an OR node is as cheap as its cheapest child, an AND node costs the sum of its children. D1 Listing 1 does exactly that. Its run (R1) shows the lesson every attack tree teaches: raise the cost of the cheapest path and the attacker's best path moves elsewhere. After the first mitigation, the cheapest way to make the robot move is no longer the Wi-Fi port (now 12 points) but getting into the house and pressing its buttons (7 points). The next two mitigations make the Wi-Fi and program-replacement branches much more expensive (20 and 22), but the cheapest attack stays at 7, because neither touches the buttons branch. R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Threat, vulnerability, risk, decision

Keep four words apart. A threat is what could happen ("someone on the Wi-Fi makes the robot drive"). A vulnerability is a specific flaw that makes it possible ("the command port accepts any well-formed frame", F11-15). Risk combines how likely and how bad; the course does not ask for numbers, because effort points, probabilities and impact scores are guesses and multiplying guesses gives a guess that looks precise. What the course asks for is a decision per threat with an owner, a reason and a test. D1

What attack trees are good and bad at

Attack trees are good at three things. They make you write down the steps, which exposes shared steps: "get into the house" appears in two branches of Figure 2, so one mitigation there (a locked room for the robot's dock) helps twice. They make cost-based reasoning explicit and arguable. And they are easy to review: a reviewer can point at a missing branch. D1

They are bad at completeness. A tree contains what its authors thought of, and the forensic lab of this chapter is about the branch nobody thought of. The costs are opinions; two teams will give different numbers, and both will be right about something. And trees grow quickly: a realistic tree for a robot has hundreds of leaves, many repeated. The course therefore uses attack trees for one goal at a time (usually the worst outcome for each asset), and uses the structured enumeration of F11-14 to find the threats a tree misses.

Two semantic details matter when you compute with a tree. First, AND nodes that share a leaf (the house appears twice) are counted twice by a plain sum; Listing 1 does that, and the effect is visible in the 15-point program branch, which an attacker who already got into the house to press the buttons would get for 9 more. A more careful evaluation treats shared steps as paid once per attack. Second, costs combine differently for different attributes: effort adds up, but "needs physical access" is a yes/no that any leaf can set, and "skill" is the maximum along a path, not the sum. R1

Scope, assumptions and the living model

Every model has a scope ("the robot, its app and its home network; not the phone's operating system") and assumptions ("the owner's phone is not compromised"). Write both on the first page. An assumption is a threat you decided not to model; reviewers attack assumptions first, which is their job. When the system changes (a new feature, a new radio, a new update channel), the model changes in the same pull request as the design document (SE courses: the design doc is the house blueprint, F12). A model that describes last year's robot is worse than none, because it gives confidence it has not earned. D1

The workshop

This course runs threat modelling as a workshop: three to five people, one system, one whiteboard or shared document, ninety minutes, with fixed roles. The owner of the system draws and answers questions. The scribe writes every threat down, even ones that sound silly, with a number. The facilitator keeps the method (now we do entry points; now we do the tree for this asset) and the time. The reviewers play attackers. At the end every threat has a decision, an owner and, for mitigations, a test. Peer review then happens a few days later by a different team, using the checklist in the lab. The roles and timings are this course's choice, not a standard.

How the hardware actually does it

For a robot or a drone, the entry points of a threat model are physical things: radios, network interfaces, buttons, USB and serial connectors, removable storage, and service or debug connectors on the boards. SS301 showed what a debug path can do to a chain of trust: whoever can halt the processor or rewrite flash does not need a software bug (F11-01, F11-06). In the threat model, every such connector is an entry point with the attacker "someone who holds the device", and the usual decision is to disable or lock it in shipped builds, which removes it from the attack surface instead of mitigating it. Whether and how a given microcontroller lets you lock its debug interface is in its reference manual; this course does not name parts. D1

The practical lesson for software entry points is that the device knows its own entry points better than its manual does. A Linux system keeps a table of its TCP sockets in /proc/net/tcp, one line per socket with the local address and port in hexadecimal and a state code. Listing 2 opens a listener that no document mentions, then finds it by reading that table. The state code 0A (decimal 10) is the listening state: the C library header in this build numbers the TCP states from TCP_ESTABLISHED = 1 to TCP_LISTEN in tenth place, and the run found its own listening socket with state 0A. L1 R2 An inventory built this way (sockets, open serial devices, enabled radios, mounted removable media) is the evidence that the "entry points" list is complete; the forensic lab shows what happens without it.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The layout of /proc/net/tcp beyond what Listing 2 uses (the column order "slot, local address, remote address, state" and the byte order of the address field) is taken from the course author's memory of the Linux kernel documentation for the proc file system and from this one run, in which the loopback address appeared as 0100007F. Check the kernel documentation of /proc/net for the kernel version you use before parsing other columns.

Worked example

Task. Evaluate the tree of Figure 2 by hand, apply the first mitigation, and predict what the program will print.

  1. Leaves. Wi-Fi branch: 3 + 2 + 1. Buttons branch: 6 + 1. Program branch: 6 + 4 + 5.
  2. AND nodes. Sum their leaves: 6, 7 and 15.
  3. OR root. The cheapest child: 6, through the Wi-Fi command port.
  4. Mitigation 1. A separate network for the robot with its own long password: the leaf "join the home Wi-Fi network" goes from 3 to 9, so the Wi-Fi branch becomes 9 + 2 + 1 = 12.
  5. New root value. min(12, 7, 15) = 7: the buttons branch. The attacker now has to get into the house.
  6. Mitigations 2 and 3. +8 on "send a drive message" (Wi-Fi branch 20) and +7 on the service port (program branch 22). The root stays at 7.
  7. Conclusion for the decision table. The next useful mitigation is on the buttons branch (a child lock, or a dock in a room the visitor cannot enter), or the team accepts "visitor in the room presses buttons" with a written reason: the visitor could also push the robot by hand.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A house has a few entrances; software has very many. Every message type, every file format the robot reads, every parameter it accepts is a door. The registry says so plainly: software has many more entrances than a house. That is why F11-14 replaces walking around the house with a systematic method.
    • 

  • A burglar has to be there; an attacker often does not. A burglar tries one house at a time. A program can try every robot on a network, or every network in a city, without anyone travelling, so "unlikely, nobody would bother" is a weaker argument for software.
    • 

  • Stolen things are gone; stolen data is copied. When a laptop is stolen you notice. When a camera stream is copied, nothing disappears, so detection needs logs, not an empty shelf (repudiation and information disclosure in F11-14).
    • 

  • A house changes slowly; software changes every release. A threat model of a house can last years; a robot's model must be updated with every new feature, as Layer 3 says.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: SS301 (F11-01 to F11-06) built chains of trust for boot and updates, and SS302 (F11-07 to F11-12) hardened code against memory bugs and untrusted input. What carries over: the castle's habit of checking every visitor at a gate, and the rule that input from outside is untrusted. What changes: you no longer start from one mechanism; you start from the whole system and ask where it can be attacked, and the answer is a written list of decisions, not code; the method must find threats you have no mechanism for yet. Lab


Goal. Hold the first threat-modelling workshop of the course on the home robot of Figure 1, produce the sheet (assets, entry points, attackers, scope, assumptions), one attack tree evaluated with Listing 1, a decision table, and a peer review of another team's result. (Course card: "Threat-modelling workshops with peer review.") C1


Hardware and software needed. No hardware. A Linux host with g++ 13.3.0 (the version recorded in this build's logs) and the lab folder university/labs/F11-13. Three to five people, or one learner playing the roles in turn and writing each role's notes separately.


Safety. None beyond normal computer use. One rule of conduct for the whole course: model and test only systems you own or are explicitly allowed to test; probing other people's devices, networks or radios can be illegal even when "nothing happens".


Steps.


    

  1. Run university/labs/run_lab.sh university/labs/F11-13 from the repository root. You should see attack_tree.out, listeners.out and forensic.out as in this chapter, all with exit code 0.
    2. 

  2. Workshop, 15 minutes: the owner draws the sheet of Figure 1 for the robot your team built or simulated in the RB courses (not the toy one). Write the scope and at least three assumptions.
    3. 

  3. Workshop, 20 minutes: the reviewers list entry points; the scribe numbers them. Then run Listing 2's idea on the robot computer (or on your simulation host): list its listening sockets and compare with the list. Every difference is a finding.
    4. 

  4. Workshop, 30 minutes: pick the worst outcome for the most important asset, write its attack tree in the format of attack_tree.in, agree on effort points, and run Listing 1.
    5. 

  5. Workshop, 25 minutes: a decision for each leaf on the cheapest two paths (mitigate, remove, transfer, accept), with an owner and a test for each mitigation; add MITIGATE lines and re-run until the cheapest attack is one you accept in writing.
    6. 

  6. Peer review, a few days later, by another team, using the checklist under Verification. The reviewers write their findings; the owners answer each one.
    7. 



Expected observations. As in R1: mitigating the cheapest leaf moves the cheapest attack to another branch; several mitigations can leave the cheapest attack unchanged. On a real robot computer, the socket inventory usually shows services that the team did not list (for example a remote-shell service enabled by the operating-system image); write down which ones you found, since this build had no robot to check.


Verification. The peer-review checklist (this course's): (1) the scope and assumptions are written; (2) every entry point in the live inventory is in the list, or excluded with a reason; (3) every asset has at least one attacker goal considered; (4) the attack tree's file runs through Listing 1 without errors; (5) every leaf on the two cheapest paths has a decision and an owner; (6) every mitigation has a test that would fail if the mitigation were removed; (7) the model has a date and the version of the system it describes.


Troubleshooting. "MITIGATE names no leaf" → the leaf text must match exactly, including capital letters. A leaf under the wrong parent → check two-space indentation. listeners prints "NO" → the machine has no /proc/net/tcp (not Linux, or a restricted container); use the operating system's own socket-listing tool and record which one.


Extension. Add a second attribute to the leaves ("physical access: yes/no") and make the program report the cheapest attack that needs no physical access. Then change the evaluation so that a leaf shared by two branches is paid once per attack.



Forensic lab


Scenario. "The door nobody listed." A family's home robot left its dock at 02:14, drove three metres and opened its camera stream. The household had a threat model (written from the robot's manual) and a small monitor built from it that watches every listed entry point. The monitor raised no alert. The family asks whether the robot "did it by itself".


Evidence pack. The household's model, the monitor's log and the robot's motor log, printed by the evidence generator forensic.cpp (synthetic household, real run): R3

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. The motor log has 8 commands; the monitor saw 5. The three missing ones are at 02:14:09 (undock), 02:14:21 (drive forward 300 cm) and 02:14:58 (open camera stream), all through the entry point bluetooth-setup, which is not in the household's model.


Hypotheses. (1) A fault: the robot moved by itself (a scheduled clean, a software bug). That predicts commands with an internal source, or none at all, and no camera stream. (2) Someone sent commands through an entry point that the model did not list. That predicts commands with an external entry point absent from the model. The motor log records an external entry point and three deliberate commands in 49 seconds, including opening the camera stream: hypothesis 2.


Root cause. The model's entry points were copied from the manual ("written from the robot's manual"); the robot's Bluetooth setup mode was not in it, so the monitor, built from the model, did not watch it. The monitor did exactly what it was built to do; its "0 alerts" was evidence about the model, not about the robot.


Fix and verification. Inventory entry points from the robot itself (its listening services, its radios and their modes), add bluetooth-setup to the model, and either disable the setup mode after pairing or require the owner's approval for it. Verify by re-running the inventory and by a test that sends a command over the setup channel and expects a rejection and an alert.


Prevention. A review item: "the live inventory matches the entry-point list" at every release (lab checklist item 2). A monitor that alerts on any command from an entry point not in its list, instead of ignoring it.


Wrong answers and why. "The monitor is broken": it logged every command on the listed entry points correctly. "The robot did it by itself": the motor log names an external entry point. "Add more monitoring": a second monitor built from the same incomplete list would also miss it. How the evidence was produced: forensic.cpp holds the model and the commands; the monitor's log is computed from the model's list; the scenario is synthetic.
