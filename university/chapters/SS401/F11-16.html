Story hook

Ayesha is flying a survey drone over a field for a farming project. Her laptop's ground-station app shows the drone forty metres away, climbing along its planned line. Then, without anyone touching the laptop, the status line changes to "landing". The drone descends into the tall grass, well away from where it should be.

Back at the van, her friend Joon-ho opens the drone's log. It says the drone received a "land" command from the ground station. Ayesha shakes her head: she never pressed anything, and the app's own history shows no such command. Two logs disagree. One of them is telling the truth about what was sent; the other is telling the truth about what was received.

A castle that receives orders by messenger must ask who sent the messenger, not only what the message says. A radio link is a gate in the open air: anyone within range can stand at it and shout. This chapter models that gate, and its forensic lab is Ayesha's question: who sent that command?

What you will be able to do

  • Draw the data-flow diagram of a drone with its telemetry radio, RC link, satellite navigation and companion computer, with the radio channel as a trust boundary reachable by "anyone with a compatible radio".
  • Enumerate and rank the drone's threats, and separate the ones a protocol can fix (spoofing, tampering, replay) from the ones it cannot (jamming).
  • Explain what link signing protects and what it leaves open, using a toy signed link that the course built and ran.
  • Diagnose a spoofed command from a receiver log and a sender log: sequence numbers, signal strength, timing and the absence of the command on the sender's side.
  • Write the drone link's rows of the threat table, with safety mechanisms for the threats that stay open.
Jargon box
Telemetry link
Simple words: the two-way radio conversation between a drone and the ground station. Analogy: messengers riding between the castle and a scout in the field. Precise: a data link (often a pair of radio modems) carrying status from the vehicle and commands, missions and parameters to it, usually with a message protocol such as MAVLink on top (F10-12); distinct from the RC link that carries stick positions. D2
Sender id (system id, component id)
Simple words: the name a message gives for who sent it. Analogy: the name written on the outside of a letter. Precise: fields in each frame of a protocol like MAVLink that identify the sending system and the component inside it; they are claims made by the sender, not proofs, unless the frame is authenticated. D2
Link signing
Simple words: adding to every radio frame a seal that only holders of a shared key can make. Analogy: the castle's wax seal on every message the scout receives. Precise: per-frame authentication with a shared secret: a truncated keyed hash over the frame and a timestamp or counter, checked by the receiver, which also rejects frames older than the last accepted one on that stream (Listing 2 is the course's toy version). D2 R2
Jamming
Simple words: drowning out a radio link with noise so that nothing gets through. Analogy: someone shouting so loudly at the gate that no messenger can be heard. Precise: denial of service on a radio channel by transmitting interference; no message protocol prevents it, so the vehicle needs a failsafe for link loss (F10-12). Deliberate jamming is illegal in many countries; check your national rules. D1 C2
GNSS spoofing
Simple words: faking the satellite signals a drone uses to know where it is. Analogy: moving the road signs so the scout walks the wrong way. Precise: transmitting counterfeit satellite-navigation signals so that a receiver computes a wrong position or time; in the DFD it is spoofing of the external entity "satellite navigation signals" and tampering of its flow. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A radio is like shouting across a field. Everyone in the field can hear you, and anyone can shout back pretending to be you. A drone that obeys every shout that starts with "this is the ground station" will obey a stranger too. So the real ground station adds a seal to every shout, made with a secret only it and the drone know, plus a number that always goes up. The drone ignores shouts without a good seal and shouts with an old number. One thing no seal can stop: someone shouting so loudly that nothing else gets through. For that, the drone needs a plan of its own, such as coming home when it stops hearing the ground station.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The drone's diagram

The drone of Figure 1 has three radio links into its zone: telemetry (ground radio to air radio and back), RC (stick frames from the transmitter) and satellite navigation (signals from space that anyone on the ground can imitate). Each crosses a boundary, and the telemetry link has a second sender drawn explicitly: "anyone with a compatible radio", in its own zone. The engine reports 7 crossing flows and 104 threats, none with a claimed control. R1

Drawing the radio channel's other users as an external entity is the key step. Without it, the diagram suggests that the air radio hears only the ground radio, and the spoofing threat on the air radio (T17) looks like a threat against a trusted neighbour. With it, the flow "anyone with a compatible radio → air radio" has its own T, I and D rows (T47–T49), and nobody can forget that the channel is shared. R1 D1

Assets

The drone's assets mirror the robot's of F11-15: people and property under and around the flight path are not hurt; the drone flies only where and when the pilot wants; the mission completes or ends safely; the flight log and the camera data (they record places and times); the keys and parameters. A spoofed land command is a hazard if the landing spot is unsafe; a spoofed "disarm" in flight is worse. The threat table marks these rows as hazards, as in F11-15.

What a protocol can and cannot fix

Sort the radio threats into two groups. Spoofing, tampering and replay of frames (T17, T44, T47 and their relatives) can be fixed by the protocol: authenticate every frame and check freshness, as F11-15 did on the robot's command path. Information disclosure (T45, T51) can be fixed by encryption if the link supports it. Denial of service (T46, T49, and jamming of the RC link and satellite signals) cannot be fixed by any protocol: a strong enough transmitter on the same frequency blocks the link. For that group, the mitigation is in the vehicle's behaviour: a failsafe on link loss (F10-12 built and tested one in simulation), geofences, and a mission design that keeps the drone safe when it hears nothing. D1 C2

Link signing, in the course's toy link

Listing 2, "ULink", is the course's toy telemetry protocol. A frame carries a sender id (system 20, component 190 for the ground station in the exercise), an 8-bit sequence number and a message. A signed frame adds a link id, a 48-bit timestamp and a 6-byte tag, the first 6 bytes of HMAC-SHA-256 over the header, the message, the link id and the timestamp. The receiver checks the tag, then refuses a frame whose timestamp is not newer than the last one accepted from the same sender and link. R2 The run shows the attacker's four tries against both receivers: an unsigned frame claiming the ground station's ids, a signed-looking frame with a made-up tag, a real signed frame with only its message changed, and a real signed frame replayed. The receiver that accepts unsigned frames takes all four; the one that requires signing takes none. R2

ULink is not MAVLink. It borrows the idea of signing so that the idea can be run and tested in this build. MAVLink, the protocol of the DN courses, has its own signing mechanism, and the real details matter (Layer 3).

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

MAVLink's sender ids and signing

Not verified — check before relying on this

From the course author's memory of the MAVLink Developer Guide, not opened in this build: MAVLink frames carry a sequence number, a system id and a component id; MAVLink 1 frames cannot be signed; MAVLink 2 defines optional message signing that appends a link id, a 48-bit timestamp and a 48-bit signature computed with SHA-256 over a secret key and the frame; receivers keep the last timestamp per stream; signing must be set up with the same key on both ends and is not on by default; and how PX4 and ArduPilot treat unsigned frames once signing is enabled is configurable. Every item of this list, including the exact construction of the signature (which may not be HMAC), the time unit of the timestamp and the configuration names, must be checked in the MAVLink Developer Guide's message-signing section and in the PX4 and ArduPilot documentation for the versions of the DN courses before it appears in a threat table.

Whatever the exact construction, the threat-model questions are the ones of F11-15, and they are what a review asks. Is signing enabled on every link that can carry commands, including the companion computer's? Are unsigned frames still accepted for some messages, and which? How is the key put on both ends, and who else has it (every laptop that ever flew the drone)? Does the freshness state survive a reboot of the flight controller? What happens when the ground station's clock jumps backwards? D1 D2

Why sequence numbers are not authentication

A sequence number lets a receiver count lost frames and spot duplicates; it does not tell who sent a frame. In ULink and in the forensic lab, the field is 8 bits wide and wraps from 255 to 0. Anyone who listens for a moment knows the next number, so a forged frame can carry a plausible one: the forensic lab's second transmitter does exactly that. What gives it away is not the number itself but its collision with the genuine sender's next frame, which arrives shortly afterwards with the same number. A receiver that keeps a duplicate counter per sender id would have shown the anomaly in real time. Detection is not prevention: by the time the duplicate arrives, the forged frame has been acted on. R3 R4

Signal strength as evidence

The receiver's signal-strength reading for each frame is evidence about where a frame came from, not proof. In the forensic run, the genuine frames arrive at -50 to -56 dBm between 40 and 52 s and the two land commands at -79 dBm. R4 A 23 dB difference in the same seconds, with the drone in the same place, is hard to explain with one transmitter. But a transmitter with more power, closer, or with a directional antenna could match the genuine level; signal strength is good for detection and investigation, never as an access-control rule. The numbers in the evidence are exercise values written into the generator, not measurements of any radio. R3

The RC link and satellite navigation

The RC link carries stick positions many times a second from the transmitter to the receiver. Whether a given RC system binds a transmitter to a receiver in a way that resists spoofing is a property of that product and its documentation; the threat table records "unknown, check the RC system's documentation" rather than assuming. The RC link's denial of service (blocking it) is answered by the RC failsafe of F10-12. C2 Satellite navigation signals are received, never authenticated by the drone in the course's design; spoofing them moves the drone's idea of where it is. The defences are cross-checks inside the estimator (does the position agree with the inertial sensors and the barometer?) and conservative failsafes when they disagree, which is DN-course estimator work. D1

The companion computer

The companion computer sits in the drone's zone in Figure 1, so the engine ranks its threats after the boundary ones (T62–T67). That ranking is only as good as the zone: if the companion computer runs a web server, a Wi-Fi hotspot or a remote shell, it has its own crossing and must be redrawn with it, and its link to the flight controller then needs the same signing as the radio. A common path into a flight controller is not the radio at all but a more exposed computer that the flight controller trusts. R1 D1

How the hardware actually does it

A telemetry radio pair is two small computers with transceivers. To the flight controller, the air radio is usually a serial port: it delivers bytes it received over the air and has no idea who transmitted them. Unless the radio modems themselves authenticate their peer (a feature of some products, documented by their makers), the boundary in Figure 1 is enforced only by whatever the protocol on top does. That is why "Air radio" is a process with its own S row (T17): the radio forwards what anyone in range sent. R1

Signing has a cost on the flight controller: a hash computation per frame, and a key in its storage. The flight controller's storage is in the threat model too (T68–T70, the Parameters store): someone who can read the parameters over USB or from the memory card may find the key. SS301's secure boot for drones (F11-06) and protected key storage decide how hard that is.

Safety

Propellers cut skin and eyes. All work in this chapter happens in simulation; any bench test of a real drone's link is done with the propellers removed, the battery handled as its maker instructs, and an arming switch you control. Drone flights are regulated in most countries: before any outdoor flight, read the current rules of your national aviation authority and fly only where allowed, supervised. Radio transmission is regulated too: transmit only with equipment and on frequencies your national rules allow, and never jam or spoof a link, including your own, outside a shielded test setup that those rules permit.

Untested on hardware and untested with real flight software: the build container has no drone, no radio modems, and no PX4 or ArduPilot SITL. Worked example

Task. Work through the forensic log the way an investigator would, with only the two CSV files and a short script, before reading the key. (The questions of the forensic lab then ask you to write it up.)

  1. Join the logs. For each frame the drone accepted (rx_log.csv), look for a frame in the ground station's send log (gcs_tx.csv) with the same sequence number and message, sent 0–50 ms earlier. The 50 ms window is chosen from the data: matched frames arrive 9 ms after sending in this simulation.
  2. Count. 187 accepted, 185 matched, 2 not sent by the ground station: two "cmd:land" frames at 46288 ms and 46791 ms. R4
  3. Duplicates. Sequence numbers 143 and 144 each appear twice within 10 s: first in the land frames, then in genuine frames 228 ms and 225 ms later. R4
  4. Signal strength. Matched frames between 40 and 52 s: -56 to -50 dBm, median -54. The two unmatched frames: -79 dBm. R4
  5. Timing. Genuine frames arrive on the ground station's 500 ms grid (at …016, …019, …516 ms in each second); the land frames at …288 and …791 ms, off the grid.
  6. Conclusion. Four independent signs say a second transmitter used the ground station's sender ids. None is proof alone; together, and with the absence of the command in the sender's log, they leave one hypothesis that explains everything.

The script that did steps 1–4 is the answer-key tool seqcheck.py; its output is in the forensic key.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A castle gate is in one place; a radio gate is wherever the signal reaches. The attacker does not need to come to the castle: anywhere within range, which depends on power and antennas and changes as the drone flies, is "at the gate".
    • 

  • Guards can see a messenger's face; a receiver cannot. Everything a receiver knows about a sender is in the bits it received (and a signal-strength reading). The "face" must be built into the message: a tag only a key holder can make.
    • 

  • A shouting crowd can be pushed away; jamming cannot be argued with. No protocol at the gate stops noise. The castle analogy suggests more guards; the right answer is a plan for when the gate is blocked (failsafes).
    • 

  • A sealed letter can be traced to one ring; a shared-key tag cannot. Every holder of the shared key can make a valid tag, so signing proves "a key holder sent it", not which one. Per-device keys narrow it down.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F11-15 authenticated the robot's command path over a network that only nearby devices could join; F10-12 built radio links and failsafes for the drone. Observation. Two received frames have no counterpart: 46288 ms, seq 143, cmd:land, -79 dBm; 46791 ms, seq 144, cmd:land, -79 dBm. Each reuses the sequence number of the ground station's next genuine frame (143 request:position sent at 46507 ms, received at 46516 ms; 144 heartbeat sent at 47007 ms, received at 47016 ms). Genuine frames around them are at -51 to -55 dBm and arrive on the ground station's grid (…016, …019, …516 ms).


Hypotheses ruled out. "The app sent it but did not log it": the app's log is complete and continuous (every sequence number from 0 upward is present, 185 frames), and a frame it sent would carry its own next number, not one it uses again 228 ms later; it would also arrive at the app's signal level. "The flight controller generated it (failsafe)": a failsafe is not logged as a received frame with the ground station's ids, and the telemetry was healthy (heartbeats every second throughout). "Radio corruption of a genuine frame": two identical, well-formed land frames, at a different level and at times when no genuine frame was sent, are not corruption.


Root cause. A second transmitter within range sent unsigned frames using the ground station's system and component ids (20, 190) and guessed the next sequence number. The drone's receiver accepted unsigned frames and treated the ids as identity. The evidence cannot say who operated the transmitter or where it was; it can say it was not the ground station.


Fix and verification. Enable signing on the telemetry link and reject unsigned commands; test in SITL by sending an unsigned and a replayed land command and confirming both are rejected (ULink's signing receiver drops both kinds in R2). Also review the landing failsafe so that an unexpected land is at least safe where it happens.


Detection. A duplicate-sequence alarm per sender id (fires twice here), a check that commands arrive only on the sender's timing grid, and an alert on a sudden signal-strength change for one sender; all are detection, not prevention.


Wrong answers and why. "The pilot pressed land": the app's log shows no land command. "Interference": interference corrupts or blocks frames, it does not produce valid, well-formed commands with the next sequence numbers. "Block frames below -70 dBm": the attacker can transmit louder. How the evidence was produced: whosent.cpp (Listing 3) simulates both logs and inserts the two frames at lines 55–61; the scenario is synthetic.
