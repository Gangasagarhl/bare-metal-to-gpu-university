Story hook

Amara and Bo live in the same block of flats. Each has a front door with a lock, but they share the laundry room in the basement, the bicycle store and the post boxes in the hall. The caretaker has a master key that opens every door, and a small office with the building's fuse boxes. One week Amara leaves a bag of washing in the laundry room overnight. In the morning it is still there, but someone has looked through it: her name badge from work is on top, not where she left it.

Nobody broke in. Whoever looked lives in the building, had every right to be in the laundry room, and used it the way it was set up. The weak point was not the front door. It was the shared room, the habit of leaving things there, and the fact that "shared" meant "open to all forty flats".

A computing cluster is a block of flats for programs. Research groups share login machines, a scheduler, compute nodes, a big shared disk and a management network with the master key to every machine. This last chapter of the course models it, and the most important attackers in the model already live in the building.

What you will be able to do

  • Draw the data-flow diagram of a small cluster: login node, scheduler, compute nodes, shared storage, container registry, monitoring and the management network with its BMCs.
  • Model insiders: other users, a compromised account, a malicious container image, a compromised node; and say which threats they make reachable.
  • Analyse reachability of the cluster's networks with a program, and explain why removing the shortest attack path can reveal the next one.
  • Explain how the file-creation mask decides who can read a job's output on shared storage, and show it on a real Linux system.
  • Diagnose a data exposure between two users from scheduler records, a file listing and an access log.
  • Produce the course project: a complete threat model for your mega project, and prepare for the timed practical exam.
Jargon box
Multi-tenancy
Simple words: many users or groups sharing the same machines. Analogy: forty flats sharing one laundry room. Precise: a system design where several tenants (users, groups, customers) share infrastructure and are kept apart only by the system's isolation mechanisms: file permissions, process isolation, containers, virtual machines or separate nodes, each with a different strength. D1
Insider (threat model)
Simple words: someone who is allowed in and can still do harm. Analogy: a neighbour in the laundry room. Precise: an attacker who holds legitimate access to part of the system (a user account, a job slot, an administrator role) or who controls something that does (a stolen account, a malicious dependency); in a cluster, the default assumption for every other user. D1
Lateral movement
Simple words: using one machine you got into to reach the next. Analogy: from the laundry room, through the unlocked door to the caretaker's office. Precise: the steps an attacker takes after a first foothold, along network paths and trust relationships, towards more valuable assets; a reachability analysis (Listing 2) lists the paths that exist. D1 R2
Management network
Simple words: the separate network that controls the machines themselves. Analogy: the caretaker's office with the master key and the fuse boxes. Precise: the network that reaches the BMCs (and switch and storage management interfaces) of a cluster: power control, remote console and firmware update for every node; anyone on it controls the hardware, below the operating systems. C3
Least privilege
Simple words: give each person and program only the access it needs. Analogy: the cleaner gets the key to the laundry room, not the master key. Precise: a design principle: every component runs with the smallest set of rights that lets it do its job, so that its compromise exposes as little as possible; in STRIDE terms it limits elevation of privilege. D1
umask (file-creation mask)
Simple words: a setting that removes permissions from every new file you make. Analogy: a rule that every bag you leave in the laundry room is closed, unless you open it on purpose. Precise: a per-process mask of permission bits; a file or directory created with requested mode m gets mode m & ~umask. With umask 0022 a file requested as 0666 becomes 0644 (others can read); with 0077 it becomes 0600. The lab shows both on this build's Linux. D4 R3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A cluster is a building where many groups live and work on shared machines. The front door (the login machine) has a good lock, but once you are a resident you can use the shared rooms: the big disk, the machines that run jobs. So the questions change: can my neighbour read what I leave on the shared disk? Can a program I run reach the caretaker's office, where one key turns every machine on and off? Can someone make a job run under my name? You still draw the arrows and walls of F11-14, but most of the people in your drawing are allowed in.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The cluster's diagram

DS303 described the parts of a small cluster: login nodes, a scheduler, compute nodes, shared storage, and the management side (F5-22, F5-23). C3 Figure 1 turns them into a DFD with eight zones, one per owner or privilege level: users and administrators outside; login, control (scheduler and its database), compute, storage, registry and management inside. The engine reports 14 flows, 11 of them crossing, and 95 threats. R1

Assets and attackers

Assets: each group's data (datasets, models, results), compute time (availability, and the cost of someone else using it), credentials (SSH keys, storage tokens, the scheduler's own keys), the correctness of "who runs as whom", and the management plane (power, console and firmware of every node). Attackers, by what they can reach: someone on the internet; any legitimate user (curious, careless, or with a stolen account); a container image that does something its user did not expect; a job that has escaped onto its node; an administrator's laptop that is compromised. The insiders matter most, because the cluster gives them, by design, a shell, a job slot and a share of the disk. D1

The threats that come first

  1. Identity of jobs: tampering with or spoofing the flow "job launch, user identity" from the scheduler to the compute node (T57; spoofing of the scheduler T11). If a compute node accepts a launch request from anything that claims to be the scheduler, any user who can reach the node's port chooses who their job runs as.
  2. Escape from a job: elevation of privilege of the compute node job (T22). A job that becomes root on its node can read every other job's memory and files on that node and use the node's network position (Listing 2's lateral paths).
  3. Shared storage: information disclosure and tampering of shared scratch (T23–T25). Default permissions decide whether every user can read every other user's results; the forensic lab is this threat.
  4. Container images: tampering of the image flow from the registry (T69). A job runs whatever the image contains, with the user's rights; an image is code from someone else.
  5. Management plane: spoofing, tampering and elevation on the BMC (T35–T40) and the monitoring that talks to it (T41–T46). Whoever reaches a BMC controls power, console and firmware of its node, below the operating system and every control the OS enforces. C3
  6. Repudiation: the audit log (T47–T50). In a shared system, "who did this?" is asked often; the scheduler's decisions and administrator actions must be logged where users and compromised nodes cannot change them.

T-numbers are those of the saved run. R1

Two views of one system

A DFD shows data. It does not show that the monitoring dashboard was published to the internet "for convenience", or that one compute node got a second network card on the management network "for debugging". Those are facts about network reachability, and they decide which threats an outsider can reach without first becoming an insider. Listing 2 takes the cluster's allowed connections and finds, for each target, the shortest chain of hops from the internet. Before the fixes, both BMCs are 2 hops away through the dashboard. Remove that rule and bmc1 is still reachable, now in 4 hops: login, scheduler, compute3, bmc1, through the second network card. Remove that too and no BMC is reachable. R2 The shortest path hid the second one; an analysis that stops after the first fix would have reported the management network safe.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Who a job runs as

The scheduler decides that job 4411 belongs to user amara and tells a compute node to run it as amara. The compute node must be able to check that the request really comes from the scheduler and that the identity in it was not changed on the way: the flow in Figure 1 carries authority, not only data. DS303's Slurm lab installed MUNGE with one shared key on all nodes for this purpose. C3

Not verified — check before relying on this

How Slurm authenticates messages between its daemons and user commands (MUNGE credentials, or other authentication plugins in newer releases), what a MUNGE credential contains, and what an attacker who reads the shared MUNGE key can do, are not stated here from a source: the Slurm documentation and the MUNGE project documentation were not opened in this build. The threat table should say "authentication of scheduler messages: mechanism as configured, see the Slurm documentation for the installed release" and treat the shared key as an asset of the highest class until checked.

Isolation has levels

Tenants on a cluster are separated by mechanisms of very different strength: file permissions on shared storage; separate processes and user ids on a shared node; containers (namespaces and control groups, F5-24) sharing one kernel; virtual machines sharing one hypervisor; separate nodes sharing a network; separate clusters. Each step costs efficiency and buys a smaller shared attack surface. A threat table states, for each pair of tenants that must not see each other's data, which level separates them and what a failure of that level exposes. A container is not a security boundary of the same strength as a virtual machine: containers share the host kernel, so a kernel bug reachable from a container is a path out of it. D1 C3

GPUs and other shared devices

Not verified — check before relying on this

Whether a GPU's memory is cleared between two jobs that use the same GPU one after the other, whether two jobs sharing one GPU (time slicing, partitioning) can observe each other, and what the scheduler does about it, depend on the GPU, its driver and the scheduler's configuration. None of it was checked in this build (no GPU). Put it in the threat table as information disclosure between tenants on the GPU, with "check the vendor's documentation for the driver version in use and the scheduler's GPU plugin documentation" as the open action.

The same question applies to every device a job can touch: local scratch disks reused between jobs, RDMA network adapters (a remote key, DS401's rkey, is a capability: whoever holds it can access that registered memory region, so it is an asset to protect), and accelerators with their own firmware. C3

The management plane

A BMC is a separate small computer on each server board, with its own network port, its own firmware and control over the server's power and console (OS305 defined the term; DS304 met OpenBMC and Redfish). C3 In the threat model it is the master key: compromise of one BMC means control of its node below the operating system, and a shared BMC password means control of all nodes. Decisions that follow: the management network is separate and reachable only from administrators' consoles; BMC credentials are per node; BMC firmware is updated as carefully as the OS (with SS301's signed updates where the platform supports them); monitoring that needs BMC sensor data reads it through a narrow, read-only path, not with administrator credentials. Listing 2 is how you check the first decision against the configuration, and the check must be repeated whenever someone adds a network card "for debugging".

Images and the supply chain

A container image is software from somewhere else, run with the user's rights on shared hardware. The threats are the update-flow threats of SS301 in another form: tampering on the way (answered by signed images and pinned digests), a malicious image from a trusted name (answered by a curated registry and review), and stale images with known vulnerabilities (answered by rebuilding and scanning). The DFD shows the registry as its own zone because it is often run by someone else. D1

Denial of service by neighbours

On a shared cluster, most denial of service is not an attack: a job that fills the shared disk, floods the file system's metadata server or holds every GPU for a week. The controls are quotas, fair-share scheduling and per-job limits (DS303's control groups). They belong in the threat table because the effect on other tenants is the same as an attack's, and because an attacker can use the same mechanisms on purpose. D1

How the hardware actually does it

File permissions on shared storage are enforced by the operating system that serves the files, and decided at creation time by the creating process's umask. Listing 4 creates a file and a directory under umask 0022 and under 0077 on this build's Linux and prints their modes with stat: 0022 gives -rw-r--r-- (644) and drwxr-xr-x (755), readable by everyone; 0077 gives -rw------- (600) and drwx------ (700). R3 The arithmetic is the definition: requested 0666 for a file, 0777 for a directory, AND NOT the mask. D4 On a network file system the server's settings and identity mapping add rules of their own, which the cluster's documentation must state.

The management network is hardware too: a BMC has its own network interface, separate from the host's (or shared with it on some boards, which changes the diagram). Which it is on your servers is in the board's documentation. Whether a node has a second network card on the management network is visible in its own interface list, which is where the reachability input should come from, not from the network diagram on the wall.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on a real cluster: the build container has no cluster, scheduler, BMC or network file system. The reachability input and the forensic records are the course's own exercise data; only the umask demonstration ran on a real Linux system (this build container, as root, in a temporary directory). Running the analysis on the DS303 virtual cluster is the lab's real part.

Worked example

Task. Read Listing 2's run and turn it into rows of the threat table.

  1. Before the fixes: from the internet, 10 nodes reachable; bmc1 and bmc2 in 2 hops through the monitoring dashboard; the scheduler and storage in 2 hops through login; admin-console not reachable. R2
  2. Interpretation: "reachable" means a connection is allowed, not that an attack works. Each hop needs a weakness on the node it reaches: for the dashboard path, spoofing or elevation of privilege on Monitoring (T41, T46) and then the BMC's own threats (T35–T40). Two hops with a web application in the middle is a short path.
  3. Fix 1: the dashboard is moved behind the login node (remove internet → monitoring). Run again: 8 nodes reachable; bmc2 not reachable; bmc1 reachable in 4 hops through compute3. R2
  4. Interpretation: the second path needs a user account (login), a job (scheduler), placement on compute3, and a way from the job to the second network card. Any legitimate user who lands on compute3 is one step from bmc1.
  5. Fix 2: remove compute3's management card. Run again: 7 nodes reachable; no BMC reachable. R2
  6. Rows: "Management plane reachable from the internet via the dashboard" — mitigate (fix 1), owner: operations, test: reachability analysis in CI on the exported firewall rules. "bmc1 reachable from jobs on compute3" — mitigate (fix 2), owner: operations, test: the same analysis plus an interface inventory of every compute node. "Scheduler and storage reachable from any user" — accept by design, with T11, T57 and T23–T25 mitigated on those nodes.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Neighbours in a building are few and known; cluster users can be many and unknown to each other. A setting that is harmless among forty flats ("bags in the laundry room") becomes an exposure among hundreds of accounts, some of them stolen.
    • 

  • A caretaker's master key is one object; a BMC password can be copied. The master key opens one door at a time and can be taken back; a shared BMC credential, once copied, opens every node from anywhere on the management network, and nobody notices it is gone.
    • 

  • Walls are physical; cluster walls are configuration. A firewall rule, a mount option or a network card added "for debugging" moves a wall without anyone seeing it. That is why reachability is computed from the configuration, not read from a diagram.
    • 

  • Looking through a bag leaves traces; reading a file may not. Unless reads are logged, the forensic lab's access would leave no trace at all; the analogy suggests evidence that a computer does not produce by default.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Bo's job 4419 (robot-lab) read model.bin and dataset-index.csv (allowed) and was refused storage-token.txt (denied), all within 3 seconds, 8 minutes after Amara's job 4411 ended. The two result files are -rw-r--r--, the key file -rw-------; all directories are drwxr-xr-x; all are owned by amara, group vision-lab; job 4411 ran with umask 0022.


Decisions. Bo is not the owner and not in vision-lab, so the "others" triple applies: r-- for the two results (allowed), --- for the key (denied).


Root cause. Amara's training program created its results with the usual requested mode 0666; the job environment's umask 0022 removed only write bits, giving 0644, readable by every user of the cluster. The directories were also open (755). The key file was created by a program that asked for 0600 explicitly, so the umask did not matter for it. The weakness is in the compute node job's environment and the shared scratch's defaults (Figure 1: Compute node job → Shared scratch, threat T24, information disclosure of Shared scratch).


Attack or mistake. Both: a configuration mistake by the cluster (default umask, no per-user directory modes) made the files readable; Bo then read files that were not his. Neither needed any break-in, which is the typical shape of insider exposure in a multi-tenant system.


Prevention. Threat row: "information disclosure of results on shared scratch to other tenants" — mitigate: umask 0077 in every job environment (scheduler prolog or system profile) and per-user scratch directories created 0700 by the cluster, with group sharing only on purpose; test: a job creates a file and a second user's job tries to read it and is refused (lab step 4). Detection: read access to other users' scratch logged and reviewed.


Wrong answers and why. "Bo stole a password": the audit shows his own job and his own identity. "The file system is broken": it applied the modes correctly, including the refusal of the key file. "Delete Bo's account": does not change who else can read the files. How the evidence was produced: forensic.cpp (Listing 3) creates the modes with 0666 & ~0022 and computes each access with mayRead; users, jobs and times are synthetic.
