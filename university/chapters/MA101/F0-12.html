Story hook

Analogy

Tomás is staring at the big rice jar on the kitchen shelf. "Noor, how many grains of rice do you think are in there?"

His sister laughs. "Count them, if you have a week to spare." Then she thinks again. "We don't need to count them all. Count the grains in one spoon. Then count how many spoons fill the jar. Multiply."

That evening their neighbour calls. Twenty-three children are going to the park picnic. Each car can take four children. "How many cars do we need?" Noor divides. Tomás frowns. "Twenty-three doesn't share into fours!"

Both questions use the four operations: adding, taking away, multiplying and dividing. Both also need good sense about the answer. A quick estimate of the rice is better than no answer. And for the cars, nobody may be left behind. This chapter is about doing the sums and judging the answers.

What you will be able to do

  • Use the four operations +, −, × and ÷, and say which story each one fits.
  • Work out a division with a remainder, and check it by multiplying back.
  • Apply the order of operations: brackets first, then × and ÷, then + and −.
  • Estimate an answer by rounding to friendly numbers, and use the estimate to catch mistakes.
  • Decide when to round up a division so that everything fits: "how many helpers for how many jobs".
Jargon box
Sum
Simple words: the answer when you add. Analogy: putting two piles of rice together into one pile. Precise: the result of addition; 7 + 5 = 12, so 12 is the sum of 7 and 5. D1
Difference
Simple words: the answer when you take away; how far apart two numbers are. Analogy: how many more spoons one jar holds than another. Precise: the result of subtraction; 12 − 5 = 7. D1
Product
Simple words: the answer when you multiply. Analogy: the rice in 64 spoons when each spoon holds the same number of grains. Precise: the result of multiplication; for whole numbers, 3 × 4 means 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. D1
Quotient
Simple words: the answer when you divide: how many in each share, or how many shares. Analogy: how many full cars of four children you can make. Precise: the result of division; in whole-number division, 23 ÷ 4 has quotient 5. D1
Remainder
Simple words: what is left over when things do not share out exactly. Analogy: the three children still waiting after five cars are full. Precise: for whole numbers a and b (b not zero), the remainder r is the number with a = quotient × b + r and r smaller than b; 23 = 5 × 4 + 3. D1
Order of operations
Simple words: the agreed order for doing the steps of a sum. Analogy: a recipe that says "mix the dry things first, then add the milk". Precise: brackets first; then multiplication and division from left to right; then addition and subtraction from left to right. D1
Rounding
Simple words: swapping a number for a nearby friendly one. Analogy: saying "about 90 grains" when you counted 87. Precise: replacing a number by the nearest multiple of 10 (or 100, 1,000…); a number exactly halfway goes up in this course, so 45 rounds to 50. D1
Estimate
Simple words: a sensible "about" answer you can get quickly. Analogy: guessing the rice in the jar from one spoon. Precise: an approximate result, usually found by rounding the inputs before calculating; it is a check, not a replacement for the exact answer when one is needed. D1
Rounding up
Simple words: taking the next whole number when anything is left over, so that everything fits. Analogy: one more car for the last three children. Precise: the smallest whole number of groups of size b that holds a items; with whole-number division it is (a + b − 1) ÷ b. Engineers call it ceiling division. D1R1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

There are four main operations. Each one fits a kind of story.

  • Adding (+) puts things together. Seven grapes and five grapes make 12 grapes: 7 + 5 = 12.
  • Taking away (−) removes some, or finds how far apart two numbers are: 12 − 5 = 7.
  • Multiplying (×) counts equal groups. Three bags with four apples each hold 3 × 4 = 12 apples.
  • Dividing (÷) shares equally, or makes equal groups. Forty-two cherries shared by 7 friends gives 6 each: 42 ÷ 7 = 6.

The sign = means "is the same amount as". It is not a command to "write the answer". You will need this idea in F0-17.

Taking away undoes adding. Dividing undoes multiplying. That is why you can check a division by multiplying back. Figure 1 shows both directions on a number line.

When things do not share out

Now the picnic. Twenty-three children, four per car. Make full cars of four: you get 5 full cars, with 3 children left. We write 23 ÷ 4 = 5 remainder 3. Check it: 5 × 4 + 3 = 23.

But how many cars do you need? The 3 children cannot stay at home. So you need one more car: 6 cars. One seat stays empty. Figure 2 shows this.

This is the rounding-up rule. When everything must fit, and something is left over, add one more group. Engineers call the groups "helpers" and the children "jobs". Later you will ask: how many teams of helpers do I need for this many jobs?

Which step first?

What is 2 + 3 × 4? If you add first you get 20. If you multiply first you get 14. Both cannot be right. So everyone agrees on an order.

  1. Work out anything in brackets first.
  2. Then multiply and divide, from left to right.
  3. Then add and take away, from left to right.

So 2 + 3 × 4 = 2 + 12 = 14. If you want the adding first, use brackets: (2 + 3) × 4 = 20.

Estimating: a good guess on purpose

Back to the rice. Tomás counts 87 grains in one spoon. It takes 64 spoons to fill the jar. Multiplying 87 × 64 in your head is hard. So round first. 87 is close to 90. 64 is close to 60. And 90 × 60 = 5,400. That is an estimate.

To round to the nearest ten, look at the ones digit. If it is 5 or more, go up. If it is 4 or less, go down. So 87 becomes 90 and 64 becomes 60. In this course, 45 rounds up to 50.

The exact product is 5,568. The estimate is close enough to answer "about how many?" The real jar is rougher anyway: spoons are never filled exactly the same.

Estimates are also mistake detectors. Before you calculate, estimate. If the exact answer is far from the estimate, look for a slip. A calculator cannot tell you that you typed the wrong number. Your estimate can.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. At level L0 this layer is optional.

Adding and multiplying can be done in any order: 7 + 5 = 5 + 7 and 3 × 4 = 4 × 3. Taking away and dividing cannot: 12 − 5 is not 5 − 12, and 12 ÷ 4 is not 4 ÷ 12. That is why "left to right" matters. 12 − 4 − 3 means (12 − 4) − 3 = 5, but 12 − (4 − 3) = 11. Likewise 24 ÷ 4 ÷ 2 = 3, but 24 ÷ (4 ÷ 2) = 12. D1R2

Division of whole numbers gives two results. For a items in groups of b, the quotient q is the number of full groups and the remainder r is what is left, with a = q × b + r and r less than b. For 23 and 4: q = 5, r = 3, and 5 × 4 + 3 = 23. D1R1

The number of groups needed so that everything fits is q when r is 0, and q + 1 otherwise. One formula does both cases with whole-number division: (a + b − 1) ÷ b, keeping only the quotient. For 23 children: (23 + 4 − 1) ÷ 4 = 26 ÷ 4, which is 6 with remainder 2, so 6 cars. For exactly 24 children: 27 ÷ 4 is 6 remainder 3, so still 6 cars, with no empty seat. D1R2

This exact formula returns in this university's GPU courses. There, a team of helpers is called a block, and a GPU program asks for (n + 256 − 1) ÷ 256 blocks of 256 helpers to cover n jobs. For n = 1,000 that gives 1,255 ÷ 256, which is 4 blocks. U1R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Nothing here is needed for the quiz.

Why does (a + b − 1) ÷ b round up? Adding b − 1 pushes any non-zero remainder past the next multiple of b, so the quotient grows by one. When the remainder is already 0, adding b − 1 is not enough to reach the next multiple, so the quotient stays the same. D1

In C++, dividing two whole numbers of type int keeps only the quotient, and % gives the remainder: run R1 printed 5 and 3 for 23 and 4. Two cautions for later. First, dividing by zero has no answer; a program must never do it, so it checks first. Second, a computer's whole-number types have a largest value (MA102 explains why), so with huge numbers even a + b − 1 can be too big. R1B2

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a computer, the processor has circuits that add numbers written in base two, built from many tiny switches. Multiplying and dividing are built from repeated adding, shifting and subtracting steps, done very fast. You will build an adding circuit from switches in HW102. For now, the important point is this: the computer does exactly the operation the program asks for, in exactly the order written. It never estimates for you, and it never checks whether the answer makes sense. B1

Worked example

Every number below was recomputed by the number-check program checks.cpp (run R2), whose real output closes this section.

Part A — the picnic cars

  1. 23 ÷ 4: how many full fours fit in 23? 5 × 4 = 20 fits; 6 × 4 = 24 is too many. So the quotient is 5.
  2. Remainder: 23 − 20 = 3.
  3. Check: 5 × 4 + 3 = 23. Correct.
  4. Everyone must go, and 3 are left, so round up: 6 cars.
  5. Seats in 6 cars: 6 × 4 = 24. Empty seats: 24 − 23 = 1.

Part B — eggs for the class bake

Lucía has 3 trays of 12 eggs and 5 loose eggs. Total: 3 × 12 + 5. Multiply first: 36. Then add: 36 + 5 = 41 eggs. (If you wrongly add first, 3 × (12 + 5) = 3 × 17 = 51. Keep this in mind for the forensic lab.)

Part C — estimate, then calculate

  1. 48 × 21. Round: 48 → 50, 21 → 20. Estimate: 50 × 20 = 1,000.
  2. Exact: 48 × 21 = 1,008. Close to 1,000, so no slip.
  3. 398 + 205. Round to hundreds: 400 + 200 = 600. Exact: 603.

Part D — helpers and jobs

A family has 30 small jobs for a clean-up day. Helpers work in teams of 8, one job each. How many teams?

  1. 30 ÷ 8 = 3 remainder 6. Three full teams cover only 24 jobs.
  2. Six jobs are left, so round up: 4 teams.
  3. Formula check: (30 + 8 − 1) ÷ 8 = 37 ÷ 8, quotient 4. Four teams have 32 places, so 2 places are empty.
  4. With 40 jobs: 40 ÷ 8 = 5 exactly, so 5 teams. With 41 jobs: 6 teams. One extra job can cost a whole extra team.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Sharing children is not sharing cake. You can cut a cake into quarters, but not a child. Whole-number division with a remainder fits children and cars; fractions (F0-13) fit cake.
    • 

  • Real cars can squeeze; formulas cannot. A family might squeeze a fifth small child into a car. A program never squeezes: if a team has 8 places, the ninth job needs a new team.
    • 

  • "Multiplying is repeated adding" stops working. It works for 3 × 4. It does not explain 2.5 × 4 or (−3) × 4 on its own. F0-13 and F0-14 extend the idea.
    • 

  • A spoonful estimate is not a count. Spoons hold different amounts. Estimating gives "about", never "exactly".
    •

Lab


Goal


Play the estimation game: estimate the grains of rice in a jar from one spoonful, and compare a quick rounded estimate with the full product.


Hardware and software needed


A jar (any size) of rice, lentils or small dried beans; a spoon; a bowl; paper and pencil. Optional: a computer with a C++ compiler. The runs in this chapter used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0, as printed in the logs.


Safety


None beyond normal care at home. Ask an adult before using food. Uncooked rice and beans are not for eating. Keep small grains away from very young children.


Steps


    

  1. Write a wild guess for the number of grains in the full jar.
    2. 

  2. Take one level spoonful. Count its grains (group them in tens, as in F0-11). Write the number.
    3. 

  3. Empty the jar into a bowl, spoon by spoon, counting spoons. Pour it all back.
    4. 

  4. Round both numbers to the nearest ten and multiply: that is your quick estimate.
    5. 

  5. Multiply the unrounded numbers: that is your full product.
    6. 

  6. Optional: write your two numbers in estimate.in, separated by a space, and run estimate.cpp.
    7.

Verification


Do step 2 again with a second spoon. If the two spoon counts are close, your estimate is steady. Your quick estimate should be within the same "ballpark" as the full product: the same number of digits, and the same first digit or one next to it.


Troubleshooting


    

  • Spoon counts differ a lot → spoons were not level → scrape each spoon flat with a knife back → count again.
    • 

  • The program prints 0 for everything → estimate.in is empty or has letters → open it → write two whole numbers.
    • 

  • Estimate and product differ by a factor of ten → a rounding slip (87 → 9 instead of 90) → redo step 4 on paper.

Mini-project


The estimation challenge. Choose three things at home you could count, such as the pages of a thick book, the stitches along a towel edge, or the dried beans in a bag. For each one, estimate first using a small sample and multiplying. Then count (or count a bigger sample) to check. Make a table: thing, sample, estimate, check count, difference.

Summary


    

  • Adding and taking away undo each other; so do multiplying and dividing.
    • 

  • Multiplying whole numbers counts equal groups; dividing shares or groups.
    • 

  • Whole-number division gives a quotient and a remainder: 23 = 5 × 4 + 3.
    • 

  • Order of operations: brackets, then × and ÷, then + and −, each from left to right.
    • 

  • Round to friendly numbers to estimate; 5 or more rounds up.
    • 

  • Estimate before calculating, to catch slips.
    • 

  • When everything must fit, round the division up: (a + b − 1) ÷ b.
