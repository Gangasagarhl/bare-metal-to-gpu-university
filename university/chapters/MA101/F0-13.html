Story hook

Analogy

Hana's grandfather bakes a round flatbread and cuts it into 8 equal slices. Hana eats 3 slices before dinner. "How much of the bread is gone?" asks her grandfather, smiling.

"Three slices," says Hana. "But three slices of what?" he asks. "If I had cut it into 4, three slices would be most of the bread!"

Hana thinks. "Three out of eight slices, then." Grandfather nods. "We write that as 3 over 8. Your cousin would say zero point three seven five. And the shop down the road would say thirty-seven and a half percent."

Three ways of saying the same amount. This chapter shows you all three, and how to move between them. It also shows what happens when a computer tries to divide 3 by 8.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what the top and bottom numbers of a fraction mean, and why the parts must be equal.
  • Find equivalent fractions, compare fractions, and add fractions with the same bottom number.
  • Work out a fraction or a percentage of an amount, such as 3/4 of 20 or 25 % of 80.
  • Convert between fractions, decimals and percentages.
  • Predict why a C++ program prints 0 for 3 / 8, and how to get 0.375.
Jargon box
Fraction
Simple words: a number that says "this many parts out of that many equal parts". Analogy: 3 slices of a bread cut into 8 equal slices. Precise: a number written a/b, with whole numbers a and b and b not zero; it means a ÷ b. D1
Numerator
Simple words: the top number of a fraction: how many parts you have. Analogy: the slices Hana ate. Precise: a in the fraction a/b. D1
Denominator
Simple words: the bottom number of a fraction: how many equal parts make the whole. Analogy: how many slices the bread was cut into. Precise: b in the fraction a/b; it must not be zero. D1
Equivalent fractions
Simple words: different-looking fractions that are the same amount. Analogy: half a bread is the same as 4 slices of 8. Precise: a/b and c/d are equivalent when a × d = b × c; multiplying or dividing top and bottom by the same non-zero number gives an equivalent fraction. D1
Decimal
Simple words: a number with a point, where the digits after the point are tenths, hundredths, thousandths. Analogy: cutting each slice into ten smaller pieces, and each of those into ten again. Precise: base-ten place value continued to the right of the ones place: the places are worth 1/10, 1/100, 1/1,000, … D1
Percentage
Simple words: "out of a hundred". Analogy: pretending the bread was cut into 100 tiny equal slices and counting how many you ate. Precise: p % means p/100; so 37.5 % = 0.375 = 3/8. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A fraction describes part of a whole. You write it with two numbers, one over the other. In 3/8, the bottom number says the bread was cut into 8 equal slices. The top number says you have 3 of them.

The bottom number is the denominator. The top number is the numerator. The parts must be equal. If one slice is huge and the others are tiny, "3 out of 8" tells you nothing useful.

Same amount, different names

Look at Figure 1. Half the bread is the same as 2 quarters. It is also the same as 4 eighths. So 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8. These are equivalent fractions.

Here is the trick. Multiply the top and the bottom by the same number. The amount does not change. 1/2 becomes 2/4 when you multiply both by 2. You only cut each slice into two smaller slices.

A bigger bottom number means smaller slices. So 1/8 is smaller than 1/4, even though 8 is bigger than 4. Ask yourself: would you rather share a bread with 8 people or with 4?

Adding and taking a fraction of something

Adding is easy when the slices are the same size. 3/8 + 5/8 = 8/8, which is one whole bread. Add the tops. Keep the bottom.

When the slices are different sizes, make them the same first. 1/2 + 1/4: change 1/2 into 2/4. Now 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4. Never add the bottoms. Slices do not get bigger because you have more of them.

What is 3/4 of 20 strawberries? Share 20 into 4 equal groups: 5 each. Take 3 of those groups: 15 strawberries. Divide by the bottom, then multiply by the top.

Decimals: place value after the point

Remember place value from F0-11. Each place is worth ten of the place on its right. Now keep going to the right of the ones. After the decimal point come tenths, then hundredths, then thousandths.

So 0.375 means 3 tenths, 7 hundredths and 5 thousandths. That is the same amount as 3/8. To turn a fraction into a decimal, divide the top by the bottom: 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375.

To compare decimals, line up the points and compare from the left. Is 0.6 bigger than 0.45? Yes! Look at the tenths: 6 tenths beats 4 tenths. Do not be fooled because 0.45 has more digits.

Percentages: out of a hundred

A percentage means "out of 100". The sign % says "per hundred". So 50 % is 50 out of 100, which is one half.

To turn a decimal into a percentage, multiply by 100. So 0.375 is 37.5 %, and 0.25 is 25 %. To find 25 % of 80 grapes, think "25 out of every 100". One hundredth of 80 is 0.8. Twenty-five of those make 20. Or notice that 25 % is a quarter: 80 ÷ 4 = 20.

Engineers use percentages to show progress. If 250 of 1,000 jobs are done, the progress is 25 %. Some fractions never end as decimals. One third is 0.333…, with threes forever. You will see a computer meet this problem in the code walk-through.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. At level L0 this layer is optional.

A fraction a/b is a division: it is the number you get from a ÷ b. That is why 3/8 = 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375, and why the denominator can never be zero. D1R1

Two fractions a/b and c/d are equal exactly when a × d = b × c. For example 3/4 and 6/8: 3 × 8 = 24 and 4 × 6 = 24, so they are equal. To add fractions, rewrite them with a common denominator, then add the numerators. D1R2

Decimal places continue base ten: the first place after the point is worth 1/10, the next 1/100, then 1/1,000. A percentage is a count of hundredths: p % = p/100. So "a fraction of an amount" and "a percentage of an amount" are both multiplications: 3/4 of 20 = 20 × 3 ÷ 4 = 15, and 60 % of 30 = 30 × 60 ÷ 100 = 18. Percentages above 100 are allowed: 150 % of 20 is 30. D1R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Nothing here is needed for the quiz.

Why does 3/8 give a decimal that ends, while 1/3 does not? Write the fraction in lowest terms (no number bigger than 1 divides both top and bottom). Its decimal ends exactly when the denominator can be made by multiplying only 2s and 5s, because only then does it divide some power of ten. 8 = 2 × 2 × 2 divides 1,000, so 3/8 = 375/1,000. 3 divides no power of ten, so 1/3 never ends. D1

Computers usually store decimals in base two, not base ten (see the next section). In base two, the same rule uses only the factor 2. So even 1/10 = 0.1 cannot be stored exactly, because 10 contains a 5. MA302 treats this properly. D1B1

How the hardware actually does it

A computer stores most decimal numbers in base two, with a fixed number of places. Numbers like 0.1 then become very close copies, not exact ones. Listing 2 shows the effect: printed with a few digits, 0.1 + 0.2 looks like 0.3, but printed with 17 digits it shows 0.30000000000000004 on the build machine (run R1). This is not a broken computer. It is the base-two version of "1/3 never ends". MA302 explains how engineers live with it. B1R1

Worked example

All numbers below are recomputed by checks.cpp (run R2), shown at the end.

Part A — Hana's bread three ways

  1. Fraction eaten: 3 of 8 slices, 3/8.
  2. Decimal: 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 (3 tenths, 7 hundredths, 5 thousandths: 0.3 + 0.07 + 0.005).
  3. Percentage: 0.375 × 100 = 37.5 %.
  4. Left over: 1 − 3/8 = 5/8 = 0.625, which is 62.5 %. Check: 3/8 + 5/8 = 8/8 = 1.

Part B — adding with different slice sizes

1/2 + 1/4. Rewrite 1/2 as 2/4. Then 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4 = 0.75. The wrong way, adding tops and bottoms, gives 2/6, which is only 0.333…: less than the 1/2 you started with. That cannot be right.

Part C — a fraction and a percentage of an amount

  1. 3/4 of 20: 20 ÷ 4 = 5, then 5 × 3 = 15.
  2. 25 % of 80: 80 ÷ 100 × 25 = 20. Grapes left: 80 − 20 = 60.

Part D — progress of jobs

A team has 1,000 jobs and has finished 250. Fraction done: 250/1,000 = 0.25 = 25 %. In F0-19 you will see why the last few helpers often have nothing to do, and the progress still reaches exactly 100 %.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Real slices are never perfectly equal. A fraction assumes exactly equal parts. Grandfather's knife is good, but not perfect.
    • 

  • You cannot eat more than the whole bread, but percentages can pass 100. 150 % of 20 is 30. The bread picture stops at 100 %; numbers do not.
    • 

  • Slices stop; numbers do not. You cannot cut bread into a million slices. A fraction like 1/1,000,000 is still a perfectly good number.
    • 

  • A computer's decimals are not grandfather's decimals. In base two, 0.1 is stored as a close copy (Listing 2). Bread arithmetic is exact; computer decimals are usually not.
