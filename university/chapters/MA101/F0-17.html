Story hook

Analogy

Kenji finds his grandmother's old kitchen balance in a cupboard: two pans hanging from a beam. When both pans hold the same weight, the beam sits level.

His sister Sofia sets a puzzle. She fills three paper bags with marbles, the same number in each, and folds them shut. She puts the three bags and 2 loose marbles on the left pan. On the right pan she puts 14 loose marbles. The beam is level.

"How many marbles are in each bag?" she asks. "You may not open them."

Kenji thinks. "If I take 2 marbles off both pans, it stays level. Then three bags weigh the same as 12 marbles. So one bag is 4." He opens a bag. Four marbles. Sofia groans: "Too easy." Kenji grins. He has solved his first equation.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what an equation is, using a balance scale, and what the = sign really means.
  • Solve one- and two-step equations such as 3x + 2 = 14 by doing the same thing to both sides.
  • Check a solution by substituting it back.
  • Write an equation from a story, including "how many jobs per team" and "which table holds this ticket".
  • Recognise when an equation has no whole-number answer, and what that tells you.
Jargon box
Equation
Simple words: a statement that two amounts are equal, with an = sign in the middle. Analogy: a balance scale with its beam level. Precise: a statement expression = expression, such as 3x + 2 = 14; it may be true for some values of its variables and false for others. D1
Unknown
Simple words: the number you are trying to find. Analogy: the marbles hidden inside one closed bag. Precise: a variable in an equation whose value is to be determined, often called x. D1
Solution
Simple words: the number that makes the equation true. Analogy: the count that, put in every bag, keeps the beam level. Precise: a value of the unknown for which both sides of the equation have the same value; x = 4 is the solution of 3x + 2 = 14. D1R1
Inverse operation
Simple words: the operation that undoes another one. Analogy: taking marbles off undoes putting them on. Precise: subtraction undoes addition and division (by a non-zero number) undoes multiplication; applying the same inverse operation to both sides keeps an equation true. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

An equation says that two amounts are the same. It has an = sign in the middle. Think of = as the level beam of a balance. Whatever is on the left weighs the same as whatever is on the right.

Some things on the scale are hidden, like the marbles in a closed bag. The number we want to find is the unknown. We usually call it x. In Figure 1, each bag holds x marbles, so the left pan holds 3x + 2. The right pan holds 14. The equation is 3x + 2 = 14.

The golden rule: same to both sides

A level balance stays level if you do the same thing to both pans. Take 2 marbles off each pan: still level. Put 5 on each pan: still level. Split each pan into 3 equal heaps and keep one heap from each: still level.

That is the whole method. You do the same thing to both sides until the unknown is alone on one side. Then the other side tells you its value.

Undo, last thing first

To build the left pan, Sofia did two things to x. She multiplied by 3 (three bags), then added 2 (two loose marbles). To find x, undo them in reverse order, like taking off shoes before socks.

  1. Undo "+ 2" by taking 2 from both sides: 3x = 12.
  2. Undo "× 3" by dividing both sides by 3: x = 4.

Taking away undoes adding. Dividing undoes multiplying. These pairs are called inverse operations. Figure 2 shows the two steps.

Always check

The solution is the number that makes the equation true. Check it by putting it back: 3 × 4 + 2 = 12 + 2 = 14. The two sides match, so x = 4 is right. Checking takes ten seconds and catches most mistakes.

More examples

x + 5 = 12. Take 5 from both sides: x = 7. Check: 7 + 5 = 12.

2x − 3 = 9. The last thing done was "− 3", so add 3 to both sides: 2x = 12. Then divide by 2: x = 6. Check: 2 × 6 − 3 = 9.

x ÷ 4 = 5. Undo dividing by multiplying both sides by 4: x = 20. Check: 20 ÷ 4 = 5.

Helpers and jobs, backwards

Equations answer "how many?" questions backwards. A family has 2,000 envelopes to fold. Eight teams share them equally. How many envelopes per team? Call it t. Then 8t = 2,000. Divide both sides by 8: t = 250.

Remember Leo's seat tickets from F0-16: ticket = table × 4 + seat. A guest holds ticket 11 and sits in seat 3. Which table? The equation is 11 = table × 4 + 3. Take 3 from both sides: 8 = table × 4. Divide by 4: table 2.

When the answer is not whole

Try 2x + 3 = 12. Take 3 from both sides: 2x = 9. Divide by 2: x = 4.5. That is a fine number. But if x is marbles in a bag, half a marble is impossible. The equation is telling you the puzzle cannot be built with whole marbles. Listening to that message is part of solving.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

An equation stays true if you add or subtract the same number on both sides, or multiply or divide both sides by the same non-zero number. Solving means using these moves to reach "x = a number". For any equation of the form ax + b = c with a not zero, the steps always give x = (c − b) ÷ a. D1R1

Solutions may be negative or fractional. x + 4 = −2 gives x = −2 − 4 = −6. For brackets, undo the outside first: 3(x + 1) = 18 gives x + 1 = 6, so x = 5. Whatever you get, substitute it back into the original equation, not into one of your working lines, because a slip in an early line would be copied into the check. D1R2

A story becomes an equation in three steps: name the unknown and say what it means; write each side in words; then turn the words into an expression. "Four identical egg boxes and 6 loose eggs make 30 eggs" becomes 4n + 6 = 30, so 4n = 24 and n = 6 eggs per box. D1R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Nothing here is needed for the quiz.

Why may we never divide both sides by zero? Because 0 × x = 5 has no solution at all, while 0 × x = 0 is true for every x. Dividing by zero would turn a meaningful equation into nonsense. Programs face the same danger, which is why the lab program checks a ≠ 0 before dividing. D1R1

Engineers often need "enough" rather than "equal". For 1,000 jobs and teams of 256, the equation 256t = 1,000 gives t = 3.90625, which is not a whole number of teams. The real question is "the smallest whole t with 256t at least 1,000", which is an inequality. F0-19 answers it: t = 4, the rounding-up rule of F0-12. D1R2

How the hardware actually does it

A computer does not "see" a balance or understand an equation. It only does the exact steps a program lists, one after another. Listing 1 does Kenji's steps: subtract, divide, then multiply and add to check. The idea of keeping both sides equal lives in the programmer's head, and the check at the end is how the program proves the steps were right. R1B1

Part A — Sofia's bags

  1. Equation: 3x + 2 = 14.
  2. Take 2 from both sides: 3x = 14 − 2 = 12.
  3. Divide both sides by 3: x = 4.
  4. Check in the original: 3 × 4 + 2 = 14. Balanced.

Part B — egg boxes

  1. Unknown: n = eggs in one box. Equation: 4n + 6 = 30.
  2. Take 6 from both sides: 4n = 24.
  3. Divide by 4: n = 6.
  4. Check: 4 × 6 + 6 = 30.

Part C — jobs and tickets

  1. 8t = 2,000: t = 2,000 ÷ 8 = 250 envelopes per team. Check: 8 × 250 = 2,000.
  2. 11 = table × 4 + 3: 8 = table × 4, table = 2.

Part D — an answer that is not whole

2x + 3 = 12: 2x = 9, x = 4.5. Check: 2 × 4.5 + 3 = 12. True, but impossible for whole marbles.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Negative numbers have no pan. You cannot put −2 marbles on a scale, but x + 4 = −2 is a perfectly good equation (x = −6). The rule "same to both sides" still works; the picture does not.
    • 

  • A real scale weighs, it does not count. The puzzle only works if all marbles weigh the same and the bags weigh nothing. An equation has no such problem.
    • 

  • You cannot divide a real marble. An equation happily gives x = 4.5. The scale cannot show it; the equation can.
    • 

  • A real scale is never perfectly level. An equation is exactly equal or it is not.
    •

Lab


Goal


Build a simple balance, set hidden-bag puzzles for each other, solve them as equations, and check by opening the bags.


Hardware and software needed


A coat hanger with two paper cups hung from its ends, or a ruler balanced on a round pencil; many identical small objects (the same kind of paper clip or dried bean); small envelopes or folded paper as "bags". Optional: a computer with a C++ compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0.


Safety


None beyond normal care at home. Keep small objects away from very young children. Hang the coat hanger from something stable at table height.


Steps


    

  1. Check the empty balance is level. If not, add a little tape to the light side.
    2. 

  2. Player 1 secretly puts the same number of objects in 2 or 3 envelopes, puts them on the left with some loose objects, and adds loose objects on the right until it is level.
    3. 

  3. Player 2 writes the equation (for example 3x + 2 = 14), solves it on paper, showing each "both sides" step.
    4. 

  4. Check by substitution on paper, then open one envelope.
    5. 

  5. Swap roles. Do at least three puzzles.
    6. 

  6. Optional: write a, b and c of one puzzle into balance_try.in and run balance_try.cpp (Listing 1 that reads its numbers).
    7. 



Expected observations


The opened envelope holds the number you solved for. If an envelope's paper is heavy compared with the objects, the scale may need one extra object to level; the equation then disagrees by one, which is a measurement problem, not a maths problem. For each puzzle: your solution substituted into the original equation gives both sides equal, and the opened envelope agrees.


Troubleshooting


    

  • The balance will not level → objects are not identical, or envelopes are heavy → use lighter paper; use one kind of object only.
    • 

  • Your answer is a fraction → the puzzle was built wrongly (the scale was not really level) → rebuild it.
    • 

  • The program prints "a must not be 0" → the first number in the input was 0 → put the number of envelopes first.

Summary


    

  • An equation says two amounts are equal, like a level balance.
    • 

  • Do the same thing to both sides and the equation stays true.
    • 

  • Undo operations in reverse order with inverse operations: + with −, × with ÷.
    • 

  • For ax + b = c (a not zero): x = (c − b) ÷ a.
    • 

  • Always check by substituting into the original equation.
    • 

  • Equations solve "how many?" backwards: 8t = 2,000 gives 250 per team.
    • 

  • A non-whole answer to a whole-thing puzzle means the puzzle is impossible; "at least" questions need inequalities (F0-19).
