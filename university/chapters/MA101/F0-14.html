Story hook

Analogy

Mei visits her grandmother, who lives in a tall building. The building has floors above the street and floors below it, where people keep bicycles and boxes. The lift buttons say 3, 2, 1, 0, −1, −2, −3, −4. The street floor is 0.

Mei gets in on floor 3. Her grandmother asks her to fetch a box from the lowest floor, seven floors down. Mei counts the buttons: 2, 1, 0, −1, −2, −3, −4. "Floor minus four!"

Next morning the garden thermometer shows a number below zero: −3. By the afternoon it shows 5. "It warmed up by 2 degrees," says her little brother. Mei shakes her head. "No. It went up 3 to reach zero, and then 5 more."

Numbers below zero are everywhere at home once you look. This chapter gives them a place to live: the number line.

What you will be able to do

  • Place positive and negative whole numbers on a number line and put them in order.
  • Add and take away with negative numbers by moving along the number line.
  • Work out the distance between two numbers, such as from −3 to 5.
  • Explain why taking away a negative number moves you up.
  • Predict what a C++ program prints for 5 − 7, and notice when a number type cannot go below zero.
Jargon box
Negative number
Simple words: a number less than zero, written with a minus sign in front. Analogy: a floor below the street, or a temperature below zero. Precise: for every number a greater than 0, its opposite −a is the number with a + (−a) = 0; numbers below 0 are negative. D1
Number line
Simple words: a straight line where every number has its own spot, smaller on the left and bigger on the right. Analogy: the lift shaft lying on its side: street floor at 0, upper floors to the right, lower floors to the left. Precise: a line with a chosen point for 0 and a fixed spacing for 1, so that each number corresponds to one point; moving right adds, moving left takes away. D1
Integer
Simple words: a whole number that may be positive, negative or zero. Analogy: a lift button: no half floors. Precise: any of …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …; the whole numbers together with their opposites. D1
Opposite
Simple words: the number the same distance from zero on the other side. Analogy: floor 3 and floor −3 are both three floors from the street. Precise: the opposite of a is −a; a + (−a) = 0. The opposite of −3 is 3. D1R2
Distance between numbers
Simple words: how many steps apart two numbers are on the number line, never negative. Analogy: how many floors the lift travels. Precise: the larger number minus the smaller one; from −4 to 3 it is 3 − (−4) = 7. D1R2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Until now, numbers started at zero. But think of the lift. Below the street floor there are more floors. We need numbers for them too. We write them with a minus sign: −1, −2, −3. We read −3 as "minus three" or "negative three".

These are negative numbers. The numbers above zero are positive. Zero is neither. Whole numbers of both kinds, with zero, are called integers.

The number line

A number line puts every number in its own spot. Zero sits in the middle. Positive numbers go to the right. Negative numbers go to the left. Each step is the same size.

Left means smaller. So −5 is smaller than −2, because it is further left. A temperature of −5 is colder than −2. Floor −4 is lower than floor −1.

Every number has an opposite on the other side of zero. Floor 3 and floor −3 are both three floors from the street. A number plus its opposite always makes zero.

Adding and taking away are moves

Adding a positive number moves you right. Taking away a positive number moves you left. That is all you need for Mei's lift. Start at 3. Go down 7: 3 − 7 = −4. Go up 2: −4 + 2 = −2. Figure 1 shows the moves.

What about adding a negative number? Adding −6 is like going down 6 floors. So 4 + (−6) = −2, the same as 4 − 6. Adding a negative moves you left.

Taking away a negative number is the tricky one. Think of the thermometer. In the morning it was −3. In the afternoon it was 5. How much warmer is it? You take away: 5 − (−3).

Count the steps on Figure 2. From −3 up to 0 is 3 steps. From 0 up to 5 is 5 more. Together, 8. So 5 − (−3) = 8. Taking away a negative moves you right. It is the same as adding its opposite: 5 + 3 = 8.

How far apart?

The distance between two numbers is how many steps apart they are. Take the bigger one minus the smaller one. From −4 to 3 is 3 − (−4) = 7 steps. A distance is never negative. The lift travels 7 floors whichever way it goes.

Numbers below zero can mean "owing", too. Kofi borrows 4 coins from his sister. His money is 0 − 4 = −4 coins. Then he earns 10 coins. Now he has −4 + 10 = 6 coins, after paying her back.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. At level L0 this layer is optional.

The integers are …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …. For every integer a, the opposite −a satisfies a + (−a) = 0. Subtracting is adding the opposite: a − b = a + (−b). That one rule explains every case: 4 − 6 = 4 + (−6) = −2, and 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8. D1R2

On the number line, a < b means a is to the left of b. So −6 < −1 < 0 < 2. The distance between a and b is the larger minus the smaller. D1R2

Multiplying follows a pattern you can check. 3 × 2 = 6, 3 × 1 = 3, 3 × 0 = 0; each step down takes away 3, so 3 × (−2) = −6. Continue the same way with −3 and you find (−3) × (−2) = 6. A negative times a positive is negative; a negative times a negative is positive. D1R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Nothing here is needed for the quiz.

Dividing negative integers needs a rule for the leftover part. On paper, −7 ÷ 2 = −3.5. A C++ int division keeps a whole number; in the build's run it gave −3, which means it cut the .5 off towards zero, not down to −4 (run R2). Engineers must know which way their language rounds. R2B2

Some number types in a computer have no negative numbers at all. Listing 2 subtracts 5 − 7 twice. With int it prints −2. With unsigned int, a type for "zero and up", the same build printed 4294967294: the result wrapped around to a huge number instead of going below zero. MA102 chapter F0-24 explains exactly why. For now, remember: if a program's count could go below zero, its type must allow negatives. R1B2

How the hardware actually does it

A computer has no minus sign inside it. It stores every number as a pattern of switches, and it uses one agreed pattern rule to stand for negative numbers. That rule is called two's complement, and MA102 (F0-24) teaches it. Listing 2 shows its effect from the outside: the same subtraction gives −2 or a huge number, depending only on how the program told the computer to read the pattern. B1R1

Worked example

All numbers below are recomputed by checks.cpp (run R2), shown at the end.

Part A — the lift

  1. Start: floor 3.
  2. Down 7: 3 − 7 = −4. (Count on Figure 1: seven jumps left from 3.)
  3. Up 2: −4 + 2 = −2.
  4. From floor −2 up to floor 4: distance 4 − (−2) = 6 floors.

Part B — the thermometer

  1. Morning −3, afternoon 5. Change = afternoon − morning = 5 − (−3).
  2. Taking away a negative is adding its opposite: 5 + 3 = 8.
  3. Check by steps: −3 to 0 is 3; 0 to 5 is 5; 3 + 5 = 8 degrees warmer.
  4. Check backwards: −3 + 8 = 5. Correct.

Part C — ordering

Put 2, −6, 0, −1 in order. Negatives first, the one furthest from zero first: −6, −1. Then 0, then 2. Order: −6 < −1 < 0 < 2.

Part D — two more moves

−2 − 3 = −5 (start at −2, move 3 left). −2 − (−5) = −2 + 5 = 3 (start at −2, move 5 right).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Buildings end; the number line does not. A lift stops at the lowest and highest floor. The number line goes on for ever in both directions. (A computer's number types do end, as Listing 2 hints; F0-24 explains.)
    • 

  • There is no floor 2.5, but there is a temperature of 2.5. Lift floors are integers only. A number line also has every fraction and decimal in between (F0-13).
    • 

  • Owing is not a pile of "negative coins". You cannot hold −4 coins in your hand. A negative number describes a direction or a debt, not a heap of objects.
    •

Lab


Goal


Build a number line on the floor from −10 to 10 and walk it to add and take away negative numbers.


Hardware and software needed


Chalk (outdoors) or masking tape (indoors), a clear floor space, paper for recording. Optional: a computer with a C++ compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0.


Safety


Use a flat, clear floor, never stairs. Walk, do not run. Ask an adult before putting tape on a floor.


Steps


    

  1. Make 21 marks, one big step apart. Label the middle 0, the right side 1 to 10, the left side −1 to −10.
    2. 

  2. Stand on 3, facing right. Take 7 steps backwards (left). Where are you? Write 3 − 7 = ?
    3. 

  3. From there, take 2 steps forwards (right). Write the sum.
    4. 

  4. Rule for negatives: to add a negative number, turn around and walk forwards. To take away a negative number, walk backwards while facing left — you end up moving right.
    5. 

  5. Walk 5 − (−3): stand on 5, face left (the "negative" direction), take 3 steps backwards. Where are you?
    6. 

  6. Make up three moves of your own, predict, walk, record.
    7. 

  7. Optional: change the numbers in Listing 1 to match your moves. Predict, run, compare.
    8. 



Expected observations


Step 2 lands on −4, step 3 on −2, and step 5 on 8, the same numbers as Listing 1 printed in run R1.


Verification


For each move, check backwards: if you went from a to b, then a + (b − a) must equal b. For the thermometer: −3 + 8 = 5.


Troubleshooting


    

  • You land one mark off → you counted the starting mark as a step → count jumps, not marks.
    • 

  • Taking away a negative moved you left → you faced the wrong way → face left (negative), then walk backwards.
    • 

  • The program prints a huge number → a variable was changed to unsigned → change it back to int.
    • 



Extension


Mark halves between the integers. Walk 1.5 − 4. Where do you land?

Summary


    

  • Negative numbers are less than zero; with zero and the positive whole numbers they form the integers.
    • 

  • On a number line, left is smaller: −5 < −2.
    • 

  • Adding a positive moves right; taking away a positive moves left.
    • 

  • Adding a negative moves left; taking away a negative moves right.
    • 

  • Subtracting is adding the opposite: 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8.
    • 

  • Distance between two numbers is bigger minus smaller, never negative.
    • 

  • In C++, int allows negatives; unsigned int does not, and wraps around silently (F0-24).
