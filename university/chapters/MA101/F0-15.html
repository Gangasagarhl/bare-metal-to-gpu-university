Story hook

Analogy

Ines takes a long strip of paper from the kitchen drawer and folds it in half. "Two layers," she says. She folds it again. "Four." Again. "Eight."

Her brother Arjun watches. "Every fold doubles it. After ten folds, how many layers would there be?"

Ines guesses twenty. Arjun guesses a hundred. Their grandmother, who is cooking, says: "Just over a thousand. One thousand and twenty-four." They do not believe her, so they sit down and double on paper: 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024. Grandmother is right.

Doubling grows surprisingly fast. So does multiplying by ten. This chapter is about these two kinds of growing, and why the second one, doubling, is everywhere inside computers.

What you will be able to do

  • Write powers of ten and powers of two with a small raised number (an exponent), such as 103 and 25.
  • Calculate powers of two up to 216 by doubling, and powers of ten up to a million.
  • Explain why n light switches can make 2n different patterns.
  • Count how many times you can halve a power of two until you reach 1.
  • Compare 210 = 1,024 with 103 = 1,000 and say why the difference matters.
Jargon box
Power
Simple words: a number multiplied by itself a certain number of times. Analogy: folding the paper again and again: each fold multiplies the layers by 2. Precise: for a whole number n, an is a × a × … × a with n copies of a; a0 = 1. D1 §2.1, §10.4
Exponent
Simple words: the small raised number that says how many times to multiply. Analogy: the number of folds. Precise: n in an; also written a^n in plain text and in program output, as in Listing 1. D1 §2.1R1
Power of ten
Simple words: 1, 10, 100, 1,000 and so on: a 1 followed by zeros. Analogy: the bowls and cups of F0-11, ten of each making the next. Precise: 10n, which is written as 1 followed by n zeros; the place values of base ten. D5 §4.1
Power of two
Simple words: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on: each one double the one before. Analogy: the layers after each fold. Precise: 2n for a whole number n; the place values of base two. D5 §4.3
Logarithm (base two)
Simple words: how many times you must double 1 to reach a number, or halve the number to get back to 1. Analogy: how many folds made 1,024 layers. Precise: log2(x) is the exponent n with 2n = x; log2(1,024) = 10. D4 §10.3R2
Bit and byte
Simple words: a bit is one switch, 0 or 1; a byte is a group of bits handled together. Analogy: one light switch; a row of eight switches, each worth double the one to its right. Precise: KID101 chapter F0-02 defines bit, and MA102 chapter F0-23 defines byte; on the machine that built this chapter, a byte had 8 bits (run R1 of Listing 2). The C++ rules leave the number of bits in a byte to each machine and report it in CHAR_BIT, so this is a measurement. R1D2 §6.7.1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Powers of ten

Remember the bowls and cups from F0-11. Ten beans make a cup. Ten cups make a bowl. So a bowl is 10 × 10 = 100 beans. Ten bowls would be 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 beans.

Writing many tens is slow. So we use a short way. We write 103 for 10 × 10 × 10. The small raised 3 is the exponent. It says how many tens are multiplied. We read it "ten to the power three", or "ten cubed".

Here is a lovely shortcut. The exponent of ten is the number of zeros. 103 = 1,000 has three zeros. 106 = 1,000,000, one million, has six. So four million is 4 × 106.

Powers of two

Now Ines's paper. With no folds there is 1 layer. Each fold doubles the layers. One fold: 2. Two folds: 2 × 2 = 4. Three folds: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.

We write 23 = 8. These numbers are powers of two: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024. Figure 2 shows how quickly they grow. Each bar is twice the bar before it.

Be careful: 25 is not 2 × 5. It is 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32. The exponent counts how many twos you multiply. It is not a number to multiply by.

And what is 20? No folds at all: 1 layer. So 20 = 1. In the same way, 100 = 1.

Light switches and patterns

A light switch is on or off. One switch can make 2 patterns. Add a second switch. For each pattern of the first, the second can be on or off. That makes 2 × 2 = 4 patterns. A third switch doubles it again: 8.

So n switches make 2n patterns. Eight switches make 28 = 256 patterns. A computer keeps its information in tiny switches called bits. That is why powers of two appear everywhere in computers.

Almost a thousand

Look at 210 = 1,024. It is very close to 103 = 1,000. The difference is only 24. But close is not equal. MA102 (chapter F0-23) shows how engineers keep the two apart with different names.

Halving back down

Doubling has an opposite: halving. Start at 1,024 and halve: 512, 256, 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. That took 10 halvings. It matches 210 = 1,024. Counting halvings is a fast way to find an exponent.

Helpers in powers of two

Remember the rounding-up rule from F0-12. Later, in the GPU courses, the first example program uses teams of 256 helpers, which is 28. For 1,000 jobs you need 4 teams: 1,024 helpers, with 24 left over. For 1,024 jobs, 4 teams fit exactly, with nobody idle.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Note

For the curious. At level L0 this layer is optional.

For a whole number n, an means n copies of a multiplied together, and a0 = 1. Multiplying powers of the same number adds the exponents: 23 × 22 = (2 × 2 × 2) × (2 × 2) = 25 = 32, and 102 × 103 = 105. Going down by one in the exponent halves a power of two, which is why 20 must be 1. D1 §10.2, §10.4R2

The logarithm base two answers the reverse question: "2 to what power gives x?" log2(1,024) = 10 because 210 = 1,024. For powers of two it equals the number of halvings down to 1. Engineers use it to count how many switches are needed: to tell apart 256 things you need log2(256) = 8 switches. D4 §10.3, §10.4R2

Powers of two are the place values of base two, in the same way as powers of ten are the place values of base ten (F0-11, Figure 1). MA102 builds on exactly this. D5 §4.3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Nothing here is needed for the quiz.

The gap between powers of two and powers of ten grows. 210 − 103 = 24, which is 2.4 % of 1,000. But 220 = 1,048,576, while 106 = 1,000,000: now the gap is 48,576. Treating them as equal gives errors that get bigger with the size of the number. That is why careful engineers name binary and decimal units differently; MA102 chapter F0-23 gives those names and their sources. D1 §2.1R2U1

Powers grow faster than any fixed number type can follow. Listing 1 stops at 216 and uses the type long long so the numbers fit. A program that kept doubling for ever would run out of room in its number type; F0-24 shows what happens then. R1D2 §6.8.2

How the hardware actually does it

In the rules of C++, a program's memory is one or more long rows of bytes. Each byte is a group of bits, and every byte has its own number, its address, so the computer can pick it out. An address is itself a number stored in bits. With n switches you can make 2n patterns (Layer 1), so a number of n bits can name 2n different places. On the machine that built this chapter, Listing 2 printed 8 bits per byte. D2 §6.7.1R1R2

Worked example

All numbers below are recomputed by checks.cpp (run R2), shown at the end.

Part A — folds and layers

  1. 5 folds: 25 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32 layers.
  2. 7 folds: double twice more: 64, then 128 layers.
  3. 10 folds: 210 = 1,024 layers (Listing 1, run R1).

Part B — switches and patterns

3 switches: 23 = 8 patterns. 4 switches: 24 = 16. 6 switches: 26 = 64. 8 switches: 28 = 256.

Part C — powers of ten

103 = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000. 106 = 1,000,000. Four million = 4 × 106 = 4,000,000. And 102 × 103 = 100 × 1,000 = 100,000 = 105.

Part D — halving and helpers

  1. Halve 1,024 until you reach 1: 512, 256, 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. That is 10 halvings, so log2(1,024) = 10.
  2. 1,000 jobs, teams of 256: (1,000 + 255) ÷ 256 = 4 teams, 4 × 256 = 1,024 helpers, 1,024 − 1,000 = 24 idle.
  3. 1,024 jobs: also 4 teams, and 0 idle.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Paper runs out; numbers do not. After a few folds the paper gets too thick to fold again. Find your own limit in the lab and write your number down. The powers of two carry on for ever on paper (but not in a computer's number types).
    • 

  • A switch pattern has no meaning by itself. 256 patterns could stand for numbers, letters or colours. The program decides what each pattern means (MA102), not the switches.
    • 

  • Real switches can stick or break. A bit, when read, is always exactly 0 or 1; a worn real switch may be half-pressed.
    •

Lab


Goal


Fold a paper strip again and again, predict and count the sections each time, and find your own folding limit.


Hardware and software needed


A long thin strip of paper (for example cut from the long side of a sheet), a pencil, a table. Optional: a computer with a C++ compiler; this build used g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0.


Safety


None beyond normal care. Paper edges can give small cuts; fold slowly.


Steps


    

  1. Make a table with columns: folds, predicted sections, counted sections.
    2. 

  2. Write the prediction for 1 fold. Fold the strip in half, always in the same direction. Unfold and count the sections between creases. Refold.
    3. 

  3. Repeat for 2, 3, 4 and 5 folds. Always predict first.
    4. 

  4. Keep folding until you cannot. Write down your own number of folds. This is your measurement, not a fact from a book.
    5. 

  5. Optional: run Listing 1. Check your predictions against its lines.
    6. 

  6. Optional: run Listing 2 on your computer and write down what it prints.
    7. 



Expected observations


Sections after n folds: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 for n = 0 to 5, matching the first lines of run R1. The sections get very small; counting after 5 or 6 folds gets hard.


Verification


Each count must be exactly double the previous one. If it is not, a fold was uneven or a crease was missed.


Troubleshooting


    

  • The count is one less than predicted → you counted creases, not sections → count the spaces between creases (including both ends).
    • 

  • Sections have different sizes → folds were not exactly in half → line the ends up before pressing the fold.
    • 

  • Listing 2 prints a number other than 8 → your machine's byte differs from the build machine → write your number on your poster with "measured on my computer".
    • 



Extension


Fold a second strip into thirds each time (like a letter). Predict the sections: 1, 3, 9, … These are powers of three.

Summary


    

  • A power is repeated multiplication: 103 = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.
    • 

  • The exponent of ten is the number of zeros.
    • 

  • Powers of two double each time: 1, 2, 4, 8, … , 1,024 = 210.
    • 

  • 25 = 32, not 2 × 5; and 20 = 100 = 1.
    • 

  • n on/off switches make 2n patterns; 8 switches make 256.
    • 

  • Halving back to 1 counts the exponent: that count is the logarithm base two.
    • 

  • 210 = 1,024 is close to, but not equal to, 1,000; the gap grows for bigger powers.
