Story hook

Leila's rehearsals with Tomás went well. But her real tray is heavier than the plastic one, and her real shoes are new. So for the last rehearsal she uses the real tray and the real shoes, in the empty hall. Tomás is not beside her any more: he narrates over a phone, from the next room, watching a video of her walk.

Something strange happens. Leila reacts to each bump a little later than before, because Tomás's words reach her after a short delay, and his video of her reaches him late too. When the delay grows (the phone line gets bad), her corrections start to overshoot: she leans left when she should already be leaning right. Nothing is wrong with her arms. The delay on the line has become part of her balance.

Hardware in the loop is that last rehearsal. The real flight controller, with its real processor, runs the flight software; the pretend world stays on the computer next to it and talks to it over a cable. This chapter shows what that reveals, and what the cable itself adds to the loop.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what hardware in the loop (HITL) makes real compared with SITL, what it still models, and where it sits on the ladder from SITL to flight.
  • Build the HITL split on one machine: world and flight software exchanging framed, checksummed bytes, with injected corruption detected and handled.
  • Compute a link budget (bytes per frame, frames per second, bit times per byte) and predict when a link saturates and how fast its latency grows.
  • Measure in simulation how added loop delay degrades a position controller, and state the delay the loop tolerates.
  • Diagnose a HITL bench that oscillates although SITL is fine, from a bench timing log.
Jargon box
Hardware in the loop (HITL)
Simple words: the real flight-controller board runs the flight program, while a computer next to it pretends to be the world. Analogy: Leila with the real tray and shoes, while Tomás narrates over the phone. Precise: a test arrangement in which the flight software runs on the target flight controller (its processor, RTOS, memory and build), its sensor inputs are replaced by simulated data sent from a host simulator over a communication link, and its actuator outputs are sent back to the simulator instead of (or as well as) to the motors; PX4's documentation describes a HITL mode of this kind. D1 D2
HITL link
Simple words: the cable and the message format that carry pretend sensor data to the board and motor commands back. Analogy: the phone line between Tomás and Leila. Precise: the physical connection (USB, a serial port, a network) plus the framing protocol that carries simulated sensor messages to the flight controller and actuator messages to the simulator; its capacity, latency, jitter and error behaviour become part of the control loop. D2 R1
Link budget (HITL)
Simple words: a calculation of whether all the messages fit through the cable in time. Analogy: whether Tomás can say everything that happens before the next thing happens. Precise: the comparison of offered load (bytes per frame × frames per second, plus framing overhead such as start and stop bits) with the link's capacity; utilisation ρ = offered / capacity must stay well below 1, because when ρ > 1 the queue, and with it the age of the data, grows without bound. C2 R3
Data age (at the flight controller)
Simple words: how old the newest sensor data on the board is. Analogy: how long ago the bump happened that Tomás is describing now. Precise: current time minus the simulated time stamp of the newest complete sensor frame the flight software has received; in HITL it adds directly to the loop's delay. R3
Test ladder (SITL to flight)
Simple words: the order of rehearsals, each making one more part real. Analogy: plastic cups, then the real tray, then real drinks in an empty hall, then the party. Precise: the sequence SITL → HITL → bench tests with real sensors and actuators and propellers removed → restrained or tethered tests where available → supervised flight, each stage entered only when the previous one passed its acceptance tests; MP7's milestones follow it. C1

Related terms from earlier courses: frame (serial link), checksum (frame check), delay margin, phase margin, bench test.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

In SITL, everything is pretend except the program. In HITL, the program runs on the real little computer that will fly in the drone. The big computer still pretends to be the world, and sends pretend sensor messages down a cable. The little computer answers with motor commands up the same cable. Now you find out whether the real little computer is fast enough, and whether the program fits in it. But the cable is slow compared with thinking, and it adds a delay. If you send too much down it, the delay grows, and the pretend drone starts to wobble.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Leila's real tray and shoes found problems the plastic cups could not; the phone line added a new problem of its own. HITL does the same two things to your flight software.

What HITL makes real

The flight software is now the binary built for the flight controller and running on it: the real processor and its floating-point unit, the RTOS scheduler and its priorities, the memory limits, the parameter storage, the boot sequence and the build configuration. Timing overruns, stack overflows, single-precision surprises and missing drivers appear here for the first time. What is still modelled: the vehicle's motion, the sensor readings (the board's own sensors are present but their data is not used for the simulated flight), the radio and the battery. PX4's user guide has a section on HITL; check your release for which simulators and airframes it supports and how the board is switched into and out of HITL mode. D1

The link enters the loop

In SITL the interface between world and software was a function call. In HITL it is a cable carrying bytes, so three new things happen: the data must be serialised into frames (with a type, a length and a checksum, so that the receiver can find frame boundaries and reject corrupted frames); the bytes take time to cross; and the cable has a capacity. Listing 1 builds this split on one machine with a real kernel socket between two threads and its own small framing protocol, so that you can see every byte. R1 C2

The flight controller has its own clock

In SITL, lockstep made the world wait for the software. A real flight controller runs its loops from its own timer and does not wait for anyone. In HITL the simulator must therefore keep up in real time, and the flight software uses the newest sensor frame it has received when its loop runs. Any delay on the way (serialisation, queueing in buffers, USB scheduling, the simulator's own step time) adds to the age of the data the controller acts on. That delay is now part of the control loop, exactly like the delay on Leila's phone line. Listing 2 measures what it does to the course controller. D3 R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

One paragraph of analogy first: the phone line taught Leila nothing about carrying drinks, but it could still make her spill. Treat the HITL link as test equipment whose own behaviour must be measured and kept out of your conclusions.

Framing, checking and the bytes on the wire

A frame of the course protocol is: sync byte 0xA5, type, sequence number, payload length, payload (13 single-precision floats for a sensor frame, 4 for a command), and a 16-bit CRC over everything after the sync byte. The receiver checks the CRC and rejects the frame on a mismatch. Three engineering decisions are visible in Listing 1:

  • Precision on the wire. The world computes in double precision; the frame carries single precision. That is realistic (flight-controller messages commonly use 32-bit floats and integers) and it changes the result: the HITL rehearsal lands 1.256 m from home while SITL landed 1.150 m from home, with the same seed. HITL and SITL results are compared with tolerances, never bit for bit. R1
  • What to do with a bad frame. The flight software rejects it and repeats its last command, marked stale. Five frames were corrupted on purpose and five were rejected; the flight still completed. A real flight stack has its own policy (and its own failsafe for sensor timeouts); find it in its documentation, and test it.
  • Resynchronisation. The course receiver assumes the stream starts at a sync byte and never loses one. A real parser scans for the sync byte after an error and validates the length before trusting it. The extension task asks you to add that.

The CRC used is the 16-bit CCITT polynomial 0x1021 with initial value 0xFFFF; the program prints its check value for the nine ASCII digits "123456789" (0x29B1) so that you can compare it with any reference implementation. R1

Link budget and queueing

For a serial link, each byte costs the start bit, the data bits, the parity bit if any and the stop bits; with the common 8N1 setting that is 10 bit times per byte (RB201's F9-14 treats UART framing). A link configured at B bit/s therefore moves B/10 bytes per second. Let the offered load be L bytes/s. The utilisation is ρ = L / (B/10). For ρ < 1 the queue stays bounded and the delay stays near the transmission time of one frame (plus the waiting caused by bursts); for ρ > 1 the queue grows by (L − B/10) bytes every second, and the age of the newest complete frame grows by (L − B/10)/(B/10) seconds per second, without limit. A design that runs a link at ρ close to 1 has no margin for a debug stream, a parameter download or a retransmission. Over USB the arithmetic is different (packet scheduling by the host), but the principle (offered load against capacity, and a queue in between) is the same; measure it on your bench. C2 R3

Delay in the loop

A pure time delay τd adds a phase lag of ω·τd radians at angular frequency ω without changing the gain, so it eats the phase margin of a loop, and enough delay makes any loop with sufficient gain unstable ("Feedback Systems" discusses time delay and the delay margin; F10-17 tuned with phase margin). The course position loop has a crossover in the region of 1–2 rad/s (its gains kv = 1.8 1/s and kp = 0.9 1/s set it), so a few hundred milliseconds of delay should start to matter. Listing 2 inserts a delay line and measures: up to 300 ms the 10 m step response is practically unchanged (overshoot at most 0.04 m, settling in 3.5–3.6 s); at 400 ms the overshoot grows to 0.22 m; at 500 ms the response takes 10.5 s to settle; at 600 ms it never settles within the 48 s of the test and still oscillates by 1.38 m in its last 10 s. The real attitude and rate loops are much faster, so their delay tolerance is much smaller: HITL delays that are harmless for a position loop can ruin a rate loop. Your flight stack's HITL may close some loops on the board and some through the link; know which. D3 R2

What to test in HITL, and how to know you passed

Run the same acceptance tests as SITL (with tolerances widened by a stated amount for the precision and timing differences), plus target-only checks: the loop period and its jitter measured on the board (the flight stack's own performance counters or logs), CPU load, free memory and stack margins, start-up time, behaviour when the link stops (the board must detect the loss of sensor data and react; that is a failsafe of the HITL configuration, not of flight), and a clean return to the flight configuration. Record the board, the firmware build, the simulator, the link and its settings in every HITL report. D1 C1

Safety

A board in HITL configuration takes its flight data from the link, not from its sensors. If such a board is installed in a vehicle and armed, the vehicle flies on a simulation. Before any flight: restore the flight configuration, verify it on the ground (sensor values move when you move the vehicle by hand, propellers off), and make "HITL configuration removed" an item on the go/no-go checklist of F10-37. On the bench, keep propellers off the motors, keep ESCs unpowered unless a test needs them, and follow the LiPo rules of F10-11 and the battery maker's instructions if a flight battery powers the bench.

How the hardware actually does it

The bytes of a HITL frame leave the desktop through the operating system's driver stack (a USB CDC-ACM device or a USB-to-serial adapter), cross the cable, and arrive at the flight controller's USB device controller or UART. On the microcontroller, a driver moves them by interrupt or DMA into a buffer; a task of the flight stack parses the frames and publishes the values where the real sensor drivers would have published theirs (uORB topics in PX4, F10-22; the HAL in ArduPilot, F10-28). Every stage has a buffer, and every buffer is a queue that can fill. The flight controller's loop then runs on its own hardware timer. The only way to know the end-to-end delay on your bench is to measure it, for example by time-stamping a frame in the simulator, echoing the stamp in the next command, and logging the round trip. Worked example

Question. A bench sends course sensor frames at 100 Hz over a serial link set to 115,200 bit/s, 8N1. Is there room for an extra 60-byte debug frame every step? If not, when will the data age reach 600 ms, where Listing 2 showed the loop no longer settles?

  1. Frame size. Header 4 bytes + 13 floats × 4 bytes + CRC 2 bytes = 4 + 52 + 2 = 58 bytes (Listing 1 prints 58).
  2. Offered load. 58 × 100 = 5,800 bytes/s; with the debug frame: (58 + 60) × 100 = 11,800 bytes/s.
  3. Capacity. 115,200 / 10 = 11,520 bytes/s (10 bit times per byte with 8N1).
  4. Utilisation. Without debug: ρ = 5,800 / 11,520 = 0.503. With debug: ρ = 11,800 / 11,520 = 1.024 > 1: the link is overloaded.
  5. Growth. The queue grows by 11,800 − 11,520 = 280 bytes/s; the age of the newest sensor frame grows by 280 / 11,520 = 0.0243 s every second.
  6. Time to 600 ms. 0.600 / 0.0243 ≈ 24.7 s after the debug stream starts. Started at 20 s, the age passes 600 ms at about 45 s.

The forensic evidence below shows a sensor age of 580 ms at 44 s and 630 ms at 46 s, as predicted, and the hold error growing after that. R3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Leila hears Tomás's words in order and knows when a sentence is garbled. A byte stream has no sentences: the receiver must find frame boundaries and detect corruption itself, and if it loses synchronisation it can misread everything that follows.
    • 

  • A bad phone line makes a delay you can hear. A queue that grows by 280 bytes per second makes a delay that grows silently; nothing on the bench looks different until the loop oscillates.
    • 

  • In the rehearsal Leila's own senses still work: she would notice if Tomás described a floor that is not there. A flight controller in HITL ignores its own sensors for flight; it cannot notice that the simulated world disagrees with the real one, which is why HITL configuration on a real vehicle is a hazard.
    • 

  • Leila uses the real tray, so the rehearsal tests her arms. In HITL the motors are not driven (or have no propellers), so nothing about real thrust, ESCs or vibration is tested; the "real" part is the processor, not the body.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Link near saturation. Symptom: HITL starts well and oscillates after a while, worse when logging or a ground station is connected. Cause: offered load close to or above capacity; the queue grows. Find it: compute ρ; log the data age or the round-trip time per frame. Fix: fewer or smaller frames, a faster link, or move debug traffic elsewhere.
    • 

  • Comparing HITL and SITL bit for bit. Symptom: "HITL disagrees with SITL by 0.1 m: bug?". Cause: precision on the wire and timing differ. Fix: compare with stated tolerances; investigate only differences beyond them.
    • 

  • Board left in HITL configuration. Symptom (on the bench, propellers off): sensor values do not change when the vehicle is moved by hand. Cause: the HITL settings were not reverted. Fix: revert, verify by moving the vehicle, add a checklist item.
    • 

  • Simulator slower than real time. Symptom: frames arrive at irregular intervals; the board's log shows gaps. Cause: the simulator cannot compute a step within the step period on this desktop. Find it: log the simulator's step time. Fix: a simpler world, a faster machine, or a lower simulated rate if the flight stack allows it.
    • 

  • No check of corrupted frames. Symptom: rare, sudden spikes in the controller's input. Cause: frames accepted without CRC check, or a resynchronisation bug. Find it: inject corruption on purpose, as Listing 1 does. Fix: check every frame; count and log rejects.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. hitl_forensic.cpp models a link of 11,520 bytes/s (a 115,200 bit/s setting, 10 bit times per byte) carrying 58-byte sensor frames at 100 Hz; at 20 s a 60-byte debug frame per step is added. The flight software runs on its own 10 ms clock using the newest complete sensor frame; the vehicle holds position in gusty wind. R3


Observation. Offered load jumps from 5,800 to 11,800 bytes/s at 20 s, above the 11,520 bytes/s capacity. The queue grows by 560 bytes per 2 s window (280 bytes/s) without limit; the sensor age grows from 10 ms to 960 ms at 60 s and 1,670 ms at 90 s, about 24–25 ms per second. The hold error stays below 0.5 m until about 56 s, then grows to 1.14 m at 60 s, 5.5 m at 66 s and 11 m at 90 s.


Root cause. Link saturation (ρ = 1.024) after the debug stream was enabled: the data the controller acts on gets older every second, and once its age passes the loop's delay tolerance (400–600 ms from Listing 2, reached at 36–46 s) the hold degrades and then oscillates. The estimator code is not involved: the age column, which the estimator cannot influence, explains the timing, and the queue grows from the moment of the change, before any oscillation.
