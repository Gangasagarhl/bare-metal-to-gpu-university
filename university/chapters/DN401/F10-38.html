Story hook

After the party there is a puddle near table 7 and broken glass by the garden rope. Nobody saw the moment it happened. The head waiter does not ask "who dropped it?". He asks for the facts. The stains start at the kitchen door and curve to the left, wider and wider. Leila's left shoe has a wet sole. The guests at table 5 say she walked past them twice, the second time farther out. Little by little the facts tell a story: something pulled her to the left from the first step, a bit more with every step, and the circles grew until she reached the rope.

A flight log is the floor after the party. It records what the flight software knew and did, many times a second. This chapter reads two logs, one of a vehicle that flew away and one of a vehicle that fell from the sky with the battery failsafe "working", and reconstructs from the numbers what happened and why.

What you will be able to do

  • Follow a forensic method on a flight log: timeline, observations, hypotheses, tests against the data, root cause, contributing factors, fixes and prevention.
  • Write a log-analysis program that turns a raw log into the few derived quantities that separate hypotheses (field strength, command-to-response angle, return charge).
  • Recognise the log signature of compass interference that grows with motor current, and connect it with the heading-error stability limit of the position loop.
  • Recognise the log signature of a battery failsafe based on a wrong capacity setting, and show from the log alone what the real remaining charge was.
  • Write a blameless incident report with evidence, root cause, contributing factors and actions.
Jargon box
Flight log
Simple words: the recording that a drone's flight computer makes of what it measured, decided and commanded during a flight. Analogy: the stains on the floor and the guests' notes. Precise: a time-stamped record written by the flight software (sensor values, estimates, setpoints, commands, modes, events, parameters, versions) at defined rates; flight stacks have their own formats (PX4 writes ULog files, F10-25; ArduPilot writes its own binary logs), and the course uses a CSV format with comment lines. A log contains only what the software knew, never the truth. D1 D2
Flyaway
Simple words: a drone flying off in a direction nobody commanded, and not coming back under control. Analogy: Leila's circles growing until she reaches the rope. Precise: loss of position control in which the vehicle departs from its intended position or path in an uncommanded way; causes include a wrong heading or position estimate, a configuration error, and loss of control authority. R2
Compass interference (current-dependent)
Simple words: magnetic fields from the drone's own power wires that fool its compass, more when the motors work harder. Analogy: a magnet in Leila's pocket that pulls a compass needle more the faster she walks. Precise: a disturbance of the magnetometer reading by the magnetic field of currents in the vehicle's power wiring, proportional to the current and fixed in the body frame; it is absent on the ground with motors stopped, so a ground calibration and pre-arm checks do not see it, and it rotates the heading estimate in flight. D3 R2
Toilet-bowling
Simple words: a drone circling around the point it is trying to hold, in growing or steady loops. Analogy: water circling in a bowl. Precise: a circular oscillation of a position loop caused by a heading error: each correction is rotated by the error, so it pushes partly sideways; above a critical error the loop is unstable and, with acceleration and speed limits, settles into circles that may grow. The informal name is common in drone communities; the course uses it with this definition. R1
Root cause and contributing factor
Simple words: the deepest reason something went wrong, and the other things that made it worse or let it through. Analogy: the slippery floor (root cause), and the missing rope and the new shoes (contributing). Precise: the root cause is the fault whose removal would have prevented the incident; contributing factors are conditions that made it possible, worse or undetected (process gaps, missing checks, configuration). A blameless analysis looks for both and asks which defence failed, not who. C1

Related terms: magnetometer, internal resistance and voltage sag, battery failsafe (F10-35), geofence, evidence currency (F10-36), check freshness (F10-37).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A drone writes a diary while it flies: where it thinks it is, which way it thinks it points, what it told the motors, how much battery it used. After a crash we read the diary like detectives. We do not guess and we do not blame. We look for things that changed, compare what the drone wanted with what happened, and test each idea against the numbers. In one diary, the compass was fooled by the drone's own wires, so every push went a little sideways, and the drone circled away. In the other, the drone was told its battery was bigger than it really was, so it started home too late.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The method

The head waiter's method works for logs too. 1. Secure the evidence: copy the log, record its source, never edit the original. 2. Context: vehicle, firmware and configuration (from the log header, not from memory), mission, weather, what the team saw. 3. Timeline: events and mode changes in order. 4. Observations: what the numbers show, without interpretation. 5. Hypotheses: at least two, each with what it predicts in the data. 6. Tests: derived quantities that separate the hypotheses. 7. Root cause and contributing factors. 8. Fixes and prevention, including which earlier defence (test, checklist, safety case) should have caught it. The written report follows the same order. C1

What a log can and cannot tell

A log records what the flight software knew: measured values, estimates, setpoints, commands. It does not record the truth. If the compass lied, the log shows the lie, faithfully. Forensics therefore looks for inconsistencies between independent sources inside the log: commanded acceleration against acceleration derived from GNSS velocity; heading from the compass against the direction of motion; battery percentage against voltage; the field strength in flight against on the ground. Real flight-stack logs contain many more signals (raw IMU, estimator innovations and test ratios, per-motor outputs, ESC telemetry) and tools to plot them; learn yours. D1 D2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The stains curved left from the first step: the shape of the evidence pointed to a cause that was present all the time and grew, not to a single slip. Derived quantities find such shapes in a log.

Heading error and the position loop

The controller computes a desired horizontal acceleration in the earth frame and converts it into the vehicle's frame with its estimated heading (dronesim.hpp line 400). The vehicle then tilts in its true frame. If the estimate is wrong by δ, every horizontal command is rotated by δ. For the linear loop of the course controller (position gain kp, velocity gain kv, drag, thrust time constant τ), writing position as one complex number p = x + iy gives the characteristic equation τs³ + (1 + τ·drag)s² + (drag + E·kv)s + E·kvkp = 0 with E = eiδ. Listing 2 finds its roots: the loop is stable up to δ = 59.2° and unstable beyond, with the rightmost root crossing into the right half-plane. With speed and acceleration limits, the unstable loop turns into circles around the hold point, and the circles grow as δ grows. That is toilet-bowling, and beyond it, a flyaway. R1 D4

Interference that the ground never sees

Current in a wire creates a magnetic field around it, proportional to the current (F10-08 on sensors; any electromagnetism text). A power lead routed near the compass adds a field that is fixed in the body frame and grows with motor current. On the ground with motors stopped the current is near zero, so the compass reads the earth's field alone: calibration and pre-arm checks pass. In flight the added field shifts the reading. Two signs in a log betray it: the measured field strength (norm) changes when the current changes, and the heading estimate drifts in step with throttle. The course log records the field strength normalised to the earth's field on the ground (mag_norm, 1.000 before arming). D3

The command-to-response angle

Listing 1 computes, for each pair of log rows, the angle from the commanded horizontal acceleration to the acceleration derived from the change in GNSS velocity, and reports its median over 20 s windows. With a correct heading, the median stays near zero. With a heading error it is offset, though not by exactly δ: drag and wind add an acceleration the controller did not command, so the angle is a biased indicator. Use it to see a trend and its sign; use the heading estimate, the field strength and the known heading of the vehicle (from a video, or the gyro-integrated heading in a real log) to size the error. R2

Battery evidence

The flight software's remaining percentage is computed, not measured: 100 × (1 − used/capacity setting). The log contains the inputs (used charge, setting, loaded voltage, current), so the analyst can recompute the real remaining charge against the pack actually flown. The loaded cell voltage gives an independent view: near the end of a pack it falls faster, and the internal resistance makes it fall further under high current (F10-11). Listing 1 also computes the charge a return would need from each position, with the formula of F10-35. R3

How the hardware actually does it

On a real flight controller, the logger is a task of the flight stack that subscribes to the topics or variables to be logged and writes them, with time stamps, to an SD card or flash memory, buffered to survive short write delays (F10-22 on PX4's uORB, F10-28 on ArduPilot's HAL). Logging can drop messages when the storage is slow or full, and a log can end abruptly on a power loss at impact: check for gaps and for the last complete record. The magnetometer may be on the flight controller or, more often for this reason, on the GNSS mast away from power wiring; the current is measured by the power module. The truth about the vehicle's heading after the fact comes from outside the log: a video, the orientation in which it was found, the observer's notes. D1 D3

Safety

After a flyaway or crash: first people, then the battery. A LiPo damaged in a crash can catch fire later; handle and store it per the maker's instructions and F10-11, away from people and flammable material. Recover the vehicle only where you are allowed to go. Then secure the log before power-cycling the flight controller again.

Worked example

Question. In Log A, at what time did the heading estimate pass the 59.2° limit, and how does that compare with the last waypoint reached? The course vehicle holds its heading fixed (the course model has no yaw rotation, dronesim.hpp line 125), and the observer's video confirms that Team B's vehicle pointed north throughout, so the true heading is 0° and the estimate is the error.

  1. From the evidence excerpt: yaw_est_deg is −30.3° at 10 s, −40.1° at 20 s, −50.6° at 30 s, −57.0° at 40 s, −64.3° at 50 s.
  2. The magnitude passes 59.2° between 40 s and 50 s (linear interpolation: about 43 s).
  3. Events: waypoint 8 at 37.69 s, then no more waypoints for the rest of the flight.
  4. So the vehicle stopped reaching waypoints a few seconds before the linear limit was crossed; near the limit the loop is already poorly damped (Listing 2: −0.25 1/s at 50°), so with a 1.0 m acceptance radius it could no longer settle onto a point.
  5. Check with the field strength: mag_norm 0.093 at 10 s with 19.1 A, against 1.000 on the ground: the field the compass sees is dominated by something other than the earth's field from the first seconds of flight.

The timeline agrees with the hypothesis "heading error growing past the stability limit". R1 R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Stains on a floor are physical traces of what really happened. A log is what the software believed; when the belief was wrong, the log is wrong in the same way, and the analyst must find inconsistencies between its sources.
    • 

  • Guests remember with their own eyes and can be asked again. A log cannot be asked again: if a signal was not logged, or logged too slowly, it is gone. Decide what to log before the flight.
    • 

  • The head waiter can find the slippery spot by walking on it. A log analyst can rarely repeat the flight; the simulator (F10-33) is the place to reproduce a hypothesis safely, as this chapter's generator does.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Reading only the end. Symptom: "it crashed because the battery failsafe triggered". The failsafe is in the log because something earlier went wrong; start at the first anomaly, not the last event.
    • 

  • One hypothesis. Symptom: the first idea is "confirmed" by any data. Fix: write at least two, each with a prediction the data could contradict.
    • 

  • Trusting computed values. Symptom: "the battery had 26 % left". The percentage was computed from a setting; recompute it against the pack that flew.
    • 

  • Ignoring the configuration header. Symptom: analysis assumes the parameters you remember. Fix: read the parameters and versions from the log.
    • 

  • Blame instead of causes. Symptom: "the pilot should have noticed". Fix: ask which defence should have caught the fault and why it did not; the actions go there.
    •

Log A: flyaway after compass interference


Context. Team B, Q2, firmware v1.5, configuration 91be; capacity 1,500 mAh, low 30 %, critical 15 %, no voltage trigger, fence 100 m. Pre-arm: field strength 1.000, voltage 16.59 V, RC valid: all checks passed on the ground.


Timeline. Armed 0.00 s; mission at 6.34 s; waypoints 1–8 reached by 37.69 s, none afterwards; battery low → RTL at 187.12 s; battery critical → LAND at 224.52 s; disarmed at 250.56 s, 177 m from home.


Observations. Field strength 1.000 on the ground, 0.070–0.430 in flight (mean 0.07–0.14 per window) with currents of 18–22 A. Heading estimate (and compass heading, which agrees with it) drifts steadily: −30° at 10 s, −57° at 40 s, −90° near 105 s, −143° at 250 s, while the vehicle pointed north. The median command-to-response angle grows from 17° to 71°. Distance from home at the end of each 20 s window during the mission: 2, 5, 18, 24, 22, 35, 33, 46, 69 m (widening loops), and 177 m at the end; in LAND the vehicle moves at about 11–12 m/s horizontally while descending.


Hypotheses. (1) Compass disturbed by current in flight: predicts a field-strength change with current, a heading drift, a rotated response; all seen. (2) GNSS fault: would show position jumps or a mismatch between position and integrated velocity, and would not change the field strength; not seen, the GNSS track is smooth. (3) Wind beyond the vehicle's ability: would push in one direction, not in growing circles, and would not change the compass; rejected. (4) Controller tuning in v1.5: would also show in SITL with v1.5 and does not explain the field strength; unlikely, though SITL with v1.5 should be checked.


Root cause. Magnetic interference from the new power wiring, proportional to motor current, dominated the compass reading in flight. The heading estimate followed the compass, its error passed the position loop's stability limit (59.2° for the course controller) at about 43 s, and the loop toilet-bowled, then flew away.


Contributing factors. No in-flight compass check with the new wiring (H5 argued nowhere, F10-36); the vehicle flew v1.5 on a readiness record for v1.4, with a three-day-old pre-arm check and one person as pilot and observer (F10-37); the fence could not act because a higher-rank failsafe was already active, and the breach was not logged; pre-arm checks cannot see current-dependent interference by design.


Fixes and prevention. Re-route power leads away from the compass, or move the compass to a mast; check compass behaviour against motor current on the bench with propellers off (some flight stacks offer a current-based compensation procedure: check yours, unverified here); add an in-flight monitor that compares field strength with the ground value and triggers a failsafe (land, not RTL, since navigation is the failing function); log every monitor's breaches; enforce identity and freshness at go/no-go.


Log B: battery failsafe too late


Context. Team C, Q3, firmware v1.4, configuration 5a10; capacity setting 2,000 mAh; low 30 %, critical 15 %, no voltage trigger; pack label 1,500 mAh.


Timeline. Ten waypoints by 241.06 s; battery low → RTL at 267.73 s about 60 m from home; impact at 283.12 s, 18 m from home.


Observations. Used charge at the failsafe about 1,400 mAh (30 % of 2,000 remaining by the software's count); at the end 1,484 mAh, close to the label. Minimum loaded voltage per cell falls from 3.42 V (240 s window) to 3.21 V (260 s window). In the last 6 s, the current drops from about 21.5 A to 12–13 A at 279.5 s while altitude falls from 15 m with vertical speed reaching −6.3 m/s: the vehicle could no longer produce hover thrust. The software still showed 25.8 % remaining at impact.


Hypotheses. (1) Capacity setting larger than the pack: predicts used charge near the label at collapse while the percentage still shows a large remainder; seen. (2) Faulty pack (the team's view): would show collapse well below the label capacity, or abnormal sag early in the flight; the voltage per cell is unremarkable until the end, and the collapse comes at 1,484 of 1,500 mAh, so the pack delivered its label capacity. (3) Higher current than usual: currents of 17–21 A are similar throughout; it does not explain the timing.


Root cause. The capacity setting (2,000 mAh) was left from the previous pack type; the battery-low threshold fired when the real pack had about 100 mAh (7 %) left, less than the return needed (about 229–304 mAh by the need column).


Contributing factors. No voltage-based backup trigger (F10-35, F5 shows it would have fired earlier); no capacity-against-label check at go/no-go after a pack change (F10-36, E4); the mission's farthest point (150 m) required a reserve beyond a 30 % threshold in wind (F10-35, worked example); the critical level was never reached in the software's count, so the landing failsafe never fired.


Fixes and prevention. Capacity setting per pack, checked against the label on the go/no-go list; voltage trigger with persistence as a backup; reserve computed from the farthest point and wind; an analyser check that compares used charge with the label; treat "change of pack type" as a configuration change that ages battery evidence.
