Story hook

The night before the party, the head waiter asks Leila one question: "Why should I believe nobody will be hurt tomorrow?" She says "I practised a lot". He shakes his head. "Tell me what could go wrong, what you did about each thing, and how you know it works. And tell me which house rules you checked, and where you read them." Leila takes a sheet of paper and writes: the floor near the kitchen is slippery, so she wears the new shoes and practised in them on Tuesday; the garden is out of bounds, and the rope is there, she saw it; children run in the hall, so she never walks through the middle. For each line she writes how she knows. For one line she has nothing to write. That empty line is the most useful thing on the sheet.

What you will be able to do

  • Find and record the rules that apply to your flight from your national aviation authority's current documents, with document, edition and date read, and never from memory, forums or videos.
  • Build a safety case for a supervised test flight: hazards, risk before and after mitigation, a claim tree argued down to evidence.
  • Check a safety case for mechanical gaps (undeveloped claims, failed or stale evidence, unargued hazards, unofficial sources) with a program, and know what the program cannot check.
  • Lead a safety-case review at MP7 gate R3 and decide, with the supervisor, whether the case is ready.
Jargon box
Safety case (flight operation)
Simple words: a written explanation of why a planned flight is safe enough, with proof for each point. Analogy: Leila's sheet for the head waiter. Precise: a structured argument, supported by evidence, that a specific operation (vehicle, firmware and configuration, site, procedure, people) is acceptably safe; it lists the hazards, the mitigations and the evidence that each mitigation works, and it is valid only for the configuration and conditions it names. SS402 treats safety cases in general (F11-18). D2 D3
Goal Structuring Notation (GSN)
Simple words: a way of drawing a safety argument as a tree of claims, ways of arguing, and proof. Analogy: each line on Leila's sheet split into smaller lines until each ends in "I know because…". Precise: a graphical notation for arguments with element types including goals (claims), strategies (how a goal is broken down), solutions (evidence), context and assumptions, and a marking for undeveloped goals, described in the GSN Community Standard; this course uses a small text format with goals, strategies and evidence only. D2
Risk matrix (severity × likelihood)
Simple words: a table that scores each danger by how bad it would be and how likely it is. Analogy: a spilled drink on the floor (minor, likely) against a tray falling on a child (severe, unlikely). Precise: a scheme that assigns each hazard a severity class and a likelihood class on ordinal scales and combines them (in this course as the product of two 1–5 scores) to compare risk before and after mitigation against an acceptance limit fixed in advance; the scores are judgements to be justified, not measurements, and the product of ordinal scores is a ranking aid, not a probability. D3
Evidence currency (configuration match)
Simple words: proof only counts if it was made with the same software and settings that will fly. Analogy: Leila practised in her old shoes, then bought new ones: the practice says little about the new shoes. Precise: the property that each item of evidence was produced with the firmware build and configuration identifier named by the safety case; a change of either invalidates evidence until the affected tests are repeated or an impact analysis argues that they still hold. R1
Regulatory question record
Simple words: a written question about the rules, with the answer and exactly where the answer was read. Analogy: "may I serve in the garden?" with "no, house rules page 2, read on Monday". Precise: an entry in the lab sheet that states one question about the rules for a flight, the answer, the official document it comes from (authority, title, edition), and the date read; answers without an official source are not accepted. D1 C1

Related terms from SS402 (taken in parallel): hazard, risk, harm, hazard log and hazard analysis are defined in F11-18 and F11-19; this chapter uses them in the same sense. From KID103: checklist.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Before a drone flies outside, two kinds of questions need answers. First: what do the rules of my country say, and where did I read them? Only the official rule book counts, read again before each flying season, because rules change. Second: what could go wrong, and why do we believe it will not? For each bad thing we write what we did about it and how we checked it. A program can then find the empty spaces: a claim with no proof, a test that failed, a test done with an older version of the software. People still have to decide whether the proof is good enough.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Reading the rules

Flights of unmanned aircraft are regulated in most countries; topics that such rules often cover include registration of the operator or the aircraft, training or a test for the pilot, where flight is allowed, how high, how far from people, buildings and airports, whether the pilot must see the aircraft, and insurance. This chapter gives no rule content at all, on purpose: the rules differ between countries, they change, and a number remembered from somewhere is exactly the kind of fact that is wrong in the one place that matters. Check your national aviation authority's current rules. D1 C1

The method is what the course teaches. Write the questions first (registration? site and height allowed? permission of the land owner? distance from people? line of sight? any zone restrictions near the site? what the supervisor needs?). Then find each answer in the authority's own current document, and record the document's title, edition or date, the section, and the date you read it. Forums, videos, a friend and your memory are pointers at best, never the answer (tier 6, AH rules of this university). Where the document is unclear, ask the authority or a qualified person, and record that answer too. The checker of this chapter refuses unofficial sources mechanically.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The list of topics above is a general description, not a statement of any country's rules, and none of it was checked against an authority's document in this build. Whether any of these topics applies to your flight, and how, is answered only by your national aviation authority's current documents.

Building the case

A safety case starts from a top claim about one operation ("the supervised hover test at the course field, with this vehicle, firmware and configuration, is acceptably safe"), names the strategy for arguing it (here: over each hazard, then over the procedure), and develops each sub-claim until it rests on evidence: test reports, analyses, checklists signed on the day. The hazards come from a hazard analysis (SS402's F11-19 teaches the methods); for each, the case records severity and likelihood before and after mitigation, and the team's acceptance limit, fixed before the scores were given. D2 D3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Leila's empty line was the most useful one; a safety case is a tool for finding empty lines before the flight, not a document for convincing someone after it.

What a program can check, and what it cannot

Listing 1 checks five mechanical properties: every node's parent exists; every goal has something below it; every evidence item passed; every evidence item was produced with the firmware and configuration the case names; every hazard is addressed by at least one claim and has a residual risk within the limit; every regulatory question has an answer, an official source and a date. These are the gaps that appear when a case is edited under time pressure. The program cannot judge whether the hazard list is complete, whether a test really demonstrates the claim above it, whether a likelihood score is honest, or whether an argument is convincing. That is the work of the review at gate R3, with the supervisor. R1

Configuration control

Evidence ages the moment the software or the configuration changes. Each test report carries the firmware build and a configuration identifier (in the course format, a short hash of the parameter set); the case names the same pair; any mismatch is a finding. After a change, either repeat the affected tests or write an impact analysis that argues which evidence still holds and why. Hardware changes (new wiring, a new battery type, a moved compass) are configuration changes too, and they can create new hazards (the forensic lab). R2

Using the risk scores honestly

Severity and likelihood scores are ordinal judgements; their product only ranks hazards. Three practices keep the scores useful: fix the acceptance limit and the scale definitions before scoring; justify each "after" likelihood by naming the mitigation and its evidence; and let the review challenge any score that moved more than one step. A mitigation that only lowers likelihood (a failsafe) leaves severity unchanged: if the failsafe fails, the harm is the same. D3

How the hardware actually does it

The safety case for a flight is about hardware in the end: the vehicle that flies is the evidence's subject. Three hardware facts must appear in it explicitly. The configuration identifier must be read from the vehicle on the day (from the ground-control program or the flight log), not from the laptop's copy. The battery that flies must be one whose capacity matches the failsafe configuration (F10-35, case F5). And any change to wiring or mounting near the compass, GNSS antenna or receiver is a change to the evidence base, because electromagnetic interference depends on the physical layout (F10-38). R2

Not verified — check before relying on this

How your flight stack exposes a firmware version string and a parameter-set identifier, and whether its logs record them, is not stated here: check its documentation for your release. The course format's "config" field is this course's convention.

Worked example

Question. The course team's case scores H2 (battery exhausted before landing) at severity 4, likelihood 3 before and 1 after mitigation, with a team limit of 6. Is the "after" score justified, and what would change it?

  1. Before: 4 × 3 = 12, above the limit: mitigation is required.
  2. Mitigations named in the case: battery failsafes tested in SITL (F4, F5 of F10-35, evidence E3), capacity setting checked against the pack label (E4).
  3. Does the evidence support likelihood 1? F4 and F5 show landing with 15.8 % and 17.4 % real charge left on the course mission. But the worked example of F10-35 showed that a 30 % threshold covers a return from 150 m only below a certain wind. The hover test stays near the pilot, so the return distance is small: the claim holds for this operation and should say so ("for operations within 50 m of home").
  4. After: 4 × 1 = 4 ≤ 6. Accepted, with the scope stated.
  5. What would change it: a longer mission, a different pack, or a firmware change; each requires E3 and E4 to be repeated.

The program checks the arithmetic and the evidence status; the reasoning in step 3 is the human part. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The head waiter knows the hall and can judge Leila's sheet from experience. A supervisor reviewing a drone safety case may not know the firmware; the case must make its evidence checkable by someone who was not there.
    • 

  • House rules are written by the head waiter and can be asked about across the room. Aviation rules are written by an authority, in legal language, and change by publication; the answer must come from the current document.
    • 

  • Leila's sheet is for one party. A safety case is for one configuration: the same team, vehicle and site with a new firmware needs the case reviewed again.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Stale evidence. Symptom: the case cites a bench test from before a firmware update. Find it: the checker's currency rule. Fix: repeat, or write an impact analysis.
    • 

  • Hazard found, then forgotten. Symptom: a hazard in the list with no claim. Fix: argue it or remove the operation that creates it; never delete the hazard to make the checker quiet.
    • 

  • Rules from memory. Symptom: "we can fly here, everyone does". Fix: a regulatory question record with an official source and a date.
    • 

  • Scores tuned to pass. Symptom: every "after" likelihood is 1. Fix: justify each with evidence; let the review challenge.
    • 

  • Evidence that does not support the claim. Symptom: "SITL matrix passed" under "the vehicle cannot leave the area", while the fence relies on a compass that was never checked with the new wiring. The checker cannot see this; the review must.
    •
