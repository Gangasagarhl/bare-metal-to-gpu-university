Story hook

Before the party, the head waiter gives Leila three rules. "If you lose sight of me and can no longer hear my signals, go back to the meeting point by the kitchen door. If your tray is getting too heavy for your arms, start back to the kitchen now, while you can still make it. And never walk past the red rope into the garden."

The rules sound obvious, but each hides a question. How long must she not hear the signals before she gives up waiting? How heavy is "too heavy", and how far from the kitchen is she when she decides? What if she is half-way back and her arms start to shake: does she keep walking, or put the tray down where she is? And what if the head waiter's signal is not gone, but frozen: the same hand sign held up, over and over, because he has fallen asleep?

A drone's failsafes are those rules. This chapter configures them in the course simulator, tests each one by injecting the fault on purpose, and looks at the case where the rule existed but never fired.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the three classic failsafes (RC loss, low battery, geofence): what each monitors, what it triggers, and what can go wrong with each.
  • Configure failsafe actions and thresholds in the course flight software and justify each choice for a given site and mission.
  • Test failsafes by fault injection in SITL, with a written expectation (which mode, within which deadline, how the flight ends) and an automatic verdict.
  • Compute the charge a return home needs from distance, wind and current, and show when a fixed percentage threshold is not enough.
  • Diagnose a failsafe that did not fire, from a test log, and name the configuration error that hid the fault.
Jargon box
Failsafe action
Simple words: what the drone does on its own when something important goes wrong. Analogy: "go back to the meeting point". Precise: the flight mode or behaviour (hold position, return to launch, land where it is, continue, or terminate) that the flight software switches to when a failsafe monitor detects its condition; flight stacks let the operator choose the action per failsafe, within limits. D1 D2
Failsafe escalation
Simple words: when several problems happen, the drone only moves to safer and safer actions, never back. Analogy: once Leila has put the tray down, she does not pick it up again because the music started. Precise: an ordering of failsafe actions (in this course: continue < hold < return < land) with the rule that a new failsafe may switch to an action of higher rank but never to a lower one, so that a second fault cannot undo the response to a more serious first one. R1
Battery failsafe (low and critical)
Simple words: rules that start the way home when the battery gets low, and land at once when it gets very low. Analogy: starting back to the kitchen while your arms still can, and putting the tray down if they start to shake. Precise: monitors on the estimated remaining charge (integrated current against a configured capacity) and/or on the loaded cell voltage, each with a threshold, a persistence time and an action; usually a "low" level that returns and a "critical" level that lands. D1 R1
Geofence
Simple words: an invisible boundary the drone is not allowed to cross. Analogy: the red rope in front of the garden. Precise: a volume defined in the flight software (in this course a maximum horizontal distance from home and a maximum altitude; in flight stacks also polygons and inclusion and exclusion zones) whose breach, by the estimated position, triggers a configured action. A geofence acts on the estimate: a wrong position estimate makes the fence wrong too. D1 D2
Fault injection (failsafe test)
Simple words: breaking something on purpose in a test to see whether the safety rule reacts. Analogy: the head waiter hiding on purpose during the rehearsal, to see whether Leila goes back to the meeting point. Precise: a test that introduces a specified fault (signal loss, a battery state, a position) at a specified time and checks the system's response against a written expectation: which action, within which deadline, and which end state. R1
Receiver output on signal loss
Simple words: what the radio receiver tells the flight controller when it stops hearing the transmitter. Analogy: the frozen hand sign of a head waiter who fell asleep. Precise: the receiver's configured behaviour when the RC link is lost: stop sending output, send a flagged failsafe state, or keep sending values (the last ones or preset ones); if the receiver keeps sending ordinary-looking values, the flight controller may not detect the loss at all. R2

Related terms from earlier courses: failsafe (RC loss), RC transmitter and receiver, telemetry radio, LiPo battery, internal resistance and voltage sag, fault and failure.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A drone carries rules for bad moments. If it stops hearing the pilot's controller for a second, it flies home on its own. If its battery gets low, it flies home while it still has enough power; if the battery gets very low, it lands right where it is. If it tries to fly past an invisible fence, it turns back. We test every rule by causing the bad moment on purpose, in a pretend world first, and checking that the drone did the right thing quickly enough. And we check that the rule can see the problem at all: a rule that never notices the problem never helps.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The head waiter's three rules each have a condition, a waiting time and an action. So does every failsafe.

RC loss

The flight controller watches the RC input. If no valid input arrives for longer than a timeout (1.0 s in the course software; a real flight stack has its own setting, look it up), it declares RC loss and switches to the configured action. Choosing the action is a site question: return to launch (RTL) is the common choice when the take-off point is clear and the path home is clear; land is chosen where flying home would cross people or obstacles; hold is chosen when the pilot is expected to regain the link quickly. "Continue" lets an autonomous mission finish; it is only acceptable when the mission itself ends safely and the rules for your flight allow flying out of radio contact (check your national aviation authority's current rules). The timeout is a trade: too short and brief dropouts trigger needless returns; too long and the vehicle flies blind for that long. D1 D2

Low battery

The flight software estimates the remaining charge by adding up the measured current over time and comparing it with the capacity it was told. This is only as good as the capacity setting and the current sensor's calibration. The loaded cell voltage is a second, independent indication, but it sags under load (F10-11), so a voltage threshold needs a persistence time to avoid triggering on a brief climb. Two levels are common: "low" starts the return, "critical" lands where the vehicle is. The low threshold must leave enough charge to return from the farthest point of the mission against the wind: a fixed percentage does that only for missions that stay close. D1 R3

Geofence

The fence is a boundary in the flight software. When the estimated position crosses it, the configured action runs. Two things follow. The vehicle overshoots: it is moving when the breach is detected, so the fence must be inside the real limit by at least the stopping distance plus the position error. And the fence trusts the estimate: if the position estimate is wrong (F10-38 shows one way), the fence is wrong by the same amount. A fence is a second line of defence, never the plan for staying inside an area. D1 R1

Several faults at once

Faults come together: the RC link drops far out, then the battery runs low on the way home. The course software ranks the actions and only allows escalation, so a later "battery low → RTL" can never pull a vehicle that is already landing back into the air. Read how your flight stack orders its failsafes and what it does when a second one fires during the first. R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

One paragraph of analogy, then the mechanism: the head waiter's rules are only as good as Leila's ability to notice that the condition has happened. Every failsafe is a monitor (a detector with a false-alarm rate and a missed-detection rate), a decision rule (threshold and persistence), and an action that must itself be safe in the place where it runs.

Detection: what the monitor can actually see

The RC monitor does not see the radio link; it sees the receiver's output. If the receiver is set to keep sending ordinary channel values after it loses the transmitter, the flight controller receives valid-looking input and declares nothing (the forensic lab). Receivers and flight stacks offer several ways for the receiver to signal loss; the combination must be configured and then tested by switching the transmitter off, with propellers removed, and watching the flight controller's RC status. The battery monitor sees a current sensor reading and a capacity setting; a capacity set too high moves every percentage threshold later (case F5). The geofence sees the position estimate. For each failsafe, write down which signal it uses and which faults would make that signal lie. R1 R2

Not verified — check before relying on this

The receiver behaviours named here (no output, a flagged failsafe state, last or preset values) are described from general knowledge of RC systems, not from a document opened in this build. Read your receiver's manual and your flight stack's RC set-up page, and verify on the bench which behaviour your combination has.

Timing: persistence and latency

Every monitor needs a persistence (how long the condition must hold) to reject noise. In the course software, RC loss needs more than 1.0 s without valid input, and the voltage monitor needs 2 s below its threshold. The total reaction time is persistence plus the loop period plus the time the action needs to take effect. The tests measure the first part: case F1 switches to RTL 0.99 s after the transmitter goes off (within one 10 ms step of the 1.0 s timeout; F7 reports 1.01 s, because which step first exceeds the timeout depends on how the accumulated time stamps compare). Write a deadline into every test (here 1.05 s) so that a later code change that delays the reaction fails the test. R1

The battery reserve, computed

The charge for a return is the current times the time of each phase: climb to the return altitude, fly home at ground speed (airspeed minus headwind), descend and land, plus a margin for wind estimates, ageing and cold. For the course vehicle (17.5 A at cruise, 5 m/s, return altitude 15 m, landing at 0.7 m/s, margin 30 %), a 1,500 mAh pack with a 30 % trigger has 450 mAh left at the trigger; that covers a return from 236 m in still air but only 95 m against a 3 m/s headwind. Beyond that the fixed threshold is too late, and the critical level lands the vehicle away from home (case F7: 58.9 m from home). Some flight stacks can estimate the return charge in flight; whatever yours does, check the result with your own measured currents. R3 R1

Actions: safe where they run

An action is safe only for the place and the state the vehicle is in. RTL climbs to a return altitude: is that altitude above the trees and below any height limit that applies (check your national aviation authority's current rules)? Land descends where the vehicle is: is that place over water, a road, people? Hold waits: for how long, and what then? The configuration therefore belongs to the site assessment and the go/no-go checklist of F10-37, and the safety case of F10-36 argues why the chosen actions reduce the risk. C1

How the hardware actually does it

RC loss is detected in the receiver first: it stops decoding valid packets from the transmitter, and then does what it is configured to do on its output (a serial protocol to the flight controller in most current set-ups, F10-12). The flight controller's RC driver turns that into a "valid / not valid" state. The battery monitor reads a power module: a voltage divider and a current sensor (a shunt resistor with an amplifier, or a Hall-effect sensor) connected to ADC inputs or a digital bus; the flight software integrates the current. The geofence runs on the position estimate of the estimator (F10-15). Each step is a place where a wrong setting or a wrong calibration hides a fault. D1 D3

Worked example

Question. MP7's survey mission reaches 150 m from home. The course vehicle uses a 1,500 mAh pack and draws 17.5 A at cruise. The forecast says up to 3 m/s of wind. Is a 30 % battery-low threshold enough? If not, what threshold would be?

  1. Return time, worst case. Climb (15 − 10) m at 2 m/s = 2.5 s; home 150 m at 5 − 3 = 2 m/s = 75 s; descend 15 m at 0.7 m/s = 21.4 s. Total 98.9 s.
  2. Charge. 98.9 s × 17.5 A = 1,731 As = 1,731 / 3.6 mAh = 481 mAh; with 30 % margin, 625 mAh.
  3. Available at a 30 % trigger. 0.30 × 1,500 = 450 mAh. Not enough: 625 > 450.
  4. Threshold needed. 625 / 1,500 = 41.7 %: set at least 42 %, or shorten the mission, or fly it only when the wind is below a stated limit. Listing 3 gives 95 m as the break-even distance in a 3 m/s headwind, consistent with this.
  5. Check the critical level. It must still land the vehicle safely with a sagging pack: test it in SITL (case F7) and keep the landing area under the mission free of people.

The same arithmetic, with your measured currents, goes into the safety case (F10-36) as evidence for the battery hazard. R3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Leila can judge "too heavy" by feeling her arms. The flight software cannot feel the battery; it computes a number from a sensor and a setting, and a wrong setting makes the number wrong without any feeling of doubt.
    • 

  • Leila knows where the meeting point is and walks around people. A vehicle on RTL flies a straight line at a configured altitude unless the flight stack plans otherwise; obstacles on the way are your problem, not the rule's.
    • 

  • The red rope is real: Leila sees it. The geofence exists only in the software, measured against an estimate.
    • 

  • Leila notices that a frozen hand sign is strange. A flight controller receiving valid-looking frozen channel values has no reason to suspect anything unless something else, such as a link-quality value, is monitored.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Receiver keeps sending values on loss. Symptom: switching the transmitter off on the bench changes nothing in the flight controller's mode. Cause: receiver output on signal loss is set to hold or preset values. Find it: watch RC status and channel values while switching the transmitter off. Fix: set the receiver and flight stack so that loss is signalled; repeat the bench test.
    • 

  • Capacity setting does not match the pack. Symptom: battery low arrives late, the critical level arrives near the field edge, or never. Cause: capacity set for a larger pack (F5). Find it: compare charge used in a log with the charge put back by the charger. Fix: correct the setting per pack; add a voltage threshold as a backup.
    • 

  • Fixed percentage for a long mission. Symptom: return starts but the critical level lands the vehicle away from home. Cause: threshold below the return charge at the farthest point. Fix: compute it (worked example), shorten the mission, or set a wind limit.
    • 

  • Fence at the real limit. Symptom: the vehicle crosses the real limit by metres before turning (F6: 2.5 m over a 50 m fence). Fix: place the fence inside by stopping distance plus position error.
    • 

  • Failsafes never tested together. Symptom: a second failsafe during the first produces an unexpected mode. Find it: two-fault cases such as F7. Fix: know and test your flight stack's priority rules.

Summary


    

  • A failsafe is a monitor, a decision rule (threshold and persistence) and an action that must be safe where it runs.
    • 

  • RC loss: the monitor sees the receiver's output, not the radio link; a receiver that keeps sending values hides the loss.
    • 

  • Battery: charge estimates depend on the capacity setting; voltage is an independent backup with persistence; the low threshold must cover the return from the farthest point against the wind.
    • 

  • Geofence: acts on the estimated position and overshoots; place it inside the real limit.
    • 

  • Failsafes escalate only; test double faults.
    • 

  • Every failsafe is tested by injection with a written expectation, first in SITL, then HITL, then on the bench with propellers off.
