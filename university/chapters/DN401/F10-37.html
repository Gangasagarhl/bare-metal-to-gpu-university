Story hook

At the kitchen door the head waiter stops every waiter for three seconds. Tray level? Glasses not too full? Path clear? Who is watching the hall? Leila once asked why he does this every single time, when everybody knows how to carry a tray. "Because the day somebody forgets, it is never the thing they forgot on purpose," he said. "And because yesterday's check was for yesterday's tray."

A field test is that kitchen door. Everything earlier in this course (simulation, failsafes, the safety case) is preparation; the procedure and its checklist make sure that the preparation is still true at the moment the vehicle is armed, on this field, today.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a field-test procedure with named roles, phases, abort criteria and a post-flight routine.
  • Design a go/no-go checklist whose items are checkable, signed, time-stamped and graded as stop items or notes.
  • Run a readiness review that checks freshness of each item, the vehicle's actual firmware and configuration, roles and sign-off, and decide GO or NO-GO.
  • Lead, under supervision and only where the rules and the R3 sign-off allow, a supervised outdoor hover test following the procedure.
Jargon box
Field-test procedure
Simple words: the written plan for a test flight: who does what, in what order, and when to stop. Analogy: the head waiter's plan for the evening, from the kitchen door to clearing the tables. Precise: a document that defines the test's objective, the roles and their authority, the phases (preparation, set-up, pre-flight, flight cards, post-flight), the abort criteria and the abort actions, and the records to be produced; it implements the procedure claims of the safety case. C1 D2
Go/no-go checklist
Simple words: the list of things that must all be true before the drone may fly, each signed. Analogy: the three-second look at the kitchen door. Precise: a list of checkable items, each with a category that sets how fresh the check must be, a status, a time, the name of the person who checked it, and a grade (stop item or note); the decision is GO only if every stop item is satisfied and fresh, the vehicle matches the record, the roles are filled and the supervisor has signed. D1 R1
Check freshness
Simple words: how long a check stays valid before it must be done again. Analogy: yesterday's check was for yesterday's tray. Precise: the maximum age of a check at the start of the test, set per category (in this course: documents a year, day items 12 h, field items 60 min); a check older than its limit counts as not done. R1
Abort criterion
Simple words: a condition, agreed before the flight, that ends it at once. Analogy: "if a child runs into your path, stop and hold the tray high", agreed before the party. Precise: an observable condition (a person entering the exclusion zone, loss of visual contact, unexpected behaviour, a failsafe, a limit exceeded) with a defined call, a defined action (land, return, or cut motors under the conditions the procedure allows), and the person entitled to call it, normally anyone on the team. C1
Flight card
Simple words: one short card per flight that says exactly what this flight will do. Analogy: "this trip: three glasses to table 4, then back". Precise: a one-page plan for a single flight: objective, manoeuvres in order, mode for each, height and area, expected duration and battery, abort criteria specific to this flight, and the data to record; flown as written, changed only on the ground. C1

Related terms: checklist (KID103), safety case (flight operation) and evidence currency (F10-36), failsafe action (F10-35).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Before a test flight, the team uses a list. Each line is something that must be true: the rules were read, the safety rules work on the bench, the battery is charged, nobody is standing near, the drone has the right software. Each line has the name of the person who checked it and the time. Old checks do not count: some must be done the same hour. If any important line is not done, the answer is "no flight today", and that is a good answer. Everyone on the team may shout "stop" during a flight.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Roles

The course procedure has three roles, held by three different people: the pilot flies and holds the transmitter; the observer watches the vehicle and the surroundings, keeps the pilot informed, and calls aborts; the supervisor owns the go/no-go decision and the authority to stop the session. Pilot and observer must be different people: one person cannot watch the screen, the vehicle and the field at once. The rules that apply to your flight may define further roles or qualifications; check your national aviation authority's current rules. C1 D3

Writing items that can be checked

A good checklist item is observable and has one answer: "pre-arm checks clear (compass, GNSS, RC, battery)" can be read off the ground-control screen; "vehicle OK" cannot. Each item names the evidence where it exists ("failsafe matrix passed on firmware v1.4"), so that the checklist and the safety case stay linked. Items are graded: a stop item must be satisfied for GO; a note is recorded but does not stop the test (in the course record, a missing spare battery limits the session to one flight; it does not make the first flight unsafe). Keep the list short enough to be read aloud at the field: a checklist nobody reads checks nothing. "The Checklist Manifesto" describes how checklists in other fields were designed to be short and used at defined pause points. D2 R1

Freshness and identity

Two checks matter more than any single item. Freshness: each item records when it was checked, and each category has a maximum age at the start time; the review program rejects anything older. Identity: the vehicle's firmware and configuration, read from the vehicle on the field, must equal those the record and the safety case were prepared for (F10-36, evidence currency). The forensic lab shows a record that failed both, and the flight that followed it. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The head waiter's three seconds work because they happen at the same door every time; a procedure is only as good as the moments at which it forces a look.

Designing the procedure

Start from the safety case: every procedure claim (exclusion zone, roles, signed record) becomes a step and a checklist item; every failsafe's evidence becomes a "day" item tied to the flying build. Then add the field: wind and weather limits from the vehicle maker's documents and your own test plan (the readiness record carries each limit with its source, and the review program contains no limit of its own), the exclusion zone, the take-off and landing area, emergency contact information, and the location of a fire extinguisher or sand for battery fires per your supervisor's rules. Write abort criteria as observable conditions with a call ("abort, abort") and a pre-agreed action. Write a flight card per flight; change it only on the ground. C1

The readiness review as code

Listing 1 turns the review into a program so that the mechanical part is never skipped: it reads the record, computes each item's age at the start time, applies the freshness limits, checks signatures, compares the vehicle's reported firmware and configuration with the record's, checks the three roles and their separation, checks every limit against its measured value and its source, and requires the supervisor's signature. Its output is GO or NO-GO with every reason. The program cannot see whether the zone is really clear or whether a signature was given after a real check; the supervisor still looks. R1

During and after the flight

During the flight the pilot flies the card; the observer calls what they see; anyone may call an abort. After landing: disarm, confirm disarmed, approach only then; disconnect the battery and check its temperature and voltage per the maker's instructions; download and back up the flight log before the next flight (F10-38 analyses it); record anything unexpected, however small, in the debrief. A blameless debrief asks what in the procedure allowed a problem, not who caused it. C1

How the hardware actually does it

Several checklist items are read from the hardware itself, and the procedure should say how. The firmware version and configuration come from the flight controller through the ground-control program (or from the log header after a flight). Pre-arm checks are performed by the flight software on the ground: in the course software they check RC, compass field strength and battery voltage (dronesim.hpp lines 293–302 of F10-33); real flight stacks check more and report the reasons. Pre-arm checks run with motors stopped: a compass that is fine on the ground can be disturbed by motor current in flight, which no ground check sees (F10-38, Log A). Battery voltage under no load says little about capacity under load (F10-11). D4

Not verified — check before relying on this

The pre-arm checks of PX4 and ArduPilot, how they are reported, and which of them can be disabled are not described here: their documentation could not be opened in this build. Read the pre-arm and arming-check pages of your flight stack for your release and list the checks in your procedure.

Safety

Outdoor flight only after the R3 sign-off, only where the rules allow it (check your national aviation authority's current rules), only with the land owner's permission, and only supervised. Propellers go on last, after the bench and pre-arm checks, and nobody stands inside the exclusion zone when the vehicle is armed. LiPo batteries are charged, transported and stored per the makers' instructions and F10-11; a swollen or damaged pack does not fly. Learners below L3 do not take part in outdoor flights except as briefed spectators outside the zone.

Worked example

Question. The course team's record (Listing 2) starts at 09:30. Their bench test Y2 was signed at 22:30 the evening before. Is it fresh? What if the start slips to 11:00 because of wind?

  1. Day items have a 12 h (720 min) limit.
  2. At 09:30 the age of Y2 is 11 h = 660 min ≤ 720: fresh (the output shows 660 m).
  3. At 11:00 it is 12 h 30 min = 750 min > 720: stale. Y1 (22:00) would be 780 min: stale too.
  4. Also the field items (P1–P5, checked 09:05–09:25) would be 95 to 115 min old, above 60: stale.
  5. Decision at 11:00: NO-GO until Y1, Y2 and P1–P5 are repeated. Y1 is a SITL run on the laptop and Y2 a props-off bench test; both can be redone at the field if the equipment is there. Plan for it: the procedure should say where the bench test is done on the day.

Change the start time in a copy of gonogo.in to 2026-10-12T11:00 and run the program to check this answer. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The head waiter sees the tray with his own eyes. A team cannot see firmware or configuration; it must read them from the vehicle, and the review must compare them with the record.
    • 

  • A spilled drink is cleaned up. A field-test mistake can hurt someone outside the team; that is why the decision belongs to a supervisor and the rules of an authority apply.
    • 

  • The kitchen door is always in the same place. Fields, weather and people change; the checklist must cover what is new today, not only what was there last time.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Ticking from memory. Symptom: items checked days ago marked ok today. Fix: time stamps and freshness limits; the program rejects them.
    • 

  • One person, two roles. Symptom: the pilot is also the observer "because the others were late". Fix: no observer, no flight.
    • 

  • The vehicle on the field is not the vehicle in the record. Symptom: firmware updated "only a little" the evening before. Fix: read the identity on the field; mismatch is NO-GO.
    • 

  • Abort criteria not agreed. Symptom: hesitation during an incident. Fix: write them on the flight card; brief them; anyone may call.
    • 

  • Logs not saved. Symptom: after an incident there is no evidence. Fix: download and back up after each flight, before the next battery.
    •

  • A field test follows a written procedure: roles (pilot, observer, supervisor, all different people), phases, abort criteria, post-flight routine.

  • Freshness and identity matter most: old checks count as not done; the vehicle must report the build the record was prepared for.
    •
