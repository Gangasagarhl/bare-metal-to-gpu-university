Story hook

Leila will carry a tray of drinks across a crowded hall at her cousin's party. The day before, she rehearses in the empty hall with plastic cups full of water. Her friend Tomás walks beside her and narrates: "now a child runs past on your left", "now someone bumps your elbow", "now the floor is wet". After each sentence he waits until Leila has reacted, then he describes the next moment. They rehearse the same walk ten times, with the same bumps in the same order, until Leila never spills.

Then Tomás tries something different. He talks at his own speed, without waiting. Sometimes Leila is still correcting the last bump when the next one arrives. The rehearsal is different every time, and on some walks she spills. Was it her technique, or Tomás's timing?

Flight software is rehearsed the same way. The real code flies a pretend drone in a pretend world on a desktop computer, a thousand times, before anyone touches a battery. This chapter shows how to build that rehearsal, how to make it repeatable, and how to tell a real bug from a rehearsal artefact.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what software in the loop (SITL) runs for real and what it replaces with models, and draw the loop between the flight software and the simulator.
  • Run a lockstep SITL mission with written acceptance tests and show that a lockstep run is bit-for-bit repeatable.
  • Compute an error budget for an acceptance test from the controller and the simulated disturbances, and choose a tolerance that is not inside the budget.
  • Design a SITL test campaign (scenarios × disturbances × seeds) and decide what SITL can and cannot prove before HITL and flight.
  • Diagnose a flaky SITL test in continuous integration from its run history.
Jargon box
Software in the loop (SITL)
Simple words: the real flight program flies a pretend drone inside a computer. Analogy: Leila rehearsing her real walk in an empty hall while Tomás narrates the bumps. Precise: a test arrangement in which the flight software, compiled for a desktop or server computer, runs in a closed loop with a simulator that models the vehicle, its sensors, its actuators and its environment; the flight software's hardware drivers are replaced by an interface to the simulator. PX4 and ArduPilot both document SITL for their flight stacks. D1 D2
Simulator (world model)
Simple words: the program that pretends to be the drone, the air, the sensors and the radio. Analogy: Tomás and everything he describes. Precise: software that integrates a model of the vehicle's dynamics and produces synthetic sensor data (with modelled noise and faults) from the true simulated state, and that applies the flight software's actuator commands to the model; its fidelity is whatever its authors modelled, nothing more. D3 R1
Lockstep simulation
Simple words: the pretend world waits for the flight program's answer before it moves on. Analogy: Tomás waits until Leila has reacted before narrating the next moment. Precise: a scheme in which simulated time advances by one step only after the flight software has consumed that step's sensor data and returned its commands, so that the result does not depend on how fast either program runs on the host; PX4's documentation describes a lockstep mode of its simulation. D1 R1
Free-running simulation
Simple words: the pretend world moves on with the clock, whether or not the flight program has answered. Analogy: Tomás talking at his own speed. Precise: a simulation whose time follows the host's wall clock (or a fixed multiple of it); when the host is busy, the flight software misses steps and the simulated vehicle flies on stale commands, so results depend on host load. R2
Acceptance test (SITL)
Simple words: a written pass/fail rule checked automatically after a simulated flight. Analogy: "no water spilled and the tray reached the table". Precise: a predicate over the simulated flight's record (events, trajectory, energy) with a stated tolerance, for example "landing point within 1.5 m of home"; the tolerance must exceed the error the design itself allows, or the test fails without a defect. R1
Flaky test
Simple words: a test that sometimes passes and sometimes fails with no change to the code. Analogy: the rehearsal that spills on some walks because of Tomás's timing, not Leila's hands. Precise: a test whose verdict depends on uncontrolled inputs (host timing, unseeded randomness, test order, wall-clock reads) or whose tolerance sits inside the system's normal variation; it destroys trust in the suite and must be fixed, not re-run. R3
Error budget (test tolerance)
Simple words: how far off a correct system can normally be, added up from every allowed source of error, so a test knows what "too far" means. Analogy: how far from the table Leila may stop and still be on target, given how wide the table is and how much the floor wobbles. Precise: the sum (or a stated combination) of the deviations a correct design is allowed to produce for a metric (for a landing point: acceptance radius, estimator noise, wind drift during descent); a test tolerance must lie outside this budget with a stated margin, or the test fails on correct code. R1 R3

Related terms defined in earlier courses: deterministic simulation, sim-to-real gap.

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the loop. On the right is the flight software you will later put on a flight controller: estimator, controller, modes and failsafes. On the left is the simulator: the vehicle's motion, the battery, the sensors with their noise, the radio. The two meet at one narrow interface: sensor data in, actuator commands out. In SITL both sides run on the same desktop computer.

Figure 2 shows why the step clock matters. In lockstep, every step has an answer. Free-running, the world keeps moving while the flight software waits for the processor, and the vehicle flies on an old command.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Before you carry real drinks, you practise with plastic cups. Before a drone flies, its program practises in a pretend world inside a computer. The pretend world tells the program what its sensors would feel. The program answers with what the motors should do. The pretend world moves the pretend drone a tiny bit and asks again. If the pretend world always waits for the answer, every practice run is exactly the same, so you can compare them and find mistakes. The pretend world only knows what someone taught it, though. A real hall has surprises that the rehearsal never had.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Rehearsing with plastic cups is cheap, safe and repeatable; that is why you do it a hundred times. SITL has the same three properties, and the engineering below is about keeping all three.

What is real and what is modelled

In SITL the flight software is the real code: the same estimator, controllers, mode logic and failsafes that will run on the flight controller, compiled for the desktop. What is replaced are the parts that touch hardware: the sensor drivers, the motor outputs and the radio. Both PX4 and ArduPilot document this arrangement, and both run their flight stacks against a simulator on a desktop computer. D1 D2

This course uses its own small simulator so that every line can be read and every run is recorded. dronesim.hpp (Listing 1) contains both halves of Figure 1. The World is a point mass with a first-order lag standing in for the attitude loops of F10-16, linear drag, wind, a battery whose voltage sags under load, GNSS with noise, a compass that can be disturbed by motor current, and an RC link. The Fsw (flight software) has a heading estimator, a position-velocity controller, five modes and three failsafe monitors. It is far simpler than PX4 or ArduPilot, on purpose: the habits you build with it (interfaces, determinism, acceptance tests, test matrices) carry over unchanged. R1 D3

Watch out

Every number inside dronesim.hpp (gains, drag, battery curve, currents, noise levels) is an invented teaching value. None of them describes a real vehicle, battery or sensor, and none should be copied into a real configuration.

The loop, one step at a time

Each step of 10 ms of simulated time does three things, in this order (Listing 2, lines 34–36): the world produces one sample of sensor data from its true state; the flight software consumes it and returns one command; the world applies the command and integrates its state forward by one step. That is the whole of SITL. Everything else is bookkeeping: logging, tests, fault injection.

Why lockstep

If the world waits for each answer (lockstep), the simulated flight depends only on the code, the inputs and the random seeds. Run it twice and you get the same flight, bit for bit; Listing 2 checks exactly that as its test A6. If instead the world follows the wall clock, a busy host makes the flight software miss steps, and the simulated drone flies on stale commands for a while. The flight then depends on whatever else the computer was doing. PX4's documentation describes a lockstep mode for its simulation for this reason; the details of which simulators support it and how it is switched on are release-specific. D1 R2

Acceptance tests

A SITL run is only useful if a program decides whether it passed. Listing 2 states six tests in its output: all waypoints reached, maximum altitude, landed and disarmed, landing point within 1.5 m of home, no crash, and repeatability. Each has a number, and each number must come from the design, not from a hope. The worked example below computes where the 1.5 m comes from. R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

One paragraph of analogy first: a rehearsal is only as good as its narrator. Tomás can describe bumps, but not the weight of real glass or the smell of the food that distracts you. A SITL campaign is designed around that limit: you use it to prove the logic exhaustively and to find which real-world tests are still needed.

Where to cut: the interface

The cut between flight software and simulator should sit where the hardware drivers sit on the target, so that the code above the cut is the code that will fly. Cut higher (feeding the estimator's output directly) and the estimator is not tested; cut lower (simulating register reads of a sensor chip) and the simulator becomes a hardware emulator, which is the job of HITL (F10-34) and of bench tests. In dronesim.hpp the cut is the pair Sensors / Command (lines 105–121). In PX4 the corresponding boundary is between the simulator interface and the flight stack's sensor topics; in ArduPilot it is the simulated hardware layer. Read their architecture sections for your release before you write a module that depends on it. D1 D2

What the model leaves out, and what that means

Write down what your simulator does not model, because those are the things SITL cannot test. For the course simulator: no attitude dynamics (a lag stands in for them, F10-16 and the DN201 simulator have the full model), no vibration, no motor or ESC dynamics, no propeller wash near the ground, no magnetic disturbances except a current-proportional one you switch on, GNSS without multipath or outages, a battery curve that belongs to no real cell, constant wind, and a perfect timing model of the flight controller. Each omission is a line in the safety case of F10-36 ("hazard H, not covered by SITL, covered by bench test B or by flight test procedure P"). D3 C1

Sources of non-determinism

Lockstep removes one source of variation. Others remain, and a test campaign must control each:

  • Random numbers. Every noise source takes an explicit seed. dronesim.hpp uses its own generator (lines 66–83) rather than a library distribution, because the C++ standard specifies the output of the engines but not of distributions such as normal distributions, so a different standard library could produce different noise from the same seed. D4
  • Threads. If the flight software runs several threads (it does in PX4 and ArduPilot), their interleaving can change results unless the simulation scheduler controls it.
  • Wall-clock reads. Any code that reads the host clock instead of simulated time breaks repeatability; search for them.
  • Floating point. The same source compiled with different flags or on a different architecture can round differently. Repeatability is promised for one build on one machine type; across machines, compare with tolerances.

A test campaign, not a test

A single passing flight proves little. A campaign is a matrix: scenarios (missions and manoeuvres) × disturbances (wind speeds and directions, sensor noise levels, injected faults) × seeds. Each cell has expected behaviour and an acceptance rule. For MP7 the minimum matrix is the failsafe matrix of F10-35 plus your own feature's tests, run on every change in continuous integration, with every run's log kept. The cost is CPU time; measure it on your own machine (time the lab run, then time one run of your project's SITL) before you decide how many cells run on every change and how many run nightly. C1

What SITL cannot tell you

SITL proves the logic under the conditions you modelled. It does not prove timing on the flight controller's processor, behaviour under the real sensor noise and vibration (F10-09), electromagnetic interference, the real battery, the real radio, or the people. Those are found by HITL (timing and the real processor), bench tests (real sensors and actuators, propellers removed) and supervised flight tests, in that order, as bridge BR-08 describes. The forensic logs of F10-38 show two failures that a SITL campaign with default settings would never have produced. C1

How the hardware actually does it

In SITL the flight software runs on a desktop processor under a general-purpose operating system, with double-precision floating point everywhere and gigabytes of memory. On the flight controller the same source runs on a microcontroller under an RTOS (NuttX for PX4, ChibiOS for ArduPilot, DN302 and DN303), with a floating-point unit that may support only single precision, far less RAM than a desktop, and real interrupts from real sensors. The differences that matter for testing are: execution time (a loop that takes microseconds on the desktop may not fit its period on the target), numerical precision (code that silently relies on double can behave differently in float), memory (allocation that works on the desktop can fail on the target), and concurrency (the RTOS scheduler interleaves tasks differently from the host scheduler). SITL tests none of these; HITL tests the first three directly. D1 D2

Not verified — check before relying on this

Whether a particular flight controller has a single- or double-precision floating-point unit, and how much RAM it has, is a property of its processor: take it from the datasheet of the processor on your kit's board (dossier part F), not from this chapter. This chapter gives no number for either.

Worked example

Question. Test A4 of Listing 2 requires the landing point to be within 1.5 m of home. A teammate wants to tighten it to 1.2 m "to catch regressions". With the wind of the test (1.0 m/s north, 0.5 m/s east) and the controller of dronesim.hpp, how large can the landing error be when nothing is wrong?

  1. Where LAND starts. RTL switches to LAND when the vehicle is within accept_radius = 1.0 m of home horizontally (Listing 1, line 377, the RTL case). LAND then holds the horizontal position it had at that moment. So the start of the descent can already be up to 1.0 m from home.
  2. Wind offset during the descent. In a steady state with zero velocity, the thrust acceleration must cancel the drag force from the wind: a = −drag × wind. The controller produces a = kv(kp(sp − p) − v) with v = 0, so kvkp(sp − p) = −drag × wind, and the offset is p − sp = drag × wind / (kvkp). With drag = 0.35 1/s, |wind| = √(1.0² + 0.5²) = 1.118 m/s, kvkp = 1.8 × 0.9 = 1.62 1/s²: offset = 0.35 × 1.118 / 1.62 = 0.24 m, downwind.
  3. Noise. The controller reacts to GNSS position noise with a standard deviation of 0.15 m per axis in this model (line 143), which moves the vehicle by a few centimetres around the offset.
  4. Budget. Worst case without any defect: about 1.0 + 0.24 = 1.24 m plus a few centimetres. The recorded run landed 1.150 m from home (sitl_mission.out), inside the budget.
  5. Verdict. A tolerance of 1.2 m sits inside the design's own error budget: the test will fail on correct code whenever the RTL-to-LAND switch happens on the far side of the acceptance circle. Either keep 1.5 m, or tighten the design first (a smaller acceptance radius for the final RTL leg, or an integral term that removes the wind offset) and then the test.

This arithmetic is the root cause of the forensic lab below. Keep it in mind when you read the evidence. R1 R3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Tomás can invent any bump he likes, including ones nobody planned. A simulator produces only the disturbances its authors coded; whatever is not in the model (vibration, magnetic interference, a receiver set up wrongly) never happens in SITL, however many runs you do.
    • 

  • In the rehearsal, Leila's arms are real; only the hall is pretend. In SITL even the "arms" (motors, ESCs, the attitude response) are models, so a rehearsal can pass with arms the real drone does not have.
    • 

  • Tomás waiting for Leila is harmless. In flight, nothing waits: the real world is free-running. Lockstep is a property of the test, and it hides any timing problem of the flight software, which is why HITL exists.
    • 

  • Leila learns from each rehearsal. Flight software does not learn between SITL runs; a passing run tells you nothing about the next configuration unless the test campaign covers it.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Tolerance inside the error budget. Symptom: a test fails now and then with no code change. Cause: the threshold is smaller than the normal variation the design allows (the worked example). Find it: compute the budget; plot the metric over many seeds. Fix: derive tolerances from the design, write the derivation next to the test.
    • 

  • Free-running simulation in CI. Symptom: failures cluster on nights when the build machine is busy. Cause: the flight software misses steps under load. Find it: compare verdicts with host load or the simulator's real-time-factor messages; re-run a failing case with lockstep. Fix: lockstep in CI; keep free-running runs for separate soak tests.
    • 

  • Unseeded noise. Symptom: a failure cannot be reproduced. Cause: noise or test order uses an unseeded generator or the clock. Fix: every random source takes a seed that is printed in the log.
    • 

  • Testing the simulator instead of the software. Symptom: a fix "works in SITL" by changing the simulator's parameters. Cause: the model was tuned to make the test pass. Fix: model parameters are reviewed like code, and every change says which measurement justifies it.
    • 

  • Believing SITL about timing. Symptom: the code that passed SITL misses deadlines on the flight controller. Cause: SITL ran on a fast desktop. Fix: HITL and on-target timing measurements (F10-34).
    •

Mini-project


A SITL test runner for MP7. Write sitl_runner: it reads a folder of scenario files (mission, wind, seed, injected faults, acceptance rules with tolerances), runs each in lockstep with the course simulator (or, if you have it, drives your PX4 or ArduPilot SITL through its documented interfaces), writes one log per run, and prints a summary table with an exit status that CI can use. Summary


    

  • SITL runs the real flight software against a modelled world on a desktop; the cut sits where the hardware drivers sit.
    • 

  • Each step: sensor sample out of the world, command out of the software, world integrates one step.
    • 

  • Lockstep makes a run depend only on code, inputs and seeds; free-running results depend on host load.
    • 

  • Acceptance tests need tolerances derived from the design's own error budget; a tolerance inside the budget produces flaky tests.
    • 

  • A campaign is a matrix of scenarios, disturbances and seeds, run on every change, with every log kept.
    • 

  • SITL proves logic under modelled conditions; timing, real sensors, interference and people need HITL, bench and flight tests.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. ci_flaky.cpp flies the same square mission with world seed 42 every night. On nights with "other CI jobs", the flight software is stepped free-running with a seeded model of host stalls (probability 0.03 per answered step, 5–44 missed steps); on 2026-09-10 lockstep is on. The tolerance of A4 is 1.2 m. R3


Observation. All three failures (09-02: 1.218 m, 09-05: 1.202 m, 09-08: 1.212 m) are on nights with other jobs and lockstep off. Every quiet night gives exactly 1.150 m, with zero missed steps. Loaded nights show 2,201–2,748 missed steps and errors spread from 1.091 to 1.218 m.


Root cause (two parts). (1) The CI simulation was free-running, so the result depended on host load: the code did not change, the timing did. (2) The 1.2 m tolerance lies inside the design's own budget of about 1.24 m (worked example), and the nominal result 1.150 m is only 0.05 m below it, so any perturbation can push it over. Lockstep on 09-10 restored 1.150 m with the same load, which proves part (1); part (2) means the test would still be fragile with a different wind or seed.


Wrong hypotheses. "Intermittent bug in the landing logic": a code bug would be deterministic in lockstep with fixed seeds, and quiet nights never fail. "GNSS noise": the noise seed is fixed (42) and quiet nights are identical to the millimetre.


Fix. Lockstep on in CI; tolerance back to 1.5 m with the derivation written next to it (or a design change first, then a tighter test). Prevention. The CI job fails if the simulator is not in lockstep; it prints the seed and the missed-step count; a weekly 100-seed Monte Carlo run reports the distribution of every metric so that tolerances can be checked against it. Do not block the release for a logic bug; do record the flaky test as a defect in the test suite.
