Story hook

Four kitchen halls stand in a row, each with its own helpers and its own pantry. Tonight they cook one giant soup together: each hall cooks a quarter of the vegetables, but the seasoning must be decided from the taste of all four pots. Every hall needs the same total before it can continue.

Ana, in hall 0, proposes the obvious plan: everybody sends a bowl of their broth to her, she mixes the four, and sends a bowl of the mix back to everyone. It works, but Ana's door is jammed with delivery carts while the other three halls wait with nothing to do. Bilal suggests something odd: sit around a round table, pass bowls to the right, each person adds a spoon of their own broth and passes again. After two laps everybody holds the same mix, and nobody's door was ever busier than anybody else's.

Both plans compute the same thing. That "same thing" has a name, all-reduce, and it belongs to a small family of group operations called collectives. This course is about computing them fast across GPUs. This chapter is about what they are, exactly, and the one rule that makes them work at all: everyone comes to the table.

What you will be able to do

  • Define broadcast, reduce, all-reduce, reduce-scatter, all-gather and all-to-all by what each rank holds before and after, and recognise them in a program.
  • Show by computation that all-reduce equals reduce-scatter followed by all-gather, the identity behind the ring algorithm of F8-02.
  • Call the six collectives through a real library (Open MPI) and check every result against the definition.
  • Explain the matching rule of collectives and diagnose a job that hangs because one rank skipped a collective.
  • Count the data each rank must receive for each collective, the starting point of the cost models in F8-03.
Jargon box
Rank
Simple words: the number that identifies one participant in a group job. Analogy: the seat number at the round table, or the number of a kitchen hall. Precise: an integer from 0 to N−1 that identifies a process (or a GPU) inside a communicator; collectives are defined in terms of ranks. D3 D1
Communicator
Simple words: the list of participants that take part in a group operation together. Analogy: the set of halls that agreed to cook tonight's soup together. Precise: an object that names a group of ranks and a private communication context; every collective is called on a communicator and involves all of its ranks (MPI_COMM_WORLD in MPI; a communicator object in NCCL and RCCL). D3 D1
Collective (collective operation)
Simple words: an operation that every member of a group must call, and that moves or combines data among all of them. Analogy: passing bowls around the table: it only works if everyone is seated. Precise: a communication operation called by all ranks of a communicator, whose result is defined for the whole group (broadcast, reduce, all-reduce, reduce-scatter, all-gather, all-to-all and others). D3 D1
Broadcast (collective)
Simple words: one participant's data are copied to everyone. Analogy: the head of hall 0 sends the same recipe card to every hall. Precise: after the call, every rank's buffer equals the root rank's buffer. D3
Reduce
Simple words: everyone's data are combined (for example added) and only one participant gets the result. Analogy: every hall sends a bowl to Ana, who mixes them. Precise: element i of the root's result is op(x0[i], …, xN−1[i]) for an associative operation op such as sum, max or min. D3
All-reduce
Simple words: a reduce whose result everybody gets. Analogy: after the bowls went round, every hall holds the same mix. Precise: every rank receives the element-wise reduction of all ranks' buffers. D3 D1
Reduce-scatter
Simple words: everyone's data are combined, and each participant keeps only its own slice of the result. Analogy: each hall ends up responsible for the finished mix of one quarter of the soup. Precise: the reduced vector is split into N blocks and rank r receives block r. D3 D1
All-gather
Simple words: everyone contributes a piece and everyone ends with all pieces. Analogy: each hall sends its finished quarter to all others, so every hall has the whole soup. Precise: every rank receives the concatenation, in rank order, of all ranks' buffers. D3 D1
All-to-all
Simple words: everyone sends a different piece to each other participant. Analogy: each hall sorts its ingredients into one crate per hall and sends each crate to its owner. Precise: block j of rank i's send buffer becomes block i of rank j's receive buffer (a transpose of blocks across ranks). D3
Root
Simple words: the special participant that sends in a broadcast or receives in a reduce. Analogy: Ana in the first plan. Precise: the rank given as an argument to rooted collectives (broadcast, reduce, gather, scatter); all ranks must pass the same root. D3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When many kitchens work on one dish, they must sometimes share. There are only a few ways to share, and each has a name. Copy one kitchen's recipe to all: broadcast. Mix everyone's broth into one pot: reduce. Mix everyone's broth and give every kitchen a bowl of the mix: all-reduce. Each kitchen finishes one part of the mix: reduce-scatter. Each kitchen hands its part to everybody: all-gather. Each kitchen sends a different crate to each other kitchen: all-to-all. The one rule: these are group games. If one kitchen does not play, the others wait at the table forever.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why collectives, and why GPUs care

A program that runs on several GPUs has one process (or one thread) per GPU, each with its own memory (BR-04). Most of the time each one works alone. At certain points they must agree: in data-parallel training, after every step, all copies of the model must apply the same gradient, which is the element-wise sum of the gradients each GPU computed on its share of the batch. That sum, delivered to everyone, is an all-reduce. D8 C1 Splitting a large layer across GPUs needs all-gather and reduce-scatter; routing different data to different GPUs needs all-to-all. C1 The communication libraries of this course, NCCL for NVIDIA GPUs and RCCL for AMD GPUs, are libraries of exactly these operations; MPI, the standard of the cluster world, defines them too. D1 D2 D3

Definitions by "before and after"

A collective is fully described by what each rank holds before and after the call. With N ranks and rank r holding a vector xr of n elements:

CollectiveAfter the callData each rank must receive at least
Broadcast (root q)every rank holds xqn (every rank except the root)
Reduce (root q)rank q holds Σr xr; others unchangedroot: depends on the algorithm (F8-02)
All-reduceevery rank holds Σr xr2(N−1)/N · n with the best algorithms (F8-02, F8-03)
Reduce-scatterrank r holds block r (n/N elements) of the sum(N−1)/N · n
All-gather (each rank contributes m)every rank holds x0, …, xN−1 concatenated(N−1) · m
All-to-all (blocks of n/N)block j of rank i is now block i of rank j(N−1)/N · n

The definitions are from the MPI Standard and the NCCL documentation (title only, to be confirmed). D3 D1 The right column is arithmetic you can check: in an all-gather a rank already holds its own m elements and must obtain the N−1 other pieces; in a reduce-scatter its result block depends on N−1 other ranks' blocks of n/N elements. The all-reduce figure is derived in F8-02.

All-reduce = reduce-scatter + all-gather

Do a reduce-scatter: rank r holds block r of the sum. Then do an all-gather of those blocks: every rank gets all blocks of the sum in order, which is the whole sum. Listing 1 checks this identity by computation and prints "yes". R1 The curriculum names this identity as the key concept of the track, and it is the structure of the ring all-reduce. C1 D4

The matching rule

Every rank of the communicator must call the same collectives in the same order, with matching arguments (the same root, the same reduction operation, consistent counts and types). D3 A collective is not a function that one rank runs on its own; it is an agreement. If one rank does not call it, the others cannot finish: they wait for data that will never come. The forensic lab of this chapter is such a job, run for real with Open MPI. R3

Note

The word kernel in this course means a GPU kernel, the function that GPU threads run (F6-01); communication libraries implement collectives partly as GPU kernels. It is unrelated to the operating-system kernel of F3.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

In one line of analogy: the soup recipe says what each hall must hold at the end; how the bowls travel is a separate decision, and that decision is the whole performance story of this course.

Semantics versus algorithm

The definitions above say nothing about who sends what to whom. A reduce may be computed by every rank sending to the root, by a tree, or by a reduce-scatter followed by a gather; an all-reduce by a ring, a tree, recursive doubling or a combination. D5 D4 A library is free to choose, and it may choose differently for different message sizes and machines: Open MPI 4.1.6 in this build lists six selectable all-reduce algorithms in its own help text (F8-02 runs all of them). H2 Your program must therefore depend only on the semantics.

What "sum" means with floating point

Reductions assume an associative operation, but floating-point addition is not associative: (a+b)+c can differ from a+(b+c) in the last bits (MA302). Different algorithms add in different orders, so an all-reduce of float data can differ in the last bits from a sequential sum, and between libraries or message sizes. F8-05 measures it. D3 The listings of this chapter use integers so that every result is exact.

Buffers, in-place calls and blocking

A collective call names a send buffer and a receive buffer; libraries also offer an "in-place" form in which the result overwrites the input (in MPI, the constant MPI_IN_PLACE, used in F8-06). D3 H1 The MPI calls of Listing 2 are blocking: when MPI_Allreduce returns on a rank, that rank's receive buffer holds the result. D3 GPU libraries work differently: their calls are enqueued on a GPU stream and return to the host before the data have moved, like a kernel launch (F6-28). F8-06 treats this in detail.

Not verified — check before relying on this

That NCCL and RCCL collectives take a GPU stream argument, return before completion, and must be called by all ranks of a communicator in the same order, and that NCCL provides broadcast, reduce, all-reduce, reduce-scatter and all-gather (and, depending on the version, all-to-all through point-to-point calls or a dedicated function): none of this could be read in this build. Check the NCCL documentation (sections "Collective Operations", "CUDA Stream Semantics" and the API reference) and the RCCL documentation for the versions you install, and record them in the dossier.

Volume lower bounds

The right column of the definitions table gives a floor that no algorithm can beat on bandwidth: in an all-gather each rank must receive (N−1)/N of the final data. An all-reduce must at least do a reduce-scatter's worth of reduction traffic and an all-gather's worth of distribution traffic, which gives the 2(N−1)/N · n that Patarasuk and Yuan show to be optimal for bandwidth and that the ring attains. D4 These numbers turn into the "bus bandwidth" factor of F8-03.

How the hardware actually does it

A collective on GPUs is made of two kinds of work: moving bytes between GPU memories over links, and arithmetic (the additions of a reduction) done by GPU threads. Inside one machine the bytes move over PCIe or over dedicated GPU-to-GPU links (NVLink, xGMI), by copy engines or by kernels that load and store directly into a peer GPU's memory (F1-62). D7 C1 Between machines they cross network cards (DG402). Which path the bytes take, and how many links are used at once, is the topology question of F8-04. In this build the "ranks" of Listing 2 are CPU processes on one machine; Open MPI moves their data through shared memory, which is a different mechanism from GPU links but obeys the same definitions. R2

Worked example

Task. Eight GPUs each hold a gradient of 100 MiB (float). (a) After an all-reduce, what does each GPU hold? (b) After a reduce-scatter, how many bytes does each GPU hold, and at least how many must it have received? (c) An all-gather of those blocks follows. How much does each GPU receive in it? (d) Compare (b)+(c) with the all-reduce lower bound.

  1. (a) The element-wise sum of the eight gradients: 100 MiB, the same on all eight.
  2. (b) One block: 100 MiB / 8 = 12.5 MiB. Each element of its block depends on the 7 other GPUs' corresponding elements, so it receives at least 7 × 12.5 MiB = 87.5 MiB = (N−1)/N × 100 MiB.
  3. (c) The 7 blocks it does not hold: 7 × 12.5 MiB = 87.5 MiB.
  4. (d) 87.5 + 87.5 = 175 MiB = 2 × 7/8 × 100 MiB, exactly the bound 2(N−1)/N · n. Sending everything to one root instead would make that root receive 7 × 100 = 700 MiB: Ana's jammed door.

The counting of steps 2–3 is what algorithms.cpp in F8-02 confirms by simulation (its "sent/count" column is 1.750 for the ring with N = 8). D4

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Bowls are not split. At the round table a whole bowl travels; a real ring all-reduce cuts the buffer into N segments and the segments into chunks, so all ranks send at the same time and every link is busy at once (F8-02). The registered mapping says this: "messages are split into chunks and pipelined". D4
    • 

  • Mixing is not free and not exact. Adding broth is instant and order does not matter; adding floating-point numbers costs GPU time and the order changes the last bits (Layer 3).
    • 

  • Nobody notices an empty chair. A cook would see that hall 3 is missing and start without it. A rank waiting in a collective has no idea why data are not arriving: it simply waits, possibly forever (forensic lab).
    • 

  • The roads matter more than the table. The table suggests that every neighbour is equally close. Between GPUs some links are many times faster than others (F8-04).
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the job hangs at a collective; some ranks' logs stop at "entering all-reduce". Cause: one rank skipped the call or called a different collective (the forensic lab). How to find it: log every collective call with the rank, name and count; compare the last line of each rank. Fix: every rank calls every collective, with a neutral contribution (zero for a sum) when it has no data.
    • 

  • Symptom: wrong sizes or a crash in an all-gather. Cause: passing the total count instead of the per-rank count as the receive count (in MPI the receive count is per rank). How to find it: print the counts on every rank. Fix: follow the definition table; Listing 2, line 67.
    • 

  • Symptom: results differ between ranks after a reduce. Cause: expecting the result on non-root ranks. Fix: use all-reduce, or broadcast afterwards.
    • 

  • Symptom: mpirun refuses to start. Cause: running as root, or more processes than slots. How to find it: its own message names the options. Fix: as in run.sh; on a normal account the root option is not needed.
    • 

  • Symptom: a float all-reduce differs in the last digits from a CPU sum. Cause: a different summation order, not a bug. Fix: compare with a tolerance (F8-05, MA302).
    •

Summary


    

  • Collectives are group operations defined by what each rank holds before and after.
    • 

  • Six matter most here: broadcast, reduce, all-reduce, reduce-scatter, all-gather, all-to-all.
    • 

  • All-reduce equals reduce-scatter followed by all-gather; that is the shape of the ring algorithm.
    • 

  • Each rank must receive at least 2(N−1)/N of the data in an all-reduce; sending all to one root makes it receive (N−1) times the data.
    • 

  • Every rank must call every collective, in the same order, with matching arguments; otherwise the job hangs.
    • 

  • The definition fixes the result; the library chooses the algorithm.
    • 

  • Floating-point sums depend on the order; integer tests are exact.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Ranks 0, 1 and 2 printed "entering MPI_Allreduce" and never printed "total". Rank 3 printed "empty shard, skipping MPI_Allreduce" and never printed "past the barrier". The run was stopped by the time limit (exit code 124).


Root cause. perRank = ceil(9/4) = 3, so ranks 0–2 take items 1–9 and rank 3 gets first = 9 = items: an empty shard. With 3 ranks, every rank has 3 items, so the bug was invisible. Rank 3 then calls MPI_Barrier while the others are inside MPI_Allreduce: the collectives do not match, the all-reduce cannot complete without rank 3, and the barrier cannot complete without ranks 0–2. Everyone waits forever.


Wrong answers and why. "A deadlock bug in the library": the same library completes all collectives in Listing 2 (R2); the evidence shows a mismatch in the program. "A slow rank": the job made no progress for 10 s on numbers that take microseconds, and rank 3 printed that it skipped. "Wrong item count": the total is correct (1..9); the distribution is the problem, but it matters only because of the skip.


Fix. Every rank calls MPI_Allreduce unconditionally, contributing 0 when its shard is empty, and the barrier is removed (the all-reduce already synchronises). Verify with 1 to 12 ranks and item counts that leave empty shards; the total must be 45 every time. Prevention: a collective logger (mini-project), tests with more ranks than items, and a rule in code review: no collective inside a rank-dependent branch. How the evidence was produced: run.sh ran skip.cc with 4 processes under timeout 10 and killed the leftover processes.
