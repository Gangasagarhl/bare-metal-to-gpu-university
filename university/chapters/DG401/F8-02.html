Story hook

Back at the round table of the four kitchen halls. Bilal's first try was slow: one full bowl travelled around, and three of the four people sat idle at every moment. So he changed the rule. Every pot of broth is poured into four small cups, one per quarter of the soup. At every tick of the clock, everyone passes one cup to the right, and the receiver adds a spoon from their own pot to the cup they receive. After three ticks, each person holds one cup that contains everybody's broth. Three more ticks of passing the finished cups to the right, and everybody holds all four finished cups.

Next door, a bigger team of sixteen halls tries Bilal's idea and finds it slow for a pinch of salt: thirty ticks to share a pinch. They use a different plan for small things: pair up, swap, add; pair up with someone else, swap, add; four rounds and done. Two algorithms, the same answer, and which one wins depends on how much you pass. This chapter is about those plans.

What you will be able to do

  • Count rounds and bytes per rank for the ring, recursive doubling, recursive halving and doubling, and a binomial tree, and check the counts with a simulator.
  • Explain why the ring is bandwidth-optimal for large messages and why trees and recursive doubling win for small ones.
  • Force each of Open MPI's all-reduce algorithms and verify that they all give the same result.
  • Diagnose an all-reduce that is correct on 4 and 8 ranks and wrong on 6.
Jargon box
Round (communication step)
Simple words: one tick of the clock in which every participant sends at most one message and receives at most one. Analogy: one tick at the round table: everyone passes one cup. Precise: the unit of the step count of a collective algorithm; the cost of an algorithm is its number of rounds on the critical path times the per-message start-up cost, plus the bytes of its rounds over the bandwidth (F8-03). D5
Ring all-reduce
Simple words: everyone passes pieces to the right neighbour; first adding, then copying. Analogy: passing cups around the round table, each person adding their spoon. Precise: an all-reduce made of a reduce-scatter in N−1 rounds and an all-gather in N−1 rounds, in which rank r only sends to rank r+1 and each message carries one of N segments; bandwidth-optimal for large messages. D4 C1
Segment and chunk
Simple words: the buffer is cut into N pieces (segments), and each piece into smaller pieces (chunks) that travel one after the other. Analogy: the four cups; and spoonfuls poured from one cup to the next. Precise: in the ring, segment g holds elements [g·n/N, (g+1)·n/N); chunks are the pipelining unit (F8-05). D4
Recursive doubling
Simple words: pair up, swap everything, add; pair up with someone twice as far away; repeat. Analogy: the sixteen halls pairing up. Precise: an all-reduce for N a power of two in log2 N rounds, in round k rank r exchanging its whole buffer with rank r XOR 2k and adding. D5
Recursive halving and doubling (Rabenseifner's algorithm)
Simple words: pair up and swap half, then a quarter, then an eighth; then put it back together the same way. Analogy: two halls split the soup in two and each finishes one half, then pairs of pairs split again. Precise: a reduce-scatter by recursive halving followed by an all-gather by recursive doubling: 2 log2 N rounds and the same bytes per rank as the ring. D5
Binomial tree
Simple words: data flow up a tree to one root and then back down. Analogy: pairs pass to one person, those pass to one, until one person holds the mix; then it goes back down the same paths. Precise: a reduce in ⌈log2 N⌉ rounds, round k having ranks whose lowest set bit is 2k send to rank − 2k, followed by a broadcast along the reverse tree. D5
Bandwidth-optimal
Simple words: no algorithm can move fewer bytes per participant. Analogy: no seating plan needs fewer cups per person. Precise: for all-reduce, each rank sends (and receives) 2(N−1)/N · n elements, the lower bound proved by Patarasuk and Yuan for their model. D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

If one person mixes everything, everybody else waits and that one person is overloaded. In the ring, everybody works all the time: at every tick, each person passes one cup and receives one cup. Cups are small, because each pot was split into as many cups as there are people. For a big soup, this is the fastest way. For a pinch of salt, the many ticks are what cost time, so a plan with fewer ticks wins, even if each tick carries more.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The ring, exactly

Number the ranks 0 to N−1 around a ring and cut every buffer into N segments. In reduce-scatter step s (s = 0 … N−2), rank r sends segment (r−s) mod N to rank r+1, which adds it into its own copy of that segment. After N−1 steps, rank r holds the complete sum of segment (r+1) mod N. In all-gather step s, rank r sends segment (r+1−s) mod N, which is complete, to rank r+1, which overwrites its copy. After N−1 more steps every rank holds every complete segment. D4 Figure 1 and the trace in Listing 1's output show it for N = 4. R1

Counting: 2(N−1) rounds; in each round every rank sends one segment of n/N elements; so every rank sends 2(N−1)/N · n elements. That equals the lower bound of F8-01, which is why the ring is called bandwidth-optimal. D4 Listing 1's table confirms the count for N = 2, 3, 4, 8, 16 (for N = 8 the busiest rank sends 420 of the 240-element message's elements in total: 420/240 = 1.750 = 2 × 7/8). R1

Recursive doubling

For N a power of two: in round k = 0, 1, …, log2 N − 1, rank r exchanges its whole buffer with rank r XOR 2k and adds. After round k, each rank holds the sum over a group of 2k+1 ranks; after log2 N rounds, over all. D5 Few rounds, but every round sends the whole buffer: log2 N · n elements per rank, 4.000 × n for 16 ranks against the ring's 1.875 × n. R1

Recursive halving and doubling

Rabenseifner's algorithm combines the two ideas: a reduce-scatter in which each round exchanges half of the current range with a partner (round 1: half of the buffer; round 2: a quarter; …), then an all-gather that undoes the halving in reverse order. D5 Listing 1 shows 2 log2 N rounds (8 for N = 16) and exactly the ring's bytes (1.875 × n for N = 16). R1 In the pure cost model of F8-03 it is never slower than the ring; on real machines its partners at distance 2k may share links, which F8-04 shows matters.

Trees

A binomial tree reduces to rank 0 in ⌈log2 N⌉ rounds and broadcasts back in as many. Each round carries the whole buffer, and the root sends in every broadcast round: 4 × n for 16 ranks. R1 Trees work for any N; the binomial tree also handles N = 3 in Listing 1's table. R1 Real libraries pipeline trees: they cut the message into chunks and send chunk k+1 down a level while chunk k goes further, so a large message's tree time is no longer the full message times the depth. The curriculum lists "trees and double binary trees (latency-friendly)" next to the ring. C1

Not verified — check before relying on this

That NCCL implements ring and tree algorithms for all-reduce, that its tree is a "double binary tree" (two complementary trees, each carrying half of the data, so that every rank is a leaf in one tree and an inner node in the other), and that NCCL chooses between them per message size and topology: this build could not read the NCCL documentation or source. The same holds for RCCL's choices. Check the NCCL documentation (environment variables and algorithm selection) and the RCCL documentation for the versions you install.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

In one line of analogy: the ring is the table where every cup moves at every tick; the other plans move fewer but fuller cups, and the right plan depends on whether the ticks or the cups are the expensive part.

Pipelining inside the ring

In the round model, a step cannot start before the previous step's segment has fully arrived. Real implementations cut each segment into chunks: the receiver adds chunk k while chunk k+1 is in flight, and forwards chunk k in the next step before the rest of the segment has arrived. The reduction work then hides behind the transfers, and no rank waits for a whole segment before it can forward the first part of it. The price is one start-up per chunk, so chunks must not be too small (F8-03). D4 The registered analogy names this exactly: "messages are split into chunks and pipelined, so all bowls move at once". F8-05 builds it.

Ring order and topology

The ring needs only N links, one per neighbour pair, and uses all of them in both phases. That is its strength and its weakness: the slowest link in the ring sets the pace for everyone. Choosing the ring order so that neighbours share fast links is the topic of F8-04. Recursive doubling and halving-doubling talk to partners at distance 1, 2, 4, …; on a machine where distant ranks share a slow link, those rounds collide. D5

Non-powers of two

Recursive doubling and halving-doubling as written need N to be a power of two. MPI implementations handle other sizes, for example by first folding the extra ranks into partners so that a power-of-two subset runs the algorithm and then sending the result back. D5 Forgetting this is the forensic case of this chapter. Open MPI 4.1.6 ran its recursive-doubling and Rabenseifner choices correctly with 3 processes in this build (lines "ranks 3" of the forced run). R3

What a real library offers

Open MPI's help text, printed by ompi_info in this build, lists the all-reduce algorithms of its "tuned" collective component: basic linear, nonoverlapping (reduce then broadcast), recursive doubling, ring, segmented ring and Rabenseifner, selectable with coll_tuned_allreduce_algorithm when coll_tuned_use_dynamic_rules is true. How the hardware actually does it


On GPUs, each round of a ring is a transfer over one link plus a reduction kernel on the receiving GPU. With direct peer access the sending GPU's threads, or its copy engines, write into a buffer in the receiver's memory over PCIe or NVLink/xGMI (F1-62). D6 A ring uses each GPU's link to its successor in one direction and its link from its predecessor in the other, all at once; on a fully connected link fabric many rings can run in parallel over different links, each carrying a part of the data. C1 How many rings a library builds and over which links is decided at run time from the topology it detects (F8-04; unverified for NCCL and RCCL above). In this build, the MPI "ranks" are CPU processes and every "link" is a copy through shared memory, so the timing of the forced run says nothing about GPUs.

Watch out
The times in the forced run are not evidence about the algorithms. They were measured with 3 and 4 MPI processes on a container with 4 logical CPUs shared with other jobs; the same ring took 40.085 ms with 4 processes and 1.169 ms with 3 in the same run (R3). That spread is noise from sharing CPUs, not a property of the ring. The only conclusion this run supports is correctness: all six algorithms gave 0 wrong elements with 4 and with 3 processes. F8-03 shows how to measure properly.



Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Cups do not wait for each other at real tables, but steps do in the round model. Real rings pipeline chunks so steps overlap; the "tick" is an accounting device, not a clock.
    • 

  • Passing to the right is not free of direction. A link may be faster in one direction than the other, or shared with other traffic; the table has no roads at all (F8-04).
    • 

  • Adding a spoon is not instant. Each reduce-scatter step needs a reduction kernel on the receiver, which costs GPU time and memory bandwidth; the analogy hides it.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: correct on 2, 4, 8 ranks; wrong on 3, 5, 6, 7. Cause: a power-of-two algorithm without a plan for the extra ranks (forensic lab). How to find it: test every N from 2 to 9, not only powers of two; use contributions that identify ranks. Fix: fold the extra ranks in first, or use a ring or tree.
    • 

  • Symptom: ring result wrong only in some segments. Cause: off-by-one in the segment index formula (for example sending (r−s−1) in the reduce-scatter). How to find it: the bit-mask trace of Listing 1. Fix: compare your indices with the trace for N = 4.
    • 

  • Symptom: an all-reduce result that sometimes includes a rank's data twice. Cause: reading the buffer after it was already updated in the same round. How to find it: simulate with distinct contributions (10r). Fix: separate send and receive buffers (Listing 1, line 158).
    • 

  • Symptom: "algorithm X is 30 times slower" from one run on a shared machine. Cause: noise (the warning above). Fix: repeat, use medians, pin processes, and use an idle machine (F8-03).

Summary


    

  • The ring is a reduce-scatter (N−1 adding steps) followed by an all-gather (N−1 copying steps), always sending to the next rank.
    • 

  • Each rank sends 2(N−1)/N of the message: the bandwidth lower bound.
    • 

  • Recursive doubling needs only log2 N rounds but sends the whole message each round.
    • 

  • Halving-doubling has the ring's bytes and 2 log2 N rounds, but talks to distant partners.
    • 

  • Binomial trees work for any N; libraries pipeline them for large messages.
    • 

  • Power-of-two algorithms need a plan for other N; test every N.
    • 

  • Correctness can be checked on any machine; speed only on the real one, measured properly.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. For N = 6, ranks 2 and 3 hold "111100": they contain ranks 0–3 once and ranks 4 and 5 not at all. The other ranks are correct. N = 3, 5, 7 are also wrong; 2, 4, 8 are correct.


Root cause. Trace N = 6 round by round. After mask 1 (pairs 0↔1, 2↔3, 4↔5) the groups are {0,1}, {2,3}, {4,5}. With mask 2, ranks 0–3 pair 0↔2 and 1↔3 and now hold 0–3; ranks 4 and 5 have partners 6 and 7, which do not exist, so they skip and still hold 4–5. With mask 4, ranks 0↔4 and 1↔5 exchange, so ranks 0, 1, 4 and 5 end with the full sum; ranks 2 and 3 have partners 6 and 7 again and skip, so they never receive 4–5. That is exactly "111100" on ranks 2 and 3.


Why it looks like "drifting copies". In data-parallel training the two wrong ranks apply a different gradient from the others, so their model copies diverge (F8-06).


Wrong answers and why. "A race": the replay is sequential and deterministic, and the wrong digits are always the same. "Wrong partner formula": XOR is correct for powers of two, where all results are right.


Fix. Handle the r = N − 2⌊log2 N⌋ extra ranks: before the doubling rounds, each extra rank sends its data to a partner inside the power-of-two group; after them, the partner sends the result back (D5 describes this kind of scheme); or fall back to a ring or tree. Prevention: test every N from 2 to 9 with rank-identifying contributions. How the evidence was produced: rd_bug.cpp, run by run_lab.sh.
