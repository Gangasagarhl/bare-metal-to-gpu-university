Story hook

The delivery service between the kitchen halls charges in a simple way. Every trip costs a fixed amount of time, whatever is on the cart: the porter must be called, the doors opened, the cart signed for. Then every crate on the cart adds a little more time. A cart with one spoon of salt costs almost the same as a cart with a whole crate of salt, because the fixed part dominates. A cart with a hundred crates costs almost exactly a hundred crates' worth, because the fixed part disappears in the total.

Nadia, who plans the soup-sharing for eight halls, writes this down as two numbers: the fixed cost of a trip and the time per crate. With them she can predict, before anybody moves a cart, how long Bilal's ring of cups will take for a pinch of salt and for a tank of broth, and when the pairing plan of the sixteen halls is better. Then she checks the prediction against a stopwatch. When the stopwatch disagrees, she learns something about the halls that the two numbers did not know.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the alpha-beta time of the ring, recursive doubling, halving-doubling and tree all-reduce, and compute it for a given machine and message size.
  • Compute algorithm bandwidth and bus bandwidth from a measured time, and explain what the factor 2(N−1)/N means.
  • Find the message size at which a ring reaches half of its link bandwidth, and the size at which one algorithm overtakes another.
  • Measure α and β with a ping-pong, then predict and measure an all-reduce, and explain the difference.
  • Diagnose a scaling report whose small-message all-reduce gets slower with more GPUs.
Jargon box
Alpha–beta model
Simple words: a message costs a fixed start-up time plus a time per byte. Analogy: every cart trip costs the porter's call, plus the time per crate. Precise: T(m) = α + m/β for a message of m bytes, with α the latency per message and β the bandwidth; an algorithm's time is the sum over the rounds on its critical path (first defined in F1-62). D5 C1
Algorithm bandwidth (algbw)
Simple words: message size divided by the time the collective took. Analogy: how much soup per minute the whole sharing seemed to move, judged from outside. Precise: algbw = S/t, where S is the size of the data each rank contributes and t the collective's time; the name is that of the nccl-tests performance notes (title only). D6 C1
Bus bandwidth (busbw)
Simple words: algorithm bandwidth corrected by how much data each link really had to carry, so that you can compare it with the link's speed. Analogy: the crates per minute that actually crossed each road, not the soup per minute that came out. Precise: for all-reduce, busbw = algbw × 2(N−1)/N, the factor being the ring's bytes per rank divided by S; for a bandwidth-optimal algorithm on a uniform machine it approaches the per-link bandwidth for large messages and does not depend on N. D6 R1
Half-performance size (n½)
Simple words: the message size at which you reach half of the best possible speed. Analogy: the cart load at which the porter's call costs as much as the crates. Precise: for one message, m = α·β; for the ring all-reduce, S = N·α·β, because each round carries S/N. R1
Ping-pong
Simple words: send a message to a partner who sends it straight back, and time the round trip. Analogy: send a cart to the next hall and time its return. Precise: a two-process benchmark whose one-way time, half of the median round trip for each size, is fitted to α + m/β. D3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 plots the ring all-reduce's bus bandwidth against message size for the invented teaching machine TN-1 (eight GPUs, α = 10 µs and β = 25 GB/s per link direction; invented numbers, chosen for easy arithmetic). The curve rises from almost nothing for small messages to the link bandwidth for large ones, and crosses half of it at S = Nαβ.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every message has a fixed price and a price per byte. Tiny messages pay almost only the fixed price, so sending them in fewer rounds helps. Huge messages pay almost only the per-byte price, so sending fewer bytes helps. A good engineer predicts the time from these two prices before running anything, then measures, and treats every difference as a clue.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From rounds and bytes to time

F8-02 counted rounds and bytes. The alpha-beta model turns a round that carries m bytes into α + m/β. Assuming every link has the same α and β, and ignoring the time of the additions, the all-reduce of S bytes per rank on N ranks takes: D5 D4

AlgorithmTime
Ring2(N−1) · (α + S/(Nβ)) = 2(N−1)α + 2(N−1)/N · S/β
Recursive doublinglog2 N · (α + S/β)
Halving-doubling2 log2 N · α + 2(N−1)/N · S/β
Binomial tree, not pipelined2⌈log2 N⌉ · (α + S/β)

Each line is the round count of F8-02 times the per-round cost; the halving-doubling line adds rounds of S/2, S/4, …, S/N twice. Listing 1 evaluates them. R1 A third term, γ per byte for the reduction arithmetic, is part of the model in Thakur, Rabenseifner and Gropp; this chapter leaves it out and Layer 3 shows what that costs. D5

Reading Listing 1's table

For TN-1 with N = 8 (invented values), the model says: at 8 B, recursive doubling (30 µs) beats the ring (140 µs) because the ring pays 14 start-ups; from 1 MiB on, halving-doubling and the ring, which send the fewest bytes, are fastest; at 1 GiB the ring takes 75,301.93 µs against 257,758.04 µs for the unpipelined tree; the ring overtakes the tree from 512 KiB. R1 Halving-doubling is never slower than the ring in this model, because it has the same bytes and fewer rounds; on real machines its partner links are not uniform (F8-04), which the model's "every link the same" hides.

Algorithm bandwidth and bus bandwidth

A benchmark measures one time t for a collective of S bytes. Dividing gives the algorithm bandwidth algbw = S/t. For all-reduce, algbw is a poor number to compare with hardware: with more ranks, each rank sends more than S (up to 2S), so algbw falls even when every link runs at full speed. The bus bandwidth multiplies by the ring's traffic factor: busbw = algbw × 2(N−1)/N. D6 Substituting the ring time shows why this is useful:

busbw = [2(N−1)/N · S] / [2(N−1)α + 2(N−1)/N · S/β] = (S/N) / (α + (S/N)/β),

which tends to β as S grows, whatever N is, and equals β/2 when S/N = αβ. R1 Listing 1 prints busbw 24.954 GB/s at 1 GiB for β = 25 GB/s, and the half point at S = 2,000,000 bytes. R1 So a measured busbw can be compared directly with the link bandwidth: a large-message busbw far below the slowest link's bandwidth means something is wrong.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The names "algorithm bandwidth" and "bus bandwidth" and the all-reduce factor 2(N−1)/N are attributed by the curriculum to the nccl-tests performance notes; the factors for the other collectives used there (for example (N−1)/N for all-gather and reduce-scatter, 1 for broadcast and reduce) follow from the same traffic argument (F8-01) but were not read in this build. Check the nccl-tests (and rccl-tests) performance notes in the version you use, and note which size those tools mean by S for each collective.

Measuring α and β

The two parameters are measured, not assumed (F1-62). A ping-pong times round trips of messages from 8 B to 8 MiB; half of the median round trip is the one-way time. Two simple fits: α = the time of the smallest message, β from the slope between the two largest sizes; or least squares over all sizes. D3 On this container, between two MPI processes (Open MPI's shared-memory path, no GPU), Listing 2 measured α = 0.26 µs and β = 10.080 GB/s with the two-point fit, and α = 1.49 µs, β = 10.047 GB/s with least squares. R2 The two α values differ by a factor of almost six: least squares is dominated by the large messages, whose times are thousands of microseconds, so a tiny error in the slope moves the intercept a lot. For α, trust the smallest messages; for β, the largest.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

In one line of analogy: Nadia's two numbers describe one road; a ring uses many roads at once, waits for the slowest, and someone also has to mix the soup.

Predict, then measure: what this build saw

Listing 3 used the ping-pong's α and β to predict a ring all-reduce on 4 processes, forced Open MPI's ring algorithm so that the formula and the algorithm match, and measured the median of 20 runs per size. R3 The measured times were 1.05 times the prediction at 8 B and between 2.0 and 7.0 times it for all larger sizes; the large-message busbw was about 1.7 GB/s against a model β of 10.08 GB/s. R3 This is not a failure of the method; it is the method working. The model's assumptions were wrong for this machine, and each gap points at one:

  • Links are not independent. The ping-pong ran two processes; the ring ran four, all copying through the same memory system of one shared virtual machine at once. The "links" share one resource, so each gets less than β.
  • The reduction is not free. Each reduce-scatter step adds S/N floats on the receiver; the γ term is missing from the model.
  • CPUs are shared. The container has 4 logical CPUs shared with other jobs (log line "machine"), and the job used all four; any interruption of one rank delays the whole ring, because a collective ends when its slowest rank ends (Listing 3 times the maximum over ranks).

Each hypothesis is testable: run 2 instead of 4 processes, replace the sum by a copy (an all-gather) to remove γ, or run on an idle machine. Those are lab steps. On GPUs the same structure holds: a model from the link's α and β, a measurement, and an explanation of the gap; the curriculum's milestone F2 asks for the bus bandwidth curve and a comparison with NCCL or RCCL. C1

Measurement protocol for collectives

  • Warm up before timing (Listings 2 and 3 discard five runs): the first calls set up connections and buffers.
  • Synchronise before each timed call (a barrier) and take the maximum time over ranks: the collective is done when its last rank is done.
  • Report the median and a spread (MA202), for many sizes, never one size (the trap named in BR-04).
  • For GPU collectives, time with GPU events on the stream the collective runs on, or synchronise the stream before reading a host clock (F6-06).
  • Write down the machine, the versions and the placement of every rank, and label every number "measured on …" (AH-23).

Where the model stops

The model has one α and one β for every pair of ranks. Real machines have links of different speeds (PCIe, NVLink, xGMI, network), links shared by several ring hops, and switches and root complexes in between. F8-04 replaces the single β by the bottleneck of each hop's path. Pipelining (F8-05) cuts each segment into k chunks: every round then costs about kα + (S/N)/β instead of α + (S/N)/β, but the reduction of one chunk overlaps the transfer of the next, which removes most of the missing γ term from the critical path. The chunk size trades start-ups against overlap. D4

How the hardware actually does it

α collects every fixed cost of one message: the software call, setting up a copy or launching a kernel, the link's own latency, and any synchronisation between sender and receiver. β is the sustained rate of the narrowest part of the path, which on a GPU machine may be a PCIe link, an NVLink or xGMI link, a PCIe switch's upstream port, or the CPUs' interconnect when traffic crosses sockets (F1-62, F8-04). C1 Link signalling rates from a specification are upper bounds; packet headers and flow control reduce the payload rate (F1-62). This chapter quotes no link speed of any product: every number here is either TN-1's invented value or a measurement on this container (AH-21, AH-23).

Worked example

Task. TN-1 (invented: α = 10 µs, β = 25 GB/s), N = 8, all-reduce of 16 MiB = 16,777,216 bytes. (a) Ring time. (b) algbw and busbw. (c) Recursive doubling time. (d) The size at which the ring's busbw reaches 12.5 GB/s.

  1. (a) Each round carries S/N = 2,097,152 bytes; at 25 GB/s = 25,000 bytes/µs that takes 83.886 µs. Per round: 93.886 µs. Ring: 14 × 93.886 = 1,314.41 µs.
  2. (b) algbw = 16,777,216 / 1,314.41 µs = 12.764 GB/s; busbw = 12.764 × 14/8 = 22.337 GB/s.
  3. (c) 3 × (10 + 16,777,216/25,000) = 3 × 681.09 = 2,043.27 µs: slower, because it sends the whole 16 MiB three times.
  4. (d) S = Nαβ = 8 × 10 µs × 25,000 bytes/µs = 2,000,000 bytes (about 1.91 MiB).

All four numbers are in Listing 1's output (row 16 MiB and the last two lines). R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Carts do not share roads in the analogy; messages do. Four ring hops copying through one memory system slowed each other down (Layer 3). A model with independent links misses that.
    • 

  • The porter's fee is not one number. α includes software, synchronisation and the link; it changes with the library, the algorithm and even the message size (protocol changes inside libraries).
    • 

  • Nobody mixes soup on a cart. The reduction costs time that is neither α nor β (the missing γ).
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: "all-reduce bandwidth halves when we go from 2 to 8 GPUs". Cause: comparing algbw across N. How to find it: compute busbw. Fix: compare busbw with the link bandwidth.
    • 

  • Symptom: busbw above any link's bandwidth. Cause: wrong factor for the collective, wrong S (total instead of per rank), or GB vs GiB. Fix: recompute by hand for one row.
    • 

  • Symptom: prediction and measurement disagree by a constant factor at all large sizes. Cause: β measured on an unloaded pair, used for a loaded ring; or the reduction cost. How to find it: vary N; replace all-reduce by all-gather. Fix: measure β under the real traffic pattern.
    • 

  • Symptom: a "regression" that disappears on rerun. Cause: one run, one size, shared machine. Fix: the protocol of Layer 3.
    • 

  • Symptom: α from a least-squares fit is several times the smallest message's time. Cause: large messages dominate the fit (R2). Fix: fit α and β separately, or weight by 1/t.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Ring at 8 KiB: 20.33, 60.49, 140.57, 300.61 µs for N = 2, 4, 8, 16; algbw 0.403, 0.135, 0.058, 0.027 GB/s. At 256 MiB the ring's busbw is 24.954, 24.907, 24.815, 24.633 GB/s: practically the link bandwidth for every N. (R4)


Root cause. The 8 KiB time divided by 2(N−1) is 20.33, 10.08, 10.04, 10.02 µs: almost exactly α = 10 µs per round. The ring's 2(N−1) start-ups dominate small messages, so time grows linearly with N. The links are fine: the 256 MiB busbw does not degrade.


Wrong answers and why. "Degrading links": the large-message busbw is unchanged across N. "Congestion": the model has no congestion and still reproduces the data.


Fix. Use a latency-friendly algorithm for small messages (recursive doubling: 41.31 µs at N = 16 in the same table, about 7 times faster than the ring's 300.61 µs; or a tree), and send fewer, larger all-reduces by bucketing gradients (F8-06). Prevention: scaling reports with busbw over a range of sizes for each N, and a model curve beside the measured one.
