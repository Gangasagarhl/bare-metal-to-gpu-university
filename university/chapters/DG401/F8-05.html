Story hook

Bilal has explained the ring three times now: everyone adds what comes from the left and passes the result to the right. Tonight the team must actually do it, with real bowls, real cooks and no Bilal at the table. The first try goes wrong at once. Ayasha passes a whole pot of soup to the right before she has finished adding; Ravi waits for a pot that is still being carried; Joon's helper passes a bowl and then pours the soup into it afterwards, so for a moment his neighbour picks up an empty bowl and adds nothing.

The fix is a few simple rules. Pass small bowls, one ladle each, so the next cook can start adding while you fill the next bowl. Never take more than two bowls on your side of the table. Never hand over a bowl before the soup is in it. This chapter turns those rules into code: first with threads on a CPU, where every mistake can be caught by a tool, then with GPUs, streams and events.

What you will be able to do

  • Implement a ring all-reduce as reduce-scatter followed by all-gather, for any number of ranks and any message size, including sizes that do not divide evenly.
  • Chunk and pipeline the transfers so that receiving, reducing and sending overlap, with bounded buffering (flow control).
  • Verify it against a reference sum for FP32 and integer data, and explain why the floating-point result differs from a sequential sum but is the same on every rank.
  • Order GPU copies and reduction kernels across devices with streams and events, without blocking the host.
  • Find a synchronisation bug in a "faster" handoff with ThreadSanitizer.
Jargon box
Chunk
Simple words: a small piece of a segment, sent on its own. Analogy: one ladle of soup in its own bowl. Precise: a contiguous sub-range of a ring segment; transfers and reductions are done per chunk so they can overlap (F8-02). R1
Pipelining (communication)
Simple words: starting the next piece before the previous one is completely done everywhere. Analogy: while your neighbour adds the first bowl, you already fill the second. Precise: overlapping the transfer of chunk k+1 with the reduction of chunk k (and with transfers on other hops), so the per-step time approaches the larger of transfer and reduce time instead of their sum. D4 R1
Flow control
Simple words: the sender waits when the receiver has no room. Analogy: never more than two bowls waiting on your side of the table. Precise: a bound on the data in flight between two ranks, enforced by the receiver's free buffer slots; Listing 1 uses a mailbox of capacity 2, Listing 2 two receive slots per GPU. R1
Data race
Simple words: two threads touch the same memory at the same time, at least one writes, and nothing orders them. Analogy: picking up a bowl while someone is still pouring into it. Precise: in C++, two conflicting accesses from different threads, not both atomic, with no happens-before relation between them; the behaviour of the program is then undefined (DS201). D12
Event (GPU)
Simple words: a marker in a GPU's work queue that other queues can wait for. Analogy: a bell rung when a bowl has been delivered. Precise: a CUDA or HIP event recorded on a stream completes when all work before it on that stream has completed; cudaStreamWaitEvent makes another stream, possibly on another device, wait for it without blocking the host (installed header). H1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows why chunks help. Without chunks, a step is "send the whole segment, then reduce it": the link and the adder take turns. With chunks, the link sends chunk 2 while the adder works on chunk 1, and only the first and last pieces are not overlapped.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

You already know the ring: everyone adds what comes from the left and passes it to the right, and after two laps everyone has the whole sum. Doing it for real needs three rules. Use small bowls, so the next person can start while you fill the next one. Do not let bowls pile up: if your neighbour already has two waiting, you wait. And only say "here you go" after the soup is in the bowl. If you break the last rule, the answer is sometimes right and sometimes wrong, which is the worst kind of wrong.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Which segment, when

With N ranks the buffer is cut into N segments. In reduce-scatter step s (0 ≤ s < N−1), rank r sends segment (r − s) mod N and receives and adds segment (r − s − 1) mod N. After N−1 steps rank r holds the finished segment (r + 1) mod N. The all-gather is the same pattern shifted by one, with "add" replaced by "copy" (F8-02). Listing 1 writes both phases as one loop: send segment (r − s + phase) mod N, receive segment (r − s − 1 + phase) mod N (lines 80–81). R1

Sizes that do not divide

The milestone requires message sizes from 4 bytes up. With 4 bytes (one float) and 4 ranks, three segments are empty. Listing 1 gives segment g the elements from ⌊g·count/N⌋ to ⌊(g+1)·count/N⌋, and cuts every segment into the same number of chunks, some possibly empty (lines 55–60). Every rank then goes through the same number of sends and receives, whatever the size, and no rank waits for a message that never comes. For 4 bytes on 8 ranks the busiest rank sent 2 elements, not the 1.75 of the formula 2(N−1)/N·count, because the one element is not shared out evenly. R1

Chunks, mailboxes and flow control

In Listing 1 each rank has a mailbox that only its left neighbour puts into. put waits while the mailbox already holds 2 chunks; take waits while it is empty (lines 22–37). Each rank, for every chunk, puts its next chunk to the right and then takes one from the left and adds it. Because the left neighbour can be one chunk ahead, receiving and adding chunk k overlaps with the neighbour sending chunk k+1. The capacity of 2 is the flow control: a fast rank can never run more than two chunks ahead of a slow neighbour, so memory use is bounded. R1

Verifying the sum

The tests fill rank r's element i with (r+1)·(i mod 251). Every partial sum is a small integer, exactly representable in int32 and float, so every rank's result must equal N(N+1)/2 · (i mod 251) bit for bit. With random floats the ring adds in a different order than a sequential loop, and the results differ by rounding: 23,196 of 100,000 elements differed from the sequential sum, by at most 4.77·10−7, yet all four ranks had bit-identical results. R1 All ranks agree because, in the all-gather, every rank copies the same finished bits instead of computing them again. That matters for data-parallel training (F8-06): replicas stay identical.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

In one line of analogy: the round table becomes four kitchens with their own clocks, carts and bells; nobody waits by the door, everyone leaves a note saying "start when that bell rings".

From threads to GPUs

On GPUs, Listing 2 drives all GPUs from one host thread and never blocks it inside the ring. Each GPU has a copy stream and a compute stream, and two receive slots of one chunk each. A tick is one chunk of one step. In every tick, each GPU copies a chunk to its right neighbour's slot with cudaMemcpyPeerAsync on its copy stream, then each GPU adds (or copies) the chunk in its own slot into its buffer with a kernel on its compute stream. Four waits keep it correct (lines 82–101):

  • A copy waits until the chunk it sends is final: consumed[r][t−chunks], the add that finished it in the previous step.
  • A copy waits until the neighbour's slot it overwrites is free: consumed[next][t−2], the add that last used that slot.
  • An add waits until its chunk has arrived: copied[prev][t].
  • An add waits until this GPU's own send of the tick has finished reading the buffer: copied[r][t].

The installed CUDA 12.0 header documents that cudaStreamWaitEvent "Makes all future work submitted to stream wait for all work captured in event", and that the event "may be from a different device than stream". H1 That cross-device wait is what lets one host thread pipeline the whole ring.

What a library does that Listing 2 does not

Listing 2 is a teaching ring. Its copies and its adds are separate operations, each with its own launch and event; the chunk size is a constant; it uses one ring in one direction; and its reduction kernel is a plain loop, not your tuned E3 kernel from CU302. Production libraries are generally described as fusing receive, reduce and send into one long-running kernel that moves data through GPU memory or across links directly, using several rings ("channels") at once and tuning the chunk size per message size. How NCCL and RCCL do it is not verified here; the curriculum's milestone F2 asks you to compare against them, not to copy them. C1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Check the NCCL and RCCL documentation and the nccl-tests performance notes.

Choosing the chunk size

From F8-03: each chunk pays a start-up cost α; the per-step time with c chunks of a segment of m bytes is roughly c·α + m/β plus the part of the reduction that is not hidden. Small chunks hide more of the reduction and make the pipeline fill faster; large chunks pay fewer start-ups. A good chunk is large enough that α is a small fraction of its transfer time, yet small enough that a segment has several. Listing 1 uses 64 KiB chunks for the tests and 1 MiB for the large run; measuring the best chunk on your GPUs is a lab step. R1

How the hardware actually does it

A cudaMemcpyPeerAsync between two GPUs is carried out by a copy engine of one GPU and travels over the path between them: a direct link, or the PCIe tree (F8-04). D7 The header itself only promises that the copy "is asynchronous with respect to the host" and is ordered on its stream; it describes no path. H1 Because copy engines and compute units are separate, a copy into the neighbour and a reduction kernel can run at the same time on one GPU, which is the overlap of Figure 1. The reduction itself is memory-bound: it reads two arrays and writes one, so its speed is set by GPU memory bandwidth (CU302), which on most GPUs is far higher than the link's, so it is easy to hide. On CPUs (Listing 1), all of this is threads sharing the same memory and cores, and the "link" is a memory copy.

  1. (a) Segment g = [⌊10g/4⌋, ⌊10(g+1)/4⌋): [0,2), [2,5), [5,7), [7,10).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Cooks see each other; GPUs do not. At the table, you see that the bowl is full. A GPU sees only memory; "the data is there" must be made true by an event or an atomic flag with the right ordering, never by looking.
    • 

  • Bowls do not get rounded. Soup added in a different order is the same soup; floats added in a different order are not the same number (R1).
    • 

  • The table has no host. In Listing 2 one host thread arranges all the work in advance and then waits; no one at the story's table plans everyone's moves before the meal.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the program hangs for some sizes. Cause: ranks disagree on the number of chunks (each computed its own from its own segment). Fix: derive the chunk count from the largest segment, the same on all ranks (Listing 1, lines 70–71).
    • 

  • Symptom: wrong results, but only sometimes. Cause: a handoff without proper synchronisation (forensic lab). How to find it: ThreadSanitizer on the CPU version of the same protocol. Whether a GPU race checker covers ordering between devices and streams was not checked in this build, so build and test the CPU model first.
    • 

  • Symptom: GPU results correct for small sizes, wrong for large. Cause: a missing wait: a slot is overwritten before it was consumed, which only happens when the pipeline has more than two chunks in flight. Fix: list every buffer each operation reads and writes, and the event that protects it (Listing 2's four waits).
    • 

  • Symptom: float results differ from a CPU sum. Cause: different addition order. Fix: test with exact integer-valued data; for random data compare with a tolerance and check that all ranks are bit-identical.
    • 

  • Symptom: busbw lower with more chunks. Cause: start-up cost per chunk dominates. Fix: measure the chunk size sweep (lab step 4).
    •

Summary


    

  • A ring all-reduce is two loops of N−1 steps: send segment (r−s+phase) mod N, receive (r−s−1+phase) mod N, add or copy.
    • 

  • Same chunk count on every rank handles every size; bounded mailboxes give flow control.
    • 

  • Chunking overlaps transfer and reduction; each chunk costs a start-up α.
    • 

  • Test with exact integer-valued data; float results differ from a loop but are identical across ranks.
    • 

  • On GPUs, events and cross-device stream waits order copies and kernels without blocking the host; each buffer needs a named protecting event.
    • 

  • A plain flag is not a handoff: ThreadSanitizer finds it on the CPU model.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Warnings name lines 21 and 27 (writes and reads of ready), 22 and 30 (the vector data: written by assign, read by the copy), and 31 (ready = false) against 21; the callers are lines 52 and 53. The program reports that most of 20 runs gave a wrong result, and the run exits 66. (R3)


Ruling out. (a) The same indices as Listing 1 (lines 50–51 vs 80–81), which passes every test (R1). (d) Values are at most 10·99. (b) and (c) are both real.


Root cause. Bug 1: ready is a plain bool written and read by two threads: a data race, undefined behaviour, and no guarantee that the data written before the flag is visible after it. Bug 2: even with an atomic flag, send sets ready = true on line 21 before copying on line 22. Interleaving 1: the receiver sees the flag between lines 21 and 22 and copies the old or half-assigned vector. Interleaving 2: the receiver resets the flag (line 31) while the sender is still inside assign on line 22; the sender's next send then overwrites the slot before the first data was consumed.


Fix. Minimal: copy first, then ready.store(true, std::memory_order_release) with std::atomic<bool>; receiver loads with memory_order_acquire, copies, then stores false with release; sender waits for false with acquire. Structural: go back to Listing 1's mailbox (mutex and condition variables), which is also flow control. Verification: ThreadSanitizer reports nothing and 20 of 20 runs are right; run many times, since a race can hide. Prevention: keep a CPU thread model of every GPU protocol and run it under ThreadSanitizer in CI. How the evidence was produced: run.sh step race, which trims the full report to frames in ring_race.cc.
