Story hook

The eight kitchen halls of the teaching campus are not built in a row. They stand in two wings. Inside each wing, neighbouring halls share a private passage, wide enough for two carts side by side. Every pair of halls also opens onto a corridor that leads to the wing's central hall, and the two central halls are joined by one long, narrow bridge over the courtyard.

Tonight Joon's team gets four halls for its soup: halls 0, 1, 4 and 5, "because those were free". They run Bilal's ring of cups and it is exactly half as fast as last week, when they had halls 0, 1, 2 and 3. Nothing is broken. The cups are simply crossing the narrow courtyard bridge, twice per lap. The plan was the same; the roads were not. This chapter is about reading the map of roads before you seat the cooks.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe the links inside a multi-GPU machine: PCIe trees with switches and root complexes, the CPU-to-CPU link, and direct GPU-to-GPU links (NVLink and NVSwitch on NVIDIA systems, Infinity Fabric/xGMI on AMD systems).
  • Read a topology matrix: classify each GPU pair's path and find its bottleneck.
  • Compute a ring's bandwidth from its hops' paths, including links shared by several hops, and choose a good ring order and a good GPU placement.
  • Build the milestone F1 tool: a peer access and peer bandwidth matrix with the CUDA runtime (and the HIP equivalent for the attributes).
  • Diagnose an all-reduce running at half speed from a topology matrix and the communication library's chosen path.
Jargon box
Topology (of a node)
Simple words: the map of which parts of a computer are connected to which, and by what kind of link. Analogy: the map of passages, corridors and the courtyard bridge between the halls. Precise: the graph of devices (CPUs, root complexes, PCIe switches, GPUs, NICs) and links (PCIe, CPU-to-CPU, GPU-to-GPU) of a machine; the path between two GPUs and its narrowest link set their peer bandwidth. C1 D9
Root complex (PCIe)
Simple words: the part of the CPU where the PCIe tree starts. Analogy: the wing's central hall that every corridor leads to. Precise: the root of the PCIe hierarchy, connecting the CPU and memory to root ports, below which switches and endpoints sit (F1-45). D9
PCIe switch
Simple words: a box that splits one PCIe connection into several. Analogy: a junction where two halls' corridors join before the central hall. Precise: a PCIe device with one upstream port and several downstream ports; traffic between two of its downstream devices can be routed inside the switch without crossing its upstream link. D9
NVLink / NVSwitch
Simple words: NVIDIA's direct GPU-to-GPU connections, and a switch chip joining many GPUs that way. Analogy: private passages between neighbouring halls, and a hub where many passages meet. Precise: NVIDIA's GPU-to-GPU interconnect and its switch (defined in F1-62). D10
Infinity Fabric / xGMI
Simple words: AMD's direct connections between its GPUs (and CPUs). Analogy: AMD's private passages. Precise: AMD's interconnect family; xGMI is the name of its inter-socket and inter-GPU links (defined in F1-62). D11
Topology matrix
Simple words: a table with one row and one column per GPU, saying how each pair is connected. Analogy: the campus map turned into a distance table. Precise: for every GPU pair, the class of the path between them (direct link, same PCIe switch, through a root complex, across CPU sockets) and, in a measured matrix, its bandwidth and latency; vendor tools print one, and Listing 1 prints the university's own. C1 R1
Ring order
Simple words: the order in which the GPUs pass data around the ring. Analogy: the seating order at the round table. Precise: a cyclic permutation of the ranks; its hops' paths, and the links they share, decide the ring's bandwidth. R1
Peer access (P2P)
Simple words: one GPU reading or writing another GPU's memory directly. Analogy: passing a crate through the passage to the next hall. Precise: direct device-to-device access when the platform allows it; CUDA reports it with cudaDeviceCanAccessPeer and enables it with cudaDeviceEnablePeerAccess, one direction per call (installed header). H1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is TN-1, the university's invented teaching node, used by Listing 1 and by the forensic lab. All its bandwidths are invented teaching values, not those of any product. Its structure follows the generic picture of F1-62: a PCIe tree per CPU socket, a link between the sockets, and direct links between some GPUs.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Inside a big computer, GPUs are not all equally close. Some share a fast private passage. Some must go up to a junction and back down. Some must walk all the way through the CPU, and some even cross from one CPU to the other over a narrow bridge. A ring is only as fast as its slowest road, and if two hops of the ring use the same road at the same time, they share it. So before you choose which GPUs work together, and in which order, look at the map.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The kinds of links

Every GPU in a typical server sits on the PCIe tree of one CPU socket: from the socket's root complex, through zero or more PCIe switches, to the GPU (F1-45, F1-62). D9 Two GPUs under the same switch can exchange data inside the switch; GPUs under different switches of one socket go through the root complex; GPUs on different sockets also cross the link between the CPUs. Many multi-GPU machines add direct GPU-to-GPU links: NVLink, possibly through NVSwitch, on NVIDIA systems, and Infinity Fabric/xGMI links on AMD Instinct systems; these do not use the PCIe tree at all. D10 D11 C1 The curriculum's milestone F1 asks you to explain each of these link types in your own machine's topology view. C1

Not verified — check before relying on this

This chapter gives no lane counts, link counts, link rates or topologies of any real product, and does not say how many NVLink or xGMI links a given GPU has, whether NVSwitch makes all GPU pairs of a node equivalent, or whether a given platform allows PCIe peer-to-peer between GPUs under different root complexes (that depends on the platform and its IOMMU settings). None of this could be read in this build. Check the PCI Express Base Specification, NVIDIA's NVLink and NVSwitch documentation and the architecture whitepaper of your exact GPU, AMD's Instinct platform and CDNA architecture documents, and your machine's own topology output.

Reading a topology matrix

Listing 1 prints TN-1's topology matrix in the university's own notation: for each GPU pair, a class and the bottleneck bandwidth of its path. R1 BR (direct bridge, 50 GB/s) for 0–1, 2–3, 4–5, 6–7; RC (through root complex 0 or 1, 25 GB/s) within a socket; SOCK (12.5 GB/s) for every pair across sockets. There is no SW class in TN-1's matrix: the two GPUs under each switch also have a bridge, and the shortest path prefers it. Vendor tools print matrices in their own notation.

Not verified — check before relying on this

NVIDIA's nvidia-smi has a topology view (nvidia-smi topo -m) that prints a GPU-by-GPU matrix with abbreviations for direct links and for PCIe paths through one switch, several switches, a host bridge or across sockets; AMD's rocm-smi and amd-smi have topology views too. The exact options, abbreviations and their meanings were not read in this build, and none of these tools is installed in it (host_topo.out). Use the legend that your tool prints with its matrix, and the tool's documentation for your driver version.

A ring's bandwidth on a real topology

Every hop r → r+1 of a ring follows a path. All hops send at the same time. A link (in one direction) used by k hops gives each of them 1/k of its bandwidth. The ring runs at the speed of its slowest hop, and for large messages its busbw approaches that speed (F8-03). R1 Listing 1 tries every ring order of a GPU set and prints the best and the worst: R1

  • GPUs 0, 1, 2, 3: best 0→1→2→3→0 at 25 GB/s; worst 0→2→1→3→0 at 12.50 GB/s, because hops 0→2 and 1→3 both climb from SW0 to RC0 and share that link.
  • GPUs 0, 1, 4, 5: best 0→1→4→5→0 at 12.50 GB/s: every ring over two sockets crosses the CPU-to-CPU link twice, once in each direction, and that link is the bottleneck. Worst 6.25 GB/s.
  • All eight GPUs: best 12.50 GB/s (0→…→7→0), worst 3.12 GB/s (0→4→1→5→2→6→3→7→0, crossing sockets on every hop).

So the order matters by a factor of two to four, and the placement (which GPUs) decides the best possible order. With a uniform model and one β, both effects are invisible.

Asking the runtime

The CUDA runtime describes peer links directly. The installed CUDA 12.0 header documents cudaDeviceCanAccessPeer ("Returns in *canAccessPeer a value of 1 if device device is capable of directly accessing memory from peerDevice"), cudaDeviceEnablePeerAccess (access "is unidirectional", a symmetric call is needed for the other direction), cudaMemcpyPeerAsync for copies between devices, and cudaDeviceGetP2PAttribute with cudaDevP2PAttrPerformanceRank, "A relative value indicating the performance of the link between two devices. Lower value means better performance (0 being the value used for most performant link)". H1 The HIP 5.7 header has the same functions with the hip prefix, but a different enumeration: hipDevP2PAttrPerformanceRank = 0, while CUDA's cudaDevP2PAttrPerformanceRank is 1 (p2p_enums.out). H2 R3 Use the names, never the numbers, when porting.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

In one line of analogy: the campus map says which roads exist; the stopwatch says how fast they are today; the library's planner decides which roads the cups take; you are responsible for checking all three.

The F1 milestone: measure, do not assume

The curriculum's milestone F1 is: "Know your machine's GPU topology and its measured link bandwidths and latencies", built as "a peer bandwidth and latency matrix tool in your harness (unidirectional and bidirectional)", accepted when "Your matrix agrees with p2pBandwidthLatencyTest (cuda-samples) or the ROCm bandwidth test within 10 %" and "The log explains each link type (PCIe switch, CPU root complex, NVLink, xGMI) seen in the topology view". C1 Listing 2 is the bandwidth part of that tool. It times copies of 64 MiB with cudaMemcpyPeerAsync and events, one direction and both directions at once, median of 9 after a warm-up. The latency part is the same with small messages (an exercise). On this build it stops at cudaGetDeviceCount: there is no GPU (R2).

Shared links and bidirectional traffic

A ring uses each hop in one direction; an all-reduce on two rings in opposite directions, or a bidirectional test, uses both. PCIe and most GPU links carry traffic in both directions at once, so a bidirectional measurement can show close to twice the unidirectional figure, unless something in the path is shared or half-duplex (F1-62). D9 Listing 1 models each direction of a link separately for this reason: the two crossings of the CPU link in the job 0, 1, 4, 5 go in opposite directions and do not share; the two climbs from SW0 in the worst 0–3 ring go in the same direction and do. R1

Placement is a scheduler decision

Who chose GPUs 0, 1, 4, 5? Usually a cluster scheduler or a launcher, from the GPUs that were free. Once a job holds GPUs on two sockets, no ring order can avoid the slow link; only the placement can (DG403, DS303). A topology-aware allocator prefers GPUs that share a switch or a direct link. Libraries can also build several rings, or trees, over different links at once; how NCCL and RCCL do it is their documented behaviour, not verified here.

Not verified — check before relying on this

That NCCL detects the node's topology (PCIe tree, NVLink, NICs), builds several rings or trees ("channels") over it, can print the detected topology and its chosen rings when its debug output is enabled (an environment variable such as NCCL_DEBUG=INFO, with further subsystem filters), and can dump the detected topology to a file; and that RCCL behaves similarly: none of this could be read or run in this build. Check the NCCL documentation (environment variables, troubleshooting) and the RCCL documentation for your versions, and paste a real log into your lab record.

CPU memory and NUMA

The two sockets' root complexes are also two NUMA nodes (F5-28). A process that drives GPU 4 from a CPU core of socket 0, with its host buffers in socket 0's memory, sends its host-to-GPU copies across the CPU link too. Pin each GPU's process to the socket of its GPU. This container has one socket and one NUMA node (host_topo.out), so it cannot show the effect. R3

How the hardware actually does it

A peer copy over PCIe travels as memory-write transactions from the source GPU's DMA engine (or its threads, with peer access) up the tree to the lowest common switch or root complex, and down to the destination GPU's memory; across sockets, the CPUs' interconnect carries them between root complexes. D9 Direct GPU links carry loads, stores and atomics between GPU memory systems without touching the PCIe tree (F1-62). D10 D11 cudaDevP2PAttrNativeAtomicSupported exists because native atomics over a link are a property of the link (installed header: "Native atomic operation over the link supported"). H1 The container of this build has no GPU and a flat virtual PCI bus with virtio devices only (host_topo.out): a reminder that a topology view is only as good as the machine you run it on. R3

Worked example

Task. On TN-1, a job gets GPUs 0, 2, 4, 6. (a) Classify every ring hop of the order 0→2→4→6→0. (b) Which links do the hops share, and what is the ring's bandwidth? (c) Is there a better order of the same GPUs? (d) What placement of four GPUs is best?

  1. (a) 0→2: RC (SW0→RC0→SW1); 2→4: SOCK (SW1→RC0→RC1→SW2); 4→6: RC (SW2→RC1→SW3); 6→0: SOCK (SW3→RC1→RC0→SW0).
  2. (b) Directed links: RC0→RC1 is used by 2→4 only, RC1→RC0 by 6→0 only; RC0→SW1 only by 0→2 (2→4 climbs the other direction, SW1→RC0). No directed link is shared. The slowest link on any hop is the CPU link: 12.5 GB/s.
  3. (c) Any order of these four GPUs crosses the sockets at least twice (once each way), so 12.5 GB/s is the best; orders that cross four times, such as 0→4→2→6→0, share the CPU link and drop to 6.25 GB/s (Lab Engineer's check run of this job: best 0→2→4→6→0 at 12.50, worst 0→4→2→6→0 at 6.25).
  4. (d) Four GPUs of one socket, ordered so that bridge pairs are neighbours: 0→1→2→3→0 at 25 GB/s (Listing 1, first job).

Check (b) and (c) by adding the line 0 2 4 6 to topo_sim.in (lab step 2).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Roads have two lanes. A corridor in the story is used by one cart at a time; most links carry full traffic in each direction at once, so two hops crossing in opposite directions do not slow each other (Listing 1 models this).
    • 

  • The map can lie. Halls are where the map says; a GPU's PCIe link may run at a lower speed or width than its slot allows (a card in the wrong slot, a riser, power saving), so the measured matrix can disagree with the topology view. That is why F1 requires a measurement.
    • 

  • Bridges can be switched. An NVSwitch-style hub connects many GPUs over direct links at once, which no courtyard bridge does; the "distance" between GPUs may then be the same for all pairs (unverified above).
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: all-reduce busbw is half of what the same job reached last week. Cause: different GPUs allocated, now across sockets (forensic lab). How to find it: the job log's GPU list against the topology matrix. Fix: topology-aware placement.
    • 

  • Symptom: the peer matrix is not symmetric. Cause: peer access enabled in one direction only (it is unidirectional, H1), or a different path per direction. Fix: enable both directions; measure both.
    • 

  • Symptom: cudaDeviceEnablePeerAccess fails with "already enabled" on the second call. Cause: calling it twice. Fix: tolerate cudaErrorPeerAccessAlreadyEnabled (Listing 2, lines 82–86).
    • 

  • Symptom: a ported HIP program reports nonsense link ranks. Cause: a numeric enum value copied from CUDA code (1 ≠ 0, R3). Fix: use the HIP names.
    • 

  • Symptom: host-to-GPU copies of one process are slow, those of others are fine. Cause: process or memory on the other socket. Fix: bind processes to the GPU's NUMA node.
    •

Summary


    

  • GPUs in a node connect through PCIe switches and root complexes, across CPU sockets, or over direct links (NVLink/NVSwitch, xGMI).
    • 

  • A topology matrix classifies each pair's path; its bottleneck sets the pair's bandwidth.
    • 

  • A ring runs at its slowest hop; hops that share a directed link share its bandwidth.
    • 

  • Ring order changed TN-1's ring bandwidth by up to 4×; placement across sockets halved the best possible.
    • 

  • The runtime reports peer access and link attributes; HIP and CUDA enum values differ: use names.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Pairs 1–4 and 5–0 are SOCK (12.5). The planner's best ring for 0, 1, 4, 5 is 0→1→4→5→0 at 12.50 GB/s; slowest hop GPU1>SW0>RC0>RC1>SW2>GPU4 (SOCK). For 0, 1, 2, 3 the best is 25.00 GB/s. The large-message busbw is 24.907 vs 12.477 GB/s; at 8 KiB both are about 0.2 GB/s (0.203 vs 0.201). (R1, R4)


Root cause. The scheduler placed the job across the two sockets. Every ring over two sockets has two hops through both root complexes and the CPU-to-CPU link, whose bandwidth (12.5, invented) is half of the PCIe paths' 25 within a socket. The ring runs at its slowest hop, so the large-message busbw halves.


Ruling out the others. (a) Library version: the same model with only the placement changed reproduces the halving. (b) A degraded link: the measured curve's plateau equals the CPU link's bandwidth exactly, and the topology explains it; a peer matrix measurement would confirm the link rates. (d) Message sizes: at small sizes the two curves are equal (latency-bound); the difference appears only where bandwidth dominates.


Fix. Rerun on GPUs of one socket (0–3 or 4–7) with the ring order following bridges (0→1→2→3). For the cluster: topology-aware GPU allocation that keeps small jobs inside one socket or one direct-link group. Verification: rerun the benchmark; the large-message busbw should return near the within-socket ring bandwidth. Prevention: log the GPU list and the library's chosen rings in every job; alert when busbw falls below a threshold set from the topology. How the evidence was produced: Listing 1 with topo_sim.in, and busbw_curve.cpp, both run by run_lab.sh; no real library log exists in this build (unverified box).
