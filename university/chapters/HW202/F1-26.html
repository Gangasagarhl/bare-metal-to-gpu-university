Story hook

On a quiet Monday the restaurant has one cook on the line, Tomás. He takes a ticket, reads it, preps the ingredients, cooks, plates, and only then takes the next ticket. Every dish is perfect, and the queue of tickets grows longer and longer.

On Friday the owner tries something new. Five people stand in a row of stations: one takes tickets off the rail, one reads them and lays out ingredients, one cooks, one fetches from the pantry, one plates. Every time a small bell rings, each dish slides one station to the right. No single dish is ready any sooner; in fact each dish now waits a little at every hand-over. But once the row is full, a finished plate comes out at every bell. The queue of tickets shrinks.

Then the owner notices two things. The pantry station is the slowest, so the bell can only ring as fast as the pantry allows. And at the start of the evening, the first plate still takes five bells. This chapter is about that row of stations, the pipeline, and about the honest arithmetic of how much faster it really is.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain pipelining with the precise words throughput, latency, stage and pipeline register.
  • Draw a pipeline diagram (instructions against clock cycles) for a short program and count its cycles.
  • Calculate the speed-up of a pipeline from stage delays, including the slowest stage, register overhead and fill time.
  • Name the three kinds of hazard that stop a pipeline from reaching one instruction per cycle.
  • Diagnose why a pipeline is less than "five times faster" from timing evidence.
Jargon box
Pipelining
Simple words: working on several instructions at once, each at a different step. Analogy: five kitchen stations in a row; at every bell each dish moves one station on. Precise: an implementation technique in which the execution of an instruction is split into stages, and successive instructions overlap, one per stage. D1 D2
Pipeline stage
Simple words: one step of the instruction's journey, done in one clock cycle. Analogy: one station in the row. Precise: one part of the datapath between two pipeline registers; the classic five are IF, ID, EX, MEM, WB. D1
Pipeline register
Simple words: a set of storage cells between two stages that holds one instruction's values for the next stage. Analogy: the tray at the hand-over between two stations. Precise: a clocked register (IF/ID, ID/EX, EX/MEM, MEM/WB) that captures the outputs of one stage, including the instruction's control signals, at each clock edge. D1 D2
Throughput
Simple words: how many jobs finish per unit of time. Analogy: plates out of the kitchen per hour. Precise: the rate of completed instructions, for example instructions per cycle or per second. D1
Latency
Simple words: how long one job takes from start to finish. Analogy: the time from a ticket's arrival to its plate leaving. Precise: the time from the start to the completion of one instruction; pipelining does not reduce it and usually increases it slightly. D1
CPI (cycles per instruction)
Simple words: on average, how many clock ticks each instruction costs. Analogy: bells per plate, averaged over the evening. Precise: total clock cycles divided by instructions executed; an ideal scalar pipeline approaches 1. D1
Hazard
Simple words: a situation where the next instruction cannot go ahead in the next cycle. Analogy: a station that cannot start because it is missing something. Precise: a condition that prevents the next instruction from executing in its designated cycle; the kinds are structural, data and control hazards. D1 D3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A processor that does one whole instruction at a time leaves most of its parts idle. While the ALU is adding, the part that fetches instructions has nothing to do. Pipelining puts every part to work at the same time, each on a different instruction.

Picture the row of kitchen stations again. At every bell, every dish moves one station along. While dish 3 is being cooked, dish 4 is being prepared and dish 5 is being read. No single dish gets faster. But plates come out much more often, one per bell once the row is full.

There is a catch. The bell can only ring as fast as the slowest station. If the pantry needs more time than the stove, everyone waits for the pantry. And at the very start, the row is empty: the first plate still needs five bells. So a five-station row is less than five times faster, and the lab will tell you exactly how much less.

There is a second catch. Sometimes a dish needs something that the dish in front of it has not finished yet. Then the row has to wait. Those waits are called hazards, and the next two chapters are all about them.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Stages and pipeline registers

The five regions of the single-cycle datapath become five stages: IF (fetch), ID (decode and register read), EX (ALU operation or address calculation), MEM (data memory access) and WB (write back to the register file). D1 D2 Between stages, pipeline registers capture each stage's results at the clock edge, so in the next cycle the following stage works on them while the earlier stage starts on the next instruction. D1 Everything an instruction needs later must travel with it in these registers: its operand values, its destination register number and its control signals. If the control signals were not carried along, the MEM stage would use the signals of the instruction currently in ID, a different instruction. D1

Counting cycles

With k stages and no hazards, the first instruction finishes after k cycles and then one more finishes every cycle, so n instructions take k + (n − 1) cycles. D1 For the five-stage pipeline that is n + 4. Listing 2's real runs agree: six instructions (five addi and halt) take 10 cycles, and the 30 executed instructions of the sum program take 34 cycles in the ideal mode. R1 R2 The CPI is cycles divided by instructions: 34 / 30 ≈ 1.13, which approaches 1 as programs get longer.

From cycles to time

Fewer cycles per instruction is only half the story; the length of a cycle matters too. Execution time = instructions × CPI × clock period. D1 Pipelining raises CPI from 1 (single-cycle) to a bit more than 1 but shortens the clock period, because one cycle now only has to cover one stage. The period is set by the slowest stage plus the delay of the pipeline register, so unbalanced stages and register overhead keep the speed-up below the number of stages. D1 D2 Listing 3 computes this for made-up stage delays.

Why real pipelines do not reach CPI = 1

The ideal count assumes each instruction can always enter the next stage. Three kinds of hazard break that assumption: structural hazards (two instructions need the same hardware in the same cycle), data hazards (an instruction needs a result that an earlier instruction has not yet written) and control hazards (the pipeline does not yet know which instruction comes after a branch). D1 D3 U16 avoids the classic structural hazard by having separate instruction and data memories, so IF and MEM never compete. Data hazards are F1-27; control hazards are F1-27 and F1-28. In the ideal mode of Listing 2 the model ignores all of them, which is why its diagrams look perfect.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Speed-up formula. With stage delays d1…dk, register overhead r and n instructions, single-cycle time is n·Σd and pipelined time is (k + n − 1)·(max d + r). For large n the speed-up tends to Σd / (max d + r), which equals k only if all stages are equal and r = 0. D1 R3
  • Deeper pipelines. Splitting stages further shortens the period but adds register overhead per stage and makes every hazard cost more cycles (a branch resolved in stage 10 wastes more fetches than one resolved in stage 3). There is therefore a depth beyond which performance falls. D3
  • Register-file timing trick. Textbook five-stage pipelines write the register file in the first half of a cycle and read it in the second half, so an instruction in ID can read a value being written by an instruction in WB in the same cycle. The model in Listing 1 uses this rule. D1 R1
  • A model, not a circuit. Listing 1 does not simulate pipeline registers gate by gate. It executes the program with the U16 machine and then computes, from stated rules, the cycle in which each instruction enters each stage. This is a standard way to study timing; the next step would be a cycle-accurate HDL pipeline, a good mini-project for HW201 graduates. R1
Not verified — check before relying on this

The stage delays of Listing 3 (IF 200, ID 100, EX 200, MEM 250, WB 100, overhead 20) are invented "delay units", not measurements of any circuit. The number of pipeline stages of real processors and the claim that very deep pipelines lose performance are taken from D1 and D3 as named: their chapters were located in the verification pass, but the books' text was not opened, so no number of stages of any real processor is given. The pipeline itself is a timing model computed from rules (Layer 3), not a gate-level simulation.

How the hardware actually does it

A pipeline register is a bank of flip-flops (F1-16) clocked by the same clock as every other register (F1-18). At each rising edge, all four pipeline registers capture their inputs at once; between edges, the combinational logic of each stage computes from the values its left-hand register holds. D2 So "an instruction moves one stage right" really means "the values describing it are copied from one register to the next at the edge". To hold an instruction in place (a stall), the hardware disables the write of a pipeline register and inserts a "do nothing" instruction, a bubble, into the next stage; to cancel one (a flush), it clears that instruction's control signals to zero so it changes no state. D1 The model shows stalls as lower-case stage names and flushes as xx.

Worked example

Task. Using Listing 3's stage delays (IF 200, ID 100, EX 200, MEM 250, WB 100, register overhead 20, in made-up delay units, du), compare 1,000 instructions on the single-cycle and pipelined machines.

  1. Single-cycle period. All stages in one cycle: 200 + 100 + 200 + 250 + 100 = 850 du.
  2. Pipelined period. The slowest stage plus the register: 250 + 20 = 270 du.
  3. Single-cycle time. 1,000 × 850 = 850,000 du.
  4. Pipelined cycles. 5 + (1,000 − 1) = 1,004 cycles; time 1,004 × 270 = 271,080 du.
  5. Speed-up. 850,000 / 271,080 ≈ 3.14.
  6. Limit for very long programs. 850 / 270 ≈ 3.15. Not 5, because MEM is slower than the average stage and every cycle pays the register overhead.

The real run of Listing 3 prints the same numbers in its 1000 row (271080 and 3.14). R3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A dish is a physical thing; an instruction is a set of values. Nothing travels: at each clock edge every pipeline register copies new values in. Two "copies" of an instruction's data can exist at once in different registers (its result in EX/MEM and again in MEM/WB). D2
    • 

  • Kitchen stations can be a little early or late; pipeline stages cannot. All stages move at the same clock edge. A station that finishes early still waits for the edge; there is no "sliding along when ready". D1
    • 

  • A cook can see the dish ahead; a stage sees only its own register. If a dish needs something from the one in front, a cook would shout. Hardware needs explicit extra wires (forwarding) and detection logic for that, which F1-27 adds. D1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: expecting "5 stages = 5× faster". Cause: ignoring the slowest stage, register overhead and fill time. How to find it: compute Σd / (max d + r). Fix: use the formula; balance stages. (Forensic lab.)
    • 

  • Symptom: thinking pipelining makes each instruction faster. Cause: mixing up latency and throughput. How to find it: in the run, the first addi still needs 5 cycles. Fix: say "more instructions per second", never "faster instructions".
    • 

  • Symptom: a diagram with two instructions in the same stage in the same cycle. Cause: forgetting that each stage holds one instruction. How to find it: check every column has each stage name at most once. Fix: shift the later instruction right.
    • 

  • Symptom: counting n × 5 cycles. Cause: treating the pipeline as single-cycle with five steps. Fix: n + 4 for the ideal five-stage pipeline.
    •

Summary


    

  • Pipelining overlaps instructions: each stage works on a different instruction every cycle.
    • 

  • Pipeline registers between stages hold each instruction's values and control signals.
    • 

  • Ideal cycle count: k + n − 1; for five stages, n + 4 (real run: 30 instructions, 34 cycles).
    • 

  • Throughput improves; the latency of one instruction does not.
    • 

  • The clock period is the slowest stage plus register overhead, so speed-up is below the number of stages.
    • 

  • Short programs gain little or lose, because of pipeline fill.
    • 

  • Structural, data and control hazards keep CPI above 1; the next chapters measure and reduce them.

Forensic lab answer key


Root cause. Two effects. (1) The clock period is set by the slowest stage (MEM, 250 du) plus the register overhead (20 du), so even for very long runs the speed-up is 850 / 270 ≈ 3.15, not 5. (2) For short runs, filling the pipeline dominates: five instructions take 5 + 4 = 9 cycles of 270 du = 2,430 du, compared with 4,250 du single-cycle (1.75×); a one-instruction test is slower (0.63×).


Evidence. Listing 3's real output: periods 850 and 270; speed-ups 0.63 (n = 1), 1.75 (n = 5), 3.14 (n = 1000), 3.15 (n = 1,000,000). Ms Okafor measured latency: one instruction now takes 5 × 270 = 1,350 du instead of 850 du (the n = 1 row). Her measurement is right, and so is a throughput gain: 1.75× for five instructions, 3.15× in the limit. The team's mistake is the claim "5×", not the pipeline.


Fix. Balance the stages (for example split MEM, or move work out of it), reduce register overhead, and benchmark with long programs. Balanced 170-du stages give 850 / 190 ≈ 4.47 (lab step 4).


How the evidence was made. The Lab Engineer chose delays with one slow stage and ran Listing 3; no fault was injected into code, the "fault" is the team's claim.


Wrong answers and why. "Hazards": the delay model has none, and the shortfall appears anyway. "The pipeline needs more stages": more stages without balancing do not move the slowest stage. Prevention: report speed-up with the formula, the measured n and both clock periods, never as "k stages = k×".
