Exam papers of HW202 The CPU: instruction sets, datapaths and pipelines (level L2, 4 credits), written under owner ruling B4 by the Exam Writer and Lab Engineer pass. Everything here is answerable from chapters F1-23 to F1-31 and their lab listings. U16 is the course's 16-bit teaching ISA (F1-23); every pipeline number is a result of the course's rule-based timing model of F1-26 and F1-27 (a teaching stand-in, not a real processor); every stage delay in "delay units" (du) is an exercise value (owner ruling A4), not a property of a real part. Nothing in these papers was run on hardware (ruling C3); the real ARM64 and RISC-V code quoted was compiled and disassembled only, not executed.

Quizzes (Q)

Each chapter's "Check yourself" is its quiz: eight questions per chapter, each set with an "explain in your own words", an "apply to a new case" and, as the L2 rule of guide 11.3 requires, a "predict, then run" question checked against a real run. Learners answer in writing after reading the chapter and before its lab, then mark themselves against the chapter's answers; a mentor checks the "in your own words" and "predict, then run" answers with the generic rubric of guide 11.4. The nine quizzes are worth 10 % together (about 1.1 % each).

  • Quiz 1 — F1-23 Check yourself (ISA versus microarchitecture; decoding and encoding U16 words; the immediate's range; branch targets; fixed- and variable-length real encodings).
  • Quiz 2 — F1-24 Check yourself (the five regions; tracing sw through the datapath; the write-back multiplexer; reading and writing one register in a cycle; the single-cycle clock period; adding jr).
  • Quiz 3 — F1-25 Check yourself (the control row of lw; why the control unit is combinational; "don't care" entries; the exhaustive diff; the branch condition; hardwired versus microprogrammed; a row for sll).
  • Quiz 4 — F1-26 Check yourself (throughput and latency; k + n − 1; control signals in pipeline registers; the speed-up limit; the three hazards; splitting a stage).
  • Quiz 5 — F1-27 Check yourself (stalls with and without forwarding; r0; which pipeline register forwards; the load-use bubble; taken-branch cost; the multiply program's cycles).
  • Quiz 6 — F1-28 Check yourself (why predict; loop branches under three predictors; cycles lost; the BTB; sorted versus unsorted data; an alternating branch).
  • Quiz 7 — F1-29 Check yourself (superscalar versus out-of-order; the dependence bound; the reorder buffer; renaming; IPC; four running sums; a product tree).
  • Quiz 8 — F1-30 Check yourself (why multicore; shared variables made safe; a lost-update interleaving; what coherence does not guarantee; Amdahl's law; races and repeated runs).
  • Quiz 9 — F1-31 Check yourself (the disassembly command; where arguments and results live; -O0 versus -O2; AT&T to Intel syntax; conditional moves; vectorised code; a benchmark that vanished).

Final (F)

Cumulative over F1-23 to F1-31, with one forensic question whose evidence comes from real runs in university/labs/HW202-P/. Weight 25 %. Time: 150 minutes. Allowed: a calculator and one page of your own notes; no computer. Written answers are marked with the generic rubric of guide 11.4 (0–4, scaled to the points shown); the forensic question with the rubric of guide 11.2. 100 points. The pipeline is always the course model: five stages; without forwarding a reader's EX comes after the writer's WB; with forwarding an ALU result can be used in the next cycle's EX and a load's value one cycle later (one bubble when the very next instruction uses it); a taken branch is resolved at the end of EX and costs 2 cycles; cycles = instructions + 4 + data-stall cycles + branch cycles.

Part E — forensic question: "The fast loop that lies" (18 points)

The routine raises every reading below 20 to 20, in place, and then outputs the sum of the six readings. After F1-27 he moved one instruction up "to fill the load-use bubble", exactly as the chapter did for the slow loop. The profile improved from 113 to 107 cycles with zero data stalls, and he merged it. The next day the dashboard shows a sum of 85 instead of the usual 236. Ravi says: "My change cannot have done that: the pipeline model shows the same 77 instructions, no stalls, and only one line moved. Today's sensor data must be different."

Evidence 1. The routine as it was (clamp.s):

; clamp.s - raise every reading below 20 to 20 (in place), then output the sum of the readings
;           data 12 75 4 51 9 50 -> after the clamp 20 75 20 51 20 50, sum 236

        addi r1, r0, 0      ; r1 = address of the next reading
        addi r2, r0, 6      ; r2 = readings left
        addi r6, r0, 20     ; r6 = the floor, 20

        slt  r5, r4, r6     ; r5 = 1 if reading < 20

        sw   r6, 0(r1)      ; replace the reading by the floor
keep:   addi r1, r1, 1      ; next address
        addi r2, r2, -1     ; one reading fewer
        bne  r2, r0, loop
        addi r1, r0, 0      ; second loop: add up the six readings
        addi r2, r0, 6
        addi r3, r0, 0
sum:    lw   r4, 0(r1)
        addi r1, r1, 1
        add  r3, r3, r4
        addi r2, r2, -1
        bne  r2, r0, sum
        out  r3
        halt

Practical (P) — hand-trace a short program through a five-stage pipeline

Weight 25 %. Time limit: 120 minutes, at a computer with g++ (the course flags of run_lab.sh). This is the course card's practical, "hand-trace a short program through a 5-stage pipeline", followed by the check that engineers would make: put the hazard rules into code and compare. Everything is the course's timing model; no hardware is used.

Allowed documentation: the HW202 chapters and their lab folders (including labs/F1-23/u16.h, labs/F1-24/cpu.cpp, labs/F1-26/pipe.h and labs/F1-27/hazards.cpp for reading), the g++ manual, your own notes. You may not include pipe.h or run hazards.cpp on the exam program before Part 1 is handed in; Part 1 is collected after 40 minutes.

Starting files

Candidates receive only pipe_exam_start.cpp and pipe_exam_start.in from university/labs/HW202-P/ (ruling B7), placed in a folder next to the course's labs/F1-23 (the start file includes ../F1-23/u16.h). pipe_exam_start.in is the exam program range.s: it outputs the difference between the largest and the smallest of four readings (14 3 27 9) and stores it at data address 4. pipe_exam_start.cpp is a five-stage timing model built on the U16 machine; the scheduler and the diagram printer are given, and four functions marked TODO (7 points each in the file) are empty: the registers an instruction reads, the earliest EX cycle of a reader, the load-use test, and CPI with the accounting identity. As handed out it builds with the course flags, prints wrong diagrams and ends its self-check (the sum program of F1-23, whose numbers F1-27 prints: 62 / 47 cycles) with self-check: 7 failures and exit code 1. That is the expected starting state.

The task

  1. Part 1 — hand trace, on paper (40 minutes, handed in before you compile anything). (a) Draw the pipeline diagram of the first 16 rows of range.s as the model prints them (executed instructions and flushed fetches, in order) with forwarding: for every row, the cycle of IF, ID, EX, MEM, WB, with stalls as lower-case cells and flushed fetches marked. (b) The same rows without forwarding. (c) For the whole run with forwarding: the number of executed instructions, of taken branches, of data-stall cycles (name each load-use pair) and the total cycles from the accounting identity; and the CPI.
  2. Part 2 — the rules in code (50 minutes). Complete the four TODO functions of pipe_exam_start.cpp and nothing else. Build with g++ -std=c++20 -Wall -Wextra -Wpedantic -Werror -g -fsanitize=address,undefined pipe_exam_start.cpp -o pipe_exam_start and run ./pipe_exam_start 16 < pipe_exam_start.in until the self-check prints self-check: 0 failures and the summary lines say identity holds. Then compare the printed diagrams and totals with your Part 1, cell by cell: for every difference, say whether your hand trace or a rule was wrong, and why (do not change Part 1).
  3. Part 3 — remove the stalls (20 minutes). Reorder range.s (as range_sched.s) so that it has no data-stall cycle with forwarding and still outputs the same value and stores it at address 4. Run it through your completed model and through the U16 computer of F1-24 (labs/F1-24/cpu.cpp), and record both outputs. Say which cycles your scheduling cannot remove, and why.
  4. Log (throughout, about 10 minutes in all). A short written record: each build or self-check failure, what it said, and what you changed.

Course project — a working simulated CPU running a program you wrote in its machine language

Weight 20 %. Brief per guide 11.5. The course card's project is built in the steps the chapters' mini-projects and labs already define: design an extra instruction (F1-23 mini-project), add it to the CPU and run machine-code programs (F1-24 lab and mini-project), give it its control row and check the control table (F1-25), and measure and schedule your programs on the pipeline model (F1-27 mini-project).

Goal

Extend the course's simulated U16 CPU with one instruction of your own design in an unused opcode (12–15), and run on it at least three programs that you wrote directly in U16 machine language (hex words), with a regression test proving the original instructions still work and a pipeline measurement of your programs.

Requirements

  • Functional: in a copy of u16.h, the new instruction in the opcode list and names, the encoder and the disassembler, the control function and Machine::step; its effect defined for every case, including r0 as destination. Programs, each a hex file with one word per line and a comment giving its assembly and fields: (1) the first eight Fibonacci numbers with out, in a loop; (2) a program that uses your new instruction (for example 5 × 8 = 40 with a shift); (3) one program of your own with a loop, a load and a store.
  • Tests: a checker that re-encodes every hand-written word from its commented assembly and reports any difference; each program's output compared with its expected value; the regression test that F1-23's sum program still outputs 29 in 30 instructions; the control row of the new opcode printed in F1-25's table layout and compared with your design.
  • Quality: builds with the course flags (-std=c++20 -Wall -Wextra -Wpedantic -Werror -g -fsanitize=address,undefined) with no warning and no sanitizer report; exit code 0 only when every check passes.
  • Measurement: for every program that uses only base opcodes (or with a model you extended), the cycles, data stalls and branch cycles with and without forwarding from the course pipeline model, and a scheduled version with the identity checked.

Constraints

Hardware: none; everything runs on a computer with g++ (the course toolchain). Time: about ten hours, spread over F1-23 to F1-27. Programs are written as hex words: an assembler may be used only to check your words, not to produce them.

Deliverables

  1. The extended header and the CPU program, with a header comment giving the new instruction's format, encoding and exact effect.
  2. The hex programs, the checker and the unedited outputs of all runs, with the build commands and the compiler version as printed by the compiler.
  3. A pipeline table for your programs (model results, labelled as such), with the scheduled versions and proof that their outputs are unchanged.
  4. A two-page design note: the instruction, its encoding and control row, what the ISA gains and what it costs the hardware (F1-23 mini-project), and which instruction sets the clock period of a single-cycle U16 (F1-24 mini-project).
  5. A five-minute demo: run one program, read one line of its datapath trace aloud, and answer one question about the encoding.
