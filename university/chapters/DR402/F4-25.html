Story hook

The new building's lockers are numbered differently. In the old school every class used a three-level map: corridor, then row, then locker. Here the map has the same idea with different numbers on it, and a new rule painted on every locker door: some lockers may be opened and read but never written in, some may be written in but must never hold anything that is acted on as instructions. The doorbell panel is different too. In the old school each doorbell had a wire to the office; here there is a front desk that receives every ring from outside, a small desk outside each teacher's room for that teacher's own bells, and a hatch in each teacher's wall through which the ring is finally passed. A teacher who answers a bell must say at the hatch "I have finished with that one", or the hatch stays closed for everything less urgent.

And the clock: the building has one master clock that counts steadily, and each teacher has a small alarm that rings when the master clock passes a number the teacher wrote down. A teacher who answers the alarm and forgets to write down the next number will hear it ring again, immediately, forever. This chapter builds all three for the AArch64 kernel of F4-24: translation tables with write-xor-execute permissions, the GICv3 interrupt controller, and the generic timer as a 10 ms scheduler tick, with a device interrupt from the UART to prove the whole path. That is the arch half of milestone D2.

What you will be able to do

  • Build VMSAv8-64 translation tables (4 KiB granule, 39-bit addresses) with blocks, pages and per-section permissions, and turn on the MMU and caches.
  • Program MAIR_EL1, TCR_EL1, TTBR0_EL1 and SCTLR_EL1, and explain each field you set.
  • Walk a translation in software and predict translation and permission faults before they happen.
  • Bring up a GICv3: distributor, the right redistributor for this CPU, the CPU interface through system registers; enable a PPI and an SPI.
  • Use the generic timer as a periodic tick, with the frequency read from CNTFRQ_EL0.
  • Diagnose an interrupt storm from a log, and refuse an unsupported interrupt controller clearly.
Jargon box
Translation table (AArch64)
Simple words: the tables the MMU reads to turn a virtual address into a physical one. Analogy: the map of lockers: locker number to real cupboard. Precise: in VMSAv8-64 with a 4 KiB granule, tables of 512 eight-byte descriptors; each level resolves 9 bits of the address; a descriptor is invalid, a table pointer, a block (1 GiB at level 1, 2 MiB at level 2) or a page (4 KiB at level 3). D1
MAIR (memory attribute indirection register)
Simple words: a register holding up to eight kinds of memory, which descriptors refer to by number. Analogy: a legend on the locker map: colour 0 means "fragile, handle in order", colour 1 means "ordinary shelf". Precise: MAIR_EL1 has eight 8-bit attribute fields; a descriptor's AttrIndx (bits 4:2) selects one; this kernel uses 0x00 (Device-nGnRnE) at index 0 and 0xff (Normal, write-back cacheable) at index 1. D1
W^X (write xor execute)
Simple words: no memory may be both writable and executable. Analogy: a locker is either for notes you may change, or for the instructions you follow, never both. Precise: a mapping policy: code pages read-only and executable, data pages writable and execute-never (PXN/UXN), read-only data neither writable nor executable. D1
GIC (Generic Interrupt Controller)
Simple words: Arm's standard interrupt controller. Analogy: the doorbell panel: front desk, desks outside each teacher's room, a hatch in each wall. Precise: in GICv3, a distributor for shared interrupts, one redistributor per CPU for its private interrupts, and a CPU interface reached through ICC_* system registers; interrupt IDs 0–15 are SGIs, 16–31 PPIs, 32 and up SPIs. D2
SGI, PPI, SPI
Simple words: the three kinds of ordinary GIC interrupt: from software, private to one CPU, shared. Analogy: a note one teacher slips under another's door; a teacher's own alarm; the front door's bell. Precise: software-generated interrupts (IDs 0–15, sent by writing ICC_SGI1R_EL1), private peripheral interrupts (16–31, one copy per CPU, such as the timer), shared peripheral interrupts (32–1019, routed to a CPU by the distributor). D2
Generic timer
Simple words: the architecture's built-in clock and per-CPU alarms. Analogy: the master clock and each teacher's alarm. Precise: a system counter read through CNTPCT_EL0 at the frequency in CNTFRQ_EL0, plus per-CPU timers such as the EL1 physical timer (CNTP_TVAL_EL0, CNTP_CTL_EL0) whose interrupt is a PPI. D1
Interrupt storm
Simple words: an interrupt that fires again as soon as it is handled, forever. Analogy: the alarm that rings again because nobody set the next time. Precise: a level-triggered source whose condition is never cleared: the controller signals it again immediately after end-of-interrupt, and the CPU does nothing else. D2 R5

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

The locker map (page table) tells the school which real cupboard each locker number means, in three steps: corridor, row, locker. Each locker door also carries a rule: read only, or read and write, and whether its contents may be followed as instructions. The doorbell panel has a front desk for bells from outside (the distributor), a desk outside each teacher's room for that teacher's own bells (the redistributor) and a hatch into the room (the CPU interface). A teacher who answers must say "finished" at the hatch. The master clock counts steadily; each teacher's alarm rings when the clock passes the number written on it.

Mapping: locker map = translation tables; rule on the locker door = permission bits; front desk = GIC distributor; desk outside the room = redistributor; hatch = CPU interface; saying "finished" = end of interrupt; master clock = system counter; a teacher's alarm = the per-CPU timer.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

In F3-21 you walked x86-64's four-level page tables. AArch64's tables follow the same idea with different parameters. With a 4 KiB granule each table has 512 eight-byte entries, so each level resolves 9 bits of the address. The kernel chooses how many address bits to translate with a field of TCR_EL1; this chapter chooses 39 bits, which makes the walk start at level 1 (three levels instead of four). An entry can point to the next table, or stop the walk early with a block: 1 GiB at level 1, 2 MiB at level 2. At level 3 every entry is a 4 KiB page. D1

The tables are an identity map: every virtual address equals its physical address, so the kernel keeps running at the addresses it was linked for when the MMU comes on. The first gigabyte (devices on virt) is one block of Device memory, never executable. The second gigabyte is RAM, mapped as far as the devicetree says RAM goes. Its first 2 MiB, where the kernel image lives, is mapped page by page so that each part of the kernel gets its own permissions: code readable and executable, read-only data only readable, everything else readable and writable but never executable. That is W^X, and the lab proves it with three deliberate faults. R2

The interrupt controller is new hardware compared with the PC's APIC. On QEMU virt with gic-version=3 it is a GICv3: a distributor that owns the shared interrupts (devices such as the UART), one redistributor per CPU that owns that CPU's private interrupts (such as its timer), and a CPU interface that the core talks to through system registers. To take an interrupt the kernel acknowledges it (which also tells it the interrupt's number), handles the device, and signals end-of-interrupt. D2

The timer is part of the architecture, not a separate chip: a system counter that runs at a fixed frequency, readable from CNTFRQ_EL0, and per-CPU timers that raise an interrupt when the counter passes a compare value. For a periodic tick, the handler sets the next deadline every time. The lab ticks 50 times at 10 ms, and also takes interrupts from the UART when QEMU feeds it the text "hello". D1 R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Translation control: what each register field does here

MAIR_EL1 = 0xff00: attribute 0 is 0x00 (Device-nGnRnE: no gathering, no reordering, no early write acknowledgement), attribute 1 is 0xff (Normal memory, inner and outer write-back cacheable with read and write allocation). Descriptors select one with AttrIndx. TCR_EL1 = 0x400803519 decodes as T0SZ = 0x19 = 25, so the lower region covers 264−25 = 239 bytes and walks start at level 1; IRGN0 = ORGN0 = 1 (table walks use write-back cacheable accesses); SH0 = 3 (inner shareable walks); TG0 = 0 (4 KiB granule); EPD1 = 1 (no walks through TTBR1: the kernel has no upper half yet); IPS = 4, copied from ID_AA64MMFR0_EL1.PARange so that the output address size is what this CPU supports. TTBR0_EL1 holds the level-1 table's address. D1 R2

The order of operations matters. Write MAIR, TCR and TTBR0; execute dsb ish so the table writes are complete and visible to the table walker; isb; invalidate the TLB (tlbi vmalle1) and complete it with another dsb ish and isb; only then set SCTLR_EL1.M (MMU), .C (data cache) and .I (instruction cache), followed by isb. R2 shows SCTLR going from 0xc50838 (F4-24) to 0xc5183d: bits 0, 2 and 12 added. With the MMU on, the same instructions continue at the same addresses because the map is an identity map. D1 R2

Descriptors and permissions

The level-3 entry of the kernel's code page reads 0x0040000040083787 in R2. Bits 1:0 = 3 (valid, page). Bits 4:2 = 1 (AttrIndx: Normal). Bit 7 = 1 (AP[2]: read-only). Bits 9:8 = 3 (inner shareable). Bit 10 = 1 (access flag: without it the first access raises an access-flag fault, which a kernel may use for page ageing; here every entry has it set). Bits 47:12 = 0x40083 (output address 0x40083000). Bit 54 = 1 (UXN: not executable at EL0); bit 53 = 0 (PXN clear: executable at EL1). The data page's entry 0x0060000040088707 differs in bit 7 (writable) and bit 53 (PXN set). D1 R2

The linker script places .text, .rodata and .data each at a 4 KiB boundary (R1: 0x40080000, 0x40087000, 0x40088000) so that a page never holds two kinds of content. This is a W^X requirement, not a cosmetic one: a page containing the end of the code and the start of the data would have to be either writable or executable. R1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The MAIR encodings 0x00 and 0xff, the TCR field positions (T0SZ, IRGN0, ORGN0, SH0, TG0, EPD1, IPS), the descriptor bit positions (AttrIndx, AP, SH, AF, PXN, UXN), the SCTLR bits M, A, C, I, the barrier and TLB-invalidation sequence, and the PARange value 4 meaning 44 bits are recalled from the Arm Architecture Reference Manual, chapter on the AArch64 Virtual Memory System Architecture (D1), not opened in this build. Evidence from the run: QEMU walked these tables (the kernel kept running after the MMU came on, and the three faults below have the classes and fault status codes the descriptors predict). That is consistent with the encodings used, not a proof of every bit's meaning, and QEMU's TCG is more forgiving than hardware about missing barriers.

Three faults that prove the map

A store to the kernel's own code page gives ESR 0x9600004f: data abort at the same EL, write bit (ISS bit 6) set, fault status 0x0f (permission fault, level 3). A read of 0x80000000 gives ESR 0x96000005: fault status 0x05, translation fault at level 1, because level-1 entry 2 is invalid. A call into the data page that holds a ret instruction gives ESR 0x8600000f: class 0x21 (instruction abort, same EL) with permission fault at level 3, and FAR equal to the branch target. In F4-24 with the MMU off, a bad address could only produce an external abort; with tables, faults say which level and which rule. R2

One detail from writing this lab: the first version of the code-page test wrote through a C++ pointer, and with -mstrict-align the compiler split the 32-bit store into four byte stores because it could not prove alignment; the handler resumed after the first faulting store and the second one faulted again. The test now uses one str wzr instruction in inline assembly. Tests of the MMU must control exactly which instructions run. D9

GICv3 bring-up

The distributor is set up once: disable it, wait until the register-write-pending bit clears, read GICD_TYPER (R2: 0x37a0008, whose low five bits 8 mean 32 × (8 + 1) = 288 interrupt IDs), and enable it with affinity routing (ARE) and group 1. Each CPU then sets up its own parts. It finds its redistributor by walking the redistributor frames (two 64 KiB frames per CPU) until GICR_TYPER's affinity field matches its own MPIDR_EL1; this matters in F4-26 where eight CPUs share the region. It clears ProcessorSleep in GICR_WAKER and waits for ChildrenAsleep to clear; it puts its SGIs and PPIs into group 1. Then it enables the system-register interface (ICC_SRE_EL1.SRE), opens the priority mask (ICC_PMR_EL1 = 0xff), sets no priority grouping and enables group 1 (ICC_IGRPEN1_EL1). D2 R2

A PPI is enabled in the CPU's own redistributor (priority byte, then the bit in GICR_ISENABLER0). An SPI is enabled in the distributor: group, priority, trigger mode (the PL011's line is level-sensitive), routing with GICD_IROUTER to this CPU's affinity, then the enable bit. The handler reads ICC_IAR1_EL1, which returns the interrupt ID and marks it active (1023 means spurious: nothing to end), and writes the same ID to ICC_EOIR1_EL1 when done. R2's dump after the test shows GICR_ISENABLER0 = 0x40000000 (only bit 30, the timer, enabled), nothing pending or active, and the running priority 0xff (idle). D2 R2

Not verified — check before relying on this

The GIC register offsets and bits in gic.cc (GICD_CTLR ARE and EnableGrp1 and RWP; GICD_TYPER.ITLinesNumber; GICD_IROUTER at 0x6000; the redistributor frame size 0x20000, GICR_TYPER's affinity in bits 63:32 and Last in bit 4; GICR_WAKER bits; the SGI_base offsets; the ICC_SGI1R_EL1 layout) are recalled from the GIC Architecture Specification for GICv3 and GICv4 (D2), not opened in this build. The run shows QEMU's GICv3 model delivering the timer PPI and the UART SPI with this code, and F4-26 shows SGIs delivered to eight CPUs. Group and security settings that matter on hardware with a secure world (where firmware must configure group 1 non-secure) were not exercised.

Generic timer as a tick

The counter frequency is read, never assumed: QEMU reports 62500000 Hz in CNTFRQ_EL0, so a 10 ms tick is 625000 counts. The EL1 physical timer is armed by writing the number of counts to CNTP_TVAL_EL0 (a down-counting view of the compare value) and setting ENABLE in CNTP_CTL_EL0. Its interrupt ID comes from the devicetree, as worked out in F4-23: the second entry of the timer node's interrupts is PPI 14, INTID 30. The handler re-arms the timer before anything else; that write moves the compare value into the future, the timer's condition (ISTATUS, bit 2 of the control register) clears, and the interrupt line drops. R2 counts 50 ticks in 32079182 counts of the virtual machine's counter, 513 ms of counter time: 50 intervals plus the time to handle them. D1 R2

"Counter time" is chosen on purpose. The counter belongs to the emulated machine; under TCG it advances with the host's clock, but the guest runs slower than hardware and its instructions take host-dependent time. The test checks ticks against the counter (between 50 and 60 intervals for 50 ticks), never against wall-clock time, and the chapter prints no hardware timing. D10

Refusing what you do not support

Milestone D2's second acceptance test says that switching QEMU to GICv2 must work or fail "with a clear 'unsupported' message". The kernel probes the devicetree before touching any interrupt hardware. On gic-version=2 it finds arm,cortex-a15-gic, prints that this kernel supports GICv3 only, and powers off; R3 is that run. A kernel that assumed GICv3 would write to redistributor registers that do not exist at those addresses. Supporting GICv2 is a good exercise (the mini-project): its CPU interface is memory-mapped instead of system registers, and its SGIs are sent through a distributor register. D8 R3

How the hardware actually does it

On real silicon the MMU's table walker is a hardware unit that reads descriptors through the cache hierarchy (with the TCR settings above) and stores results in TLBs. Because it reads memory on its own, the kernel must make its table writes visible with a dsb before the walker can see them, and invalidate stale TLB entries after changing a valid entry; F4-26's secondary CPUs reuse the same tables, so the barrier sequence runs on each of them. QEMU's TCG implements the walk in software and is more forgiving: a missing barrier that would cause an intermittent fault on hardware may never show here. D1

A GIC on a real SoC is a block of logic between the peripherals' interrupt lines and the cores; on large systems the redistributors are physically close to the cores they serve. The interrupt lines of peripherals are wired to SPI numbers by the SoC designer, which is why the devicetree, not the kernel, knows that the UART is SPI 1. The generic timer's counter is driven by a fixed-frequency clock that firmware is expected to describe in CNTFRQ_EL0. D2

Not verified — check before relying on this

That firmware programs CNTFRQ_EL0 on real boards (and that some boards' firmware leaves it wrong, which kernels then work around with a devicetree clock-frequency property on the timer node) is recalled, not checked. Untested on hardware. On the Raspberry Pi of F4-27 the counter frequency differs from QEMU virt's; F4-27's QEMU stand-in prints its own value.

Worked example

Task. Predict the walk and the fault for a read of address 0x9000018 (the PL011's flag register) and for a write to address 0x40087010 (in .rodata), using only the tables' rules; then check against the code and R2.

Address 0x9000018. Level-1 index = bits 38:30 = 0x9000018 >> 30 = 0. Entry 0 is a block (bit 1 clear) of 1 GiB: output address 0 + (0x9000018 & 0x3fffffff) = 0x9000018, AttrIndx 0 (Device-nGnRnE), read-write, PXN and UXN set. A read succeeds; the walk stops at level 1. R2's walk of the UART base 0x9000000 shows exactly this: "L1[0] = 0x0060000000000401 block → PA 0x9000000, AttrIdx 0, read-write, not executable". R2

Write to 0x40087010. Level-1 index 1 (0x40087010 >> 30 = 1), table. Level-2 index (0x40087010 >> 21) & 511 = 0x200 & 511 = 0, table to the kernel's level-3 table. Level-3 index (0x40087010 >> 12) & 511 = 0x87 = 135. Page 0x40087000 is in .rodata (R1: .rodata starts at 0x40087000, __rodata_end is 0x40088000), so ram_page_attrs gives AP read-only and PXN: the write is a permission fault at level 3, ESR class 0x25 with the write bit set and fault status 0x0f, FAR 0x40087010: the same syndrome as the code-page test in R2 (0x9600004f) with a different FAR. R1 R2

Tick arithmetic. CNTFRQ 62500000 Hz ÷ 100 = 625000 counts per tick. 50 ticks need at least 50 × 625000 = 31250000 counts. R2: 32079182 counts, 829182 more (about 1.3 ticks' worth): the time from arming the first deadline to the 50th interrupt being handled, plus handler time. The test accepts anything from 50 to 60 intervals. R2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The locker map is consulted by a machine that remembers. A map on paper is read fresh every time; the MMU caches translations in TLBs and reads tables through caches. Changing the map without barriers and TLB invalidation leaves the old answers in use. The analogy has no "sticky note" step unless you add the TLB explicitly (the registry's mapping for TLB).
    • 

  • Not every bell needs the hatch. In the analogy every ring passes the front desk and a hatch. PPIs never touch the distributor, SGIs are generated by CPUs themselves, and on newer GICs message-based interrupts (LPIs) bypass most of the path. The panel picture fits SPIs best.
    • 

  • The alarm is not a sound. A timer interrupt is a level held high while the condition holds, not a single ring. The forensic lab shows the consequence: an alarm that "rings once" could not cause a storm; a level can.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Clearing the UART's "data waiting" status when enabling its interrupt. While writing this lab the first version wrote all ones to the PL011's interrupt-clear register in enable_rx_interrupt. The six characters QEMU had already delivered stayed in the FIFO without an interrupt, and no further characters arrived: 0 received, D2 FAILED. The fix clears only the stale non-receive status. Evidence (last five lines of that run):

    • 

  • Mapping RAM as Device memory (or devices as Normal). Device memory forbids unaligned and speculative accesses; Normal memory for MMIO allows the CPU to merge, reorder or prefetch device accesses. Check AttrIndx in explain's output for one RAM and one device address.
    • 

  • Sections sharing a page. W^X cannot be enforced if code and data share a page. Check: readelf -S shows each section on a 4 KiB boundary (R1).
    • 

  • Forgetting the access flag. Every first access to a page faults with an access-flag fault (fault status 0x09–0x0b). Fix: set AF, or handle the fault deliberately.
    • 

  • Enabling the CPU interface before finding the redistributor. On one CPU it may work by luck (the first frame is CPU 0's); on several CPUs every CPU programs CPU 0's frame. Fix: match GICR_TYPER's affinity (F4-26 relies on it).
    • 

  • Assuming the counter frequency. A kernel that hard-codes a frequency ticks at the wrong rate on another machine. Fix: CNTFRQ_EL0, and compare against the counter, not against the wall clock.
    • 

  • Not re-arming the timer first. The forensic lab.
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F4-24's AArch64 boot and vectors; OS303's paging (B3–B5, four-level x86-64 tables, CR3, INVLPG), interrupts through the local APIC and IOAPIC, and the APIC timer or HPET as a tick (B8–B10). What carries over: the idea of a multi-level walk with present, writable and no-execute bits; acknowledge-and-end for interrupts; a periodic tick driving the scheduler; the neutral tests (rerun with the MMU and caches on). What changes: attributes are indirect through MAIR; the walk depth is a kernel choice (T0SZ); permissions use AP and two execute-never bits; there is no IDT, one IRQ vector for every interrupt and the ID comes from the GIC; the timer is part of the architecture, with its frequency in a register; barriers and TLB maintenance are explicit instructions the kernel must place. What comes next: F4-26 starts the other CPUs through PSCI, gives each its own data, and meets the weak memory model (milestone D3).

Lab


Goal. Milestone D2: "Your B3–B9 subsystems running on ARM64: translation tables, interrupts through GICv3, scheduler tick from the generic timer." Build: "Arch implementations of the page-table, interrupt-controller and clock interfaces you defined in Track B." Accept: "B3, B4, B5, B8, B9 and B10 acceptance tests pass on qemu-system-aarch64 virt with GICv3. Changing to GICv2 in QEMU works if you support it, or fails with a clear 'unsupported' message." D8


What this lab covers and what remains. The lab builds and proves the three arch pieces (tables, GIC, timer) and the GICv2 refusal. Plugging them into your own OS303 kernel's interfaces, so that your B3–B10 tests run on AArch64, is your part of the milestone: the lab's functions are written to sit behind those interfaces (mmu::init/enable_this_cpu for the page-table interface, gic::enable_*/ack/eoi for the interrupt controller, gtimer::arm for the clock event).

Summary


    

  • AArch64 translation with a 4 KiB granule resolves 9 bits per level; T0SZ = 25 gives 39-bit addresses and a walk starting at level 1; blocks shorten the walk.
    • 

  • MAIR holds memory types, TCR the walk's shape, TTBR0 the root; barriers and a TLB invalidate come before SCTLR.M.
    • 

  • W^X needs page-aligned sections and per-page AP and PXN bits; three deliberate faults prove the map.
    • 

  • GICv3: distributor for SPIs, a redistributor per CPU for SGIs and PPIs, system-register CPU interface; acknowledge with IAR1, end with EOIR1.
    • 

  • The generic timer's frequency is read from CNTFRQ_EL0; a periodic tick re-arms first, or a level-triggered storm follows.
    • 

  • An unsupported interrupt controller is detected from the devicetree and refused with a clear message.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. 64 − 25 = 39 bits; the walk starts at level 1 (levels 1, 2, 3 resolve 9 + 9 + 9 bits above the 12-bit page offset); one level-1 entry covers 230 bytes = 1 GiB.
    2. 

  2. Bits 1:0 = 3: valid page (level 3). AttrIndx (4:2) = 1: Normal memory. AP[2] (bit 7) = 0: writable. SH (9:8) = 3: inner shareable. AF (bit 10) = 1. Output address 0x40088000. PXN (53) = 1 and UXN (54) = 1: never executable. A writable, non-executable data page.
    3. 

  3. The write to code (permission fault on a write) and the call into data (instruction abort, permission fault) show that the two halves of W^X are enforced. The read of 0x80000000 (translation fault at level 1) shows that the walk reads the tables the kernel built: the fault level matches the missing entry.
    4. 

  4. SGI: software-generated, IDs 0–15 (a cross-CPU wake-up). PPI: private to one CPU, 16–31 (the timer). SPI: shared, 32 and up, routed to a CPU (the UART).
    5. 

  5. Frames are not guaranteed to be ordered by CPU number, and with several CPUs each must program its own frame; the affinity in GICR_TYPER is the only reliable link between a frame and a CPU.
    6. 

  6. The timer's interrupt is level-sensitive: it stays asserted while the counter is past the compare value. Re-arming moves the compare value into the future, which drops the line; doing it first means that by the time the handler writes EOI, the source is no longer asserting, and nothing that happens later in the handler (a long print, an early return) can leave it asserted.
    7. 

  7. 16 + 13 = 29. Only the INTID changes; the kernel enables and recognises whatever intid_from_devicetree returns, which is the point of reading it from the devicetree.
    8. 

  8. Prediction: 50 intervals of 62500 counts = 3125000 counts minimum, plus handler overhead, which is now a larger fraction of each interval; the 50–60-interval window may or may not hold depending on how long the handler takes under TCG. Run it and report the measured counts; the lesson is that tick overhead is measured in the same units as the tick.
    9. 

  9. The counter is the emulated machine's clock and the thing the timer compares against. Host wall-clock time depends on the host's load and on emulation speed, so it says nothing about the kernel's correctness (and the course prints no hardware timing).

Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. R5 shows tick 9000000 at counter 931553659 and tick 100000 at 10500265: 8900000 interrupts in 921053394 counts, about 103 counts per interrupt, against an intended 625000. 90 progress lines were printed, so at least 9 million timer interrupts were taken in under 15 seconds.
    2. 

  2. Bit 0 ENABLE = 1, bit 1 IMASK = 0, bit 2 ISTATUS = 1: the timer is enabled, not masked, and its condition is met. The compare value is in the past, so the timer asserts its interrupt continuously.
    3. 

  3. The CPU takes the next interrupt as soon as it unmasks or returns: the main loop gets a few instructions between interrupts at most, and its loop condition plus printing never completes in practice; in this run it never reached the "ticks:" line (0 such lines).
    4. 

  4. "GIC never ends the interrupt": then the interrupt would stay active and not be taken again; instead R5 shows millions of separate entries, so EOI works. "The source is never cleared": ISTATUS = 1 at every progress line shows the condition still met while the handler runs, which is consistent only with the timer never being re-armed. The second hypothesis is proven.
    5. 

  5. Re-arm the timer (gtimer::arm(g_interval)) as the first action for the timer INTID, before EOI. The fault was injected by compiling d2_main.cc with -DFORENSIC_NO_REARM, which removes line 31. Position matters because EOI with the line still high re-signals the interrupt at once.
    6. 

  6. In the handler: after re-arming, assert that CNTP_CTL_EL0's ISTATUS bit is 0 (panic with the value otherwise). In the test: a maximum rate check, for example "no more than 2 ticks per interval of counter time", which fails at the first few interrupts. Wrong answers: "the GIC priority mask is wrong" (interrupts are delivered, PMR is not the issue), "the UART interrupt is stuck" (the progress lines are printed only for the timer INTID).
    7.
