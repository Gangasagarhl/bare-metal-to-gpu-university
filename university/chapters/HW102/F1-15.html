Story hook

Analogy

In the kitchen of F1-14, the head chef has started ringing a bell. At every ring, a waiter collects whatever is on the counting row and carries it to the dining room. The bell rings at a steady pace, no matter what the cooks are doing.

Most of the time this works. But Ravi notices something odd on busy nights: some orders arrive at the tables with the wrong count. They are always the orders where the runner's token had to travel all the way down the row. The waiter took the plate before the last cook had finished.

Nobody made a mistake in the recipe. The recipe needs time, and the bell did not wait for it. Every real circuit has the same problem. In this chapter you measure how long gates take, find the slowest path, and see what happens when a result is read too early.

What you will be able to do

  • Define propagation delay and explain why a circuit's output is only correct after it has settled.
  • Find the critical path of a circuit by counting gate delays, and predict the worst-case delay of an N-bit ripple-carry adder.
  • Simulate gates with delays and read a timing diagram, including wrong intermediate values.
  • Explain a glitch (hazard) in a multiplexer and remove it with a covering term.
  • Diagnose results that are wrong only for long carry chains as a timing fault, not a logic fault (the forensic lab).
Jargon box
Propagation delay
Simple words: the time from an input changing to the output reaching its new, final value. Analogy: the time between an order arriving and the plate being finished on the counter. Precise: the maximum time from a change at a circuit's input until its output is stable at the new value; for a path through several gates it is the sum of the gate delays along the path. D8 §6.4, §7.7
Contamination delay
Simple words: the shortest time before an output can start to change after an input changes. Analogy: how soon the first cook can touch the plate after the order arrives. Precise: the minimum time from an input change until any output begins to change; together with the propagation delay it bounds when an output may be changing. D8 §6.4, §7.7
Critical path
Simple words: the slowest route from any input to any output. Analogy: the order that has to visit every station in the row. Precise: the path through a combinational circuit with the largest sum of gate delays; it sets the circuit's propagation delay. D8 §7.7, §12.2.2
Settle
Simple words: to stop changing and show the final answer. Analogy: the moment the last cook puts down the spoon. Precise: a combinational output has settled when, after an input change, no further change will happen until the inputs change again; before that it may show wrong intermediate values. W1 R2
Glitch (hazard)
Simple words: a short wrong pulse on an output that should not have changed. Analogy: a plate that briefly disappears from the counter while two cooks swap places, then comes back. Precise: an unwanted transient on a combinational output caused by different path delays, for example a momentary 0 on an output whose correct value stays 1 (Figure 2). D8 §7.7, §7.7.1 R3
Timing diagram
Simple words: a picture of signals changing over time, one row per signal. Analogy: the kitchen's order log with a time for every step. Precise: a plot of signal values against time; high and low levels for single bits, and labelled segments for multi-bit values (buses). D7 L11 R2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Until now, gates answered instantly. Real gates do not. When an input changes, the transistors inside need a little time to switch and to charge the wires they drive. Only then does the output change. That little time is the gate's delay.

A signal that passes through many gates collects many delays. In a ripple-carry adder, the carry may have to pass through every bit, one after the other. So a wide adder can take much longer than a narrow one for the same kind of sum.

While the carry is still travelling, the sum bits show wrong numbers. In Figure 1 the adder says 12, then 8, and only then the right answer. If another circuit reads the sum too early, it gets a wrong number, even though every gate is wired correctly.

The cure is patience, or speed. Either wait long enough for the slowest case, or build a faster adder. Engineers do both: they find the slowest path and design around it.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Delay of a gate and of a path

A gate's propagation delay is the time from an input change until its output is stable at the new value. In CMOS the delay comes from charging and discharging the capacitance of the wires and gate inputs that the output drives, through the resistance of the transistors that are on (F1-09, Layer 3). The delay of a path is the sum of the delays of the gates along it. The contamination delay is the opposite bound: the shortest time before an output can begin to change. D7 L5 D8 §4.3.1, §6.4, §7.7

In this chapter we use a model: every NAND gate takes exactly 1 time unit. Verilog writes this as nand #1. The simulator then moves every output change 1 unit later than the input change that caused it. Real gates have different delays for different gate types, loads and directions of change; the model keeps only the idea. D5 R1

The critical path of a ripple-carry adder

The critical path is the slowest path from any input to any output. In the nine-NAND full adder of F1-13, a change on cin reaches cout through two NANDs (g5, then g9), and reaches s through three (g5, then g6 or g7, then g8). For an N-bit ripple-carry adder whose carry must travel the whole way, the carry into bit N−1 arrives after 2(N−1) units, and that bit's sum needs 3 more: 2(N−1) + 3 = 2N + 1 units. The carry out arrives at 2N. So the worst case grows in proportion to N. W1

The simulation agrees. With a = all ones and b changing from 0 to 1, the last output change comes after 9, 17 and 33 units for 4, 8 and 16 bits: 2N + 1 each time. With a = 0, the same change of b settles after 4 units at every width, because no carry has to travel. Delay depends on the data, but a design must allow for the worst case. R1 W1

Wrong values before the output settles

Figure 1 shows the 4-bit case unit by unit: the carries rise at 102, 104, 106 and 108, and the sum passes through 1100 and 1000 before it settles at 0000 (with cout = 1) at 109. A combinational output is only meaningful after it has settled. Between the input change and the end of the propagation delay, it may show any value. R2 D8 §7.7

Glitches

A glitch is a short wrong pulse on an output that should not change at all. Figure 2's multiplexer computes y = NAND(NAND(d0, NOT s), NAND(d1, s)). When s falls, the term with s turns off after one delay, but the term with NOT s turns on only after two (NOT s itself needs a delay). For one unit neither term holds y at 1. Adding the term NAND(d0, d1) as a third input of the output NAND "covers" the gap: when d0 = d1 = 1 it holds y at 1 whatever s does. In Boolean algebra the extra term changes nothing (it is the consensus term d0 AND d1), but in time it removes the hazard. D8 §7.7.1 R3 W1

Reading results at a deadline

In a computer, a combinational block's output is read by the next stage at fixed moments, set by a clock (F1-18 in HW201). If the time between readings is shorter than the critical path, results with long carry chains are read before they settle. The forensic lab shows exactly this: an 8-bit adder read 8 units after its inputs change gives correct sums for short carry chains and wrong ones for long chains. Read at 20 units, longer than its 17-unit worst case, every sum is right. R4 R5

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Because the ripple-carry delay grows in proportion to N, fast adders compute the carries differently. A carry-lookahead adder computes, for each bit, whether it generates a carry (a AND b) or propagates one (a XOR b), the two terms named in F1-13, and combines them for groups of bits in parallel, so the carry into a far bit does not wait for every bit before it. Prefix adders push the same idea further; their delay grows much more slowly with N (in proportion to log N), at the cost of more gates. D8 §12.2.3, §12.4 D7 L17 D1 §2.4

A path that is too slow breaks the maximum-delay (setup) rule between storage elements, and a path that is too fast can break the minimum-delay (hold) rule, which is where contamination delay matters. HW201 introduces setup and hold with flip-flops. D7 L11

Not verified — check before relying on this

This chapter contains no real gate delay. "1 time unit per NAND" is our model's choice, and the numbers 9, 17 and 33 are counts of those units from our simulation. Real delays depend on the manufacturing process, supply voltage, temperature and load, and are given in the datasheet of a named part or in a cell library's timing data. If you build an adder from real logic chips, take the delay from that part's datasheet (AH-21), or measure it and label it as your measurement. D7 L4

In industry, timing is not checked by simulating all inputs: static timing analysis tools add up the documented delays of every cell along every path, for worst-case and best-case conditions, and report paths that are too slow (setup violations) or too fast (hold violations, where contamination delay matters). This description of static timing analysis tools was not checked against any tool's documentation in the verification pass.

How the hardware actually does it

On a chip, every gate output drives the capacitance of the wires and the gates it is connected to; switching means moving charge through transistors that are on. More load, longer wires and weaker transistors mean longer delays. This is why designers care about how many gates hang on one output and how far signals travel, and why the clock speed of a processor is limited by the slowest path between its storage elements. A processor splits long paths into stages with storage in between, which is pipelining (F1-26 in HW202). D7 L5, L11 D8 §11.5, §11.6.2

Worked example

Predict the settle time of a 4-bit adder, then check

Model: 1 unit per NAND; the nine-NAND full adder of F1-13. Input change: b0 from 0 to 1 at time 100, with a = 1111.

  1. Bit 0: t1 = NAND(a0, b0) falls at 101; cout0 = NAND(t1, t4) rises at 102. So c1 rises at 102.
  2. Each further bit has the carry in on g5 and the carry out on g9: 2 units per bit. c2 at 104, c3 at 106, c4 (cout) at 108.
  3. Bit 3's sum: c3 arrives at 106; g5, then g7, then g8: s3 settles at 109.
  4. Formula: 2N + 1 = 2 × 4 + 1 = 9 units after the change, so at 109.

The run of Listing 2 prints the carries rising at 102, 104, 106, 108 and the last sum change at 109 (Figure 1), and Listing 1's run prints "4-bit 9". W1 R1 R2

Which sums go wrong if you read early?

An 8-bit adder is read 8 units after the inputs change. The carry reaches bit k after about 2k units, and a sum bit needs a little more. So a sum is safe only if its carry chain is short. 15 + 1: the carry passes bits 0 to 3 into bit 4, about 8 units, plus the sum delay, too late; the reading shows 8 instead of 16. 2 + 3: no long chain; correct. Predict the others before reading the forensic evidence. W1 R4

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A waiter can see that a plate is not finished; a reading circuit cannot. Nothing in a combinational output says "not settled yet"; a wrong intermediate value looks exactly like a right one. That is why timing must be guaranteed by design, not checked at run time. D8 §7.7
    • 

  • Cooks work at different speeds from day to day; our model's gates all take exactly 1 unit. Real delays vary between gate types, chips, temperatures and supply voltages; the model hides all of that (Layer 3). D7 L4
    • 

  • A plate that disappears for a moment is a nuisance; a glitch can be read. If a later circuit happens to read during the glitch, it gets the wrong value, so glitches matter wherever an output is used without waiting for it to settle.
    •

Summary


    

  • Gates take time: the propagation delay is the time until an output is stable after an input change.
    • 

  • A path's delay is the sum of its gate delays; the critical path is the slowest one.
    • 

  • A ripple-carry adder's worst case grows in proportion to its width: 2N + 1 units in our model (measured 9, 17, 33 for 4, 8, 16 bits).
    • 

  • Before it settles, an output may show wrong values; in Figure 1 the adder shows 12 and 8 before 16.
    • 

  • Glitches come from unequal path delays; a covering (consensus) term removes the multiplexer's glitch without changing its truth table.
    • 

  • A result read before it settles is wrong only for slow inputs: that is the signature of a timing fault.
    • 

  • Model time units are not real delays; real numbers come from a named part's datasheet or a measurement.

Observation: 15 + 1 reads 8 (should be 16), 127 + 1 reads 120 (should be 128), 255 + 1 reads 248 (should be 256); the other five are right (R4). In each wrong case the reading is 8 too small: when it is read, bits 0–2 already show their final 0s, but the carry has not yet passed bit 3, so bit 3 and the bits above it still show their old values (for 15 + 1, 00001000 instead of 00010000). Common factor: the three wrong sums have carry chains of 4, 7 and 8 bits (from bit 0 upward); the right ones have no chain longer than 2 bits (3 + 1 has a 2-bit chain; 85 + 170 and 64 + 64 have none that ripples from bit 0 upward through several bits, and 100 + 27 has no carry at all). Hypotheses: (1) a logic fault in the adder; (2) the result is read before it settles. The same adder and vectors read after 20 units are all correct (R5), and its logic is the tested nine-NAND design; that rules out (1). Root cause: the reading moment (WAIT = 8, Listing 6 line 4) is shorter than the adder's worst-case settle time, 2 × 8 + 1 = 17 units (R1 measured 17). Fixes: read later (WAIT ≥ 17, with a margin; costs speed), or use a faster adder such as carry-lookahead (costs gates). Prevention: derive the reading time from the critical path, and test with the worst-case vectors (all ones + 1), not only typical sums. How the evidence was made: Listing 6 with its default WAIT of 8 units, on the delay-model adder of Listing 1, in this build. Wrong answers: "the display is broken" (with WAIT = 20 the same display reads correctly); "the adder has a wiring fault like F1-14's" (the wrong vectors are exactly the long carry chains, and the logic passes every vector when read late).
