Story hook

Analogy

Kenji works the late shift in the restaurant's basement. The sink in the middle has a pipe up to the roof tank and a pipe down to the drain. Today the plumber has fitted two taps on each pipe, and each tap has its own control pipe, labelled a and b.

On the drain pipe, the two taps sit one after the other. Water can only drain if both are open. On the roof pipe, the two taps sit side by side, so water can come in if either one is open.

Kenji tries all four ways of setting a and b and writes down when the sink is full. It is full in three cases and empty in only one: when a and b both have pressure. "That's NOT AND," he says. He is right. He has built a NAND gate, the gate this whole course leans on.

What you will be able to do

  • Draw the symbols and write the truth tables of NOT, NAND and NOR.
  • Explain how switches in series make an AND-like condition and switches in parallel make an OR-like condition.
  • Build CMOS NAND and NOR gates from four transistors each, using the rule that the pull-up network is the "mirror" of the pull-down network.
  • Describe the gates transistor by transistor in Verilog and test them exhaustively in a simulator.
  • Diagnose a NOR gate whose pull-up was wired in parallel from its truth-table output (the forensic lab).
Jargon box
Logic gate
Simple words: a small circuit that computes one logic function, such as NOT or AND, from its inputs. Analogy: one sink with its own set of taps, whose fill level answers one yes/no question. Precise: an electronic circuit implementing a Boolean function of one or more binary inputs; in CMOS, a pull-up and a pull-down network of transistors. "Gate" also names one terminal of a transistor (F1-09). D2 D1
NAND gate
Simple words: "not and": the output is 0 only when all inputs are 1. Analogy: Kenji's sink: it drains only when both drain taps are open. Precise: the Boolean function y = NOT (a AND b); in CMOS, two nMOS in series to ground and two pMOS in parallel to the supply. D2 R1
NOR gate
Simple words: "not or": the output is 1 only when all inputs are 0. Analogy: a sink with two drain taps side by side and two roof taps one after the other. Precise: the Boolean function y = NOT (a OR b); in CMOS, two nMOS in parallel to ground and two pMOS in series to the supply. D2 R1
Series and parallel (switches)
Simple words: series means one after another, so all must be on; parallel means side by side, so any one is enough. Analogy: two taps on one pipe, one after the other, or two pipes joined side by side. Precise: switches in series conduct only if every switch conducts (an AND of their conditions); switches in parallel conduct if at least one conducts (an OR). D2 D3
Gate symbol
Simple words: the standard drawing for each kind of gate, with inputs on the left and the output on the right. Analogy: the little picture on a kitchen door that tells you what happens in that room. Precise: a schematic shape: a triangle for a buffer, a D shape for AND, a curved shield for OR; a small circle (bubble) on an output or input means inversion. D2
Exhaustive test
Simple words: trying every possible input and checking every output. Analogy: tasting every dish on the menu, not only the favourites. Precise: a test whose vectors cover all 2n combinations of n binary inputs; for small combinational circuits this proves the circuit matches its truth table. D1 W1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Put two switches one after the other on a wire. Current gets through only if both are on. That is AND. Put two switches side by side. Current gets through if either one is on. That is OR. Every gate in a computer uses these two patterns.

Now remember the inverter from F1-09. It had one switch to ground and one to the supply. For a NAND gate, you put two nMOS switches in series to ground. The output is pulled to 0 only when a and b are both 1.

At all other times the output must be pulled to 1. So the pMOS switches go side by side to the supply. If a is 0 or b is 0, at least one pMOS is on. The output is 1 in exactly the three cases where it is not 0.

A NOR gate is the mirror image. Its nMOS switches sit side by side, so either input can pull the output to 0. Its pMOS switches sit in series, so the output is 1 only when both inputs are 0. Four switches, one more gate.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Switches compose like Boolean operators. Two switches in series conduct only if both conduct: the condition is an AND. Two switches in parallel conduct if at least one conducts: the condition is an OR. This is the bridge between transistors and the Boolean algebra of F0-27. D2 D3

A CMOS gate is designed from its pull-down network. The nMOS network decides when the output is 0. For NAND, the output must be 0 exactly when a AND b, so the two nMOS go in series. The pMOS network must conduct in every other case. By De Morgan's law, NOT (a AND b) equals (NOT a) OR (NOT b). A pMOS conducts on a 0, so "NOT a OR NOT b" is two pMOS in parallel. D2 W1

The same reasoning gives the NOR gate. Its output is 0 when a OR b, so the nMOS go in parallel. It is 1 when (NOT a) AND (NOT b), so the pMOS go in series. The general rule: the pull-up network is the dual of the pull-down network, with series and parallel swapped. That guarantees exactly one network conducts for every input, as Figure 2 of F1-09 requires. D2

Notice what CMOS gives you for free and what it does not. A single CMOS stage always inverts: a 1 on an nMOS gate pulls the output toward 0. So NAND and NOR cost 4 transistors each, but AND and OR need an extra inverter: AND = NAND followed by NOT, which is 4 + 2 = 6 transistors in this construction. That is one reason designers like NAND and NOR so much. D2 W1

To check a gate you run an exhaustive test: every input combination, every output compared with the expected value. Two inputs give 2 × 2 = 4 rows. Listing 2 runs all four rows through NOT, NAND and NOR, and compares each with Verilog's own operators ~ (NOT), & (AND) and | (OR). The run prints ALL PASS for 12 checks. R1 D5

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. The series-parallel method extends to more inputs: a three-input NAND has three nMOS in series and three pMOS in parallel. It also builds compound gates in one stage, such as NOT ((a AND b) OR c), by nesting series and parallel groups. Textbooks discuss why long series chains are avoided: each switch in a chain adds resistance, so the gate gets slower. Designers also size transistors differently because pMOS and nMOS do not conduct equally well. These are analog effects outside the ideal-switch model. D2

Not verified — check before relying on this

Statements of the form "NAND gates are faster or smaller than NOR gates in CMOS" depend on transistor sizing and on a particular manufacturing process. This build has no source open for them. Treat any such comparison as unverified until it is checked in D2 (the CMOS chapter) or in the documentation of a named cell library.

How the hardware actually does it

A chip designer seldom draws single transistors. Tools combine pre-designed gates, called cells, such as inverters, NANDs and NORs of several input counts. Each cell is a small CMOS network like Figure 2, laid out once and reused many times. When you write a circuit in a hardware description language, a synthesis tool chooses cells for it; you will see a synthesis tool count gates in F1-11. D2 D6

Worked example

Check NAND row by row

Use Figure 2 and the two switch rules from F1-09. "Down" is the series nMOS pair; "up" is the parallel pMOS pair.

In every row exactly one of "down" and "up" says yes. The output column is NAND. W1 R1

Design a gate from its pull-down

Task: a CMOS gate for y = NOT (a OR b). Steps:

  1. y is 0 when a OR b. An OR of nMOS conditions is parallel: nMOS a and nMOS b side by side to ground.
  2. Swap series and parallel for the pull-up: pMOS a and pMOS b in series to the supply.
  3. Check one row: a = 0, b = 1. nMOS b is on, so y is pulled down. pMOS b is off, so the series chain is broken. y = 0. Correct for NOR.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Taps in series slow the water a little; transistors in series slow the gate a lot more as the chain grows. The analogy hides that each extra switch in a chain adds resistance and delay; real designs limit chain length (Layer 3). D2
    • 

  • A sink can be half full; a gate output should never stay in between. In the ideal model the output is joined to exactly one rail, so it is 0 or 1. A half-full sink has no equivalent in correct CMOS logic. D2
    • 

  • Kenji reads the sink after it has filled; real outputs take time to change. The analogy hides delay entirely. F1-15 measures it in a simulator.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: NOT and NAND are correct in all rows; NOR prints x in rows 0 1 and 1 0 (FAIL: 2 of 12 checks, R2). Meaning: x means both networks conducted (a short). Hypotheses: (1) the nMOS pull-down is wrong; (2) the pMOS pull-up is wrong. In row 0 1 the correct NOR output is 0, so the pull-down is supposed to conduct, and it does (nMOS b on). The short therefore comes from a pull-up that also conducts: pMOS a (gate 0) is on and reaches y directly. That rules in (2). Root cause: in cmos_gates_bad.v, lines 25–26 join both pMOS from vdd straight to y (parallel), and the inside wire mid is declared but never used. The comment says "series", the code says parallel. Fix: pmos p1(mid, vdd, a); and pmos p2(y, mid, b);, as Listing 1; rerun: ALL PASS (R1). Prevention: the exhaustive testbench caught it; a lint rule or review checklist "every declared inside wire must appear in two switch lines" would catch it before simulation. How the evidence was made: Listing 1 with the NOR's pull-up changed from series to parallel, run with Listing 2 in this build. Wrong answers: "the NOR pull-down is wrong" (rows 0 0 and 1 1 are correct, and the pull-down does conduct in the failing rows as it should); "the simulator is random" (the run is repeatable).
