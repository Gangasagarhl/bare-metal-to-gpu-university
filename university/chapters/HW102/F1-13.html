Story hook

Analogy

The kitchen counts finished plates on a row of small flip boards. Each board shows only an empty side or a full side: 0 or 1. Hana is in charge of the board on the right. When a plate is finished, she flips her board.

If her board was already full, it cannot show "2". So she flips it back to empty and sends a runner with a token to the next board on the left. That board's cook adds the token to whatever arrives there.

Hana's rule is all of binary addition: "write the last digit here, carry one to the left". In this chapter you turn that rule into gates. One board with two inputs is a half adder. A board that also takes the runner's token is a full adder.

What you will be able to do

  • Add binary numbers by hand, column by column, with carries.
  • Derive the half adder (XOR for the sum, AND for the carry) from its truth table.
  • Build a full adder from two half adders and an OR gate, and from nine NAND gates.
  • Test both full adders exhaustively against ordinary addition in a simulator.
  • Spot a testbench width mistake that makes 1 + 1 + 1 look like 1.
  • Find a missing carry term in a full adder from its truth-table output (the forensic lab).
Jargon box
Sum bit and carry bit
Simple words: when you add one column, the sum bit is the digit you write down and the carry bit is what you pass to the next column. Analogy: Hana's board after the flip (sum) and the runner's token (carry). Precise: for inputs whose total in one column is T (0 to 3), the sum bit is T mod 2 and the carry bit is T div 2 (integer division). W1 D1 §2.2.1 D3 ch.14
Half adder
Simple words: a circuit that adds two bits and gives a sum bit and a carry bit. Analogy: one board with no runner arriving. Precise: a combinational circuit with inputs a, b and outputs s = a XOR b and c = a AND b, so that 2c + s = a + b. D1 §2.3 D3 ch.14 R1
Full adder
Simple words: a circuit that adds three bits: two digits and a carry coming in. Analogy: a board whose cook also takes the runner's token. Precise: a combinational circuit with inputs a, b, cin and outputs s = a XOR b XOR cin and cout = (a AND b) OR (cin AND (a XOR b)), so that 2·cout + s = a + b + cin. D1 §2.2.1 D3 ch.14 R1
Carry in, carry out (cin, cout)
Simple words: the carry arriving from the column to the right, and the carry leaving to the column to the left. Analogy: the token the runner brings, and the token sent on. Precise: the carry input and carry output of a full adder; chaining cout of one bit to cin of the next makes a multi-bit adder (F1-14). D1 §2.3 D3 ch.14
Expression width (Verilog)
Simple words: how many bits Verilog uses to work out an expression. Analogy: a board that only has room for one digit: a total of 3 is written as 1 and the rest is lost. Precise: Verilog sizes an expression from its operands and its context; adding three 1-bit values in a 1-bit context keeps only the low bit. The run width_demo shows it. D5 R3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Adding binary numbers works like adding decimal numbers. You start at the right and add one column at a time. In binary a column can only hold 0 or 1. So 1 + 1 is "0, carry 1", the same way 5 + 5 in decimal is "0, carry 1".

A half adder adds one column with two bits. The digit you write down is 1 when exactly one bit is 1. That is XOR. The carry is 1 only when both bits are 1. That is AND. Two gates, and a machine can add.

But most columns have three things to add: two digits and the carry from the right. A full adder handles all three. It adds the two digits with one half adder, then adds the carry with a second half adder. If either step made a carry, it sends one carry to the left.

Line up four full adders and you can add four-bit numbers. That is the next chapter.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

One column of binary addition

In a column, the total T of the bits being added is 0, 1, 2 or 3. The sum bit is T mod 2 and the carry bit is T div 2. Writing T in binary gives both at once: 2 = 10 (carry 1, sum 0), 3 = 11 (carry 1, sum 1). This is ordinary place value from F0-21. W1

The half adder

With two inputs, T = a + b. The truth table in Figure 1 shows s is 1 exactly when a and b differ: s = a XOR b. The carry is 1 only for a = b = 1: c = a AND b. So a half adder is one XOR and one AND. Its outputs satisfy 2c + s = a + b for all four rows. W1 R1 D1 §2.3

The full adder

A full adder adds a, b and a carry in cin. Its sum is 1 when an odd number of the three inputs are 1: s = a XOR b XOR cin. Its carry out is 1 when at least two inputs are 1. D1 §2.2.1 W1

Figure 2 builds it from parts you have. Half adder 1 adds a and b, giving s1 = a XOR b and c1 = a AND b. Half adder 2 adds s1 and cin, giving s = s1 XOR cin and c2 = s1 AND cin. The carry out is c1 OR c2. Why OR and not another adder? If c1 = 1 then a = b = 1, so s1 = 0 and c2 = 0. The two carries are never 1 together, so OR is enough. W1 R1

In the NAND-only style of F1-11, a full adder needs nine NANDs. Two four-NAND XORs make the sum, and they share inside wires with the carry: the first XOR's t1 = NAND(a, b) and the second XOR's t4 = NAND(x1, cin) are exactly the inverted carry terms, so cout = NAND(t1, t4) costs one more gate. Listing 1 contains both versions, and the testbench checks both against ordinary addition. W1 R1

Testing arithmetic

A full adder has three inputs, so an exhaustive test has 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 rows. The reference is plain arithmetic: the 2-bit number {cout, s} must equal a + b + cin. Here a trap waits. Verilog works out an expression width from the operands; three 1-bit values added in a 1-bit context give a 1-bit answer, so 1 + 1 + 1 prints as 1. Storing the total in a 32-bit integer first gives 3. The run width_demo shows both lines, and Listing 2 uses the integer. R3 D5

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The carry-out formula can be written in several equivalent ways. "At least two of three" is the majority function: cout = (a AND b) OR (a AND cin) OR (b AND cin). Figure 2 uses (a AND b) OR (cin AND (a XOR b)), which reuses the XOR already needed for the sum. The terms a AND b ("this column generates a carry by itself") and a XOR b ("this column passes an incoming carry on") get names, generate and propagate, in the faster adders of F1-15. D7 L17 W1

Gate counts depend on what you count. Our structural full adder uses 2 XOR, 2 AND and 1 OR. The NAND-only version uses 9 NANDs. Yosys, given the one-line behavioural adder and asked for NAND cells, reports 7 NAND and 5 NOT cells: 12 NAND-equivalents in our counting, more than the hand design, as with the XOR in F1-11. Note

The course book D1 builds a HalfAdder and a FullAdder as its first arithmetic chips: the half adder's sum and carry are the low and high bits of a + b, the same functions as Xor and And, and the full adder adds three bits. The nine-NAND construction in this chapter is the course's own design, tested in this build (R1); D1 does not prescribe it, so do not expect to find it in the book. D1 §2.2.1, §2.3

How the hardware actually does it

A processor's arithmetic unit contains adders built from full-adder logic or from faster relatives of it. The ideas of this chapter, a sum bit and a carry bit per column, survive unchanged; what changes in fast designs is how quickly the carry reaches the left end, which F1-15 measures in simulation. Chip designers also use full-adder cells built directly from transistors rather than from separate gates; the function is the same. D7 L17 D1 §2.4

Add 0111 + 0011 by hand

Check the carries never collide

Row a = 1, b = 1, cin = 1: half adder 1 gives s1 = 0, c1 = 1. Half adder 2 adds s1 = 0 and cin = 1: s = 1, c2 = 0. cout = 1 OR 0 = 1, s = 1: total 3. Row a = 0, b = 1, cin = 1: s1 = 1, c1 = 0; then s = 0, c2 = 1; cout = 1. In no row are c1 and c2 both 1. W1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Hana flips her board and then the runner leaves; in a full adder there is no "then". The sum and carry are both computed by gates reacting at the same time; only signal delay orders them (F1-15). D8 §7.7
    • 

  • A runner can carry more than one token; a carry is one bit. The most any column of a full adder can pass on is a single 1, because 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 = binary 11. The analogy suggests tokens could pile up; in binary addition they cannot. W1
    • 

  • Boards count plates that exist; an adder's bits mean whatever the program says. The same bits can be a count, a negative number in two's complement (F0-24) or part of a letter; the adder does not know. D8 §3.2.1.2
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: rows a b cin = 0 1 1 and 1 0 1 fail: Kofi's cout is 0 where the reference total is 2 and the 9-NAND column shows 1. The sum column is correct in every row (R2). Common factor: in both failing rows exactly one of a, b is 1 and cin is 1: the carry must come from cin. Hypotheses: (1) the carry ignores cin; (2) the sum is wrong and the reference misreads it. The sum column matches the 9-NAND stand-in in all rows, which rules out (2). Root cause: Listing 5, line 8: assign cout = c1; uses only a AND b; the term (a XOR b) AND cin (c2 in Figure 2) and the OR are missing. Effect on 4-bit sums: any addition where a carry must pass through a column with exactly one 1 goes wrong, for example 0001 + 0011 (1 + 3): column 1 has a = 0, b = 1, cin = 1, so the carry is lost and the result is 0000 instead of 0100 (4). Additions with no carry chain through such a column, such as 0001 + 0010, stay right. Why six of eight is not enough: an adder must be right for every input; a program cannot avoid the failing rows. Fix: add and g4(c2, s1, cin); and or g5(cout, c1, c2);; rerun: ALL PASS. How the evidence was made: Listing 1's full adder rewritten without the second carry term, run with Listing 2 in this build. Wrong answers: "the testbench reference is wrong" (the 9-NAND stand-in agrees with the reference in all 8 rows).
