Story hook

Analogy

Amara is helping in the restaurant's basement, where all the pipes of the building meet. A big pipe brings water from the tank on the roof. Another pipe leads to the drain. Between them sits a small sink with a float, and a sign on the wall says what each float position means: up is "yes", down is "no".

The water pipes have taps. But nobody turns these taps by hand. Each one has a thin control pipe. When the thin pipe has pressure, the tap opens. A tiny push of water controls a big flow.

"So the thin pipe decides," Amara says, "and the big pipe does the work." That is exactly the idea of a transistor. In this chapter you meet the tap that runs every computer, and you wire two of them into your first logic circuit.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain a transistor as a switch controlled by a voltage, using the tap analogy and the precise words gate, source and drain.
  • Tell an nMOS switch from a pMOS switch by the input that turns it on.
  • Explain how a CMOS inverter turns 0 into 1 and 1 into 0 with one pull-up and one pull-down switch.
  • Name the four possible states of an output wire (1, 0, floating, short) and say which wiring causes each.
  • Run a switch model in C++ and a transistor-level description in a logic simulator, and read their output.
  • Diagnose an inverter built with the wrong kind of transistor from its test output (the forensic lab).
Jargon box
Transistor
Simple words: a tiny electrical switch with no moving parts, turned on and off by a voltage. Analogy: a tap in the building's plumbing, opened by a small control valve. Precise: a semiconductor device with three terminals in which the voltage on one terminal controls the current between the other two; in digital logic it is used as an on/off switch. D2 D3
Gate, source, drain (of a transistor)
Simple words: the three wires of a transistor: one that controls it, and two that are joined when it is on. Analogy: the thin control pipe (gate), and the two ends of the big pipe through the tap (source and drain). Precise: in a MOS transistor the gate terminal controls whether a conducting channel forms between the source and drain terminals. The word "gate" also names a whole logic circuit (F1-10); the context says which one is meant. D2
nMOS and pMOS
Simple words: the two kinds of transistor switch: nMOS turns on when its gate is 1, pMOS turns on when its gate is 0. Analogy: two kinds of tap: one opens when the control pipe has pressure, the other opens when it has none. Precise: n-channel and p-channel metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors; in the ideal-switch model of digital logic, an nMOS conducts when its gate is at logic 1 and a pMOS conducts when its gate is at logic 0. D2 R2
CMOS
Simple words: a way of building logic where every output has pMOS switches to the supply and nMOS switches to ground. Analogy: each sink has one set of taps from the roof tank and a matching set of taps to the drain, and only one set is open at a time. Precise: complementary MOS: logic built from a pull-up network of pMOS transistors and a complementary pull-down network of nMOS transistors, so that for every input exactly one network conducts. D2
Pull-up and pull-down network
Simple words: the switches that connect the output to 1 (pull-up) or to 0 (pull-down). Analogy: the taps between the sink and the roof tank (pull-up), and between the sink and the drain (pull-down). Precise: in a CMOS gate, the pull-up network joins the output to the supply rail and the pull-down network joins it to ground; their switch patterns are complements of each other. D2
Inverter (NOT gate)
Simple words: a circuit whose output is the opposite of its input. Analogy: a sink that fills when the control pipe is empty and drains when it has pressure. Precise: the logic function y = NOT a; in CMOS, one pMOS pull-up and one nMOS pull-down with their gates joined to the input. D2 R2
Floating (Z) and short (X)
Simple words: floating means the output is connected to nothing; a short means it is connected to 1 and 0 at the same time. Analogy: a sink with every tap shut, so nobody can say how full it is; or a sink with the roof tank and the drain both open, so water rushes straight through. Precise: Verilog writes a floating (high-impedance) value as z and an unknown value, such as two strong drivers in conflict, as x; in a real circuit a short between supply and ground draws a large current. D5 R3
Hardware description language (HDL)
Simple words: a language for writing down a circuit as text: its parts and the wires between them. Analogy: the written plan of the building's pipes, which a practice kitchen can follow exactly. Precise: a computer language whose descriptions specify the structure and behaviour of digital hardware so that tools can simulate it or turn it into gates; Verilog is the HDL used in this course. D2 D5
Logic simulator, testbench
Simple words: a program that pretends to be a circuit; a testbench is the extra code that tries inputs and checks outputs. Analogy: a practice kitchen where you test a recipe before the real dinner, and a tasting checklist. Precise: a simulator computes the values of the wires of a circuit description over time; a testbench is a description that applies input values (test vectors) to the design under test and compares its outputs with expected values. D1 D5

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the smallest useful CMOS circuit, the inverter, twice. On the left the input is 0; on the right it is 1. In each picture exactly one switch is on, and it joins the output to the supply or to ground.

Figure 2 shows all four things an output wire can be joined to. Only two of them are good logic values. The other two are bugs, and you will meet both in the forensic lab.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

In HW101 you met voltage as pressure and current as flow. A wire at a high voltage, the supply, counts as 1. A wire at ground counts as 0. A computer is built from switches that connect wires to 1 or to 0.

A light switch on the wall needs a finger. A transistor needs no finger. Its switch is controlled by another wire, called the gate. Put a 1 on the gate and the switch changes. So one wire can control another wire. That is the whole trick of digital electronics.

There are two kinds of transistor switch. An nMOS switch is on when its gate is 1. A pMOS switch is on when its gate is 0. Think of two taps: one opens when its control pipe has pressure, the other opens when it has none.

Now take one of each. Put the pMOS between the supply and an output wire. Put the nMOS between the output and ground. Join both gates to one input. If the input is 0, the pMOS is on and the output becomes 1. If the input is 1, the nMOS is on and the output becomes 0. You built an inverter: it says the opposite of what it hears.

One rule keeps this safe. Exactly one switch must be on. If both are off, the output floats and means nothing. If both are on, the supply is joined straight to ground. Good circuits never let either happen.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A MOS transistor has three terminals that matter here: the gate, the source and the drain. The gate voltage decides whether a conducting channel forms between source and drain. In digital design we treat this as an ideal switch: closed (conducting) or open. This ideal-switch model ignores many analog details, which Layer 3 lists. D2 D4

In the ideal-switch model the two kinds behave as opposites. An nMOS conducts when its gate is at logic 1. A pMOS conducts when its gate is at logic 0. Circuit drawings mark the pMOS gate with a small circle, the same "not" circle you will see on logic gate symbols in F1-10. D2

CMOS logic always builds an output from two networks. The pull-up network of pMOS switches joins the output to the supply. The pull-down network of nMOS switches joins it to ground. The two networks are designed as complements: for every input pattern, exactly one of them conducts. That is why a CMOS output is always a firm 0 or a firm 1. D2

Figure 2 lists all four combinations. Pull-up on and pull-down off gives 1. Pull-up off and pull-down on gives 0. Both off leaves the output floating: it is joined to nothing, so its value is undefined. Verilog writes this as z. Both on is a short: the supply drives the wire to 1 while ground drives it to 0. Verilog writes the resulting unknown value as x. D5 R1 R3

The inverter is the simplest CMOS gate: one pMOS on top, one nMOS below, both gates joined to the input a. Its truth table has two rows: a = 0 gives y = 1, and a = 1 gives y = 0. This is the NOT function from MA102, now built from real switches. Listing 1 models it in C++, and Listing 2 describes it transistor by transistor for a logic simulator. Both runs print the same table. R1 R2

Why two kinds of transistor instead of one? With only nMOS switches, you could pull an output down firmly but not up. The two-network design gives a firm value in both directions. In the ideal model it also means no path from supply to ground while the inputs are steady. D2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. A real MOS transistor is not a perfect switch. It turns on gradually as the gate voltage passes a threshold, it has some resistance when on, it leaks a little when off, and charging the wires it drives takes time. An nMOS passes a 0 well but a 1 poorly, and a pMOS the reverse; this is one reason CMOS puts pMOS on top and nMOS below. Textbooks such as D2 and D4 treat these effects with equations. HW102 keeps the ideal switch, and F1-15 brings back one of the effects: time. D2 D4

Not verified — check before relying on this

This chapter gives no threshold voltage, supply voltage, on-resistance or switching time for any transistor. Such numbers belong to a named part and its datasheet (guide AH-21). If you build an inverter from real parts, write the part number in your lab notes and copy the numbers from that part's datasheet, not from this page.

How the hardware actually does it

A chip contains a very large number of transistors made side by side on one piece of silicon. In the ideal-switch picture each one is a switch whose gate is driven by another wire. A processor is, at this level, a huge network of pull-up and pull-down switches. Every chapter of HW102 builds on this one picture: gates from switches (F1-10), every gate from one kind of gate (F1-11), and finally adders and an ALU (F1-14). D2 D3

Before transistors, engineers built the same logic from relays, switches moved by an electromagnet. The idea "one wire controls another" did not change; the switch got smaller, faster and quieter. D3

Worked example

Which switch is on, and what is the output?

Take the inverter of Figure 1 and fill in the table row by row, using only the two rules: nMOS on when the gate is 1, pMOS on when the gate is 0.

Each row has exactly one switch on, so the output is always firm. W1

The wrong transistor on top

Now suppose someone uses an nMOS for the pull-up as well. Use the same rules.

  1. a = 0: the top nMOS has gate 0, so it is off. The bottom nMOS has gate 0, so it is off. Both off: the output floats (Z).
  2. a = 1: both nMOS have gate 1, so both are on. Supply and ground are joined: a short (X).

The circuit never produces a good value. Listing 1 prints exactly these two rows, and the simulator prints z and x for them in the forensic lab. W1 R1 R3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Water flows downhill by itself; charge needs a closed loop. A tap left open on a pipe end spills water. An open-ended wire does not "spill" charge; current flows only around a complete circuit (HW101). D2
    • 

  • A tap is either open or shut; a transistor turns on gradually. Between "clearly off" and "clearly on" there is a range of gate voltages where it conducts partly. Digital design keeps inputs out of that range, but the range exists (Layer 3). D4
    • 

  • A tap's control pipe uses up a little water; an ideal MOS gate draws no steady current. The gate is insulated from the channel, so in the ideal model a steady gate voltage costs nothing. Changing it does cost some charge, which matters for speed and power. D2
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: HW101 gave you voltage, current and switches you press by hand; F0-26 gave you NOT as a truth table. What carries over: 1 means "joined to the supply", 0 means "joined to ground", and a truth table still lists every input. Summary


    

  • A transistor is a switch controlled by a voltage on its gate: one wire controls another.
    • 

  • In the ideal-switch model, nMOS conducts when its gate is 1; pMOS conducts when its gate is 0.
    • 

  • CMOS builds every output from a pMOS pull-up network and a complementary nMOS pull-down network.
    • 

  • The inverter is one pMOS and one nMOS with joined gates: 0 → 1, 1 → 0.
    • 

  • An output can be 1, 0, floating (z) or shorted (x); a correct CMOS gate only produces 1 or 0.
    • 

  • A testbench tries every input and compares with !==, so bad states are counted as errors.
    • 

  • Real transistors are not ideal; their numbers come only from a named part's datasheet.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: for a = 0 the output is z (floating); for a = 1 it is x (short); FAIL on 2 of 2 vectors (R3). Hypotheses: (1) the testbench is wrong; (2) the circuit is wrong. The same testbench prints ALL PASS for the correct inverter (R2), which rules out (1). Root cause: Listing 4, line 6: the pull-up is declared nmos, not pmos. With a = 0 both nMOS are off (floating); with a = 1 both are on (short), exactly the Worked example. Why the comments mislead: the comment describes the intended pMOS behaviour, but the simulator follows the code. Fix: change line 6 to pmos p1(y, vdd, a); and rerun: ALL PASS. Prevention: exhaustive testbenches with !==, and reviewing each switch line against its rail. How the evidence was made: Listing 2 with the pull-up primitive changed from pmos to nmos, run with Listing 3 in this build. Wrong answers: "the grader is broken" (it passes a correct inverter); "the input is wrong" (the testbench prints the inputs 0 and 1 correctly).
