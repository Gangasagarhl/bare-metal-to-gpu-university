Story hook

Dinner service is slow. The head chef is sure she knows why: "The grill is too small." She orders a bigger grill. Service is still slow. So for one evening she puts a helper with a stopwatch and a tally sheet at every station: how long does each dish wait at each place, and how many times does a cook walk to the storeroom?

The answer surprises everyone. The grill is fine. The cooks lose most of their time unwrapping each portion of butter from its own paper, one at a time, hundreds of times a night.

A profiler is that helper with the stopwatch and the tally sheet. This chapter asks you to guess where a program spends its time, then to measure with three different tools, and to notice that even the tools do not always agree.

What you will be able to do

  • Time a program's steps repeatably and summarise several runs by the median.
  • Profile a program with Valgrind's callgrind (instruction counts) and with gprof (sampling), and read inclusive and self costs.
  • Find the hot spot, change only that code, and measure again.
  • Recognise quadratic behaviour from how cost grows when the input doubles.
  • Explain why different profilers can give different answers, and what to do about it.
  • Diagnose a program that became slow as its input grew.
Jargon box
Profiler
Simple words: a tool that measures where a program spends its time or work, function by function. Analogy: the helper with a stopwatch and a tally sheet at every station. Precise: a tool that attributes a cost (time, samples, executed instructions, cache misses) to functions or source lines of a running program. D1 D2
Hot spot
Simple words: the small part of a program where most of the time goes. Analogy: the butter-unwrapping station. Precise: a function or loop with a large share of a profile's total cost; in this chapter's first profile, parse_line had 60.82 % of the executed instructions. R3
Wall-clock time
Simple words: the time that passes on a clock on the wall while the program runs. Analogy: how long the customer waits. Precise: elapsed real time, including time spent waiting or running other programs; measured here with std::chrono::steady_clock and bash's time (%R). R1 R6
Sampling profiler
Simple words: a profiler that looks at what the program is doing many times per second and counts what it sees. Analogy: the helper glancing at the kitchen every few seconds and making a tally mark at whichever station is busy. Precise: a profiler that interrupts the program at intervals and records the current function; gprof printed "Each sample counts as 0.01 seconds". R4 D2
Instruction-count profiler
Simple words: a profiler that counts every machine instruction the program executes, per function. Analogy: counting every movement of every cook instead of timing them. Precise: callgrind runs the program on Valgrind's synthetic CPU and counts executed instructions ("Ir", instructions read); the counts are repeatable but are not times. R3 D1
Inclusive cost
Simple words: the cost of a function including everything it calls. Analogy: the time of the whole fish course, including the sauce made for it. Precise: in callgrind, --inclusive=yes attributes to each function its own instructions plus those of its callees; main is close to 100 %. R3
Self cost
Simple words: the cost of a function's own code only, not the functions it calls. Analogy: the time the fish cook spends with her own hands. Precise: also called exclusive cost; gprof's "self seconds" column and callgrind's default listing; in the queue profile, self cost was attributed by source file, showing basic_string.h code inlined into take_oldest. R7 R4
Quadratic time
Simple words: work that grows with the square of the input: twice as much input, four times as much work. Analogy: every new ticket makes the cook move every waiting ticket one place along. Precise: running time proportional to n2 (written O(n2)); recognisable by a factor of about 4 when n doubles. D4 R6

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

When a program is slow, it is tempting to guess why and start changing things. Engineers have learned that their guesses are often wrong. So they measure first.

The simplest measurement is a stopwatch: how long does each part take? Run it a few times, because the computer is never exactly as fast twice. A profiler goes further: it tells you, for every function, how much of the work happened there.

Then you change only the part that takes the most time, and measure again. If the number did not go down, your change did not help, however clever it looked. And when a program gets much slower as its input grows, look at how fast it grows: twice the input taking four times as long is a warning sign.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Guess first, then time it

hotspot.cc (Listing 1) makes 200,000 text lines such as 17,rice,1234, parses each into an Order, sorts the orders by price and adds up the prices. Before reading on, guess which of the three steps takes longest. Most people guess the sort.

The program times its own steps with std::chrono::steady_clock (the Stopwatch class, lines 20–33). Built in a Release-like way (-O2 -g) and run five times, the medians of the five runs had parse as the largest of the three steps, in every timing run made for this chapter. R1 Two rules from this step:

  • Measure the build you ship. -O0 and sanitizer builds have very different costs (F2-29). -g adds names for the profiler without changing the generated code's optimisation level. D5
  • Repeat and take the median. The minimum and maximum of each step differed noticeably between runs on the shared build machine (see the min and max columns). The median is less disturbed by one slow run than the mean. R1

Count with callgrind

valgrind --tool=callgrind ./hotspot runs the program on Valgrind's simulated processor and counts every executed instruction per function; callgrind_annotate --inclusive=yes prints them. The program executed about 625 million instructions; parse_line accounted for 379,867,900 (60.82 %), sort_by_price for 18.75 % and make_lines for 18.28 %. R3 The sort, the usual first guess, is not the hot spot.

Why is parsing expensive? For every line, the original parse_line builds a std::istringstream, three std::string objects and calls std::stoi twice. hotspot_fast.cc changes only that function: it splits the line with std::string_view (no copies) and converts numbers with std::from_chars. D5 Measured again: parse_line fell from 379.9 to 49.4 million instructions; the other two steps were exactly unchanged; the total fell from about 625 to about 294 million; and the parse step's timed median fell to roughly a third or less of its earlier value. R5 R1 Same output (total 229937956 cents), same tests, less work.

Sample with gprof

gprof is a sampling profiler: build with -pg, run, then gprof -b -p program gmon.out prints a flat profile. It said "Each sample counts as 0.01 seconds". With 200,000 lines the whole run gave only a handful of samples, so the percentages were too coarse to trust. With 2,000,000 lines it ranked std::__introsort_loop (the sort) and make_lines highest, and parse_line at only a few percent. R4 That disagrees with callgrind. The Layer 3 section explains the most likely reason; the lesson for now is that a profile is evidence to be checked, not a verdict.

The usual tool that did not work

On Linux, the most common sampling profiler is perf, which uses the processor's performance counters through the kernel. In the build container it printed "WARNING: perf not found for kernel 6.18.44-fc" and exited with status 2. R2 This is common in containers and virtual machines. Know a second tool (callgrind needs no kernel support), and try perf on your own Linux machine: perf stat ./hotspot and perf record / perf report. D3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Why the tools disagreed. callgrind counts every instruction, including those inside shared libraries such as libstdc++, and charges them to the calling function in inclusive mode. gprof's -pg instruments only code compiled with -pg; time spent inside shared libraries, where most of the istringstream and stoi work happens, is not attributed to parse_line the same way. D2 Also, instruction counts are not time: a cache miss or a slow division costs more time than its one instruction suggests. When tools disagree, use the change-and-measure test: making parse_line cheaper reduced the timed parse step, which supports callgrind's ranking here. R5
  • Instruction counts are stable; times are not. callgrind's per-function counts were identical across the runs made for this chapter, while the timed medians moved by several milliseconds between runs. Use counts to compare versions of code precisely; use times to know what users feel. R3 R1 Valgrind runs the program much more slowly than normal; its counts are not affected by that, but timings taken under it are meaningless.
  • Growth beats constants. Making a function faster by a constant factor helps; changing how the cost grows with input helps more as input grows. The forensic lab shows the classic case: removing the first element of a std::vector moves all the others, so emptying it from the front costs about n2/2 moves in total. D4 D5
  • Correctness first. The fast to_int ignores the error result of std::from_chars; it trusts the input format, which is fine for text this program generated itself but not for text from a file or user. A faster function must pass the same tests (F2-30), including invalid inputs.
Not verified — check before relying on this

The explanation for the gprof versus callgrind disagreement (that gprof's -pg does not attribute time inside uninstrumented shared libraries to the caller in its flat profile) is from the title of the gprof manual (D2) and was not tested here, for example by linking the C++ library statically. The two gprof runs in R4 show the disagreement but not its cause. perf could not be run (R2); everything said about it is from its documentation title (D3). No hardware performance counters were read in this build.

How the hardware actually does it

A sampling profiler depends on interrupts: a timer (or, with perf, a hardware performance counter) interrupts the program at intervals, and the handler records the instruction address that was running; the profiler later maps addresses to functions with the symbol table (F2-32). D2 D3 That is why gprof's resolution is "0.01 seconds" per sample (R4). Performance counters are registers inside the processor that count events such as cycles, instructions retired and cache misses, which is how perf can report more than time. D6 callgrind takes the opposite approach: it does not run your machine code directly, but translates and executes it on a software model, adding a counter to every instruction; that is why it is repeatable, needs no kernel support, and is slow. D1 In the forensic profile, 30.93 % of instructions were in a C library function named __memcpy_avx_unaligned_erms: the library picked a copy routine that uses the processor's wide vector registers (AVX) to move bytes. R7

Worked example

Task. From the forensic numbers, decide whether queue.cc is linear or quadratic in the number of tickets n. R6

  1. callgrind counts: n = 10,000 → 2,105,051,433 instructions; n = 20,000 → 8,408,124,813.
  2. Ratio: 8,408,124,813 / 2,105,051,433 ≈ 3.99.
  3. Linear growth would give a ratio near 2; quadratic near 22 = 4. The measured ratio is 3.99: quadratic.
  4. Per ticket squared: 2,105,051,433 / 10,0002 ≈ 21 instructions, and 8,408,124,813 / 20,0002 ≈ 21 instructions. A constant per n2, as expected.
  5. Prediction for a busy evening of 100,000 tickets (5 × 20,000): about 52 × 8.4 billion ≈ 210 billion instructions. Not measured; try it if you have the patience.
  6. Wall-clock check: in the run shown in the forensic evidence, the time grew by a factor of about three to four between 10,000 and 20,000 tickets; times are noisier than counts (measured once each).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The helper slows the kitchen. A quiet helper with a stopwatch hardly disturbs cooks. Profilers change the program: callgrind runs it much slower, -pg adds code to every function (R4). The measured program is not exactly the shipped one.
    • 

  • Counting is not timing. Counting every hand movement (callgrind) is not the same as timing the dish; a few slow movements (cache misses) can matter more than many quick ones.
    • 

  • Kitchens are parallel. Many cooks work at once; this chapter's programs use one thread. Profiling parallel programs (CPU threads, GPUs) needs other tools, which come in the GPU tracks.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a "speed-up" that disappears on the next run. Cause: one run each, on a noisy machine. Fix: several runs, the median, and the min and max to see the noise (R1).
    • 

  • Symptom: profiling a Debug or sanitizer build. Cause: convenience. Fix: profile the optimised build with -g; Debug-only costs mislead.
    • 

  • Symptom: the profile shows only main. Cause: functions were inlined. Fix: inclusive view, line-level annotation, or temporary [[gnu::noinline]] as in Listing 1.
    • 

  • Symptom: optimising the function you expected to be slow, with no effect. Cause: guessing. Fix: profile first; here the sort was the guess and parsing the hot spot (R3).
    • 

  • Symptom: a program fine in tests, slow in production. Cause: tests use small inputs, and the cost grows faster than linearly. Fix: measure at two sizes and compute the ratio (worked example).
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Instructions grew from 2,105,051,433 to 8,408,124,813, a factor of 3.99, when tickets doubled. take_oldest has 99.91 % of the inclusive cost; 30.93 % of all instructions are in the C library's memory-copy routine, and 42.82 % (self) is basic_string.h code inlined into take_oldest. R6 R7


Hypotheses. (1) Slow printer hardware: against, the program never talks to a printer; print_all only counts characters, and it still slows down. (2) Longer ticket texts: against, characters grew exactly in proportion (137,500 to 275,000), not four times. (3) A data-structure cost that grows with the queue: for, a factor of about 4 for a doubling, and all the cost inside take_oldest.


Root cause. Line 18, waiting.erase(waiting.begin()): removing the first element of a std::vector moves every remaining element one place forward (here, moving each Ticket's std::string: the basic_string.h and memory-copy costs). With k tickets waiting, one removal costs about k moves; emptying a queue of n costs about n2/2 moves. D5 Line 17 also copies the ticket before erasing it.


Fix. Use std::deque and pop_front() (and move the ticket out instead of copying), as in queue_fixed.cc. Measured: 4,995,885 instructions at 10,000 tickets and 8,017,759 at 20,000 (ratio about 1.6, close to linear; the remainder is fixed start-up work), and wall times of a few milliseconds. R8 At 10,000 tickets that is about 421 times fewer instructions than before.




Prevention. A benchmark at two sizes in the project (as in the mini-project) with the growth ratio written down; a code-review habit: "removing from the front of a vector inside a loop" is a known quadratic pattern. A new printer would not have helped at all.


Wrong answers and why. "Reserve more capacity in the vector": reserving changes allocation, not the moves on every erase. "Use -O3": a better compiler setting changes the constant, not the growth; the count would still roughly quadruple.
