Story hook

Analogy

Farah rides home on a gravel path in the dark. She has two ways of feeling that she is turning. One is counting the turns of her wheels: if the left wheel of a tricycle turned more than the right, she went right. The other is the balance sense in her inner ear, which feels every turn of her head and body directly.

Each has its own weakness. On loose gravel a wheel spins without gripping, and the count says "you turned" when she did not. The inner ear never slips, but after a long ride it has a slight constant lean: it reports a gentle turn even when she rides straight, and the small error adds up to a big one over an hour.

A rider who trusts only the wheels gets lost on gravel; one who trusts only the ear drifts in a slow circle. A rider who listens to both can notice two things: when the wheels suddenly disagree with the ear, the wheels are slipping; and when they disagree slightly but always in the same direction, the ear has a lean, which can be learned and subtracted. That is sensor fusion, and this chapter builds it as an EKF.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why wheel odometry and a gyro have complementary failure modes (slip versus bias and drift).
  • Augment a filter's state with a sensor bias and explain why the bias becomes observable only when two sensors are combined.
  • Tune the gyro measurement noise from the MA202 rest recording and justify the other noise settings.
  • Reject outliers with an NIS gate and choose its threshold from the chi-square distribution.
  • Diagnose a filter whose configuration has made a bias unlearnable, from its own innovations.
  • Design an EKF localisation node, the RB302 course project and seed of MP6.
Jargon box
Sensor fusion
Simple words: combining several sensors so that each covers the others' weaknesses. Analogy: riding by both the wheel count and the inner ear. Precise: estimating one state from several measurement models in one filter, each weighted by its noise covariance; the result is better than any single sensor only if the models (including biases and outliers) are right. D1
State augmentation
Simple words: adding a sensor's error to the list of things the filter estimates. Analogy: Farah learning how much her inner ear leans. Precise: extending the state vector with parameters such as a bias b, given a motion model (here a slow random walk) and appearing in the measurement model (here zgyro = ω + b + noise). D1D2
Validation gate
Simple words: a rule that throws away a reading that is far too surprising. Analogy: when the wheels report a sharp turn the ear never felt, ignore the wheels. Precise: skipping an update when its NIS y²/S exceeds a threshold; for one measurement and a consistent filter, P(NIS > 9) = 0.0027 (run R4). D1R4
Wheel slip
Simple words: a wheel turning without moving the robot the same distance. Analogy: a tyre spinning on gravel. Precise: a violation of the rolling-without-slipping assumption of odometry; it produces large, short, non-Gaussian errors in the odometry speed and turn rate. D1
Localisation node
Simple words: a self-contained program that receives sensor messages and publishes where the robot is and how sure it is. Analogy: the part of Farah's brain that keeps track of where she is from all her senses. Precise: a software component with message inputs (odometry, IMU, landmark readings), an internal filter, time-stamp handling and a pose-with-covariance output at a fixed rate; the RB302 project and an MP6 component. W1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot has two ways to feel itself turn. Its wheels count turns: if one wheel turns more than the other, the robot turned. A little chip inside, the gyro, feels the turning directly, like the balance sense in your ear.

On a slippery floor a wheel can spin, and then the wheels say "we turned!" when the robot did not. The gyro is never fooled by slippery floors, but it has a tiny mistake of its own: it always says the robot is turning a little bit, even when it is standing still. Over two minutes that tiny mistake adds up to a big wrong turn.

So the robot listens to both. When they disagree a lot, suddenly, it ignores the wheels for that moment: they are probably slipping. When they disagree a little, always the same way, it learns the gyro's tiny mistake and subtracts it. Together they know the robot's direction much better than either alone.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Two sensors, two failure modes

Wheel odometry (F9-09) measures speed v and turn rate ω from encoder counts. Its errors are small, zero-mean on a good floor, and occasionally huge when a wheel slips. A rate gyro (F9-10, MA202 F0-62) measures ω plus a bias b plus noise. Its noise is small, it never slips, but integrating the bias gives a heading drift that grows linearly in time: 0.3°/s for 120 s is 36° (run R4). D1D2R4

The augmented model

The state is s = [x, y, θ, ω, b]T. The prediction uses the odometry speed v as an input and lets the turn rate and the bias change slowly:

x' = x + vΔt cos θ, y' = y + vΔt sin θ, θ' = θ + ωΔt, ω' = ω + wω, b' = b + wb.

The two turn-rate readings are linear in the state: zgyro = ω + b + noise, Hg = [0 0 0 1 1], and zodo = ω + noise, Ho = [0 0 0 1 0]. The prediction is non-linear (cos θ, sin θ), so this is an EKF; the updates are plain Kalman updates. D1W1

Why b can be learned: with the gyro alone, only the sum ω + b is measured; the filter cannot split it, and the bias stays at its initial guess (the "gyro only" row of run R2 ends with b̂ = 0.007°/s for a true 0.3°/s). The odometry measures ω alone. Their difference is a measurement of b. Each sensor is needed for the other to be corrected. R2W1

Tuning from measurements

The RB302 card asks for noise covariances tuned from MA202 measurements. Listing 2 does this for the gyro: it reads the MA202 rest recording imu_rest.csv (lab F0-62, a simulated IMU lying still) and takes the sample standard deviation of the z-axis rate: 0.00174 rad/s, 0.0995°/s (run R2). The simulator of this chapter was set to 0.1°/s so that the two agree; on a real robot you record your own IMU at rest, motors on. The odometry turn-rate noise (0.02 rad/s) and the random-walk sizes of ω (0.05 rad/s per step) and b (10−5 rad/s per step) are model choices, checked by the results; on a robot σodo is measured on a test drive with ground truth (F9-32's method). R2R1

The odometry is 132 times noisier than the gyro in variance (run R4), so the filter follows the gyro for fast changes and uses the odometry slowly, over many samples, to learn the bias. R4

Rejecting slip with a gate

During a slip the odometry turn rate is wrong by about 0.6 rad/s. With S ≈ 0.0004 (rad/s)² that is an NIS of about 900, while a consistent 1-dimensional innovation exceeds 9 only 0.27 % of the time (run R4). Listing 2 skips any odometry update with NIS > 9. On the recording (25 slip samples) the gate rejected 29 updates, 25 of them during slips; about 3 false rejections were expected in 1200 updates (run R4). R2R4

Results

Final heading errors after 120 s (run R2): odometry only 85.8° (the two slips add 51.6° + 34.4° = 85.9°, run R4); gyro only 35.0° (the bias, 36° expected); fusion without the gate 85.6°; fusion with the gate 0.26°, with an RMS heading error of 1.09° and a bias estimate of 0.296°/s for a true 0.3°/s. R2R4

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why fusion without a gate is worse than the gyro alone

During a slip the gyro says "ω + b is small", the odometry says "ω is 0.6 rad/s". The filter's model can explain this only by raising ω and lowering b. The tightly measured gyro keeps ω + b honest, so the slip is pushed into the bias estimate: at the end the ungated filter believes b = −0.41°/s for a true +0.30°/s (run R2). From then on it subtracts the wrong bias from every gyro reading, and its heading drifts as badly as odometry alone. An unmodelled fault in one sensor does not stay in that sensor: the filter spreads it into every state that is linked by the model. That is why gating, and more generally fault detection, belongs in every fusion filter. R2W1

Choosing the gate

The threshold trades missed faults against rejected good data. At 9 (three standard deviations for one measurement) a consistent filter loses 0.27 % of its updates. A lower gate rejects more good data; a much higher gate lets moderate slips through. The gate works only if S is honest: an overconfident filter (F9-32's forensic lab) rejects good readings, and if it rejects too many it starves and becomes more overconfident still. A practical safeguard is to count consecutive rejections and, after a limit, reset or inflate P. W1D1

What the gate does not catch

The slips also add 0.15 m/s to the odometry speed for 2.5 s, 0.375 m of false distance (run R4). The speed is an input to the prediction, not a measurement, so it is never tested against anything, and the gated filter's final position error is 0.347 m (run R2). To catch speed slip, the filter needs a second source of speed or position: an accelerometer (weak, it needs integration), a camera, or landmark readings (F9-34). The course project combines them. R2R4W1

The complementary filter

Once its gains have settled, the fusion filter combines a high-frequency source (gyro) with a low-frequency correction (odometry) through constant weights. Fixed-gain filters built on exactly this split are called complementary filters; they are cheaper than an EKF and are used for drone attitude in DN301 (the Mahony–Hamel–Pflimlin paper is the course's reference). The EKF's advantages are that its gains come from the measured noise, it reports its uncertainty, and gating and extra sensors fit into the same framework. D3W1

Different rates and delays

Here both sensors arrive together at 10 Hz. On a robot the gyro is read faster than the encoders and both have delays. A fusion filter then predicts to each reading's time stamp, applies that reading's update alone, and continues; a reading that arrives late is either applied to a stored past state or dropped. This is what the project's node must do. D1W1

How the hardware actually does it

The gyro is one axis of an IMU (HW302 F1-65, RB201 F9-10), read over a serial bus at a configured rate; for a ground robot the axis that matters is the one perpendicular to the floor. Encoders (HW302 F1-66) are counted by timer hardware; the turn rate follows from the two wheel speeds and the wheel separation (F9-09). D2W1

Three hardware facts decide whether the fusion works: the IMU's axes must be aligned with the robot's (or the misalignment measured and rotated out); the gyro's bias changes with temperature and with each power-up, which is why it must be estimated on-line rather than calibrated once; and the encoder and IMU clocks must share a time base. D2

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware: no robot, IMU or encoders were available in this build. The bias (0.3°/s), noise levels, slip size and the claim that bias varies with temperature and power-up are simulator settings or general statements, not measurements of any part; the "Small Unmanned Aircraft" text, cited title only, is expected to discuss gyro bias, but no edition or section was checked. A real IMU's noise and bias stability come from its datasheet (dossier, AH-21) and from your own rest recordings at several temperatures.

Worked example

Numbers from the recording of Listing 1 and the forensic run.

  1. Bias in rad/s: 0.3°/s × π/180 = 0.0052 rad/s.
  2. Drift of the gyro alone: 0.3°/s × 120 s = 36°. The "gyro only" run ends 35.0° off (R2).
  3. Slip: 0.6 rad/s for 1.5 s = 0.9 rad = 51.6°; 0.6 rad/s for 1.0 s = 34.4°; total 85.9°. Odometry alone ends 85.8° off (R2).
  4. Gate: S ≈ σodo² = 0.0004; a 0.6 rad/s slip gives NIS = 0.36/0.0004 = 900 ≫ 9. P(NIS > 9) for a consistent innovation = 0.0027, so about 3.2 false rejections in 1200 updates.
  5. Detecting the bias from innovations: if the filter ignores the bias, every odometry innovation has mean −0.0052 rad/s. With σ = 0.02, the mean of 200 innovations has a standard error 0.02/√200 = 0.0014, so the shift is 3.7 standard errors: detectable in 20 s, although the NIS is still about 1.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The inner ear adapts; a gyro does not. Your balance sense stops reporting a steady rotation after a while and recalibrates itself in ways a gyro chip does not; the filter must do the learning. The analogy's "lean" is a convenient picture of bias, not a model of how the ear works. W1
    • 

  • Farah notices slip by feel; the filter only by numbers. She hears the gravel. The filter sees a slip only as an innovation that does not fit; a slow slip that fits the noise passes the gate. R2
    • 

  • Two friends, but they are not independent of the model. The registered Kalman analogy weighs two guesses. Here the guesses are linked through hidden states (ω and b), and one friend's lie changes what the filter believes about the other (Layer 3). R2

Lab


Goal


Fuse wheel odometry and a gyro in an EKF with a bias state and a gate, with the gyro noise tuned from the MA202 rest recording, and show on a recording with ground truth that the fused heading beats both sensors.


Hardware and software needed


A computer with a C++ compiler (this build: g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0) and the MA202 lab folder university/labs/F0-62 with imu_rest.csv. Real variant (untested on hardware here): a differential-drive robot with encoders and an IMU, a rest recording of the IMU, and a dock or floor marks to measure the heading at the end of a drive.
