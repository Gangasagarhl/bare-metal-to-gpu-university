Story hook

Analogy

A cycling club of five hundred riders is spread along a long ring road at night, and nobody knows where the club's leader, Lin, actually is. Each rider makes a guess by standing somewhere on the ring: "maybe he is here". Every minute, all of them ride one kilometre forward, wobbling a little, as Lin does.

Then Lin radios: "I am passing a lit shop window." Riders standing next to a shop window shout "that fits me!"; riders in the dark stay quiet. The club then reshuffles: riders whose guess fitted are copied (a friend joins them), riders whose guess did not fit leave. After a few such messages, most of the club is crowded around one or two places, and that crowd is the club's belief about where Lin is.

That is a particle filter: a belief made of many guesses, moved with the motion model, weighted by the reading, and resampled. Unlike the Kalman filter's single bell, a crowd can stand in two places at once, and it can be any shape.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how a set of weighted samples represents a belief, and why it can hold several hypotheses.
  • Carry out the three steps (sample the motion, weight by the likelihood, resample) by hand on a few particles.
  • Compute the effective sample size and implement systematic resampling.
  • Compare a particle filter with a grid Bayes filter on the same data and judge how many particles are needed.
  • Diagnose particle deprivation and explain the defences against it.
Jargon box
Particle filter
Simple words: a Bayes filter whose belief is a crowd of guesses. Analogy: the cycling club spread along the ring. Precise: a sequential Monte Carlo implementation of the Bayes filter: the belief is approximated by N samples (particles) with weights; prediction samples each particle from the motion model, the update multiplies weights by the measurement likelihood, and resampling replaces low-weight particles by copies of high-weight ones. Used for robot localisation under the name Monte Carlo localisation. D1
Importance weight
Simple words: how well one guess fits the readings. Analogy: how loudly a rider shouts "that fits me!". Precise: a non-negative number wi attached to particle i, multiplied by p(z | xi) at each update and normalised to sum to 1. D1
Resampling
Simple words: copying good guesses and dropping poor ones. Analogy: the club's reshuffle. Precise: drawing N new particles from the current ones with probability proportional to weight and resetting the weights to 1/N; systematic (low-variance) resampling uses one random offset and N equally spaced pointers. D1
Effective sample size (ESS)
Simple words: how many of the guesses are really doing work. Analogy: if only three riders are shouting, the club is effectively three riders. Precise: ESS = 1/Σwi²: N for equal weights, 1 when one particle holds all the weight; a common rule resamples when ESS < N/2. W1R5
Particle deprivation
Simple words: the crowd has collapsed into a few clones, so no guess is near the truth any more. Analogy: the whole club standing in one spot, sure of itself, while Lin is elsewhere. Precise: loss of diversity in the particle set through repeated resampling, especially without motion noise; the filter can no longer represent hypotheses away from its clones and cannot recover. D1
Kidnapped robot problem
Simple words: the robot is picked up and put somewhere else without being told. Analogy: Lin is driven to another part of the ring in a van. Precise: a test of whether a localisation filter can recover from a belief that is confidently wrong; it requires the filter to keep some probability everywhere. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Imagine five hundred little robot ghosts, each standing where the real robot might be. Nobody knows which ghost is right.

When the real robot drives one step forward, every ghost also drives one step forward, each a little differently, because the real robot's wheels are not perfect either. When the real robot sees a door, every ghost checks: "is there a door where I am standing?" Ghosts next to a door become important; ghosts next to a wall become unimportant.

Then the unimportant ghosts disappear and the important ones make copies of themselves, so there are five hundred again. After a few doors, almost all the ghosts are standing in the same place, and that is where the robot probably is. If two places fit equally well, two crowds of ghosts stay, until a reading decides between them.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A belief made of samples

F9-31's grid stored a probability for every cell. A particle filter stores N sample states x1…xN with weights w1…wN. The probability of any region is approximated by the total weight of the particles in it; any average is approximated by a weighted average over particles. By the law of large numbers, these approximations improve as N grows, with an error that shrinks roughly like 1/√N (MA202). D1D2

The three steps

  • Move: for each particle, draw a new state from the motion model p(x' | x, u). The prediction's spread comes from the particles' different random draws, not from a formula.
  • Weight: multiply each weight by the likelihood p(z | xi) of the reading, then normalise.
  • Resample: when the weights have become uneven, draw N particles with probability proportional to weight; set all weights to 1/N.

These are the two steps of the Bayes filter (F9-31): the move is the prediction, the weight is the update. Only the representation changed. Note what the particle filter does not need: no Gaussian assumption, no Jacobians, no linear models. It only needs to sample the motion model and to evaluate the likelihood. D1W1

When to resample

After a few updates, a few particles hold most of the weight and the rest are wasted effort. The effective sample size ESS = 1/Σwi² measures this: in the worked example, six particles with weights 0.316, 0.316, 0.018, 0.316, 0.018, 0.018 have an ESS of 3.33 (run R1). Resampling too rarely wastes particles; resampling too often throws away diversity, because every resampling step duplicates some particles and drops others. A common compromise, used in Listing 2, is to resample only when ESS < N/2. R1W1

The corridor again, continuous this time

Listing 2 is the corridor robot of F9-31 with a continuous position on a 30 m ring, doors as 1 m stretches, a 1 m step with sd 0.3 m, and the same door sensor (0.9 / 0.05). It runs a 500-particle filter and a 0.1 m grid filter on the same simulated readings. At step 16 (second door seen), the particle filter puts 0.765 of its mass within 1 m of the truth, the grid 0.801; at step 29, 0.988 and 0.990 (run R2). With 50, 500 and 5000 particles, the mean difference from the grid's mass is 0.103, 0.024 and 0.012: more particles, closer to the exact Bayes filter. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why particles: shapes a Gaussian cannot hold

An EKF holds one bell; F9-30's "banana" after a long drive with heading noise, the two-peak belief after the first door, and a robot that is lost (F9-34's linearisation limit) are not bells. A grid can hold them but costs memory in every cell of the state space, most of which have almost no probability: F9-30 counted 11.5 million cells for a floor robot. Particles go where the probability is. For the 1-dimensional corridor the grid is cheaper (Listing 2's grid prediction costs 7800 multiply-adds per step, run R5); in three or more dimensions the comparison turns around. D1R5

Systematic resampling

Multinomial resampling (N independent random draws) adds unnecessary randomness: even with equal weights, only about 316 of 500 particles would survive as distinct copies (run R5). Systematic resampling uses one random offset u0 in [0, 1/N) and pointers u0 + m/N; with equal weights, every particle is picked exactly once, and in general each particle is copied either ⌊Nwi⌋ or ⌈Nwi⌉ times. It also costs only one pass over the cumulative weights. R5W1

Diversity: the motion noise is the only source

Resampling never creates new positions; it only copies. New diversity comes only from the random part of the motion model. A filter whose particles move without noise (or with too little) loses distinct positions at every resampling and never regains them. If then the truth is somewhere no particle is, every particle gets the same small likelihood, the weights stay equal, the ESS stays at N, and nothing in the weights reveals the problem. The forensic lab is this failure. Defences: honest motion noise, resampling only when ESS is low, and injecting a small share of random particles so that some probability exists everywhere. Adaptive versions inject more random particles when the average likelihood of the readings drops. D1R3R4

Not verified — check before relying on this

The adaptive injection of random particles driven by the average likelihood, and the statement that the course book presents particle filters for localisation under the name "Monte Carlo localisation" with such variants, are written from general knowledge; "Probabilistic Robotics" was not opened in this build. The fix tested here (run R4) is the simpler fixed 2 % injection. The Source Researcher should confirm the book's sections on Monte Carlo localisation and its recovery variants.

How many particles?

The number needed grows with the volume of state space the belief covers and with how peaked the likelihood is. A tracking filter with a small belief needs few; global localisation in a large building needs many at the start and few once localised, which motivates filters that adapt N. With too few particles the estimate is noisy and, worse, the true state may fall between particles; Listing 2's 50-particle run differs from the grid by 0.10 in mass on average (run R2). The cost per step is linear in N, and each particle's work is independent, so particle filters parallelise well. R2D1W1

How the hardware actually does it

On a robot, a particle filter for localisation runs on the main computer, not on a microcontroller: each particle with a scanning range sensor needs the likelihood of a whole scan, which means many ray or map look-ups per particle per update. The likelihood comes from the sensor model and a map of the building; the motion samples come from the odometry model of F9-34 with noise. Particle count, update rate and the share of random particles are the main tuning knobs. D1W1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware: this build ran only a 1-dimensional simulated corridor. No statement here about particle counts, update rates or processing load on any real robot computer was measured. Robot software stacks include ready-made particle-filter localisation components; their names, parameters and behaviour were not checked in this build and belong in MP6 and RB401 after the Source Researcher has confirmed them.

Worked example

Six particles on the corridor of Listing 2, at 1.3, 4.1, 6.6, 11.2, 16.4 and 25.0 m, equal weights 1/6. The robot reports "door". Doors are at 1–2, 4–5, 11–12, 19–20 and 21–22 m.

  1. Likelihoods: particles 0, 1 and 3 stand at doors: 0.9 each; particles 2, 4 and 5 at walls: 0.05 each.
  2. Unnormalised weights: likelihood × 1/6; sum = (3 × 0.9 + 3 × 0.05)/6 = 0.475.
  3. Normalised: 0.15/0.475 = 0.3158 for the door particles, 0.00833/0.475 = 0.0175 for the others.
  4. ESS: 1/(3 × 0.3158² + 3 × 0.0175²) = 3.33 of 6: half the particles are wasted.
  5. Cumulative weights: 0.3158, 0.6316, 0.6491, 0.9649, 0.9825, 1.0000.
  6. Systematic resampling with u0 = 0.1/6 = 0.0167: pointers 0.017, 0.183, 0.350, 0.517, 0.683, 0.850. 0.017 and 0.183 fall below 0.3158 (particle 0); 0.350 and 0.517 below 0.6316 (particle 1); 0.683 and 0.850 between 0.6491 and 0.9649 (particle 3).
  7. New set: 1.3, 1.3, 4.1, 4.1, 11.2, 11.2, each with weight 1/6. The next move will spread the copies apart again.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Riders think; particles do not. A rider might reason "I am far from any shop, let me ride over there". A particle only moves by the motion model and is weighted; it never seeks the truth. That is why diversity must be designed in (Layer 3). R3
    • 

  • Copying a rider is not free in the real world; copying a particle is. Resampling is cheap and instant, which tempts developers to do it at every step: the cause of the forensic lab's failure. R3
    • 

  • The registered "two friends" analogy has two guesses with stated reliability. A particle carries no "give or take" of its own; the uncertainty is the spread of the whole crowd. D1

Forensic lab


Scenario: "The robot that would not believe it had been moved"


Release 1.1 of the corridor robot's particle filter was praised for its "smooth, steady" position display. During a demonstration, a visitor, Noor, picked the robot up at step 20 and set it down 13 m further along the corridor. The display never noticed; for twenty more steps it reported positions that had nothing to do with where the robot now was. The release notes say: "motion model simplified; resampling after every reading for a steadier estimate".

Summary


    

  • A particle filter represents the belief by N weighted samples; move by sampling, weight by the likelihood, resample when the ESS is low.
    • 

  • It needs no Gaussian, no linearisation and no Jacobians; it can hold several hypotheses and any shape.
    • 

  • ESS = 1/Σw²; systematic resampling is cheap and adds little randomness.
    • 

  • More particles approach the exact Bayes filter (lab: 0.103, 0.024, 0.012 mean difference for 50, 500, 5000).
    • 

  • Diversity comes only from motion noise; without it the filter suffers particle deprivation and cannot recover from a kidnapping, while its ESS looks normal.
    •
