Story hook

Analogy

Lin's bike computer has broken. Farah still reads the kilometre posts as they pass, but now Lin wants to know their speed: are they going fast enough to reach the lake before dark? Nobody can read speed from a post. Yet after three posts, ten minutes apart, Farah says: "We did about 3 km in 20 minutes, so roughly 9 km/h." She has estimated something she never measured, from a series of things she did.

And the two numbers hang together. If she misread the last post as further than it really was, she will believe both that they are further along and that they are faster. An error in one becomes an error in the other. A good estimator has to keep track not only of how unsure it is about each number, but of how their errors are linked.

The Kalman filter in matrix form does exactly this. The state becomes a list of numbers (position and speed), the variance becomes a covariance matrix whose off-diagonal entries record the links, and the same predict–update cycle of F9-32 now estimates the speed it never sees.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a linear motion and measurement model in the form xk = F xk−1 + noise, zk = H xk + noise, and say what each matrix means.
  • Derive the process-noise matrix Q for a constant-velocity model with random accelerations.
  • Implement the matrix Kalman filter in C++ with a small matrix type, including the Joseph-form covariance update.
  • Explain how a filter estimates an unmeasured state through the off-diagonal covariance, and read a covariance ellipse.
  • Check a filter with ground truth (NEES) and without it (NIS), and recognise a fault that both checks miss.
Jargon box
State vector
Simple words: the list of numbers the filter estimates. Analogy: "where we are and how fast we go" on the bicycle trip. Precise: a column vector x with n entries chosen so that the next state depends only on the current state, the inputs and fresh noise (the state of a dynamic system, F0-67); in this chapter x = [position, velocity]T. D1
State-transition matrix (F)
Simple words: the rule that moves the state one step forward. Analogy: "in ten minutes at 9 km/h we move 1.5 km further, and our speed stays the same". Precise: the n × n matrix of the linear motion model xk = F xk−1 + B uk + wk; for constant velocity, F = [[1, Δt], [0, 1]]. D1
Measurement matrix (H)
Simple words: the rule that says what a sensor would read for a given state. Analogy: the kilometre post shows position, never speed. Precise: the m × n matrix of the linear measurement model zk = H xk + vk; for a position sensor, H = [1, 0]. D1
Innovation covariance (S)
Simple words: how large the filter expects the next surprise to be. Analogy: how far the next post may plausibly differ from where Farah expects it. Precise: S = H P− HT + R, an m × m matrix; it replaces P− + R of F9-32. D1
Joseph form
Simple words: a longer but safer way to write the covariance update. Analogy: checking a sum by adding the column twice in different orders. Precise: P = (I − KH) P− (I − KH)T + K R KT; algebraically equal to (I − KH) P− for the optimal K, but it keeps P symmetric and positive semidefinite under rounding errors. D1W1
NEES (normalised estimation error squared)
Simple words: the real error measured in units of the error the filter claims. Analogy: how far off Farah's guess of position and speed really was, compared with her own "give or take". Precise: ε = eT P−1 e with e = x̂ − xtrue; needs ground truth; its mean is n (the number of states) for a consistent filter. W1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot drives along a straight line. A sensor tells it where it is, a little wrongly, ten times a second. Nothing tells it how fast it goes. The robot wants to know both.

It keeps two guesses now, "where" and "how fast", and also remembers how sure it is about each, and how the two are linked. Each step, it says: "if I was here, going this fast, a tenth of a second later I will be a bit further on." Its "where" moves by its "how fast", and both become a little less sure.

Then the sensor speaks. If the sensor says "you are further than you thought", two things are probably true: you are further on, and you were going a little faster than you thought. So the robot nudges both guesses. How much to nudge each is what the matrices compute. After a few seconds of this, the robot knows its speed well, though no sensor ever measured it.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The model in matrices

Let the state be x = [p, v]T. Over one interval Δt the robot moves with a random acceleration a that is constant during the interval, with zero mean and standard deviation σa. Then

pk = pk−1 + Δt vk−1 + ½Δt² a, vk = vk−1 + Δt a,

which is xk = F xk−1 + G a with F = [[1, Δt], [0, 1]] and G = [½Δt², Δt]T. The noise term G a has covariance Q = σa² G GT: a full matrix, because one random acceleration moves both position and velocity. The position sensor reads z = H x + v with H = [1, 0] and R = σz². Matrix multiplication and transpose are those of MA201 (F0-50, F0-51). D1D3W1

The five lines

The one-dimensional equations of F9-32 become, step for step:

  • Predict: x̂− = F x̂ (+ B u when there is a control input); P− = F P FT + Q.
  • Innovation: y = z − H x̂−; S = H P− HT + R.
  • Gain: K = P− HT S−1.
  • Update: x̂ = x̂− + K y; P = (I − KH) P− (I − KH)T + K R KT.

Why F P FT? If e is the error before the step, F e is the error after it, and the covariance of F e is F Cov(e) FT (expand E[(Fe)(Fe)T]). Why is K a matrix? It has one row per state variable: it says how much of the innovation each state variable takes. With one reading per step, S is a 1 × 1 matrix and S−1 a division. D1D2W1

How the unmeasured velocity is estimated

Write out K for H = [1, 0]: K = [P−pp, P−vp]T / (P−pp + R). The velocity's share of the innovation is proportional to the off-diagonal entry P−vp, the covariance between position error and velocity error. Prediction creates that covariance: F P FT adds Δt Pvv to the off-diagonal. So the velocity is estimated because, and only as much as, the model links it to the position. This is the tilt in Figure 2. W1R5

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why the Joseph form

The short update P = (I − KH)P− is a difference of two matrices. When P− is large and R small (a very good sensor after a long gap), the difference is a small number computed from two large ones, and rounding can make P slightly non-symmetric or give it a negative eigenvalue: a "negative variance". From then on the gain can have the wrong sign and the filter diverges. The Joseph form is a sum of two terms of the form A M AT with M positive semidefinite, so it stays symmetric and positive semidefinite whatever K is, and it is also correct for a gain that is not optimal (for example a fixed gain loaded from a file). It costs roughly twice the arithmetic. Listing 2 uses it and the worked example uses the short form; comparing the two on the lab data is lab step 3 (not run in this build). D1W1D4

Choosing Q: the model is part of the measurement

σa²GGT assumes that the acceleration is constant during each interval and independent between intervals. A different assumption (acceleration as continuous white noise) gives a different matrix with entries Δt³/3, Δt²/2 and Δt instead of Δt⁴/4, Δt³/2, Δt². Neither is "the" Q of a real robot. Q describes what your model leaves out (driver commands, bumps, wheel slip), and is tuned like F9-32's: from a test drive with ground truth, and checked with NEES and NIS. In this lab the simulator generates exactly the piecewise-constant model, so the match is perfect by construction; a real robot never gives you that. D1W1

Consistency tests and their limits

For a consistent filter with n states, NEES has mean n and follows a chi-square distribution with n degrees of freedom if the errors are Gaussian; with 2 states, a single NEES value exceeds 5.99 about 5 % of the time (run R5). NIS works the same way with m. NEES needs ground truth and is a lab tool; NIS works on the robot. Both test only what the filter's model can compare. The forensic lab shows a fault that leaves NIS perfect: a wrong time step makes the filter consistently believe in a wrong velocity, and the position readings still agree with its predictions. Innovation tests check the model's predictions of what is measured, not the meaning of the hidden states. W1R3R5

When a state cannot be estimated

If F and H give no link between a state variable and the readings, its variance only grows and its gain stays 0: the state is unobservable. With H = [0, 1] (a speed sensor only) the position would drift exactly like odometry. Observability is a property of the model pair (F, H), and a formal test exists in control theory (MA301 and the "Feedback Systems" text treat it); here the practical test is enough: if a variance in P grows without bound while the filter runs, that state is not being observed. D5W1

Cost

The prediction F P FT costs about 2n³ multiply-adds with general matrices, the gain needs an m × m inverse. For n = 2 and m = 1 one step is a few dozen operations; for the 5-state filter of F9-35 a few hundred; filters with hundreds of states (mapping) need structure-aware algorithms. Note also that P and K do not depend on the readings at all: for a fixed rate, they can be computed in advance, and the steady-state gain can replace the whole covariance computation on a small controller (a "steady-state Kalman filter"), at the price of losing the start-up behaviour. W1D1

How the hardware actually does it

On a robot, the matrix filter runs in the control software at the rate of its fastest sensor or of a fixed timer. Three practical points decide whether it works: W1

  • Time stamps: F and Q depend on Δt. A sensor that reports late, skips samples or changes rate must be handled by computing Δt from the time stamps at every step (the forensic lab). R4
  • Number format: this chapter uses double precision on a computer. On a controller with only single-precision hardware, the Joseph form and keeping P symmetric (average P with PT after each step) matter more; more robust square-root and factorised forms exist and are beyond this course. D1
  • Libraries: production code uses a linear-algebra library rather than a hand-written Mat; the hand-written one here exists so that every operation is visible. W1
Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware: the filter ran only on simulator recordings in this build. The statements about single-precision behaviour and factorised (square-root) forms come from general numerical knowledge and were not tested or sourced here; the Source Researcher should confirm them against a numerical-methods source before a learner relies on them. Worked example

Farah's bicycle with Δt = 1 (one time unit between posts, numbers kept small). Previous belief: position 0, speed 1, P = I (sd 1 for each, uncorrelated). Random acceleration sd 0.5, so Q = 0.25 G GT with G = [0.5, 1]T. Post reading z = 1.4 with sd 0.5 (R = 0.25).

  1. Q: 0.25 × [[0.25, 0.5], [0.5, 1]] = [[0.0625, 0.125], [0.125, 0.25]].
  2. Predict the mean: x̂− = F x̂ = [0 + 1·1, 1] = [1, 1].
  3. Predict the covariance: F P FT = [[2, 1], [1, 1]]; plus Q gives P− = [[2.0625, 1.125], [1.125, 1.25]]. The off-diagonal 1.125 is the tilt: position and velocity errors are now linked.
  4. Innovation: y = 1.4 − 1 = 0.4; S = P−pp + R = 2.3125.
  5. Gain: K = [2.0625, 1.125]T / 2.3125 = [0.8919, 0.4865]T.
  6. Update the mean: x̂ = [1 + 0.8919·0.4, 1 + 0.4865·0.4] = [1.3568, 1.1946]. The speed rose although no one measured it.
  7. Update the covariance (short form): P = (I − KH)P− = [[0.2230, 0.1216], [0.1216, 0.7027]]: sds 0.472 for position (from 1.436) and 0.838 for speed (from 1.118).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Farah reasons about speed explicitly; the filter does not. The filter never divides distance by time. Velocity changes only through the gain, which comes from the covariance. If the model's F is wrong (the forensic lab), the filter's "reasoning" is wrong without any warning. R3
    • 

  • Friends do not know how their errors are linked; the filter must. The link (off-diagonal covariance) is the whole mechanism. A friend's "give or take" has no tilt. W1
    • 

  • The registered analogy has two friends; here one "friend" is a vector. The reading speaks only about position; the filter decides how its message is shared out over position and velocity. D1

Method hints


The filter predicts the next position as p + Δt·v. If the true interval is different from Δt, which value of v makes the predictions match? Innovation statistics compare predicted readings with real ones; ask whether a wrong velocity can produce right predictions.

  • The matrix filter: x̂− = F x̂, P− = F P FT + Q; y = z − H x̂−, S = H P− HT + R; K = P− HT S−1; x̂ = x̂− + K y, P in Joseph form.

    •
