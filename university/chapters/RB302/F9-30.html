Story hook

Analogy

Nia cycles home from her aunt's house every Thursday. Today she left late, and by the time she reaches the long road by the river it is nearly dark. She cannot read the street signs any more. Still, she is not lost: she knows she turned onto the river road about five minutes ago and that she rides at her usual pace, so she must be "somewhere around the old boathouse, give or take a bit".

The longer she pedals without seeing anything she recognises, the bigger that "give or take" becomes. After ten more minutes she would only say "somewhere between the boathouse and the bridge". Then the lit window of the corner bakery appears on her left, and suddenly she is sure again: the bakery is exactly 300 metres before her turn.

Nia never knew her position exactly. What she carried in her head was something richer: a spread of possible places, with some more likely than others, that grew while she rode blind and shrank when she saw a landmark. A robot needs the same thing. This chapter gives it a name, belief, and three ways to store it in a computer.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why a robot's software must keep a belief (a probability distribution over states) instead of a single number for its position.
  • Represent a belief as a histogram over cells, as a Gaussian (mean and covariance) and as a set of particles, and state what each can and cannot express.
  • Compute how uncertainty grows when a robot only counts its own motion (dead reckoning): variances of independent step errors add.
  • Recognise when a single Gaussian is a poor summary of a belief (two peaks; the curved "banana" of heading uncertainty).
  • Diagnose a navigation module that reports a constant uncertainty while its real error grows.
Jargon box
State (of a robot)
Simple words: the few numbers that describe where the robot is and how it is moving right now. Analogy: Nia's place on the river road and her speed. Precise: the set of variables whose present values, together with the future inputs, determine the future behaviour; for a robot in a corridor, its position; for a robot on a floor, (x, y, θ), possibly with velocities and sensor biases. Same idea as MA301 F0-67. D1D5
Belief
Simple words: what the robot thinks about where it is: every possible place, with a probability for each. Analogy: Nia's "somewhere around the boathouse, give or take a bit". Precise: the probability distribution of the state xt given all measurements z1:t and controls u1:t so far, written bel(xt) = p(xt | z1:t, u1:t). D1
Uncertainty
Simple words: how spread out the belief is. Analogy: the size of Nia's "give or take". Precise: a measure of the spread of the belief, for example its variance (one state variable) or its covariance matrix (several); for beliefs with several peaks, no single number describes it fully. D1D2
Histogram (grid) belief
Simple words: cut the world into cells and store one probability per cell. Analogy: numbering the stretches of the river road and saying how likely each one is. Precise: a discrete approximation of the belief by the probability mass in each cell of a fixed partition of the state space; the masses are non-negative and sum to 1. D1
Gaussian belief
Simple words: describe the belief by a best guess and a spread, assuming the bell shape. Analogy: "the boathouse, plus or minus fifty metres". Precise: a belief of the form N(μ, Σ), fully described by its mean vector μ and covariance matrix Σ; it has exactly one peak. D1D2
Particles
Simple words: many sample guesses of the state; where the guesses crowd, the robot is likely to be. Analogy: a crowd of imaginary Nias, each riding with slightly different timing. Precise: a set of samples x[1], …, x[M] (optionally with weights) whose distribution approximates the belief; used by the particle filter (F9-36). D1
Dead reckoning
Simple words: working out where you are only from your own motion, never looking outside. Analogy: Nia counting the minutes she has pedalled. Precise: estimating the state by integrating measured or commanded motion from a known start without external position measurements; errors accumulate. Defined in MA301 F0-65. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot never knows exactly where it is. Its wheels slip a little, its sensors flicker, and the world is not quite like its map. If its program kept only one number, "I am at 4.00 m", the program would have no idea how much to trust that number. It would treat a wild guess and a careful measurement the same way.

So the robot keeps a belief instead: a list of all the places it might be, with a probability for each, adding up to 1. When it is sure, almost all the probability sits on one place. When it is unsure, the probability is spread out. Nia's "give or take" is exactly this spread.

Two things change the belief. Moving makes it wider, because every movement adds a little doubt: you planned to ride one minute, but you might have ridden a bit faster or slower. Seeing something you recognise makes it narrower, because places that do not match what you see become unlikely. The next chapter, F9-31, turns these two rules into an algorithm. This chapter is about the belief itself: what it is, how to store it, and how fast it grows when you only move.

There is more than one way to write a belief down. You can draw a bar for every metre of the road (a histogram). You can say "around the boathouse, plus or minus 50 metres" (a Gaussian: one best guess and one spread). Or you can imagine a hundred Nias, each with slightly different timing, and look where they bunch up (particles). All three describe the same idea with different amounts of detail and work.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Belief as a conditional distribution

Write the robot's state at time t as xt, the control it applied (for example "drive one cell forward") as ut and the measurement it received as zt. The belief is the distribution of the state given everything the robot has experienced: bel(xt) = p(xt | z1:t, u1:t). It is a conditional distribution in exactly the sense of MA202 (F0-61): the "evidence" is the whole history of controls and readings. D1D2

The book this course follows, "Probabilistic Robotics", also defines the belief immediately before the latest reading is used, written bel‾(xt) ("bel-bar"): the prediction. The distinction matters in F9-31, where the filter computes bel‾ first and then bel. D1

A histogram belief

Listing 1 divides a corridor into 20 cells and stores the belief as a std::vector<double>, one probability per cell. The robot starts knowing it is in cell 2 (probability 1). Each command "one cell forward" really moves it 0, 1 or 2 cells with probabilities 0.1, 0.8 and 0.1, a simple motion model. After one move the belief is 0.1, 0.8, 0.1 over cells 2, 3, 4; after two moves 0.01, 0.16, 0.66, 0.16, 0.01 over cells 2 to 6 (run R1). The sum stays 1.000: probability is moved, never created or lost. D1R1

The program also prints the belief's mean and standard deviation. The mean moves by exactly one cell per command (the motion model's average move is 0.1 × 0 + 0.8 × 1 + 0.1 × 2 = 1), and the sd grows 0.447, 0.632, 0.775, 0.894: the square roots of 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8. Each move adds a variance of 0.2 cell² (0.1 × (−1)² + 0.1 × (+1)² around the average move), and because the move errors are independent, their variances add (F0-58, F0-60). D2R1R4

Dead reckoning: variances add

The same rule holds for continuous positions. If the position after k steps is xk = x0 + Σ (1 m + ei) with independent step errors ei of variance σ², then Var(xk) = k σ² and sd(xk) = σ √k. Listing 2 checks this on 4,000 simulated robots with σ = 0.05 m: the measured sds are 0.050, 0.099, 0.149 and 0.199 m after 1, 4, 9 and 16 steps, against 0.050, 0.100, 0.150 and 0.200 m predicted (run R2, Figure 2). Dead reckoning never gets better on its own; only a measurement of the outside world can shrink the belief again. D2R2

When one Gaussian is enough, and when it is not

A Gaussian belief needs only the mean and the variance (in several dimensions, the mean vector and the covariance matrix, F0-60). For the corridor after two moves, mean 4 and sd 0.63 summarise the histogram well (Figure 1). The Kalman filter of F9-32 to F9-35 keeps exactly these two things and nothing else. D1

Two situations break the summary. First, several peaks: a robot that has seen a door a moment ago, in a corridor with two identical doors, is equally likely to be at either. Listing 1, part B, stores such a belief (0.1, 0.3, 0.1 around cell 4 and the same around cell 14). Its mean is cell 9, a wall where the probability is 0.000, and its sd is 5.04 cells (run R1). Mean and sd describe a place the robot certainly is not. Second, curved uncertainty: when the heading is uncertain, the possible end points of a drive spread along an arc, not in an ellipse (Layer 3). The histogram and the particles of Figure 1 can express both situations; a single Gaussian cannot. D1R1R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The banana: nonlinear motion bends the belief

Part B of Listing 2 gives every simulated robot a heading error too (sd 3° per step, accumulating) and drives 20 steps of 1 m. The end points spread 2.74 m sideways but only 0.39 m along the direction of travel, and their mean, (19.72, 0.00), lies 0.28 m short of the commanded (20, 0) (run R2). Figure 3 shows why: every robot that turned a little still drove about 20 m, so the cloud follows an arc of radius 20 m around the start, and the average of points on an arc lies inside the arc. The expected forward distance is Σk E[cos θk] = Σk exp(−k s²/2) for heading errors θk with variance k s², which gives 19.715 m for s = 3° (run R4), matching the simulation. D1D2R2R4

Choosing a representation

RepresentationStoresCan expressCost grows withUsed in
Histogram (grid)one probability per cellany shape, several peaksnumber of cells: exponential in the number of state dimensionsF9-31; occupancy grids in RB401
Gaussianmean vector, covariance matrixone peak, elliptical spreadn² numbers for n state variables; updates cost about n³F9-32 to F9-35
ParticlesM samples (and weights)any shape, approximatelynumber of particles needed, which grows with dimension and with how peaked the sensor model isF9-36

The grid's cost is easy to underestimate. A floor robot's state (x, y, θ) on a 20 m × 20 m floor at 5 cm and 5° resolution needs 400 × 400 × 72 = 11,520,000 cells (run R4), every one of them updated at every step. A Gaussian for the same state needs 3 numbers for the mean and 6 distinct numbers for the symmetric covariance. That is why most robot localisation either uses Gaussians (and accepts one peak) or particles (and accepts sampling noise). D1W1R4

What the belief must contain

The belief can only summarise the past if the state is "complete": given the present state, older measurements and controls add nothing about the future. This is the Markov assumption that F9-31 uses to make the Bayes filter recursive. In practice it is violated by anything the state leaves out: a sensor bias that changes slowly, a wheel that is slightly larger than its nominal size, a person walking past. The engineering answer is either to add such quantities to the state (F9-35 adds the gyro bias) or to inflate the noise models to cover them. D1

Honest uncertainty

A belief is useful only if its spread is honest: if the robot says "sd 5 cm", its real errors must be about that size. A module that reports too little uncertainty makes every later consumer (a planner, a safety monitor, a filter that fuses its output) trust it too much. The forensic lab of this chapter is such a module; F9-32 measures honesty with the innovation statistics of a Kalman filter. D1R3

How the hardware actually does it

A belief is ordinary data in memory: a histogram is an array of floating-point numbers, a Gaussian a short vector plus a small matrix, a particle set an array of states. Nothing in the processor "knows" about probability. Three practical consequences follow. W1

  • Memory and time. The grid of Layer 3 holds 11.5 million values; in double precision (8 bytes each, F0-73) that is 92,160,000 bytes, about 92 MB (decimal megabytes) per copy (run R4), and the update touches every cell at the sensor rate. A 3 × 3 Gaussian is 72 bytes. The choice of representation decides whether estimation fits in a microcontroller's memory or needs a larger computer. D3W1
  • Very small numbers. Probabilities multiplied over many steps can become smaller than the smallest representable floating-point number and turn into zero (underflow, MA302 F0-73). Grid and particle filters therefore normalise after every update, and some implementations store log-probabilities. D3D1
  • Where the numbers come from. The motion model's spread comes from the wheels and encoders (RB201 F9-09), the sensor model's spread from the sensors (MA202 F0-62). The 0.05 m per step of this chapter is a simulator setting, not a property of any real robot; a real robot's values come from its datasheets and, above all, from your own measurements (AH-21). U1
Not verified — check before relying on this

Robot software frameworks pass beliefs between programs as messages that carry a covariance matrix next to the estimate (ROS 2 is the framework of RB303). The exact message types, the layout of their covariance fields (row-major order, units, which variables) and the conventions for "unknown" covariance were not checked in this build. Check the ROS 2 documentation (message and interface definitions) for the distribution recorded in the RB303 dossier before relying on any field name.

Worked example

A delivery robot drives along a 40 m hospital corridor in steps of 1 m. Tests on the same floor (the measurement method of F0-62) gave a step-length error with sd 0.05 m, independent from step to step. It starts at a charging dock whose position is known exactly.

  1. After 4 steps: variance 4 × 0.05² = 0.01 m², sd 0.10 m.
  2. After 25 steps: variance 25 × 0.0025 = 0.0625 m², sd 0.25 m.
  3. When does the sd reach 0.5 m? 0.05 √k = 0.5 gives √k = 10, k = 100 steps: four times the error needs sixteen times the distance, because the sd grows with the square root.
  4. A door 0.30 m wide must be found with 95 % confidence (±2 sd): 2 × 0.05 √k ≤ 0.15 m gives √k ≤ 1.5, k ≤ 2.25. After only two steps of dead reckoning, the robot must look for the door instead of trusting its count.
  5. A module that keeps reporting sd 0.05 m after 25 steps claims 0.05 m when the truth is 0.25 m: a factor of 5 in sd, 25 in variance. The share of runs whose error exceeds its claimed 2-sd band (0.10 m) is then P(|e| > 0.1 m) for e with sd 0.25 m, about 69 % (run R4), instead of the 5 % an honest module would show.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nia's belief is vague; the robot's is exact arithmetic. Nia feels "give or take a bit". The robot stores numbers that must sum to 1 and that are only as right as its motion and sensor models; it has no common sense to notice that "the bakery cannot be on the left today". D1
    • 

  • Nia sees continuously; a robot's loop does not. The registered F9 mapping already warns that a computer loop runs at a fixed rate and sees only what its sensors measure (analogy registry, control loop). Between two readings the robot's belief changes only by its prediction. U1
    • 

  • Nia can be "sure" with no reason. People are often overconfident. A filter is overconfident only when its models say so, and then it is overconfident in a way you can measure (the forensic lab here and in F9-32). R3
    •

Troubleshooting


    

  • sd grows linearly with k instead of √k → the same random error is reused for every step (for example the Gaussian is drawn once outside the loop) → print the first few step errors.
    • 

  • Belief sum drops below 1 → probability walked off the end of the grid → start further from the end or add a wall cell.
    • 

  • Results change between runs → engine not seeded with a constant → seed it.

Summary


    

  • A robot keeps a belief, the distribution of its state given all controls and readings, not a single number.
    • 

  • Three representations: histogram (any shape, cost grows with the number of cells), Gaussian (mean and covariance, one peak), particles (samples, any shape, approximate).
    • 

  • Moving spreads the belief; measuring the outside world sharpens it.
    • 

  • Dead reckoning with independent step errors: variances add, sd = σ √k.
    • 

  • Several peaks and curved uncertainty defeat a single Gaussian: the mean can lie where the robot cannot be.
    • 

  • Uncertainty must be honest; a module that reports a constant sd while its error grows misleads everything downstream.
    •

  1. It claims sd 0.050 m at every step: its uncertainty never grows.
    2. 

  2. RMS error 0.052 m after 1 step, 0.099 m after 4, 0.150 m after 10, 0.260 m after 25: close to 0.05 √k (0.050, 0.100, 0.158, 0.250), the random-walk law of independent step errors with sd 0.05 m per step.
    3. 

  3. About 5 % (4.55 % for a Gaussian, run R4). At step 1 the evidence shows 5.4 %, consistent with an honest claim; at step 25 it shows 71.0 % (theory 68.9 %, run R4).
    4. 

  4. Root cause: the module reports the per-step noise sd as the position sd instead of propagating the variance (variance after k steps = k × 0.05²). The safety monitor compares the reported sd with 0.2 m; since the reported sd is a constant 0.05 m, the monitor can never trigger, although the true sd passes 0.2 m after 16 steps (0.05 √16). Fix: keep a variance P, add 0.05² at every step (P ← P + Q), report √P.
    5. 

  5. A consistency test: run many simulated drives with ground truth and check that about 95 % of errors lie inside ±2 reported sd at every step (or that the mean of e²/P is near 1, the NEES of F9-33). The constant-sd module fails it from step 2 on.
    6. 



How the evidence was produced: constant_sigma.cpp (seed 130) simulates 500 runs of 25 steps with step noise sd 0.05 m and a module that adds exactly 1 m per step and reports sd 0.05 m (R3). Wrong answers and why: "the wheels are biased": the errors have zero mean (the RMS follows √k, a bias would grow like k); "the safety threshold is too high": with an honest sd the 0.2 m threshold would trigger after 16 steps, so the threshold is not the problem.
