Story hook

Analogy

Oskar is learning a new cycling route to his swimming club: a long loop road with houses, a few shops and a lot of hedges. In the dark he can see only one thing clearly: whether he is passing a lit shop window or not. At first he has no idea where on the loop he is. After passing a lit window he knows he is at one of the five shops. A minute of hedges later, he knows a little more: some shops are followed by long stretches of hedge, others by another shop soon. After the next window, only one or two places still fit everything he has seen.

Oskar is doing two things, over and over. While riding, he shifts his guesses forward along the road ("I have pedalled about one more minute"), and they blur a little because he is not sure how fast he rode. When he sees something, he keeps the guesses that fit what he saw and drops the ones that do not. Those two steps, repeated, are the Bayes filter.

What you will be able to do

  • Derive the two steps of the Bayes filter (prediction by total probability, update by Bayes' rule) and name the assumptions each step uses.
  • Compute one prediction and one update by hand on a small grid.
  • Implement a histogram Bayes filter in C++ and use it to localise a robot from door readings.
  • Explain why beliefs have several peaks during global localisation and how they collapse to one.
  • Diagnose a filter made certain of a wrong answer by a sensor model with a zero probability.
Jargon box
Bayes filter
Simple words: the recipe that updates a belief step by step: shift it with every move, sharpen it with every reading. Analogy: Oskar shifting his guesses forward and dropping the ones that do not fit the shop windows. Precise: the recursive algorithm that computes bel(xt) from bel(xt−1), ut and zt in two steps, bel‾(xt) = Σ p(xt | ut, xt−1) bel(xt−1) and bel(xt) = η p(zt | xt) bel‾(xt). MA202 introduced its update step (F0-61). D1
Motion model
Simple words: where the robot might end up when it is told to move. Analogy: "one more minute of pedalling: probably one block further, maybe a little less or more". Precise: the conditional distribution p(xt | ut, xt−1) of the new state given the control and the previous state. D1
Measurement (sensor) model
Simple words: how likely each reading is in each state. Analogy: "at a shop I almost always see a lit window; at a hedge I rarely think I do". Precise: the conditional distribution p(zt | xt); viewed as a function of xt for the reading actually received it is the likelihood (F0-61). D1D2
Markov assumption
Simple words: if you know the present state exactly, the past tells you nothing more about the future. Analogy: knowing exactly where he is, Oskar does not need to remember which windows he passed. Precise: the state xt is complete: p(xt | x0:t−1, z1:t−1, u1:t) = p(xt | xt−1, ut) and p(zt | x0:t, z1:t−1, u1:t) = p(zt | xt). D1
Normaliser (η)
Simple words: the number you divide by so that the probabilities add up to 1 again. Analogy: after crossing out the guesses that do not fit, sharing out "all of the certainty" among those left. Precise: η = 1 / Σx p(zt | x) bel‾(x), the reciprocal of the probability of the reading under the prediction. D1D2
Global localisation and position tracking
Simple words: finding where you are with no idea at the start, versus keeping track when you already roughly know. Analogy: Oskar's first ride in the dark versus his hundredth. Precise: global localisation starts from a uniform (or very wide) belief and must cope with several peaks; position tracking starts from a narrow belief and keeps it narrow. The kidnapped-robot problem is global localisation that begins after the robot was moved without being told. D1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the Bayes filter as a cycle of two steps. The control u drives the prediction, which spreads the belief; the reading z drives the update, which sharpens it; the result is the input of the next cycle.

Figure 2 follows the worked example of this chapter on a ring of five cells: a door reading, one uncertain move, a wall reading.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Imagine the road cut into numbered pieces, and a little pile of sand on each piece. The size of each pile shows how strongly you believe you are there. All the sand together is always the same amount: one bucket, the whole of your certainty.

When you ride forward, you push every pile one piece forward, and a little sand spills onto the pieces in front and behind, because you are not sure exactly how far you went. That is the prediction. When you see a lit window, you look at every piece of road: piles at shops keep most of their sand, piles at hedges lose most of theirs. Then you collect the sand that was taken away and share it out among the piles in proportion to their size, so the bucket is full again. That is the update.

Repeat this every second, and the sand slowly gathers on the pieces of road that fit everything you have seen and done. A robot does exactly this with numbers instead of sand, using two tables written by its engineers: how it moves, and how its sensor answers.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Prediction: the law of total probability

Before the reading at time t, the robot has bel(xt−1) and has executed the control ut. The probability of being in state xt is the sum, over every previous state xt−1, of "was there" times "moved from there to xt":

bel‾(xt) = Σxt−1 p(xt | ut, xt−1) · bel(xt−1)

This is the law of total probability (F0-61), and it needs the Markov assumption: the motion depends only on the previous state and the control, not on older history. For a continuous state the sum becomes an integral. D1D2

On a grid where the motion model depends only on the distance moved, the prediction is a convolution: every cell's probability is spread over the cells 0, 1 and 2 ahead with weights 0.1, 0.8 and 0.1 (Listing 1, lines 38–47). R1

Update: Bayes' rule

Then the reading zt arrives. Bayes' rule (F0-61), with the prediction as the prior, gives:

bel(xt) = η · p(zt | xt) · bel‾(xt), η = 1 / Σx p(zt | x) bel‾(x)

Here the Markov assumption is used again: given the present state, the reading does not depend on earlier readings or controls (the sensor's errors are independent from one reading to the next). The update multiplies and normalises (lines 50–63). D1D2R1

Localising in a corridor

Listing 1 is a circular corridor of 30 cells with doors at cells 1, 4, 11, 19 and 21. The robot starts somewhere unknown (in the simulation, cell 8), so its belief is uniform: 1/30 ≈ 0.033 everywhere. Each step it moves "one forward" with the motion model above, and its door detector reports "door" with probability 0.9 in front of a door and 0.05 in front of a wall. The world and the filter use the same models, the ideal case. R1

Run R1 shows the typical phases of global localisation. After the first door reading (step 2) the belief has five peaks, one at each door, and the most probable cell holds only 0.162. During the long stretch of walls (steps 3 to 9) the peaks drift and blur; the filter cannot tell them apart, because every door is followed by some wall. At step 10 a second door reading arrives after the right number of wall readings, and the probability at the true cell rises to 0.407; one wall and another door later (step 12) it is 0.756. Between doors the belief blurs again (the most probable cell holds only 0.302 at step 18), and the door at step 23 brings it back to 0.612. R1

Two lessons: the pattern of doors and walls, not any single reading, is what identifies the place (an asymmetric layout helps; a corridor with evenly spaced doors could never be resolved); and between informative readings the motion model's uncertainty steadily spreads the belief again. D1R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The derivation in four lines

Start from the definition bel(xt) = p(xt | z1:t, u1:t). Bayes' rule on the latest reading: = η p(zt | xt, z1:t−1, u1:t) p(xt | z1:t−1, u1:t). Markov: the first factor is p(zt | xt); the second is bel‾(xt). Total probability on xt−1: bel‾(xt) = Σ p(xt | xt−1, z1:t−1, u1:t) p(xt−1 | z1:t−1, u1:t). Markov again: the first factor is p(xt | xt−1, ut); in the second, the future control ut says nothing about xt−1, so it is bel(xt−1). Every step uses either a definition, Bayes' rule, total probability or the Markov assumption; nothing else. D1D2

Cost and resolution

One step of Listing 1 costs 30 × 3 multiply-adds for the prediction and 30 for the update (run R3). In general the prediction costs (number of cells) × (width of the motion kernel), and the number of cells grows exponentially with the dimension of the state: the 20 m × 20 m floor at 5 cm and 5° of F9-30 needs 11,520,000 cells (run R3). Coarse cells make the filter cheap but blur the sensor model: a door narrower than a cell cannot be represented well. D1R3

Where the models come from, and why they must not be too sharp

The filter is only as good as its two models. Their numbers come from measurements: drive the robot many times and record how far "one forward" really went (the motion model); put it in front of known doors and walls and count the readings (the sensor model, MA202 F0-61 and F0-62). Two engineering rules follow from the update formula:

  • Never use a probability of exactly 0 for something that can happen. A zero likelihood multiplies a cell's belief by zero, and no later reading can bring it back except through the motion model's spreading. One false reading then removes the true position (the forensic lab). D1R2
  • Readings are rarely as independent as the formula assumes. Ten readings of the same door taken while standing still are not ten independent pieces of evidence; the update treats them as such and becomes overconfident. Practical filters update only after the robot has moved, or use deliberately softer sensor models. D1

The kidnapped robot

A filter that has converged holds almost no probability away from its peak. If the robot is picked up and carried elsewhere, the new readings contradict the belief, but there is little probability in the right place to grow. A grid filter with a motion model that always spreads some probability recovers slowly; a particle filter (F9-36) may have no particle there at all. A common remedy is to mix a small uniform share into the belief at every step. D1

How the hardware actually does it

The "door detector" of this chapter stands for any sensor whose reading can be turned into a likelihood table: a light sensor over a floor marking, a bump switch, a distance sensor seeing an opening (RB101 F9-02, HW302). The motion model stands for the wheels and their encoders (RB201 F9-09). In a real robot the filter step runs in the control computer's main loop, once per reading, and its cost decides how fine the grid can be at a given sensor rate. D1U1

The grid update is a loop over an array with no data-dependent branching; it is a natural candidate for vectorisation on a CPU and, for very large grids, for a GPU (each cell's update is independent; the prediction is a convolution). Whether that is worth it depends on measured times on the target, not on intuition (curriculum measurement protocol, MA202 F0-63). W1

Worked example

A ring of five cells, doors at cells 0 and 3. Sensor: p(door | door) = 0.9, p(door | wall) = 0.05. Motion "one forward": 0, 1, 2 cells with 0.1, 0.8, 0.1. Prior uniform, 0.2 per cell.

  1. Reading "door". Multiply: doors 0.9 × 0.2 = 0.18, walls 0.05 × 0.2 = 0.01. Sum 0.18 + 0.18 + 3 × 0.01 = 0.39. Divide: doors 0.4615, walls 0.0256.
  2. Move one forward. bel‾(1) = 0.1 bel(1) + 0.8 bel(0) + 0.1 bel(4) = 0.0026 + 0.3692 + 0.0026 = 0.3744. In the same way bel‾ = 0.1128, 0.3744, 0.0692, 0.0692, 0.3744 for cells 0 to 4.
  3. Reading "wall". Likelihoods: doors 1 − 0.9 = 0.1, walls 1 − 0.05 = 0.95. Products 0.0113, 0.3556, 0.0658, 0.0069, 0.3556; sum 0.7953.
  4. Normalise: 0.0142, 0.4472, 0.0827, 0.0087, 0.4472. The robot is one cell past a door, in cell 1 or cell 4, with equal probability: the ring is symmetric for this sequence, and only a later reading can break the tie.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Oskar remembers; the filter does not. Oskar can think back ("that window two minutes ago was the pharmacy"). The Bayes filter keeps only the current belief; everything it learned is folded into those 30 numbers, which is exactly what the Markov assumption allows and why a wrong model can never be corrected by "remembering better". D1
    • 

  • Oskar's sand does not obey arithmetic. In the sand picture, the update and the normalisation are vague; in the filter, a likelihood of exactly 0 removes a cell for good, and a likelihood that is too sharp makes the filter too sure. People do not behave this way; filters do (forensic lab). R2
    • 

  • Oskar's road is not a ring of equal cells. Real space is continuous and two- or three-dimensional; the grid is an approximation whose resolution and cost you choose (Layer 3). D1

Transition box

Where you came from: MA202 (F0-61) applied Bayes' rule repeatedly to a world that did not change; F9-30 described beliefs and how motion spreads them. What carries over: the update step is exactly F0-61's: likelihood times prior, then normalise; the spreading is exactly F9-30's motion model. D2 What changes: the state now moves between readings, so every update must be preceded by a prediction; the hypotheses are many (every cell), so cost and resolution matter; and the models must be measured and must never contain impossible zeros. D1 What comes next: F9-32 does the same two steps for a belief that is a single Gaussian; the sums become formulas for a mean and a variance, and the filter becomes the Kalman filter.

Summary


    

  • The Bayes filter alternates prediction (total probability with the motion model) and update (Bayes' rule with the sensor model, then normalise).
    • 

  • Both steps rely on the Markov assumption: the state is complete, readings are independent given the state.
    • 

  • On a grid, the prediction is a convolution and the update a multiplication; the cost grows with the number of cells, exponentially in the dimension.
    • 

  • Global localisation passes through beliefs with several peaks; the pattern of readings resolves them.
    • 

  • Models must be measured; a zero likelihood for a possible reading makes the filter certain of a wrong answer.
    •
