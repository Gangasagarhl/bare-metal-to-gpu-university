Story hook

Analogy

Farah and Lin are cycling to a lake on a road with kilometre posts. Lin's bike has a little computer that counts wheel turns. "We are 10.0 km from the bridge," he says, "but I set the wheel size roughly, so give or take half a kilometre." Farah has glimpsed a kilometre post half hidden by a bush. "I think it said 10.6, but I only saw it for a second. Give or take 300 metres."

Who is right? Neither, exactly, and both a little. Farah's guess is more reliable, so the best combined guess should lie closer to hers, but not on top of it, because Lin's count also carries information. The two of them settle on about 10.4 km, and, surprisingly, they are now more sure than either was alone.

That is the whole Kalman filter in one dimension: combine two uncertain guesses, trusting the more reliable one more, and get a guess that is better than both. The registered F9 analogy says exactly this; this chapter turns it into four lines of C++ and runs them on a recording with ground truth.

What you will be able to do

  • Derive the one-dimensional Kalman update from the product of two Gaussians, and the prediction from the sum of independent errors.
  • Implement the four-line filter in C++ and run it on recorded data with ground truth (the RB302 course lab).
  • Tune the noise variances: R from a rest recording (the MA202 method of F0-62), Q from ground truth, and check them with a sweep.
  • Test a filter's honesty with its innovations: the normalised innovation squared and the lag-1 autocorrelation.
  • Diagnose an overconfident filter (the RB302 course forensic lab) from innovation statistics alone.
Jargon box
Kalman filter
Simple words: a recipe that keeps a best guess and how unsure it is, moves both with the robot, and blends in each new reading according to how trustworthy it is. Analogy: combining two friends' guesses, trusting the more reliable one more (registered F9 analogy). Precise: the Bayes filter for a linear system with Gaussian noise; the belief stays Gaussian and is carried as a mean and a variance (covariance matrix in several dimensions). The filter is named after R. E. Kalman, whose 1960 paper on linear filtering is the historical original. D1D2
Process noise (Q)
Simple words: how much doubt one prediction step adds. Analogy: how wrong Lin's wheel count can become in one minute of riding. Precise: the variance (covariance) of the random error added by the motion model in one step. D1
Measurement noise (R)
Simple words: how much a single reading can be off. Analogy: Farah's "give or take 300 metres". Precise: the variance (covariance) of the measurement error, assumed zero-mean, Gaussian and independent between readings; measured, for example, from readings at a known position (F0-62). D1R3
Kalman gain (K)
Simple words: the share of the surprise that the filter believes. Analogy: how far towards Farah's number the pair moves (0 = ignore her, 1 = take her number). Precise: in one dimension K = P / (P + R), between 0 and 1; the update is x ← x + K (z − x). D1
Innovation
Simple words: how much the reading surprised the filter. Analogy: the 0.6 km between Lin's 10.0 and Farah's 10.6. Precise: the difference y = z − x̂− between the reading and its prediction, with predicted variance S = P− + R; for a correct filter the innovations are zero-mean, have variance S and are uncorrelated in time. D1W1
NIS (normalised innovation squared)
Simple words: the surprise measured in units of "how surprised the filter expected to be". Analogy: Farah's 0.6 km surprise divided by the pair's combined "give or take". Precise: ν = y² / S; for a consistent filter with one measurement its mean is 1. A mean far above 1 means the filter claims too little uncertainty (overconfident). W1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot cart rolls along a rail. It has two ways to know where it is. Its wheels tell it how far it rolled, but every roll adds a little error, so this guess gets worse over time. A distance sensor at the front measures the distance to the end wall, but every reading jitters a little.

The Kalman filter keeps two numbers: the best guess and how unsure it is. Each time step it does two things. First, it moves the guess by what the wheels say and makes it a little more unsure. Second, it looks at the sensor reading and moves the guess part of the way towards it. How far? That depends on who is more trustworthy right now. If the guess is very unsure and the sensor is good, it moves most of the way. If the guess is already good and the sensor is jumpy, it moves only a little.

After the blend, the guess is less unsure than before, because two pieces of evidence agree better than one. Then the next step begins. That is all; the rest of this chapter makes "how unsure" and "part of the way" exact, and shows how to choose the two numbers that describe the wheels' and the sensor's errors.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Update: multiplying two Gaussians

Suppose the prediction is x ~ N(x̂−, P−) and the reading z = x + v with v ~ N(0, R). The Bayes update multiplies the prior density by the likelihood and normalises (F9-31). Both are Gaussian in x, and the product of two Gaussian densities in x is again proportional to a Gaussian (complete the square in the exponent). The result has precision (inverse variance) equal to the sum of the precisions, and its mean is the precision-weighted average:

1/P = 1/P− + 1/R, x̂ = P (x̂−/P− + z/R).

Rearranged with K = P− / (P− + R), the same equations become the form used in code: x̂ = x̂− + K (z − x̂−) and P = (1 − K) P−. K is between 0 and 1; the posterior variance is always smaller than both P− and R. D1D3R4

With Farah's and Lin's numbers: P− = 0.25, R = 0.09, K = 0.25/0.34 = 0.7353, x̂ = 10.0 + 0.7353 × 0.6 = 10.441 km, P = 0.0662, sd 0.257 km. The precision-weighted average gives the same 10.441 (run R4). R4

Prediction: adding independent errors

Between readings the cart moves by the odometry: xk = xk−1 + uk Δt + wk, with wk ~ N(0, Q) independent of the current error. The predicted mean moves by u Δt, and the variances add (F9-30): x̂− = x̂ + u Δt, P− = P + Q. The sum of two independent Gaussians is Gaussian (F0-59), so the belief stays Gaussian forever: the Bayes filter of F9-31 reduces to these four lines. D1D3

Is the filter honest?

Two checks need ground truth: 186 of 190 errors lie inside the filter's own ±2 sd band (an honest Gaussian would have about 95 %, here 97.9 %), and the claimed sd 0.037 m is close to the measured RMS 0.030 m. One check needs no ground truth, so a robot can run it on itself: the mean NIS, y²/S, is 0.869, near its ideal value of 1 (run R2). R2W1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Steady state

With constant Q and R, P converges. The steady-state prediction variance solves P− = (1 − K) P− + Q with K = P−/(P− + R), that is P−2 − Q P− − Q R = 0, so P− = (Q + √(Q² + 4QR))/2. For the tuned values this gives P− = 0.001738, K = 0.1979, P = 0.001394 (sd 0.0373 m), matching the run (R4, R2). A filter in steady state is a fixed linear smoother of the readings, but the Kalman filter chooses its weight from the noise variances and reaches it automatically from any start. D1R4R2

Only the ratio Q/R moves the estimate; the scale sets the honesty

K depends on Q and R only through their ratio once P has settled (divide the quadratic by R). Listing 3 confirms it: multiplying Q by 10 or dividing R by 10 gives the same RMS error, 0.0475 m, but different claimed sds (0.0591 and 0.0187 m). The estimate cannot tell you whether Q and R are both too small or both right; the claimed uncertainty and the NIS can (NIS 0.604 versus 6.042). This is why tuning has two parts: the ratio for accuracy, the absolute level for honesty. R3W1

Reading the innovations

Listing 3's sweep also shows a second, independent signature. When R is too small relative to Q, the filter follows every reading (K near 1); consecutive innovations then share the noise of the previous reading with opposite sign, and their lag-1 autocorrelation turns negative: −0.350 for R × 0.01. When Q is too small, the filter lags behind real motion, consecutive innovations have the same sign, and the autocorrelation turns positive: +0.347 for Q × 0.01. The tuned filter shows +0.050, close to 0. Together, the mean NIS (level) and the lag-1 autocorrelation (ratio) locate a mis-tuning without any ground truth (run R3). R3W1

When the assumptions fail

The filter is optimal (minimum variance among all estimators) only when the models are linear, the noises Gaussian, independent in time and of the stated variances. Real noise is often correlated (vibration), biased (a mis-set wheel diameter) or heavy-tailed (an occasional echo from the wrong wall). The filter then still runs and is often still good, but its P no longer describes its error; the innovation tests above are how you notice. Biases are handled by adding them to the state (F9-35), outliers by gating (F9-35). D1D2

How the hardware actually does it

A one-dimensional Kalman step is about ten floating-point operations: an addition for the prediction, a division for K and a few multiply-adds for the update. Any microcontroller with floating-point arithmetic can run it at its sensor rate; its cost is far below the cost of reading the sensors. What matters on real hardware is not the arithmetic but the inputs: W1

  • Time stamps. The prediction multiplies the speed by Δt. If readings arrive late or at an irregular rate, Δt must be measured, not assumed (F9-33's forensic lab is this bug). W1
  • Units and offsets. The range sensor measures to its own front face; the cart's position may be defined at its centre. A constant offset is a bias the filter cannot remove (MA202 F0-62). D4
  • Noise under real conditions. R measured on a quiet bench is smaller than R on a vibrating robot; record the rest data in operating conditions (motors on, safely). D4
Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware: no cart, rail, range sensor or encoder was available in this build. The ranges, noise levels and sample rate used here are simulator settings. For a real cart, the range sensor's measuring principle, range limits, update rate and noise must come from its datasheet (recorded in the dossier, AH-21) and from your own rest recording; the encoder's resolution from its datasheet and RB201 (F9-09).

Worked example

Lin's count: 10.0 km, sd 0.5. Farah's reading: 10.6 km, sd 0.3. Then they ride exactly 2.0 km by the count (sd 0.2 km for that stretch), and Farah reads another post: 12.3 km, sd 0.3.

  1. Update 1: P− = 0.25, R = 0.09, S = 0.34, K = 0.7353; x̂ = 10.0 + 0.7353 × 0.6 = 10.441 km; P = 0.2647 × 0.25 = 0.0662, sd 0.257 km.
  2. Check by precision weighting: (10.0/0.25 + 10.6/0.09)/(1/0.25 + 1/0.09) = 10.441 km, variance 1/(4 + 11.11) = 0.0662. Same.
  3. Predict: x̂− = 12.441 km, P− = 0.0662 + 0.04 = 0.1062, sd 0.326 km.
  4. Update 2: K = 0.1062/(0.1062 + 0.09) = 0.5412; innovation 12.3 − 12.441 = −0.141; x̂ = 12.365 km; sd 0.221 km.
  5. Lesson: the second gain (0.54) is smaller than the first (0.74) because the filter's own belief is now better than at the start; it is still larger than the lab's steady-state 0.198 because the step noise here (0.2 km) is large compared with the reading noise.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Friends can disagree for reasons; Gaussians cannot. If Farah misread a "16" as "10.6", no sensible weighting saves the result. The Kalman filter has no notion of a mistake: an outlier is only a large innovation, and it is blended in like any other (gating, F9-35, is the engineering fix). D1
    • 

  • The friends' "give or take" is a feeling; the filter's must be measured. The analogy works only if each friend's uncertainty is honest. A filter given a too-small R behaves like a friend who is sure of everything she says (the forensic lab). R5
    • 

  • Two friends at one moment versus a stream over time. The analogy shows one update. The filter also predicts, and its own estimate becomes "the first friend" at every step; its errors are then correlated with its past, which the analogy does not show. D1
    •

Summary


    

  • With linear models and Gaussian noise the Bayes filter keeps a Gaussian belief: a mean and a variance.
    • 

  • Prediction: x̂− = x̂ + uΔt, P− = P + Q. Update: K = P−/(P− + R), x̂ = x̂− + K(z − x̂−), P = (1 − K)P−.
    • 

  • The update is a precision-weighted average; the result is better than either input (course lab: 0.030 m against 0.080 m and 0.172 m).
    • 

  • Tune R from a rest recording, Q from a test drive with ground truth; the ratio sets accuracy, the level sets honesty.
    • 

  • Innovations reveal mis-tuning without ground truth: mean NIS (level) and lag-1 autocorrelation (ratio).
    • 

  • An overconfident filter (R too small) follows its sensor's noise while claiming tiny uncertainty.
