Story hook

Analogy

Lin rides around a round lake at dusk. He knows his speed from counting the turns of his wheel and his turning from the handlebars, but each loop his guess of where he is drifts a little. On the far shore there is a lighthouse. Every time he glances at it, he notes two things: how far away it looks and how far to the left of straight ahead it is.

Turning that glance into a correction of "where am I" is harder than reading a kilometre post. The distance to the lighthouse depends on his position through a square root; the angle depends on it through an arctangent and on where he is facing. These are curves, not straight lines. The Kalman filter of F9-33 only knows straight lines.

But on a bike you know this trick already: a bend in the road looks straight for the next few metres. If Lin's guess is good, the curve between "where I think I am" and "where I really am" is short enough to treat as straight. The extended Kalman filter does exactly that, at every step, around its current guess.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the motion model of a wheeled robot driven by odometry (unicycle) and the range-bearing measurement model to a known landmark.
  • Derive their Jacobians by hand and check them numerically with central differences.
  • Implement EKF localisation with known landmarks in C++ and compare it with dead reckoning and ground truth.
  • Handle angles correctly: wrap every heading and every bearing difference.
  • Explain when linearisation fails and how to see it in NEES and NIS.
Jargon box
Extended Kalman filter (EKF)
Simple words: a Kalman filter for curved models, which treats each curve as straight near the current guess. Analogy: a bend in the road looks straight for the next few metres. Precise: predicts the mean with the non-linear models g and h, and the covariance with their Jacobians evaluated at the current estimate; an approximation, not an optimal filter. D1
Jacobian
Simple words: a table of how much each output changes when each input changes a little. Analogy: how much the lighthouse angle changes if Lin is one metre further left. Precise: the matrix of first partial derivatives, Jij = ∂fi/∂xj; it is the linear approximation of f near a point (F0-64). D3
Unicycle model
Simple words: a robot that drives forward and turns, but never slides sideways. Analogy: a bicycle on dry ground. Precise: x' = x + v Δt cos θ, y' = y + v Δt sin θ, θ' = θ + ω Δt, with speed v and turn rate ω from odometry; a common model for differential-drive robots. D2D1
Range-bearing measurement
Simple words: "the landmark is this far away, at this angle from where I am facing". Analogy: Lin's glance at the lighthouse. Precise: h(s) = [√(Δx² + Δy²), atan2(Δy, Δx) − θ] with Δx = lx − x, Δy = ly − y. D1
Angle wrapping
Simple words: bringing an angle back into one turn, so that "359°" and "−1°" are the same. Analogy: a clock hand at 11:59 is one minute from 12:00, not eleven hours. Precise: mapping an angle to (−π, π] by adding or subtracting 2π; needed for every heading and every angle difference, including innovations. W1
Landmark
Simple words: a fixed, recognisable thing whose position on the map is known. Analogy: the lighthouse. Precise: a feature with known map coordinates and (in this chapter) a known identity in each reading; recognising which landmark was seen is the data-association problem. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot drives around in a room. Its wheels say how far it went and how much it turned, but every turn is counted a little wrongly, so after a while it does not know exactly where it is or which way it faces.

On the walls there are three signs at known places. Every now and then the robot sees one and measures how far it is and at what angle. If the robot thinks the sign should be straight ahead but sees it a bit to the left, it knows its guess is a bit wrong: perhaps it faces a little too far right, perhaps it is a little too far left. It corrects both, a little, trusting what is more reliable.

The hard part is that "how far" and "what angle" change in curved ways when the robot moves. The robot's trick: near its guess, it pretends the curves are straight lines, does the simple Kalman sums of the last chapter, and then repeats the trick at the next step from its new guess. And it must remember that angles go round: facing almost exactly backwards to the left and almost exactly backwards to the right are nearly the same direction.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The two non-linear models

The state is s = [x, y, θ]T: position on the floor and heading. Odometry (counting the turns of the wheels, F9-09) gives a speed v and a turn rate ω each step. The motion model is the unicycle: g(s, u) = [x + vΔt cos θ, y + vΔt sin θ, θ + ωΔt], with the heading wrapped. Odometry noise is in v and ω, with covariance M = diag(σv², σω²). The measurement model to landmark l is h(s) = [r, b] = [√q, atan2(Δy, Δx) − θ] with q = Δx² + Δy², and the reading noise has covariance R = diag(σr², σb²). D1D2

The EKF step

The EKF keeps the F9-33 structure and changes two things. The means go through the true, non-linear functions; the covariances go through the Jacobians at the current estimate:

  • Predict: ŝ− = g(ŝ, u); P− = G P GT + V M VT, where G = ∂g/∂s and V = ∂g/∂u map state errors and odometry errors into the new state.
  • Update: y = z − h(ŝ−) (bearing part wrapped); H = ∂h/∂s at ŝ−; S = H P− HT + R; K = P− HT S−1; ŝ = ŝ− + K y (heading wrapped); P in Joseph form.

The Jacobians, derived by differentiating each component (F0-64):

G = [[1, 0, −vΔt sin θ], [0, 1, vΔt cos θ], [0, 0, 1]], V = [[Δt cos θ, 0], [Δt sin θ, 0], [0, Δt]],

H = [[−Δx/√q, −Δy/√q, 0], [Δy/q, −Δx/q, −1]].

The third column of G says that a heading error turns into a sideways position error proportional to the distance driven, which is why dead reckoning curves away from the truth. The last column of H says that a heading error of +1 rad changes the bearing by −1 rad: the bearing reading measures heading directly, once position is known. D1W1

Check every Jacobian numerically

A wrong sign in a hand-derived Jacobian makes the filter worse without crashing it. Listing 2 compares each Jacobian with central differences, column j = (f(s + εej) − f(s − εej))/(2ε), with ε = 10−6. At s = (1, 2, 0.7), v = 0.5 m/s and landmark (4, 4), hand and numeric versions agree to 1.4 × 10−10 (G) and 3.3 × 10−10 (H) (run R2). Make this check part of every EKF you write. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

How good is the straight line?

The linearisation error grows with the square of the distance from the linearisation point. For the worked example's bearing (landmark 3 m ahead, 2 m to the side), a sideways error of 0.1 m gives a linearisation error of 0.0004 rad, 0.5 m gives 0.009 rad, 2 m gives 0.127 rad, that is 7°, larger than the sensor's 2° noise (run R4). The rule follows: the EKF works when the filter's uncertainty is small compared with the scale on which h and g bend (here, the distance to the landmark). A robot that is lost (F9-30's global localisation) cannot use an EKF; a robot that is tracking can. R4D1

The mean of a function is not the function of the mean

The EKF predicts the mean of x' as x + vΔt cos θ̂. But if θ is uncertain, the mean of cos θ is smaller than cos of the mean: for a Gaussian heading with sd σ, E[cos θ] = cos θ̂ · e−σ²/2. With σ = 2° the factor is 0.9994, with 10° 0.985, with 30° 0.872 (run R4). With a large heading uncertainty the EKF's predicted position is biased forwards, and its covariance too small; the filter becomes overconfident, which shows as NEES and NIS above their ideal means. Other filters (the unscented Kalman filter, particle filters of F9-36) treat this better at a higher cost; the unscented filter is beyond this course. D3R4D1

Angles live on a circle

std::atan2 returns an angle in [−π, π]. A predicted bearing of −3.10 rad and a measured one of +3.10 rad are 0.08 rad apart as directions, but their difference as numbers is 6.20. Fed into K, a 6.2-rad "innovation" pushes the heading by several radians and the position by metres. Every quantity that is an angle (the heading in the state, the predicted bearing, the bearing innovation, the heading error used for NEES) must be wrapped. The forensic lab is this bug. D4R4R5

What this chapter assumes away

Each reading here comes with the identity of its landmark. A real sensor sees "a pole at 3.6 m, −6°", and the software must decide which pole it is. A common approach compares the reading with each landmark's predicted reading and picks the one with the smallest NIS below a gate (the mini-project). A wrong association is an outlier with a confident label, and it can pull the EKF far away; gating and the ability to recover (particle filters, F9-36) are the defences. Starting the filter also needs a rough initial guess: in this lab the start is 0.2 m and 0.1 rad off, well inside the region where the linearisation holds. D1W1

How the hardware actually does it

An EKF localiser needs three inputs. Odometry comes from the wheel encoders at a fixed rate (RB201, F9-09). Landmark readings come from an exteroceptive sensor: a camera that recognises markers, or a scanning range sensor that sees poles or reflectors. A map gives each landmark's position, measured once with a tape measure or a survey. The arithmetic is small: a 3-state EKF step with one 2 × 2 inversion is a few hundred floating-point operations. W1

The practical difficulties are timing and geometry: the landmark reading is taken at a different moment from the odometry, the sensor is mounted away from the robot's centre and rotated, and readings can be missing for seconds. Each needs a transform (the sensor's pose on the robot, RB201 and HW302) and time-stamp handling (F9-33's forensic lab). W1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware: no robot, camera or range scanner was available in this build. The ranges, angles and noise levels (σr = 0.1 m, σb = 2°, odometry σ 0.05) are simulator settings, not properties of any sensor. A real marker-detecting camera or scanner must be characterised from its datasheet (dossier, AH-21) and a rest recording with the robot at a tape-measured pose, as in F9-32. The real noise is not independent between range and bearing and depends on distance; this chapter does not model that.

Worked example

Robot at (1, 2) m, heading 0.7 rad (about 40°). Landmark at (4, 4).

  1. Differences: Δx = 3, Δy = 2, q = 13, √q = 3.6056 m.
  2. Predicted reading: r̂ = 3.6056 m; b̂ = atan2(2, 3) − 0.7 = 0.5880 − 0.7 = −0.1120 rad: the landmark appears 6.4° to the right.
  3. Jacobian H: first row [−3/3.6056, −2/3.6056, 0] = [−0.8321, −0.5547, 0]: moving towards the landmark shortens the range. Second row [2/13, −3/13, −1] = [0.1538, −0.2308, −1].
  4. Linearised prediction for a shift of +0.5 m in y: Δb ≈ −0.2308 × 0.5 = −0.1154 rad. Exact: atan2(1.5, 3) − atan2(2, 3) = −0.1244 rad. Error −0.009 rad, about 0.5°.
  5. Wrapping: if the reading were b = 3.10 rad and the prediction −3.10 rad, the innovation is 6.20 − 2π = −0.0832 rad, not 6.20.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A bend looks straight only for a few metres. The analogy hides how quickly "a few" shrinks: for a landmark 3.6 m away, an error of 2 m already makes the straight-line view wrong by 7° (run R4). For a lost robot, there is no "nearby". R4
    • 

  • Lin knows which lighthouse he sees. The filter is told the landmark's identity here; a real robot is not, and a wrong identification is far worse than a noisy reading. D1
    • 

  • A rider re-checks his guess; the EKF does not. The EKF linearises once per step around its own estimate. If that estimate is wrong, the linearisation is wrong too, and the error can feed itself. D1

Lab


Goal


Implement EKF localisation with known landmarks, verify its Jacobians numerically, and show on a recording with ground truth that it beats dead reckoning and is consistent.


Hardware and software needed


A computer with a C++ compiler (this build: g++ (Ubuntu 13.3.0-6ubuntu2~24.04.1) 13.3.0). Real variant (untested on hardware here): a differential-drive robot with encoders, a sensor that reports range and bearing to recognisable markers, three markers at tape-measured positions, and a ground-truth method (floor marks).


Safety
Simulator variant: none beyond normal computer use. Real variant: simulator first, emergency stop within reach, a clear zone around the circle, speed limit configured; low-voltage battery kits only, no LiPo for learners below L3. A localisation fault can make a robot drive somewhere unexpected: test at low speed.

Method hints


Subtract 2π from the first innovation. Then think about where the predicted bearing comes from: atan2 gives values in [−π, π], but subtracting the heading can leave that range.
