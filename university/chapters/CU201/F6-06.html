Story hook

Ama wants to know how long the great kitchen hall takes to make one order of a thousand sandwiches. She starts her stopwatch, hands the order slip to the door-keeper, and stops the stopwatch the moment the door-keeper says "received". Two seconds! She is delighted and writes "the hall makes a thousand sandwiches in two seconds" in the report. The sandwiches arrive twenty minutes later.

Her second attempt is better: she waits for the sandwiches. But the first order of the morning takes forty minutes, because the ovens are cold and the pantry is still locked; later orders take twenty. And one order takes an hour, because a delivery truck blocked the pantry door. Which number goes in the report? This chapter answers both questions: where to put the stopwatch, and which of many measurements to believe.

What you will be able to do

  • Time a kernel with CUDA events (and HIP events), and explain why a host clock around a launch measures the wrong thing.
  • Apply the course measurement protocol: warm-up runs, then the median of at least 20 timed runs with device events.

  • Diagnose an impossibly fast timing.
Jargon box
CUDA event
Simple words: a marker you put into the GPU's queue of work; the GPU notes the time when it reaches it. Analogy: a slip in the order queue saying "stamp the clock when you get here". Precise: a cudaEvent_t recorded with cudaEventRecord; cudaEventElapsedTime "Computes the elapsed time between two events (in milliseconds with a resolution of around 0.5 microseconds)". H1
Warm-up run
Simple words: a run you do first and do not count, so that one-time costs are paid. Analogy: the first order of the morning, while the ovens heat. Precise: untimed executions before measurement, absorbing one-time costs such as context creation, module loading and cold caches; the curriculum's protocol starts with them. C1
Median
Simple words: the middle value when the measurements are sorted. Analogy: the order time that half the orders beat and half do not; one blocked pantry door does not move it. Precise: for n sorted values, the value at position (n+1)/2 for odd n, or the mean of the two middle values for even n; robust to a few outliers (F0-63). D4
Benchmark harness
Simple words: the program that runs your kernels, times them by the protocol, checks the answers and prints the table. Analogy: the quality desk's stopwatch, logbook and rules, the same for every order. Precise: E1's "benchmark harness (timing, correctness checks, result tables)", "in C++ with a common interface for CUDA and HIP back ends"; this course's project. C1
Effective bandwidth
Simple words: how many bytes your kernel moved per second, counted from what it must read and write. Analogy: jars carried per minute by the whole hall. Precise: (bytes read + bytes written) ÷ time; in this course reported in GB/s with 1 GB = 109 bytes (bench::gbPerS); compared with the peak in F6-09.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

To know how long a job takes, you must start the clock when the job starts and stop it when the job ends. With the hall, that is tricky: you hand over the order and walk away, and the hall works on its own. So you ask the hall to stamp its own clock at the start of your order and again at the end, and you read the two stamps after the order is done. Those stamps are events.

And one measurement is not enough. The first time is slow because everything is cold. After that, most times are close to each other, but now and then something disturbs one run. So you do a few runs to warm up, then many runs you keep, and you report the middle one. One disturbed run cannot move the middle much.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why a host clock around a launch is wrong

A kernel launch returns to the host as soon as the work is queued (F6-01). A host clock read before and after the launch therefore measures how long it takes to queue the work, not to do it. Listing 3's first measurement does exactly that, on purpose. Without a GPU this build cannot show the effect on CUDA, so Listing 5 models it on the CPU: std::async starts the work on another thread and returns at once, like a launch. Stopping the clock right after it gives a fraction of a millisecond whatever the size; waiting first gives a time that grows with the work, tens to hundreds of times larger in this build's runs. R5 (The exact values change from run to run; read them in async_model.out.) A host clock can be right if you synchronize before stopping it, but then it also includes the launch overhead and the synchronization itself.

Events

A CUDA event is recorded into the same queue as the kernel (cudaEventRecord(start), launch, cudaEventRecord(stop)). The GPU stamps each event when it reaches it, so the difference between the stamps is the time the GPU spent between them, which for a single kernel is the kernel's time. D1 Before reading the difference, the host must wait until stop has been reached: cudaEventSynchronize(stop). Then cudaEventElapsedTime(&ms, start, stop) returns milliseconds "with a resolution of around 0.5 microseconds". H1 Two warnings from the same header comment: if the events were recorded in a non-default stream, "the resulting time may be greater than expected", because "Any number of other different stream operations could execute in between the two measured events" (H1). And by default the waiting host thread busy-waits: "If the ::cudaEventBlockingSync flag has not been set, then the CPU thread will busy-wait until the event has been completed by the device" (H1). For this course: one kernel between two events, in the default stream, nothing else running.

The protocol

The curriculum's measurement protocol, verbatim: "warm-up runs, then the median of at least 20 timed runs using device events; fixed clocks if your driver allows it (record the setting); report GB/s or TFLOP/s and the percentage of the reference." C1 This course uses 3 warm-up runs and 21 timed runs (an odd number gives a single middle value). The first runs of a program pay one-time costs: on the GPU, for example, setting up the context and loading the kernel's code; on any machine, cold caches. Listing 2's part B prints the first five runs of a CPU SAXPY without warm-up; in this build's runs the effect on that CPU loop was small next to the run-to-run variation, which is itself a lesson: measure, do not assume (R2). On a GPU, expect the very first launch in a program to be much slower than later ones, and check it in the lab.

Why the median

Part A of Listing 2 summarizes a fixed list of seven times, one of them disturbed: 2.0, 2.1, 1.9, 2.0, 9.0, 2.2, 2.0 ms. The mean is 3.03 ms, larger than six of the seven values; the median is 2.00 ms, a typical value; the minimum, 1.90 ms, is the luckiest run. R2 The median is robust: up to half the runs could be disturbed before it moves far (F0-63). The minimum is sometimes used as "the true speed without interference", but it is a single lucky sample; the harness prints it, with the maximum, to show the spread, and reports the median. A large gap between median and max means something disturbed the runs: write down what else ran on the machine.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

One analogy sentence: the hall's own clock is trustworthy only for what passes through its queue, and only if the hall is not doing other orders at the same time. The engineering consequences:

  • Very short kernels. At a resolution of about 0.5 µs (H1), a kernel of a few microseconds is measured with a large relative error, and launch overhead matters. Time a batch of k identical launches between one pair of events and divide by k, and say so in the table.
  • Clocks. GPUs change their clock frequency with load and temperature. The protocol asks for "fixed clocks if your driver allows it (record the setting)" (C1). How to fix clocks is vendor- and permission-specific; this chapter does not give commands it could not test.
  • State between runs. SAXPY updates y in place, so every timed run sees different values. For a memory-bound kernel that does not change the time; for kernels whose speed depends on data (early exits, sparsity), reset the input before each run, outside the timed region.
  • What else is timed. Events around one kernel measure only the GPU's time between them; copies are timed separately (F6-09). A complete application should also be measured end to end with a host clock, and the two numbers kept apart.
  • Profilers. Nsight Systems shows the timeline of launches, copies and kernels; Nsight Compute measures single kernels in detail (it replays them, so its durations are not the harness's). Both are used from F6-08 on; this build had neither GPU nor profiler output.
  • Statistics. With 21 runs the median is stable to a few percent on a quiet machine; to compare two kernels whose medians differ by less than the spread, use the methods of F0-63 and F2-51 rather than eyeballing.

The one-time costs named here (context creation, module loading on first launch) and the claim that the first launch in a program is much slower are from memory; this build could not observe any GPU timing. Check with the lab's step 2 (print the warm-up runs' times) and in the CUDA C++ Programming Guide (D1) sections on initialization and lazy loading. The header facts quoted in Layer 2 were read in this build (H1).

How the hardware actually does it

The GPU processes the work of a stream in order: the host puts commands (record event, launch kernel, record event) into a queue in memory, and the GPU's front end reads them one by one. When it reaches an event, it writes a timestamp from its own clock to memory and marks the event as complete. D1 Because the stamps come from the device's clock, they are not affected by what the host is doing, but they are affected by anything else the GPU runs between them, which is why the header warns about other stream operations (H1). The host side of cudaEventSynchronize either spins, repeatedly checking whether the event is complete (default, lowest latency, one CPU core busy), or sleeps until woken (cudaEventBlockingSync). H1

Note

The description of a command queue read by a front end that writes timestamps is the usual model of how GPUs execute streams, consistent with the header's wording that the event record "takes place asynchronously" (H1); the details are not documented in a source opened in this build. F6-07 and the later course CU303 (streams) return to it.

Worked example

Task. A SAXPY on 224 floats is timed with the protocol and the 21 sorted event times, in ms, are: 0.96 ×4, 0.97 ×6, 0.98 ×5, 0.99 ×3, 1.02, 1.10, 2.40. (Invented numbers for this example, not a measurement.) Compute what the harness prints.

  1. Runs. 4 + 6 + 5 + 3 + 3 = 21.
  2. Median. The 11th value. Positions 1–4 are 0.96, 5–10 are 0.97, 11–15 are 0.98: median = 0.98 ms.
  3. Min, max. 0.96 ms and 2.40 ms. The max is 2.4 times the median: one run was disturbed; the median is unaffected.
  4. Mean. (4·0.96 + 6·0.97 + 5·0.98 + 3·0.99 + 1.02 + 1.10 + 2.40) ÷ 21 = 22.05 ÷ 21 = 1.05 ms: pulled up by one run.
  5. Bytes. SAXPY reads x and y and writes y: 3 × 4 × 224 = 201,326,592 bytes (the harness's 3.0 * bytes).
  6. GB/s. 201,326,592 ÷ (0.98 × 10−3) ÷ 109 = 205.4 GB/s from the median; from the mean it would be 191.7 GB/s, under-reporting the kernel because of one disturbance.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A kitchen clock on the wall is shared by everyone; GPU event stamps come from the device's clock and can only be compared with other stamps of the same device, not with the host's clock.
    • 

  • "Warming the ovens" suggests heat; on a GPU most first-run costs are software (setting up the context, loading code), plus cold caches and clock frequency ramping (unverified box in Layer 3).
    • 

  • In the kitchen a disturbed order is visible (a truck at the door); on a computer you often cannot see the disturbance, only its effect in the max. Record what else was running.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. The time does not grow with a 16,000-fold growth of the work. That alone proves the measurement is not of the work.


Plausibility. 3 × 4 × 1.6 × 107 = 1.92 × 108 bytes in 0.010 ms = 1.92 × 1013 bytes/s = 19,200 GB/s, 75 times TG-1's 256 GB/s peak: impossible.


Root cause. Line 34 reads the clock right after the launch on line 33; the launch only queues the kernel, so the interval contains the launch call alone. The synchronization on line 36 comes after the clock was stopped.


Model. Listing 5's "timed without wait" column shows a fraction of a millisecond for every size (same symptom); the "timed with wait" column grows with the work, which is what a correct host-clock measurement does (R5).


Fix. Events around the kernel, cudaEventSynchronize(stop), bench::measure(timeOnce, 3, 21), median (Listing 3, lines 41–57). For a memory-bound kernel at large sizes, the time should grow roughly 16× when the size grows 16×. How the evidence was produced: code and the CPU model are real and were run in this build; Ama's table is invented for the scenario.
