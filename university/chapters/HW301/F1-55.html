Story hook

Amara is the head chef of a small restaurant. When a guest orders the house soup, she can have it on the pass in three minutes: she knows every step, she reads ahead, she starts the onions while the stock warms. Nobody in the city is faster at one bowl. Then the restaurant agrees to cater a wedding: six hundred bowls, all served within half an hour.

Amara does the arithmetic on the back of an order slip. Three minutes per bowl, one bowl at a time, is thirty hours. Even if she cooks four bowls side by side, it is still more than seven hours. So the owner opens the banquet hall behind the kitchen. It is full of helpers who are not chefs. Each one is slower at a single bowl than Amara, and each one only follows the recipe card. But there are rows and rows of them, and while one row waits for the stock to come back from the pantry, another row is ladling. The first bowl from the hall comes out later than Amara's would. The six hundredth comes out hours earlier.

That is the whole chapter in one evening: Amara is built to make one thing finish soon; the hall is built to make many things finish per minute. Engineers call the first goal latency and the second throughput, and a GPU is a machine designed, from the transistors up, for the second.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the difference between latency and throughput, and why a design that optimises one pays for it in the other.
  • Measure latency-bound and throughput-bound versions of the same work on your own CPU and explain the ratio.
  • Apply Little's law to compute how much independent work must be in flight to keep a machine busy.
  • Describe the throughput-oriented design of a GPU (many simple lanes, many resident threads, switching instead of waiting) in vendor-neutral words.
  • Diagnose a program that cannot benefit from a GPU because its work is one long dependent chain.
Jargon box
Latency
Simple words: how long one job takes from start to finish. Analogy: the three minutes from Amara reading the order to the bowl on the pass. Precise: the time between the start of an operation (an instruction, a memory access, a request) and the moment its result is available. D2
Throughput
Simple words: how many jobs are finished per second when the machine is kept busy. Analogy: bowls leaving the hall per minute. Precise: the rate at which operations complete, measured over many operations; for one functional unit it is bounded by how often it can accept a new operation. D2
Little's law
Simple words: to finish many jobs per second when each job takes a long time, many jobs must be under way at once. Analogy: if each bowl spends ten minutes in the hall and the hall must send out twenty bowls a minute, two hundred bowls must be in the hall at any moment. Precise: in a stable system, the average number of items in the system equals their average arrival (or completion) rate times their average time in the system: L = λ × W. D4 D2
Instruction-level parallelism (ILP)
Simple words: independent steps inside one program that the processor may work on at the same time. Analogy: Amara chopping onions while the stock warms. Precise: the degree to which instructions of a single instruction stream do not depend on each other's results and can therefore overlap in a pipelined or superscalar core (F1-26). D2
Thread-level parallelism (TLP)
Simple words: many separate jobs, each with its own steps, that can run side by side. Analogy: six hundred separate bowls. Precise: parallelism that comes from multiple independent instruction streams (threads); a GPU exposes it as very many lightweight threads created by one launch. D1 D2
Latency-oriented design
Simple words: a processor built to finish one sequence of steps as soon as possible. Analogy: Amara and her well-organised station. Precise: a design that spends chip area and power on reducing the time of a single thread: large caches, branch prediction, out-of-order and speculative execution. D1 D2
Throughput-oriented design
Simple words: a processor built to finish as many jobs per second as possible, even if each one is slower. Analogy: the banquet hall full of helpers. Precise: a design that spends area on many simple arithmetic lanes and on keeping many threads resident, and tolerates long latencies by switching to other ready work instead of reducing each latency. D1 D3
Kernel (GPU)
Simple words: the function every GPU thread runs. Analogy: the recipe card handed to every helper. Precise: a function launched from the host and executed on the device by many threads; in CUDA it is marked __global__. Not the operating-system kernel of F3. D3 R3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Head chef = CPU core. Banquet hall = GPU. One helper = one GPU thread. A row of helpers who move together = a warp (NVIDIA) or wavefront (AMD). The recipe card = the kernel. The pantry down the corridor = GPU memory. These are the registered mappings of the F1 and F6 analogy worlds (analogy registry).

Some work is a chain: you cannot do step two until step one is done. Waiting for the soup to boil before you add the noodles is a chain. For a chain, only one thing matters: how fast each step is. A chef who is quick at every step wins.

Other work is a pile: six hundred bowls that do not depend on each other. For a pile, what matters is how many bowls leave per minute. A hall of slower helpers wins, as long as there are enough of them and they never stand around. The trick of the hall is that when one row of helpers waits for the pantry, another row works.

A GPU is the hall. It is great at piles and poor at chains. Before you move any program to a GPU, ask the question this chapter teaches: is my work a chain or a pile?

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Two different numbers

Every operation in a computer has a latency and a throughput, and they are different numbers. A pipelined unit (F1-26) may need several cycles to produce one result, yet accept a new independent operation every cycle; its latency is several cycles while its throughput is one result per cycle. D2 If each operation needs the result of the one before, the unit can only start the next one when the previous result arrives, and the program runs at the speed of the latency. If operations are independent, the unit can be kept full and the program runs at the speed of the throughput. D2

Listing 1 shows this on the CPU you are using. Both versions perform the same number of multiply-adds. In the first, every step needs the previous result. In the second, four independent chains share the loop. On the build container the four-chain version needed roughly a quarter of the time per step, run after run (R1). The core is not faster in the second version; it has independent work to overlap with the waiting. R1

How much work must be in flight

Little's law turns this into a sizing rule. If an operation takes W time units from start to result, and you want the machine to complete λ operations per time unit, then on average L = λ × W operations must be in progress at once. D4 For a CPU core that means enough independent instructions in the window it can look at (instruction-level parallelism). For a GPU, whose memory accesses can take hundreds of cycles (the real figure for your GPU belongs in its documentation, not in this book), it means enough threads: thread-level parallelism. D1 D3 Listing 2 does the arithmetic for a few cases, including TG-1, the university's invented teaching GPU.

Note

TG-1 is invented. To practise arithmetic without printing unverified numbers about real products (AH-21, AH-22), HW301 uses TG-1, a made-up "teaching GPU": 4 SMs, warps of 32 threads, 16 resident warps, 4 resident blocks, 16,384 registers and 32 KiB of shared memory per SM, one instruction issued per cycle per SM, a memory latency of 100 cycles, a 256-bit memory interface at 8 GT/s, and a host link of 16 GB/s with 10 µs fixed cost per copy. No real product was checked against these numbers. Replace them by your own GPU's numbers from its vendor document or your own measurement.

Two design philosophies

A CPU core is built to make one thread fast. It spends a large part of its area and power on things that do not compute: caches that turn long memory latencies into short ones, branch predictors that guess where a program goes next, and out-of-order machinery that finds independent instructions within one thread. D1 D2 That investment pays off when the program is a chain, which most programs largely are.

A GPU is built to make many threads finish per second. It spends its area on many simple arithmetic lanes, groups those lanes so that one instruction drives a whole group (the SIMT model of F1-57), and keeps the state of many groups on the chip at once so that it can switch to a ready group whenever another one waits. D1 D3 It does not try hard to make any single access fast; it tries to always have something else to do. The price is that a single thread on a GPU is slow, and a program that offers only one or a few chains leaves almost the whole chip idle. The forensic lab of this chapter is exactly that program.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

An analogy sentence first: Amara's station is optimised for her, the hall is optimised for the queue. Now the engineering trade-offs, at the level of the textbooks this course cites.

  • Latency hiding versus latency reduction. Caches, prefetchers and out-of-order execution reduce the latency a thread sees. Hardware multithreading hides latency: the latency stays, but other work fills the time. GPUs rely mainly on hiding; their caches serve mostly to reduce memory traffic and to combine accesses rather than to make one thread fast. D1 D2 F1-60 builds a model of the scheduler that does the hiding.
  • Context switching for free. A CPU operating system switches threads by saving and restoring registers, which costs time, so it does so rarely. A GPU keeps the registers of every resident thread on the chip all the time, so choosing a different group of threads on the next cycle costs nothing extra; the CUDA Programming Guide says so in its section on hardware multithreading: the execution context of each warp “is maintained on-chip throughout the warp’s lifetime” and “switching between warps incurs no cost” (3.2.2.2). D3 The cost is moved elsewhere: a very large register file (F1-58) and a limit on how many threads fit.
  • Amortised control. One instruction fetched and decoded drives many lanes at once (SIMD inside SIMT), so the control cost per arithmetic operation is small. The price is divergence: lanes that want to do different things must take turns (F1-57). D1 D2
  • Bandwidth as a design target. A throughput machine needs memory that can deliver many bytes per second to many requests at once, which is why GPUs use memories such as HBM and GDDR (F1-59) and why the curriculum calls device memory "the roof for memory-bound kernels" (curriculum 13.1). C1
  • The serial part still counts. Amdahl's law applies: if a fraction of a job is a chain, that fraction runs at single-thread speed, and a GPU's single thread is slower than a CPU core's. Real systems therefore pair a latency-oriented CPU with a throughput-oriented GPU and keep each on the work it suits. D2

Two misreadings to avoid. First, "GPUs are faster" is not a property of the chip; it is a property of a chip running a particular workload with enough independent work, with data already in the right memory (the copies of F1-62 can erase the gain). Second, "the GPU has thousands of cores" mixes vendor marketing terms with architecture: the countable hardware units are SMs or CUs, each containing many lanes, and the honest count for your GPU is printed by the device query of F1-56.

How the hardware actually does it

The CPU measurement of Listing 1 is the hardware story in miniature. The dependent loop compiles to a chain of floating-point multiply and add operations where each one reads the register the previous one wrote; the core's pipelined floating-point unit could accept a new operation before the previous one finishes, but it has nothing independent to accept, so it waits for each result. With four independent chains in the loop, the core's scheduler finds four ready operations and overlaps them, and the time per step falls by about the factor that the latency exceeds the issue interval; on the build container the ratio was close to four in every run (R1). D2 R1 What the CPU does with four chains inside one thread, the GPU does with many warps: the SM's schedulers pick, cycle by cycle, an instruction from whichever warp is ready (F1-60). D3 The forensic lab shows the opposite case in real GPU machine code: a loop compiled for an NVIDIA target in which every instruction of the hot loop writes and reads the same register, so no amount of hardware can overlap it.

Worked example

Task. TG-1 (invented) has a memory latency of 100 cycles and each SM can issue one load per cycle. How many independent loads must be in flight on one SM, and on the whole GPU, to keep the load path busy? And what does the CPU measurement tell you about the core it ran on?

  1. Little's law per SM. W = 100 cycles, λ = 1 load per cycle. L = λ × W = 1 × 100 = 100 loads in flight on each SM.
  2. Whole GPU. TG-1 has 4 SMs, so λ = 4 loads per cycle for the GPU and L = 4 × 100 = 400 loads in flight.
  3. In threads. If each thread has only one load outstanding at a time, the GPU needs 400 threads waiting on memory at every moment, in addition to the ones computing. TG-1 holds 16 warps of 32 threads per SM, 4 × 16 × 32 = 2,048 threads in total, so the requirement can be met, but only if the launch provides enough threads and each thread's resources allow 16 warps to fit (F1-58).
  4. CPU check. In the recorded runs of Listing 1, one chain took about 1.9 ns per step and four chains about 0.5 ns per step, so the core completed about 2 steps per ns when given independent work. Little's law gives 1.9 × 2.0 ≈ 3.8: about four steps had to be in flight, which is exactly what four chains supplied. Your numbers will differ: they are measurements of one machine on one date (AH-23).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Helpers are independent people; GPU lanes are not. In the hall, each helper could in principle do something different. On a GPU, the lanes of a warp or wavefront share one instruction stream; if they need different steps, they take turns (F1-57). D1
    • 

  • Amara gets faster with more independent dishes too. The analogy suggests the CPU only does one thing at a time. A real CPU core overlaps independent instructions within a thread, which is exactly what Listing 1 measured (R1), and multi-core CPUs run several threads. The difference is one of degree and design priority, not of kind. R1 D2
    • 

  • The pantry trip is not a fixed walk. The analogy implies one distance to the pantry. Real memory latency depends on caches, contention and access patterns, and its value for a real GPU must come from the vendor's documentation or from a measurement (AH-21).
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a kernel launched with very few threads is slower than the CPU. Cause: the work offered is a few chains; most of the GPU is idle. How to find it: look at the launch configuration (<<<1, 1>>> in the forensic lab) and at the dependent registers in the SASS. Fix: restructure so that independent items map to threads, or keep the work on the CPU.
    • 

  • Symptom: timing shows identical times for "optimised" and "unoptimised" loops. Cause: the loop was optimised away or not optimised at all. How to find it: print the result (Listing 1 does) and check the optimisation level. Fix: use the result; optimise the measured function (lines 9 and 17).
    • 

  • Symptom: a GPU program prints all zeros. Cause: no GPU or a failed call, unchecked. How to find it: check every call, as Listing 3 does; the recorded run shows what the check prints without a GPU. Fix: wrap every runtime call (F6-05).
    • 

  • Symptom: a "speed-up" that disappears when copies are included. Cause: only the kernel was timed. How to find it: time the whole job end to end. Fix: see F1-62.
    •

Summary


    

  • Latency is how long one operation takes; throughput is how many complete per unit time. They are different numbers.
    • 

  • Dependent work runs at the speed of latency; independent work can run at the speed of throughput.
    • 

  • Little's law: work in flight = rate × latency. Long latencies need much independent work.
    • 

  • CPUs are latency-oriented: they spend area on caches, prediction and out-of-order execution for one thread.
    • 

  • GPUs are throughput-oriented: many simple lanes, many resident threads, switching to ready work instead of waiting.
    • 

  • A single GPU thread is slow; a program must expose thousands of independent pieces of work.
    • 

  • Your CPU measured about a four-fold gain from four independent chains in this build (a measurement, not a law).

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Every FFMA in the loop is FFMA R5, R5, R4, 0.001…: it reads R5 and writes R5. The loop body was unrolled 16 times (addresses 0x140–0x250) but all 16 instructions form one chain. The launch line is integrate<<<1, 1>>>: one block of one thread.


Root cause. The work is one dependent chain run by a single thread. Each FFMA must wait for the previous result, so the kernel runs at one FFMA latency per step, on one lane of one SM, while every other lane and SM of the GPU is idle. The GPU's design hides latency by switching to other warps; here there are no other warps. Unrolling only removes loop overhead; it cannot remove a data dependence.


Wrong answers and why. "A memory problem": there is one load before the loop and one store after it; the loop touches no memory. "A slow instruction": FFMA is the GPU's basic arithmetic instruction; it is slow here only because of the chain.


Fix. Give the GPU a pile: integrate many independent systems (one per thread, for example a parameter sweep or an ensemble), or keep this chain on the CPU, which has a faster single thread. If the physics allows it, reformulate with a parallel-in-time or parallel-scan method (an advanced topic, not covered here).


How the evidence was produced. The Lab Engineer wrote the kernel exactly as a serial loop and compiled it with nvcc -arch=sm_80 -cubin; the SASS is real compiler output. No GPU was used. Prevention: before porting, answer "chain or pile?" in writing and estimate the number of independent work items.
