Story hook

Bao writes to Chidi every Sunday, without fail. One week, no letter comes. Chidi waits two days, then three. Is Bao ill? Has he moved away? Is the post slow because of a storm? Did he write and the letter get lost? Chidi needs to decide whether to take over Bao's half of the book collection, because members are waiting.

If Chidi waits too long, members wait for weeks for nothing. If he decides too soon, he takes over Bao's books, and then Bao's letter arrives, "Sorry, the post was slow", and now both of them think they are lending the same books. The second mistake can be worse than the first.

Chidi cannot look into Bao's town. All he has is the absence of letters. Deciding from that absence is what a failure detector does, and this chapter is about how to do it, how it goes wrong, and how to make it safe when it does.

What you will be able to do

  • Classify failures with the standard models (crash-stop, crash-recovery, omission, timing, arbitrary or Byzantine) and state which one a design assumes.
  • Explain the synchronous, asynchronous and partially synchronous timing models, and why perfect failure detection is impossible in an asynchronous system.
  • Build and measure heartbeat failure detectors with fixed and adaptive timeouts, and trade detection time against false suspicions using recorded numbers.
  • Explain why a wrong suspicion can create two active primaries (split brain) and how fencing tokens prevent damage.
  • Diagnose a "dead" node that was alive, from monitor and node logs, and fix both the detector and the cause.
Jargon box
Failure model
Simple words: a precise list of the ways a part is allowed to go wrong in your design. Analogy: the club agreeing "a friend may fall ill or lose letters, but never lies in a letter". Precise: the assumptions a system's design makes about how components may deviate from their specification (stop, lose messages, be late, behave arbitrarily); a protocol is correct only relative to its failure model. D2 D1
Crash-stop and crash-recovery
Simple words: a crashed computer either stays dead, or comes back later having forgotten what was only in its memory. Analogy: a friend who leaves the club for good, or one who returns after an illness with only her written notebook. Precise: crash-stop: a node halts and never takes another step; crash-recovery: a node halts and may restart, losing volatile state but keeping what it wrote to stable storage. D1 D2
Omission and timing failures
Simple words: a part that sometimes does not send or receive a message, or answers too late. Analogy: letters lost in the post, or arriving after the club meeting. Precise: omission failure: a component fails to send or to receive some messages; timing (performance) failure: a response arrives outside a specified time interval. D2
Byzantine failure
Simple words: a part that can do anything at all, including sending wrong or contradictory messages. Analogy: a friend who writes different stories to different friends. Precise: an arbitrary failure: a component may produce any output, at any time, including inconsistent messages to different peers, through bugs, corruption or malice; tolerating it needs more replicas and different protocols than crash failures. D2 D1
Timing model (synchronous, asynchronous, partially synchronous)
Simple words: what you may assume about how long messages and computations take. Analogy: "letters always arrive within three days" versus "letters arrive eventually, nobody knows when" versus "usually within three days, but sometimes much later". Precise: synchronous: known upper bounds on message delay, processing time and clock drift; asynchronous: no bounds at all; partially synchronous: bounds hold most of the time or after some unknown time, but may be violated for periods. D1 D2
Failure detector
Simple words: the part of a system that decides which other nodes seem to have failed. Analogy: Chidi deciding Bao is ill because no letter has come. Precise: a module that outputs the set of processes it currently suspects to have crashed, usually from missing heartbeats or replies; characterised by completeness (crashed processes are eventually suspected) and accuracy (correct processes are not suspected, or eventually not). D2
Heartbeat
Simple words: a small "I am alive" message sent regularly. Analogy: Bao's Sunday letter. Precise: a periodic message whose only purpose is to show liveness; the receiver suspects the sender when no heartbeat has arrived within a timeout. D2 R1
False suspicion
Simple words: deciding a computer has died when it is only slow or cut off. Analogy: Chidi taking over Bao's books while Bao's letter is still in the post. Precise: a failure detector suspecting a process that has not crashed; unavoidable in asynchronous and partially synchronous systems, so designs must remain safe when it happens. D1 R1
Split brain
Simple words: two parts of a system each think they are in charge. Analogy: the phone lines between two towns are down (registered: network partition) and each town elects its own club secretary. Precise: a state in which two nodes or groups both act as the primary or leader, often after a partition or a false suspicion, accepting conflicting updates. D1
Fencing token
Simple words: a number that grows every time a new leader is chosen, so that old leaders can be told "no". Analogy: each new club secretary gets a stamp with a higher number, and the library refuses letters stamped with an older number. Precise: a monotonically increasing number (epoch, term) issued with each grant of leadership or a lock and attached to every action; the resource rejects actions carrying a token lower than the highest it has seen. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

When a friend's weekly letter does not come, you cannot tell whether she is ill, the post is slow, or the phone lines between the towns are down. You can only decide after waiting some time. Mapping: the weekly letter = heartbeat; how long you wait = timeout; deciding "she is ill" = suspecting a failure; being wrong = false suspicion; the phone lines down = a network partition; the numbered stamp of the new secretary = fencing token.

Waiting longer makes you right more often but slower. Waiting less makes you faster but wrong more often. No waiting time is right in every case, because a slow friend and a sick friend look exactly the same from far away. So good designs do two things: they choose the waiting time from measurements, and they make sure that being wrong does no harm.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Failure models

A protocol is correct only with respect to stated assumptions about failures D2 D1. The standard models, from mildest to most general:

OmissionFail to send or receive some messages.A full queue dropping packets; a broken link.F5-09 retries; F5-12's lossy network.

Each model includes the ones above it in power: a design that tolerates omission failures also survives crashes (a crash is an omission of everything from then on), but not the reverse D2.

Timing models and why detection cannot be perfect

In a synchronous system there are known bounds on message delay and processing time; a node that has not answered within the bound has certainly crashed, so a perfect failure detector is easy. In an asynchronous system there are no bounds: a message may take any finite time, so no waiting time ever proves a crash D1 D2. Real networks and computers are best described as partially synchronous: delays are usually bounded, but queues, retransmissions and pauses occasionally exceed any bound you pick D1. The practical consequence is the theme of this chapter: every timeout-based detector makes mistakes, and every design that acts on its output must stay safe when it does. (In the asynchronous model this impossibility is the root of the result that consensus cannot be guaranteed with even one crash, studied in DS302 D3.)

Heartbeat detectors and their two numbers

A heartbeat detector has the monitored process send "alive" every interval, and the monitor suspects it after a timeout T without one D2. Two numbers describe how good it is: the detection time (from a real crash to suspicion) and the false-suspicion rate (how often a live process is suspected). Listing 1 measures both for six fixed timeouts in a model with a heartbeat every 100 ms, small jitter, 2 % of heartbeats delayed by 50–400 ms and one 600 ms stall R1:

  • T = 150 ms detects the crash in 54 ms but raised 10 false suspicions in the simulated minute.
  • T = 300 ms and T = 500 ms each had 1 false suspicion: the 600 ms stall, which silences the process for about 700 ms (from the last heartbeat before it to the first after it); no timeout below that can tell it from a crash.
  • T = 700 ms had none, and took 604 ms to detect the crash.

Detection time is about T minus the time between the last heartbeat's sending and the crash: shortening T is the only way to detect faster, and every shortening costs false suspicions (Figure 2).

Adaptive timeouts, and the limits of a model

Instead of a fixed T, a detector can learn the distribution of heartbeat gaps and set T from it, for example mean + k × standard deviation of the last 100 gaps (MA202). Listing 1 tries k = 2, 4, 8: the adaptive detectors detected the crash fastest (30–107 ms) but had 4–8 false suspicions R1. The reason is visible in the model: gaps are almost always 100 ± 3 ms, so the standard deviation is tiny and T settles near 126–203 ms, while 2 % of heartbeats are delayed by hundreds of milliseconds. Mean plus a few standard deviations describes a narrow, bell-shaped distribution well and a heavy-tailed one badly (tail of a distribution, MA202). An adaptive detector is only as good as its model of the delays; choose the statistic (a high percentile of measured gaps, or a model of the tail) from data like your real network's.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Completeness and accuracy

Failure detectors are classified by two properties D2: completeness, every crashed process is eventually suspected (by some or by every correct process), and accuracy, correct processes are not suspected (never, or only for a while: "eventually accurate"). A timeout detector is complete: a crashed process sends nothing, so the timeout always fires. It is accurate only if the timeout exceeds every possible delay, which no finite timeout guarantees in a partially synchronous system; at best it is eventually accurate, if timeouts grow after each false suspicion until they exceed the real bound. Consensus protocols such as Raft (DS302) are designed to be safe with any detector and live once the detector is eventually accurate.

Note

The formal classification of failure detectors by completeness and accuracy comes from a paper by Chandra and Toueg, which is not in the guide's F5 source registry; the claim above is tagged to the textbook D2 and the paper is proposed as an addition in this course's notes.

Acting on a suspicion safely

A suspicion is a guess. Systems turn guesses into actions such as "promote the backup", "release the lock", "elect a new leader". The action is safe only if the suspected node, should it be alive, can no longer do harm D1:

  • Fencing tokens. Every grant of leadership or of a lock comes with a number larger than all earlier ones; every write carries it; the storage rejects a write whose number is smaller than the largest it has seen. An old leader that wakes up after a pause is rejected even though it still believes in its role.
  • Majority decisions. If a new leader needs votes from a majority, and the old leader also needs a majority to act, at most one of them can succeed at a time, because two majorities of the same group always share a member (F5-08, DS302).
  • Self-demotion. A leader that cannot reach a majority within its own timeout stops acting as leader. This is necessary but not sufficient: a paused process cannot run its own check during the pause (the two leaders of F5-10).
  • Power fencing. When the resource cannot check tokens (a shared disk), some systems cut the suspected node's power or network port before taking over. This is outside DS301's labs.

Partitions

A network partition (the registered analogy: the phone lines between two towns are down) splits nodes into groups that can talk inside but not across. Each group sees the other as failed. If both groups continue to accept writes, the result is split brain and conflicting data; if only a group with a majority continues, the minority stops serving writes, trading availability for consistency D1. F5-13 returns to this trade-off.

Practical rules for timeouts

  1. Measure the distribution of heartbeat gaps (or reply times) on the real system, including its bad moments (garbage collection, checkpoints, backups, failovers), and report percentiles, not means.
  2. Choose T above the stalls the system is designed to have, or remove those stalls from the path that sends heartbeats (forensic lab).
  3. Require several consecutive misses or an indirect check (ask other nodes whether they can reach the suspect) before acting.
  4. Make the action safe under a wrong suspicion (fencing, majorities), so that the timeout trades only speed, not correctness.
  5. Avoid flapping: once a node is suspected and replaced, rejoining should be a deliberate step, not an automatic reversal on the next heartbeat.

How the hardware actually does it

A heartbeat is an ordinary small message (F5-04). What makes it late is mostly not the wire but queues and pauses: packets waiting behind others in a switch or NIC queue (F5-07), retransmission after a loss, the sending process not being scheduled (OS201), a page fault served from disk, or a long synchronous operation on the same thread, as in the forensic lab. A timer on the monitor fires from the monotonic clock (F5-10); the monitor itself can be paused too, which makes a healthy process look silent. Watchdog timers (watchdog timer, HW204) are the single-machine relative of a failure detector: hardware that resets a machine whose software stops "kicking" it.

Worked example

Explain the T = 300 ms and T = 1000 ms rows of detector.out from the model.

  1. Heartbeats are sent every 100 ms and normally take 2–5 ms. In a normal stretch the gap between arrivals is 100 ± 3 ms.
  2. A heartbeat delayed by d ms is overtaken by the next one if d > 100 (the listing sorts arrivals by time, line 92). The longest gap a single delay can cause is therefore about 200 ms (the delayed one's predecessor to its successor). Two consecutive delayed heartbeats (probability about 0.02² per pair) can stretch a gap to 300 ms and more.
  3. The stall drops the heartbeats sent from 20.0 to 20.5 s: the gap runs from the arrival of the 19.9 s heartbeat to that of the 20.6 s one, about 700 ms. Every T below that suspects the live process once.
  4. T = 300 ms: 1 false suspicion, the stall, so no pair of delays reached 300 ms in this run. Detection: the last heartbeat before the crash was sent at 59.9 s and arrived about 59.904 s; suspicion at 59.904 + 0.300 = 60.204 s, i.e. 204 ms after the crash at 60.0 s, as recorded R1.
  5. T = 1000 ms: no gap exceeded 1 s, so 0 false suspicions; detection 59.904 + 1.000 − 60.000 ≈ 904 ms.

The ratio is the lesson: going from 300 to 1000 ms removed one false suspicion per simulated minute and cost 700 ms of detection time. Whether that is a good trade depends on what a false suspicion costs, which is exactly what Layer 3's "make the action safe" changes.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A friend who was thought ill and then writes again apologises. A node that was replaced and comes back can keep acting on its old role (accepting writes, holding locks) because nobody told it: that is the danger the forensic lab shows.
    • 

  • Friends can ask a neighbour to knock on the door. Nodes can ask other nodes ("can you reach B?"), but every such check travels over the same unreliable network.
    • 

  • Letters are rarely delayed by weeks. Computer delays are heavy-tailed: most heartbeats are on time, a few are very late, and averages hide them (Layer 2).
    • 

  • A friend who lies is a social problem. A Byzantine node is a technical one that needs different protocols and more replicas; crash-tolerant designs give no protection against it.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Each DEAD alert falls inside a db2 checkpoint: checkpoint 14:02:15.000–15.534 (534 ms) and DEAD at 15.401, ALIVE at 15.601; 45.000–45.843 (843 ms), DEAD 45.402; 03:15.000–15.930 (930 ms), DEAD 15.401; 45.000–45.858 (858 ms), DEAD 45.401 R2. The last heartbeat before each stall arrived about 0.1 s before the checkpoint started, so db2 was silent for about the checkpoint length plus up to 100 ms, every time longer than the 500 ms timeout.


Hypotheses. (1) db2 crashed and restarted: ruled out, db2's log continues without a restart, and heartbeats resume within a second each time. (2) Network trouble between db2 and the monitor: possible in general, but the silences coincide exactly with checkpoints, every 30 s, and the configuration says heartbeat and checkpoint share one worker thread. (3) Checkpoints block the heartbeat: consistent with all evidence. Conclusion: db2 was alive and paused, a timing failure, not a crash.


The duplicate transaction. At 15.404 the monitor promoted db3; db3 committed txn 88121 at 15.688. db2 never learned it had been demoted ("ALIVE (as replica)" is only the monitor's view; nothing in db2's log shows a role change) and committed its own txn 88121 at 15.700 as primary. Two primaries assigned the same next transaction number to different orders: split brain caused by a false suspicion and an unfenced failover.


Fixes. Detector: timeout above the longest designed stall, or several consecutive misses (a 500 ms timeout against 534–930 ms checkpoints guarantees false alarms). db2: send heartbeats from a thread that never waits for the checkpoint, or make checkpoints incremental. Failover: fence before promoting (an epoch number that storage and clients check, so db2's writes with the old epoch are rejected; or demote db2 by an acknowledged message before db3 accepts writes). Only the fencing fix prevents the duplicate on its own: the other two make false suspicions rarer but cannot make a suspicion certain.


Verification. Re-run with the fixed configuration and a checkpoint load; assert no DEAD alerts for a live db2 over a long run; in a failover test, freeze db2, let db3 take over, unfreeze db2 and assert its writes are rejected.
