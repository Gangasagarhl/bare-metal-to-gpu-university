Story hook

Amara, Bao and Chidi keep a diary of club events, each in their own town, each with their own watch. Amara's watch is right. Bao's is forty minutes slow; Chidi's is twenty-five minutes fast. One evening they put the three diaries side by side and sort every entry by the time written next to it. The result is nonsense: Bao's diary says he received Amara's letter at 9:37, but Amara's diary says she posted it at 10:05. A letter cannot arrive before it was sent.

Chidi has an idea. From now on, everyone keeps a counter. Every time you write a diary entry or a letter, add one. Write the counter on every letter. When a letter arrives with a number bigger than yours, jump your counter past it. Now, whenever one event could have influenced another, the first one has the smaller number, whatever the watches say.

That idea is Lamport's logical clock. This chapter shows why physical clocks cannot order events across machines, what "happened before" means precisely, how Lamport clocks and vector clocks capture it, and how to reconstruct the true order of events from the logs of nodes whose clocks disagree: the course's forensic lab, "Two leaders".

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why physical clocks of different nodes disagree (skew and drift), which Linux clock to use for durations and which for timestamps, and why neither orders events across nodes.
  • Define the happened-before relation and decide, for two events in a message diagram, whether one happened before the other or whether they are concurrent.
  • Implement Lamport clocks in a three-node simulation and show that they order every message's send before its receive, where physical timestamps do not.
  • Use vector clocks to detect concurrent events, and explain what a Lamport timestamp cannot tell you.
  • Reconstruct the order of events from logs of nodes with skewed clocks, using message identities and round-trip bounds, and diagnose a "two leaders" incident.
Jargon box
Clock skew (distributed systems)
Simple words: how far apart the clocks of two computers are at the same moment. Analogy: everyone's watch is a little different (registered F5 mapping). Precise: the difference between the readings of two nodes' clocks at the same instant; not the same as clock skew in digital circuits, where the term means the difference in arrival time of one clock edge at two flip-flops (clock skew, HW201). D2 D1
Clock drift
Simple words: a clock running a little too fast or too slow, so the error grows over time. Analogy: a watch that gains a few seconds every day. Precise: the rate at which a clock's reading departs from a reference time, caused by the oscillator's frequency error; drift turns a small skew into a large one unless the clock is corrected periodically. D2
Happened-before relation
Simple words: event a happened before event b if a could have influenced b. Analogy: Amara posting a letter happened before Bao reading it, and before anything Bao wrote after reading it. Precise: the smallest relation → with: if a and b are on the same node and a comes first, a → b; if a is the sending of a message and b its receipt, a → b; if a → b and b → c, then a → c. It is a partial order. D3
Concurrent events
Simple words: two events where neither could have influenced the other. Analogy: Chidi writing in his diary while Amara posts a letter to Bao. Precise: events a and b with neither a → b nor b → a (written a ∥ b); "concurrent" here says nothing about the physical times at which they happened. D3
Lamport clock
Simple words: a counter on each node that makes causes always get smaller numbers than their effects. Analogy: numbering every letter and jumping your own counter past any number you receive. Precise: a logical clock L kept by each node: increment before each event; attach L to each message sent; on receipt set L to max(L, received) and then increment. It satisfies the clock condition: a → b implies L(a) < L(b). Ordering by (L, node id) gives a total order consistent with →. D3
Vector clock
Simple words: a list of counters, one per node, that tells you exactly which events could have influenced which. Analogy: each letter carries a little table: "how many entries from each friend I have heard about". Precise: a vector V with one entry per node; a node increments its own entry before each event, attaches V to messages, and on receipt takes the element-wise maximum before incrementing; then a → b exactly when V(a) ≤ V(b) element-wise and V(a) ≠ V(b). D2 D6
Total order (of events)
Simple words: putting every event in one line, with no ties. Analogy: one combined club diary in which every entry has its own place. Precise: an ordering in which any two distinct events are comparable; a total order consistent with → exists (Lamport clocks with a tie-break give one), but it is not unique: concurrent events may be placed either way. D3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Everyone's watch is a little different, so you cannot sort the friends' diaries by the times written in them. Instead, each friend keeps a counter, adds one for every diary entry or letter, writes it on every letter, and jumps past any bigger number that arrives. Mapping: friend = node; watch = physical clock; the difference between watches = clock skew; counter = Lamport clock; letter = message; "this letter was written after I read yours" = happened-before.

The counter does not tell you what time it is. It tells you something more useful for a distributed system: if one event could have caused another, the cause has the smaller number. Watches cannot promise even that.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Physical clocks disagree

Every node has its own clock, driven by its own oscillator (OS302, HW204). Two errors matter D2 D1:

  • Skew: at any instant, two nodes' clocks read different values.
  • Drift: each clock runs at a slightly wrong rate, so skew grows until something corrects it.

Clock synchronisation protocols periodically correct each node's clock against a reference over the network. They reduce skew but cannot remove it, because a correction message itself takes an unknown time to arrive; and a correction may step the clock, making it jump forward or backward D1 D2.

Linux gives a program two clocks with different promises (Listing 1) D4. CLOCK_REALTIME is the time of day: it can be set and stepped, so it can go backwards. CLOCK_MONOTONIC starts at an unspecified point and never goes backwards: in the recorded run, a million consecutive reads never decreased R1. Use the monotonic clock for timeouts and durations (monotonic clock, OS302); use the realtime clock only for timestamps meant for humans, and never compare realtime timestamps from different nodes to decide what happened first.

Happened-before

Lamport's paper replaces "earlier in time" with a relation that the system can actually observe D3. Events are the steps of the nodes: local events, sends and receives. Event a happened before b (a → b) if:

  1. a and b are on the same node and a comes first; or
  2. a sends a message and b receives that message; or
  3. there is a c with a → c and c → b.

Two events with neither a → b nor b → a are concurrent. In Figure 1, a1 → b2 (a1, a2 on A, message m1 to b1, then b2 on B), but a1 and c1 are concurrent: no chain of messages connects them. Concurrent does not mean "at the same physical time"; it means "neither could have known about the other".

Lamport clocks

Each node keeps an integer L, starting at 0 D3:

  • before each event, L = L + 1, and the event gets that value;
  • a send carries L in the message;
  • on a receive, L = max(L, carried value), then the increment of the first rule.

This guarantees the clock condition: if a → b then L(a) < L(b). Ordering all events by (L, node id) therefore gives a total order in which every cause precedes its effects. Listing 2 checks this on the scenario of Figure 1: ordering by physical clock places two receives before their sends (m1 and m3), ordering by (Lamport time, node) places none R2.

The converse is false: L(a) < L(b) does not mean a → b. In Figure 1, L(a3) = 3 < L(b2) = 4, but a3 and b2 are concurrent R2 R3. A Lamport timestamp can prove "b did not happen before a", never "a caused b".

Vector clocks

A vector clock keeps one counter per node. A node increments its own entry before each event, sends the whole vector, and on receipt takes the element-wise maximum before incrementing its entry D2. Then a → b exactly when V(a) ≤ V(b) in every entry and V(a) ≠ V(b); if neither vector is ≤ the other, the events are concurrent. Listing 3 computes the vectors for the same scenario and classifies pairs: a3 [A=3 B=0 C=0] and b2 [A=2 B=2 C=0] are concurrent; c1 [0 0 1] happened before a4 [4 3 3] through m3 R3. The price is size: one entry per node in every message. Replicated stores use the same idea, under the name version vectors, to detect conflicting concurrent updates (F5-12, F5-13) D6.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What logical time is used for

Lamport's paper uses the total order to build a distributed mutual-exclusion algorithm: every request is timestamped, and requests are granted in timestamp order, which all nodes agree on because the order is total and derived only from messages D3. More generally, a total order consistent with causality lets replicas apply updates in the same order (the idea behind state-machine replication, DS302). The limits: the order is only known once you have heard from every node (a node cannot know that no smaller timestamp is still in flight without hearing from everyone), and it says nothing about real-time order between events that did not exchange messages. The paper itself discusses this "anomalous behaviour": a user can observe an order through a channel outside the system (a phone call), and logical clocks cannot see it; physical clocks with a known error bound are its remedy D3.

Bounding clock offsets from messages

Logs of real systems contain wall-clock timestamps, not Lamport times. You can still bound the offset between two nodes' clocks from messages that carry identities, as the forensic lab needs. Let a message leave node X at X-clock time s and arrive at node Y at Y-clock time r, and let θ = (Y's clock − X's clock). Then r − s = delay + θ, with delay ≥ 0, so θ ≤ r − s. A message in the other direction, from Y at Y-time s′ to X at X-time r′, gives r′ − s′ = delay′ − θ, so θ ≥ s′ − r′. Each pair of messages in opposite directions brackets θ; the tightest pair gives the best bound. This is the reasoning behind round-trip-based clock synchronisation (Cristian's method in D2) D2. It needs no assumption about the clocks, only that a message cannot arrive before it was sent.

Why time-based leadership is dangerous

Many systems make one node the leader and let it act alone (accept writes, hand out locks) for as long as it believes it is leader. If that belief rests on time ("I have not been told otherwise for 3 seconds", "my lease has 2 seconds left"), then a node that pauses (a long garbage-collection or swap stall, a frozen virtual machine) or whose clock jumps can wake up and act on a belief that expired while it was not looking D1. The other nodes have meanwhile elected a new leader. For a while there are two leaders, and each believes in good faith that it is the only one. The defences, developed in F5-11 and DS302: measure lease time on the monotonic clock and leave a margin for drift; check leadership with a majority before acknowledging a write; and attach an increasing number (a term or fencing token) to every action so that storage rejects actions from an old leader.

Not verified — check before relying on this

This chapter states no accuracy for any clock synchronisation protocol (for example the Network Time Protocol or the IEEE precision time protocol standard) and no drift rate for any oscillator: no specification or datasheet was opened in this build (AH-21). Their specifications must be cited by the Source Researcher (protocol documents of the IETF and the IEEE; the oscillator's datasheet) before any number is used. Hybrid logical clocks, which combine physical and logical time, are a further topic not covered here. The Linux clock semantics stated above (realtime can be set and stepped; monotonic cannot be set) are cited to the clock_gettime documentation (D4, title only) and were consistent with the recorded run, which cannot prove a negative (no step occurred during it).

How the hardware actually does it

A node's time comes from a hardware counter driven by a crystal oscillator (the TSC or a platform timer on x86, OS302; timers in HW204). The kernel converts counter ticks to nanoseconds with a calibrated rate, keeps the monotonic clock as a count since boot, and keeps the realtime clock as that count plus an offset that synchronisation software may adjust gradually or step D4. The oscillator's frequency error, which depends on the part and its temperature, is the physical source of drift; its size for a given part is stated in that part's datasheet. In the build container both clocks reported a resolution of 1 ns R1; resolution is not accuracy: it says how fine the reported numbers are, not how close they are to true time or to another machine's clock.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on real hardware: the three nodes of the lab and of the forensic lab are simulated in one process; their clock offsets (0, −40 ms, +25 ms in the lab; −3 s and +0.8 s in the forensic lab) are chosen by the simulator, not measured. Measuring real skew between machines needs at least two machines and the bracketing method of Layer 3 (lab extension); the size you find depends on your machines and network and must be reported as your measurement (AH-23).

Worked example

Compute the Lamport times of Figure 1 by hand, then check them against lamport.out. Events in the order the simulator processes them (true time), clocks start at 0:

  1. a1 (A, local): LA = 0 + 1 = 1.
  2. a2 (A, send m1): LA = 2; m1 carries 2.
  3. c1 (C, local): LC = 1.
  4. b1 (B, receive m1): LB = max(0, 2) + 1 = 3.
  5. b2 (B, local): 4. b3 (B, send m2): 5, m2 carries 5.
  6. c2 (C, receive m2): max(1, 5) + 1 = 6. c3 (C, send m3): 7, m3 carries 7.
  7. a3 (A, local): LA was 2, so 3.
  8. a4 (A, receive m3): max(3, 7) + 1 = 8.
  9. b4 (B, send m4): LB was 5, so 6; m4 carries 6.
  10. a5 (A, receive m4): max(8, 6) + 1 = 9.

All ten values match the "Lamport" column of the recorded run R2. Check the clock condition on each message: m1 2 → 3, m2 5 → 6, m3 7 → 8, m4 6 → 9. Now the physical clocks: m1 left A at A-clock 1005 and arrived at B-clock 977. Sorting by physical clock puts the receive 28 ms "before" the send. With the bracketing rule of Layer 3, m1 gives θB−A ≤ 977 − 1005 = −28 ms, and m4 (B-clock 1010 to A-clock 1063) gives θB−A ≥ 1010 − 1063 = −53 ms: B's clock is between 28 and 53 ms behind A's. The simulator's true offset, −40 ms, lies inside that bracket.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Friends' watches are wrong by a fixed amount for a while. Computer clocks also drift and are stepped by synchronisation software, so the error changes and a clock can jump backwards.
    • 

  • Friends can compare watches by phone in a moment. Comparing computer clocks costs a message whose delay is unknown, so any comparison has an error of up to the round-trip time (Layer 3).
    • 

  • A counter on a letter feels like "the time it was written". A Lamport time is not a time at all: it cannot say how long ago something happened, and a smaller number does not mean "earlier" for concurrent events.
    • 

  • Friends notice when something happens outside the letters (a phone call). Logical clocks only see the system's own messages; causality through any other channel is invisible to them.
    •

Summary


    

  • Nodes' clocks have skew and drift; synchronisation reduces but cannot remove the error, and may step the clock.
    • 

  • Use the monotonic clock for durations and timeouts; use wall-clock time only for humans; never order cross-node events by wall clocks.
    • 

  • Happened-before (→) is defined by order on a node, messages, and transitivity; events not related by it are concurrent.
    • 

  • Lamport clocks guarantee a → b ⇒ L(a) < L(b) and give a total order with a tie-break; the converse does not hold.
    • 

  • Vector clocks characterise → exactly and detect concurrency, at the cost of one entry per node.
    • 

  • Message pairs in both directions bound clock offsets without trusting any clock; this is how to reconstruct real order from logs.
    • 

  • Time-based beliefs (leases, timeouts) can outlive their validity after a pause: two leaders.
    •

  1. Synchronisation leaves an error of at least the uncertainty of message delays, clocks drift between corrections, and corrections can step a clock backwards. Two events closer together than that error cannot be ordered by timestamps; and the order that matters (could one have influenced the other?) is defined by messages, not by time.
    2. 

  2. (a1, c2): a1 → c2 (a1 → a2 → b1 → b3 → c2). (c1, b4): concurrent (no message from C reaches B before b4). (b2, a4): b2 → a4 (b2 → b3 → c2 → c3 → a4). (a3, a5): a3 → a5 (same node, a3 first). Check with Listing 3's method: a4 [4 3 3] ≥ b2 [2 2 0].
    3. 

  3. If a → b then L(a) < L(b). The converse is false: L(a3) = 3 < L(b2) = 4, but a3 and b2 are concurrent (R2, R3).
    4. 

  4. A vector clock decides for any two events whether one happened before the other or they are concurrent; a Lamport clock only gives an order consistent with causality. Cost: one counter per node in every message and every stored version, which grows with the number of nodes.
    5. 

  5. From X to Y: θ ≤ 50.150 − 50.200 = −0.050 s. From Y to X: θ ≥ 50.160 − 50.230 = −0.070 s. So Y's clock is between 50 and 70 ms behind X's.
    6. 

  6. m5 sent at c1+ gets L = 2 on C (c1 = 1, send = 2); B receives it first: LB = max(0, 2) + 1 = 3; then b1 receives m1 (2): max(3, 2) + 1 = 4; b2 = 5, b3 = 6, b4 = 7, and the later C and A values shift accordingly (c2 = max(2, 6) + 1 = 7, c3 = 8, a4 = max(3, 8) + 1 = 9, a5 = max(9, 7) + 1 = 10). Physically, m5 leaves C at C-clock 1012 + 25 = 1037 and arrives at B-clock 1015 − 40 = 975: a new violation (C's clock is 65 ms ahead of B's, more than the 3 ms delay). Run the modified listing to confirm.
    7. 

  7. (a) realtime (for humans; label the time zone and source); (b) monotonic (a duration on one node); (c) monotonic for measuring the lease's duration on each node, with a safety margin for drift, because the lease is a duration and must not be affected by steps; never compare one node's lease expiry, as a wall-clock time, with another node's clock.
    8. 

  8. Lamport timestamps cannot show causality: L(a) < L(b) is consistent with a and b being concurrent. To tell which write could have caused which you need vector clocks (or explicit dependency information); Lamport timestamps only show which write could not have caused the other.

Forensic lab answer key


1. Clock offsets (θ21 = n2's clock − n1's clock). n1 → n2, m13: sent n1 03.500, received n2 06.509, so θ21 ≤ 3.009 s. n2 → n1, m31: sent n2 16.000, received n1 13.009, so θ21 ≥ 16.000 − 13.009 = 2.991 s. Hence n1's clock is between 2.991 and 3.009 s behind n2's. For n3: m22 (n2 12.500 → n3 13.306) gives θ32 ≤ 0.806 s; m23 (n3 13.306 → n2 12.517) gives θ32 ≥ 13.306 − 12.517 = 0.789 s: n3 is 0.789–0.806 s ahead of n2 R4.


2. True order (n2's clock). n2 became leader at 12.517; n2 accepted x=2 at 14.200; n1 accepted x=1 at n1-clock 12.050, which is n2-clock 15.041–15.059; in the same millisecond n1 processed m21 (vote request), m24, m26 and m28. m28 was sent by n2 at 15.000, consistent with the translation. n1 stepped down at the same corrected time.


3. Two leaders. No: when n1 accepted x=1, n2 had been leader of term 2 for about 2.5 s and had already accepted x=2. From 12.517 to about 15.05 (n2-clock), two nodes believed they were leader; n1 acted on that belief once, by acknowledging x=1. The engineer's conclusion came from merging the logs by wall clock, which placed n1's 12.050 before n2's 12.517 (Figure 2). Ground truth from the simulator: n2 leader at true 09:00:12.517, n1's write at true 09:00:15.050 R5.


4. What happened to n1. n1's last heartbeat was at 06.500 (n1-clock); its next lines are at 12.050, about 5.5 s later, and four messages that arrived over almost three seconds (m21 sent at n2 12.500 to m28 sent at 15.000) were all processed in the same millisecond. n1 was frozen (a process pause: in the simulator, an injected 5 s freeze; in real systems a stop-the-world pause, swapping, or a suspended virtual machine) and then worked through a backlog in arrival order: first the queued client write, still believing it was leader, then the messages that would have told it otherwise. Alternative hypothesis "network partition between n1 and the others": it would not explain the identical timestamps of messages sent seconds apart, nor n1's own silence (no heartbeats sent).


5. Happened-before. "n1 accepts x=1" and "n2 becomes leader" are concurrent: n1 had received no message from n2 before accepting the write (m21 is processed after it). Lamport times would order them arbitrarily (in the simulator's computation, the write has L = 21 and the election L = 26, which says nothing about real time). The question "was n1 still leader at that moment?" is about real time, so it needs the clock-offset reconstruction, or a protocol that makes the question unnecessary.


6. Fixes. (a) A leader acknowledges a write only after a majority has confirmed that its term is still current (in DS302's Raft, a write commits only when a majority of the current term has stored it); n1 could not have reached a majority with term 1. (b) Fencing: every write carries the leader's term, and the storage (or the other replicas) rejects writes with a term lower than the highest seen. (c) Lease-based leadership measured on the monotonic clock with the pause counted, and the leader re-checking its lease after dequeuing each request (helps, but a pause can still fall between check and action, which is why (a) or (b) is needed). Also: process client requests only after processing pending protocol messages.


How the evidence was produced. two_leaders_sim.hpp: discrete-event simulation; heartbeats every 1 s; follower timeouts 2.5 s (n2) and 3.2 s (n3); network delays 5–39 ms from a fixed linear congruential sequence; wall-clock offsets n1 −3.000 s, n3 +0.800 s; injected fault: n1 frozen from true 10.050 to 15.050 s with its incoming messages queued; client writes x=1 to n1 at true 10.200 s and x=2 to n2 at 14.200 s. The protocol is deliberately simplified and is not Raft. Output unedited.
