Story hook

Amara adds a book to the club's catalogue in her copy of the notebook, then walks to the library in her own town to show a friend. It is there. The next day she is in Chidi's town, opens Chidi's copy, and the book is gone. Two days later it is back, because her letter finally arrived. Nobody did anything wrong: the copies were updated in the order the post allowed.

Amara does not care how the post works. She wants to know what the club promises. "Every copy shows everything the moment it is written"? "You always see your own changes"? "You never see the catalogue go backwards"? "All copies agree eventually"? Each promise costs something different, and a club has to choose one and tell its members. That promise is a consistency model.

This chapter defines the main ones precisely, checks real histories against them with a program, and shows which replication settings keep which promise.

What you will be able to do

  • Define linearizability, sequential consistency, causal consistency and eventual consistency, and the session guarantees read-your-writes and monotonic reads.
  • Decide by hand and with a checker whether a history of reads and writes satisfies linearizability or sequential consistency.
  • Explain the quorum rule R + W > N, what it guarantees (overlap) and what it does not (linearizability under concurrency and failures).
  • Choose a consistency model and replication settings for an application requirement, and say what it costs.
  • Diagnose a "vanishing data" report from request logs and identify the violated guarantee.
Jargon box
Consistency model (replicated data)
Simple words: the promise a system makes about what reads can return when data has several copies. Analogy: the club's promise about what a member can find in any copy of the notebook (proposed). Precise: a contract between a replicated data store and its clients that specifies which results of read operations are allowed, given the writes performed and their timing and order; the distributed counterpart of a memory consistency model on one machine (memory consistency model). D2 D1
History (of operations)
Simple words: the list of reads and writes that clients made, with when each started and finished and what it returned. Analogy: every member's diary of when they looked into or wrote in a notebook, and what they saw. Precise: a record of operation invocations and responses with their real-time intervals and results; consistency models are defined as conditions on histories. D8 R1
Linearizability
Simple words: the system behaves as if there were one copy and each operation happened at one instant during its call. Analogy: as if there were only one notebook in the town square, and every reading or writing happened at a single moment while the member was there. Precise: a history is linearizable if there is a total order of its operations that is a legal sequential history of the object and that respects real time: if operation A completed before B was invoked, A precedes B. D8 D1
Causal consistency
Simple words: if one change could have caused another, everyone sees them in that order; unrelated changes can be seen in different orders. Analogy: nobody can read Bao's answer before Amara's question, but two unrelated news items may arrive in either order. Precise: writes that are related by the happened-before relation (F5-10) are seen by every process in that order; concurrent writes may be seen in different orders by different processes. D2 D1
Eventual consistency
Simple words: if people stop changing the data, all copies end up the same, some time later. Analogy: once nobody writes for a while, all the post arrives and the notebooks match. Precise: a liveness guarantee: if no new updates are made to an item, all replicas eventually return the same value; it says nothing about what reads return before then or how long it takes. D1 D6
Session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads)
Simple words: promises to one user: you always see your own changes, and you never see the data go back in time. Analogy: the catalogue Amara opens always has her own entries, and never loses entries she has already seen, whichever town she is in. Precise: client-centric consistency guarantees: read-your-writes requires every read by a client to reflect all of that client's earlier writes; monotonic reads requires that once a client has read a version, its later reads never return an older one. D2 D1
Quorum (read and write quorum)
Simple words: asking enough copies that a read group and a write group must share at least one copy. Analogy: Amara writes in at least two of the three notebooks, and readers look in at least two: one of the two they look in must have her entry. Precise: with N replicas, a write is acknowledged after W replicas store it and a read queries R replicas and takes the highest version; if R + W > N every read set intersects every write set. D6 D1 R2
Stale read
Simple words: reading an old value after a newer one has been written. Analogy: looking in Chidi's notebook before Amara's letter has arrived. Precise: a read that returns a value older than the latest write that completed before the read began; forbidden by linearizability, allowed by weaker models. D1

Sequential consistency is already in the glossary from the memory-model chapters (sequential consistency): one total order of all operations that respects each process's own order. This chapter uses the same definition for replicated data.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

When the club has three notebooks, a member can be told different promises. The strongest: "it is as if there were one notebook in the town square". Weaker: "everyone sees changes in the same order, maybe late". Weaker still: "an answer never arrives before its question". Weakest: "if people stop writing, the notebooks end up the same". There are also personal promises: "you always see what you wrote" and "you never see things go backwards". Mapping: notebook copies = replicas; a member's look into a notebook = a read; the promise = the consistency model; the post = replication lag (F5-12).

Stronger promises are easier to program against but need more letters and more waiting. Weaker promises are fast and keep working when phone lines are down, but members (programs) must cope with surprises.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Histories, and the two "one order" models

A consistency model is a rule about histories: lists of operations, each with an interval [start, end] and a result D8. For a register (one value, initially 0), both strong models ask: is there one total order of all operations in which each read returns the value of the latest write before it? They differ in which orders are allowed D8 D1:

  • Linearizable: the order must respect real time: an operation that ended before another started comes first.
  • Sequentially consistent: the order must respect each client's own order, but may move one client's operations before or after another client's, whatever the clock says.

Weaker models

Causal consistency requires only that writes related by happened-before (F5-10) are seen in that order by everyone; concurrent writes may appear in different orders at different replicas D2. It can be kept by replicas that stay available during a network partition, which linearizability cannot D1. Eventual consistency only promises that replicas converge once writes stop D1; the G-counter of F5-12 is eventually consistent. Between them sit the session guarantees, promises to one client: read-your-writes, monotonic reads, monotonic writes and writes-follow-reads D2. H6 violates read-your-writes; a client who sees version 17 and then 16 violates monotonic reads.

Quorums

In a leaderless store with N replicas, a write goes to all replicas and is acknowledged after W of them store it; a read asks R replicas and keeps the answer with the highest version D6. If R + W > N, every possible read set shares at least one replica with every possible write set. Listing 2 checks every pair of sets: for N = 3 and N = 5, the read sees the latest write in 100.0 % of combinations exactly when R + W > N; with N = 5, W = 2, R = 2 it does in 70.0 % R2. Small W makes writes fast and available; small R makes reads fast; the sum decides freshness.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What linearizability costs

Linearizability makes a replicated object behave like a single one, which is what locks, leader election, unique-name registration and "sell this seat once" need D1. The cost has two parts. Latency: a linearizable operation must, in general, communicate with other replicas before it returns, because a replica on its own cannot know that no newer write has completed elsewhere. Availability: during a network partition, replicas that cannot reach a majority (or the leader) must refuse operations or risk a stale answer; this is the trade-off often called the CAP theorem, which in its precise form says only that a system cannot be both linearizable and available to every non-failed node during a partition D1. Single-leader replication with reads from the leader can be linearizable if the leader is the only leader (fencing, F5-11); reads from asynchronous followers are not D1.

What R + W > N does not give

The overlap argument assumes a write has reached W replicas before the read starts, and that versions are totally ordered. In practice a quorum system can still return stale or non-linearizable results D1:

  • A write that is still in progress may be seen by one reader and not by a later one (H4's pattern) unless readers write back the newest value they saw to a quorum before returning (read repair on the read path).
  • If a write fails on some replicas and succeeds on fewer than W, it is not rolled back where it succeeded; later reads may or may not see it.
  • "Sloppy" quorums that accept writes on stand-in nodes during failures break the overlap D6.
  • Versions decided by wall-clock timestamps inherit clock skew (F5-10): "last write wins" may keep the older write.

Quorums give fresher reads, not linearizability by themselves. Linearizable operations in general need consensus (DS302).

Implementing session guarantees cheaply

Session guarantees do not require replicas to coordinate: the client (or its front end) remembers the highest version it has written or read, and each read is sent to, or waits for, a replica at least that new D2. Routing a user to the same replica ("sticky sessions") gives the same effect until that replica fails. The forensic lab is a missing session guarantee.

Checking histories is expensive

Listing 1 tries all n! orders: fine for 9 operations (H7 in the forensic key), impossible for a production log. Checking linearizability of general histories is NP-complete in general; practical checkers exploit the structure of the object and prune the search D1. Testing tools that record histories of a real store under injected faults and check them are the standard way to find consistency bugs, and the course project's checker (F5-12) is a small instance.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The statements that checking linearizability is NP-complete in general and the precise form of the CAP theorem are given from the author's memory of D1; neither source was opened in this build. Which consistency model a particular database provides, and under which settings, is a product fact to be taken from its documentation for a recorded version (AH-22).

How the hardware actually does it

The same questions arise inside one computer. Cores have caches and store buffers, so a write by one core can be invisible to another for a while; the processor's memory consistency model (HW203, SP203) says which orders are allowed, and fences and atomic operations buy stronger guarantees at a cost in waiting. Sequential consistency was defined for multiprocessors before it was used for replicated stores D2. The difference in scale is large: inside a machine "late" means nanoseconds and the hardware never partitions; between machines it means milliseconds to seconds, and partitions happen (F5-08).

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on real hardware: no replicated store and no network were used; the forensic lab's replica behaviour is scripted. The checker's results are exact for the histories given, but the histories are invented. Real replica lag depends on the network and load and was not measured.

Worked example

Check H4 by hand, then predict the checker's example order. H4: C1 w(1) [0, 10]; C2 r→1 [2, 4]; C3 r→0 [6, 8].

  1. Real-time constraints: an operation that ended before another started must come first. C2's read ended at 4 < 6, so C2:r1 before C3:r0. The write overlaps both reads: no constraint.
  2. Legality: C2:r1 needs w(1) before it; C3:r0 needs no w(1) before it. So w(1) < C2:r1 < C3:r0, but w(1) < C3:r0 makes C3 read 1. No order works: not linearizable.
  3. Sequential consistency: only each client's own order counts, and each client has one operation, so any order is allowed. C3:r0, C1:w1, C2:r1 is legal: sequentially consistent.
  4. The checker tries orders in next_permutation order over the input indices (0 = C1:w, 1 = C2:r, 2 = C3:r) and prints the first legal one: 0,1,2 fails (C3 reads 0 after w1); 0,2,1 fails; 1,0,2 fails (C2 reads 1 before w1); 1,2,0 fails; 2,0,1 is C3:r0 C1:w1 C2:r1, legal. Recorded: "yes, e.g. C3:r0 C1:w1 C2:r1" R1.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A member opening a notebook sees the whole notebook at once. Real reads touch one item, and different items can be at different versions on the same replica.
    • 

  • "As if there were one notebook in the square" sounds like a physical arrangement. Linearizability is a property of histories: a system with many replicas can have it, and a system with one server can lack it (for example with a cache in front).
    • 

  • The club's promise is written once. Real stores often let each operation choose its level (R and W per request), so one application can mix models.

Summary


    

  • A consistency model says which read results are allowed; it is a condition on histories.
    • 

  • Linearizable: one order respecting real time. Sequentially consistent: one order respecting each client's order.
    • 

  • Causal consistency orders only related writes; eventual consistency only promises convergence.
    • 

  • Session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads) are cheap and fix most "vanishing data" reports.
    • 

  • R + W > N makes read and write sets overlap (100.0 % in the exhaustive check), but is not linearizability.
    • 

  • Linearizability costs latency and, during partitions, availability.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Replica states: r1 had version 17 from 01.000; r2 from 01.900; r3 only from 05.100 R3. Ana's reads at 02.300, 02.900 and 04.200 went to r3 before it had 17 and returned 16; her reads at 01.350 and 05.600 went to r1. Ben read r1 at 01.800 and r2 at 03.600, both after those replicas had 17.


Violated guarantees. Ana: read-your-writes (she wrote 17 and read 16) and monotonic reads (she read 17, then 16). Ben: neither, by luck of routing: he never touched r3 before 05.100. The store settings explain it: write_acks=1, read_from=1, so R + W = 2 is not greater than N = 3, and the load balancer sends each request to any replica.


Checker. Run by run.sh on the printed history (with an initial write of 16): "linearizable: no", "sequentially consistent: no" R3. Not sequentially consistent because Ana's own order is w17, r17, r16, and nothing writes 16 after 17. The store is eventually consistent: all three replicas held 17 from 05.100, and every later read returned 17.


Fixes. Client side: sticky sessions (route Ana to r1, the replica she wrote to) or a version token in her session ("at least version 17"), with the front end choosing or waiting for a replica that is new enough; cost: uneven load and a fallback needed when that replica fails. Store side: write_acks=2, read_from=2 (R + W = 4 > 3); cost: every write and read waits for two replicas, and with two of three replicas unreachable, both fail. Ben's experience shows that the bug is per-session: a fix that only tests with one user will not find it.


How the evidence was produced. vanishing.cpp: three replicas with fixed replication delays (900 ms to r2, 4,100 ms to r3, described in the program as r3 catching up after a restart); request routing by a fixed-seed generator, except that three of Ana's requests are placed (01.350 on r1, 02.300 and 04.200 on r3) so that the story is shown. Output unedited; the check output was produced by run.sh from vanishing.out, not copied by hand.
