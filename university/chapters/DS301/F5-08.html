Story hook

Amara started a book-swap club in her town. Members send her letters asking for a book; she posts it to them and writes the loan in her notebook. It works beautifully until two things happen in the same month. First, the club becomes popular: there are more letters than one person can answer in a day, and more books than fit on her shelves. Second, Amara catches the flu for a week, and the whole club stops. Nobody can borrow anything, and nobody even knows whether their letter arrived.

Her friends Bao and Chidi live in two other towns. They offer to help. Bao will keep half the books; Chidi will keep a copy of the notebook so the club can continue when someone is ill. Problem solved? Not quite. Now letters between the three towns can be slow or lost. Bao's copy of the notebook says a book is on the shelf, while Amara's says it was lent yesterday. When Chidi does not hear from Amara for a week, he cannot tell whether she is ill, on holiday, or whether the post office lost her letters.

That month is this course. Spreading work over several machines is how systems grow past one machine and survive one machine's failure. It also creates every problem the next six chapters solve: messages that are slow or lost (F5-09), clocks that disagree (F5-10), failures you cannot see directly (F5-11), copies that drift apart (F5-12, F5-13), and data split across places (F5-14).

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the two main reasons to distribute a system, scale and fault tolerance, and the price paid: partial failure, unreliable messages and no shared clock.
  • Compute the availability of a service built from n machines when it needs all of them, any one of them, or a majority, assuming independent failures, and check the result with a simulation.
  • Predict how fan-out to many servers turns a rare slow server into a common slow request, and read the median and 99th percentile of a latency distribution.
  • Diagnose a correlated failure from outage logs, and explain why it breaks the independence assumption behind "two copies are enough".
  • Decide, for a given workload, whether to distribute at all, and which of the course's later techniques (replication, partitioning) the decision calls for.
Jargon box
Distributed system
Simple words: several computers that work together over a network and look like one service to the people using it. Analogy: the book-swap club run by three friends in three towns. Precise: a collection of independent computers that communicate only by passing messages over a network and coordinate to provide a service; there is no shared memory and no shared clock between them. D2 D1
Node (distributed systems)
Simple words: one computer (or one running program) that is part of a distributed system. Analogy: one friend with her shelf and her notebook. Precise: a participant in a distributed system with its own processor, memory and local clock, reachable only through messages; in this course a node is usually one process. D2
Scalability
Simple words: how well a system keeps up when it gets more work, if you give it more machines. Analogy: whether adding a friend lets the club answer more letters. Precise: the ability of a system to handle a growing load (requests per second, data volume, users) by adding resources, while meeting its performance goals; it must be stated with a load measure and a goal. D1
Fault and failure
Simple words: a fault is something going wrong inside one part; a failure is the whole service not doing its job. Analogy: Amara catching the flu is a fault; the club stopping is a failure. Precise: a fault is a component deviating from its specification; a failure is the system as a whole no longer providing the required service; fault tolerance means preventing faults from becoming failures. D1 D2
Partial failure
Simple words: some machines are broken while others still work, and the working ones may not know which. Analogy: Chidi still answers letters while Amara is ill, and he is not sure she is ill. Precise: a state in which some components of a distributed system have failed and others have not, often without the working ones being able to tell which have failed; one machine on its own usually either works or crashes as a whole. D1
Availability
Simple words: the fraction of time the service works when someone wants to use it. Analogy: the share of days on which the club can lend a book. Precise: the probability that a system is operational at a given moment, or the fraction of a period during which it is; often written as a decimal (0.999) and computed from the availabilities of its parts under stated assumptions. D2
Correlated failure
Simple words: several parts breaking at the same time for the same reason. Analogy: two friends in the same town both cut off by one snowstorm. Precise: failures of several components that are not independent because they share a cause (a power feed, a network switch, a software release, a building); the probability of simultaneous failure is then much higher than the product of the individual probabilities. D1
Fan-out
Simple words: one request that has to ask many servers before it can answer. Analogy: Amara can only reply once all the friends she wrote to have written back. Precise: the number of sub-requests a single request sends in parallel (for example to every partition of the data); the request completes when the slowest needed sub-request completes. D1
Tail latency
Simple words: how slow the slowest few requests are. Analogy: the letters that take two weeks instead of two days. Precise: the high percentiles of the response-time distribution (the 99th percentile, p99: 99 % of requests are at least this fast); see median and percentile and tail of a distribution in MA202. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

One friend with one shelf can only lend so many books, and when she is ill the club stops. Three friends in three towns can lend three times as many books, and the club keeps going when one is ill. But they can only talk by letter (messages), letters can be slow or lost, each friend has her own watch, and nobody can look into another friend's notebook. Mapping: friend = node; letters = messages; the club = the distributed system; a friend being ill = a node failing; nobody knowing for sure who is ill = partial failure.

There are two reasons to split work over many machines. The first is scale: one machine has a fixed amount of memory, disk and processing power, and some jobs need more. The second is surviving failure: if the work can continue on another machine, one broken machine does not stop the service. Both reasons are good. Both come with the same price: the machines must cooperate through a network that does not always deliver, and no machine can see the whole system at once.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Two reasons, and a third that is really a constraint

Scale. When the load (requests per second, bytes stored, users) outgrows one machine, you can buy a bigger machine (scaling up) or use more machines (scaling out). Scaling up keeps the programming model simple but has a ceiling and a price curve set by what hardware exists; scaling out has no single ceiling but makes every operation that touches several machines a distributed operation D1. To talk about scalability honestly, name the load measure and the goal: "this service answers 95 % of requests within 50 ms at up to 20,000 requests per second" is a statement that can be tested; "it scales" is not D1.

Fault tolerance. Every component eventually fails: disks, power supplies, network links, whole buildings, and, most often, software. A distributed design can keep the service running while some of its parts are broken, provided that the remaining parts can take over the work and the data D1 D2. This is the reason a small service with little load may still run on three machines.

Location. Sometimes the users or the data are already in different places (several offices, a sensor network, a phone and a server), so the system is distributed whether you like it or not D2. That case shares all the problems below and is treated the same way in this course.

The price: what a distributed system does not have

A program on one machine can rely on three things that a distributed system lacks D1 D2:

  • Reliable communication. Nodes communicate only by messages. A message can be delayed by an unknown amount, lost, duplicated or delivered out of order. TCP (F5-04) hides loss and reordering inside one connection, but not a crashed peer, a broken connection or a reply that never comes: F5-09 is about exactly that.
  • A shared clock. Each node reads its own clock, and the clocks disagree. You cannot order events on different nodes by their timestamps alone: F5-10.
  • Knowledge of the whole state. No node can look at another node's memory. If node B stops answering, node A cannot tell whether B crashed, is slow, or whether the network between them is broken. This is partial failure, and it is the defining difficulty of the field: F5-11.

Availability arithmetic

Suppose each machine is up with probability a and machines fail independently. Then the probability rules of MA202 give the availability of three designs (Figure 2):

  • All needed (the data is split over n machines with no copies, so every machine must answer): A = an. With a = 0.99, ten machines give 0.904 and a hundred give 0.366 R1. Splitting data without copies makes the service less available than one machine.
  • Any one (every machine holds a full copy and one is enough): A = 1 − (1 − a)n. Three copies give 0.999999 R1.
  • Majority (more than half must be up, the rule that consensus protocols use, DS302): the binomial sum over j > n/2. Three machines give 0.999702, five give 0.9999901 R1.

Real systems combine the patterns: data split into partitions (F5-14), each partition copied to several machines (F5-12). The arithmetic then multiplies: a request that needs 10 partitions, each with majority availability 0.999702, succeeds with probability 0.99970210.

Fan-out and the tail

Latency has the same multiplication problem. If a request must ask k servers in parallel and wait for all of them, it is as slow as the slowest. If each server is slow with probability p independently, the request is slow with probability 1 − (1 − p)k. With p = 0.01, a request that touches 100 servers is slow about 63 % of the time: a hiccup that 1 % of single requests see becomes the normal case R2. This is why distributed systems are judged by their tail latency (p99 and above), not by their average D1.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Independence is an assumption, and usually the weakest one

Every formula above multiplies probabilities, which is valid only for independent events (MA202). Real failures are often correlated: machines in one rack share a power feed and a top-of-rack switch; machines in one building share cooling; machines running the same software release share its bugs; machines administered by the same script share its mistakes D1. The third part of Listing 1 shows the effect: three copies with individual availability 0.99, all behind one power feed that fails with probability q, can never do better than 1 − q. With q = 0.01 the three copies give 0.98999901, worse than the 0.99 of a single machine on a good feed R1. Placement rules ("copies in different racks, different buildings, different software versions rolled out at different times") exist to make the independence assumption closer to true. The forensic lab of this chapter is a case where it was false.

What we assume about nodes and networks

A precise discussion needs a system model: what the network may do to messages, what nodes may do when they fail, and what can be assumed about timing D1 D2. The course builds it up chapter by chapter: messages and their loss in F5-09; timing and clocks in F5-10; failure models (crash, omission, arbitrary) and the synchronous, asynchronous and partially synchronous timing models in F5-11. Keep one consequence in mind from now on: in an asynchronous model, where messages may take arbitrarily long, a node cannot distinguish a crashed peer from a slow one; this impossibility of perfect failure detection underlies the consensus impossibility result met in DS302 D3.

The cost side: coordination

Distribution adds capacity only for work that can be done without much coordination. Anything that needs agreement among nodes (a unique order of updates, a lock, a uniqueness check) costs at least one round trip between them, and often more; those round trips set a floor on latency and a ceiling on throughput that adding machines does not remove D1. The art of the field is to find designs where most operations need no coordination (partitioning, F5-14; weaker consistency, F5-13) and the rest need as little as possible (replication protocols, F5-12; consensus, DS302).

When not to distribute

If one machine holds the data and meets the load and the availability goal (with backups and a tested restore), a single machine is the better design: simpler to reason about, test and operate. Distribute when a stated requirement cannot be met otherwise, and write down which: "the data does not fit", "the load does not fit", "the service must survive the loss of a machine without a restore", or "the users are far apart". Each answer leads to different techniques: partitioning for the first two, replication for the third, both plus careful latency design for the fourth D1.

How the hardware actually does it

The hardware facts behind this chapter come from earlier courses. Nodes communicate through network interface controllers that place frames on a link (HW205, F5-03); packets cross switches and may be dropped when a queue is full (F5-01, F5-07); each node keeps time with its own oscillator-driven counters (HW204, OS302), which is why clocks drift apart (F5-10). Failure probabilities of real parts (disk, power supply, server) are properties of named products and real fleets, documented by their makers or measured by their operators; this chapter does not state any.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The value a = 0.99 and the hiccup probability p = 0.01 used in the listings are exercise values, not measurements of any machine, network or product (AH-21, AH-22). Real component failure rates and real latency distributions must come from a named product's documentation or from your own fleet's measurements. All experiments in this chapter are simulations on one computer; nothing was run on a real cluster. Untested on real hardware: the effect of correlated failures and of fan-out on a real multi-machine service; to test it you need several machines (or virtual machines, DS303) and a fault-injection tool.

Worked example

A search service splits its index over 20 partitions; each query asks all 20 and waits for all answers. Each partition is served by one machine with availability 0.999, and each machine answers slowly (above 100 ms) in 0.5 % of queries. (a) What is the availability of the service? (b) What fraction of queries is slow? (c) The team adds a second copy of every partition, and a query uses whichever copy answers; what changes?

  1. (a) All 20 partitions are needed: A = 0.99920. Using ln(0.999) ≈ −0.0010005, 20 × −0.0010005 = −0.02001, e−0.02001 ≈ 0.98019. The service is down about 2 % of the time, twenty times as often as one machine.
  2. (b) Slow if any partition is slow: 1 − 0.99520. ln(0.995) ≈ −0.0050125; × 20 = −0.10025; e−0.10025 ≈ 0.90461. About 9.5 % of queries are slow although each machine is slow only 0.5 % of the time.
  3. (c) With two independent copies per partition, a partition is available with 1 − 0.0012 = 0.999999, and the service with 0.99999920 ≈ 0.99998. If the query sends to both copies and takes the first answer, a partition is slow only if both copies are slow: 0.0052 = 0.000025, and the query is slow with 1 − (1 − 0.000025)20 ≈ 0.0005. The price: twice the machines and twice the requests.
  4. Check against the lab model: with the same formula, Listing 2 gives 1 − 0.9910 = 0.0956 for k = 10 and p = 0.01, and the simulation gives 0.0948 R2. The independence assumption is the same in both, and (c) holds only if the two copies of a partition do not share the cause of their slowness (the same overloaded switch, the same pause).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Friends who fall ill usually recover and remember everything in their notebook. A crashed node may lose everything that was only in its memory, and when it restarts it may come back with an old copy of the data, which is more dangerous than staying away (F5-12).
    • 

  • The post office loses letters but rarely delivers the same letter twice or delivers Monday's letter after Tuesday's. Networks do duplicate and reorder messages (the registered "where it breaks" note of the letters mapping), and protocols must be written for that.
    • 

  • Friends can phone each other to check. Nodes have no channel other than messages, and that channel is the one that is failing; there is no independent way to "look" (F5-11).
    • 

  • Three friends feel like plenty. In real systems the number of nodes can be large enough that some are always failing; "a failure" is then not an event but a permanent background condition.
    •

Summary


    

  • Systems are distributed for scale (more than one machine can handle) and for fault tolerance (continue when a machine fails); sometimes because the users or data are already apart.
    • 

  • The price: messages instead of shared memory, no shared clock, and partial failure that other nodes cannot observe directly.
    • 

  • Assuming independence, a design that needs all n machines has availability an; one that needs any one copy has 1 − (1 − a)n; one that needs a majority has the binomial sum.
    • 

  • Fan-out makes rare slow servers into common slow requests: 1 − (1 − p)k; judge services by p99, not the mean.
    • 

  • Correlated failures break the multiplication: copies that share a cause are only as good as that cause allows.
    • 

  • Distribute only for a stated requirement; each requirement points to replication, partitioning or both.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Prediction. From the summary, m1 is unavailable 9/8,760 ≈ 0.00103 and m2 8/8,760 ≈ 0.00091 of the time. Independent failures predict 0.00103 × 0.00091 × 8,760 ≈ 0.008 hours of service downtime per year, roughly 30 seconds R3.


Observation. The service was down 5 hours in three outages (days 044, 161 and 276), about 600 times the prediction. Bad luck is not plausible.


Root cause. At each of the three outages, m1, m2 and m3 went down in the same hour for the same duration (day 044 21:00 for 1 h, day 161 00:00 for 3 h, day 276 16:00 for 1 h), and no machine of rack B was affected. m1, m2 and m3 are rack A. The evidence points to a shared cause in rack A: the power feed (the injected fault in outages.cpp: rack A's feed fails on three days), or its switch. Competing hypothesis "a software release that crashed m1 and m2" is ruled out because m3, which does not run the orders service, failed at the same moments, and rack B machines did not. Every other outage of m1 or m2 was alone and did not cause a service outage.


Fix and verification. Move one copy to rack B (for example to m4). Re-run the analysis on the next period: service outages should then require coincident independent failures; with rack-A-style events, the service should stay up. A direct test: in a staging environment, cut power to one rack and check that the service continues.


Prevention. Make "copies in different failure domains" a placement rule checked by the deployment tool; list shared infrastructure (power, switch, building, software release) in the design document; monitor for simultaneous failures of copies. The assumption violated is independence (Listing 1, the multiplication); it also hides in identical software releases, shared configuration, shared dependencies (one DNS server, one authentication service) and operator actions applied to all copies at once.
