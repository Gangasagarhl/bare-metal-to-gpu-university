Story hook

The club now keeps three copies of its notebook, one in each town, so that losing one notebook loses nothing. Amara is the one who writes new loans; she posts each change to Bao and Chidi. On a busy Saturday she decides to post the changes in a bundle at the end of the day, which saves stamps. That evening her house floods. The notebook is ruined, and the day's bundle was never posted. Bao takes over, with a notebook that ends on Friday. On Sunday, Bao lends out the "free" copies of three books that Amara had already lent on Saturday.

Next time, Amara posts each change before she tells the member "it's yours", and waits for Bao's postcard saying "copied". It is slower, but now nothing she promised can be lost with her notebook. There is a new problem, though: if the flood comes after Bao copied a change but before the member heard "it's yours", the member asks again, and Bao, now in charge, writes the loan twice.

Each way of keeping copies fails in its own way. This chapter builds the main designs, breaks each one deliberately, and shows the fix for each break. The lab's counter is the smallest possible "notebook"; the course project grows it into a key-value store.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why systems replicate data (availability, durability, read capacity, latency) and the three main architectures: single-leader (primary-backup), multi-leader and leaderless.
  • Compare synchronous and asynchronous replication by what each loses in a failover, using the lab's recorded results.
  • Implement a replicated counter in seven designs and explain each failure: duplicate increments, lost updates, divergence, lost acknowledged writes, double application after failover.
  • Use operation ids, idempotent merges (a G-counter) and anti-entropy to make replicas converge.
  • Diagnose a failover that lost acknowledged writes from the logs of the primary, the backup and the client.
Jargon box
Replication
Simple words: keeping copies of the same data on several computers. Analogy: everyone's copy of the shared notebook (registered F5 mapping). Precise: maintaining multiple copies (replicas) of data on different nodes and propagating every change to all of them, so that the data survives node failures and can be read from several places. D1 D2
Replica
Simple words: one of the copies. Analogy: Bao's copy of the notebook. Precise: a node that stores a copy of some data and applies the changes propagated to it. D1
Primary-backup replication
Simple words: one computer accepts all changes and copies them to the others. Analogy: the friend who writes the order (registered: leader), posting each change to the others. Precise: single-leader replication: one replica, the primary (leader), executes all writes and sends the resulting changes, in order, to the backups (followers); if the primary fails, a backup is promoted (failover). D1 D2
Synchronous and asynchronous replication
Simple words: waiting for the copies before saying "done", or saying "done" first and copying afterwards. Analogy: waiting for Bao's "copied" postcard before telling the member "it's yours", or posting the day's bundle in the evening. Precise: synchronous: the primary acknowledges a write to the client only after the backups (all, or a chosen number) confirm they have it; asynchronous: the primary acknowledges after its local write and propagates later, so acknowledged writes can be lost if it fails. D1 R1
Replication lag
Simple words: how far behind a copy is. Analogy: how many days of loans are still in the post to Chidi. Precise: the delay (in time or in number of changes) between a write being applied at the primary and at a given replica; with asynchronous replication it is unbounded in principle. D1
Failover
Simple words: switching to a copy when the main computer fails. Analogy: Bao taking over the notebook after the flood. Precise: the procedure of detecting the primary's failure (F5-11), choosing a new primary (preferably the most up-to-date replica), redirecting clients and fencing the old primary. D1
Lost update
Simple words: a change that disappears because someone else overwrote it with a value computed from an older state. Analogy: two friends both read "5 books", both write "6", and one of the two new books is forgotten. Precise: an anomaly in which two read-modify-write cycles interleave and the second write overwrites the first without incorporating it. D1 R1
CRDT (G-counter)
Simple words: a kind of data that copies can change independently and always merge into the same result. Analogy: each friend counts only her own loans on her own line, and copies of the page are combined by keeping the biggest number on each line. Precise: a conflict-free replicated data type: a data type whose replicas can be updated without coordination and whose merge is commutative, associative and idempotent, so replicas that have seen the same updates converge; a grow-only counter (G-counter) keeps one count per replica, merges by element-wise maximum, and reads the sum. D7 R1
Anti-entropy
Simple words: copies regularly comparing notes and filling in what they are missing. Analogy: friends swapping their notebooks once a month to copy anything they missed. Precise: a background process in which replicas exchange state (or summaries of it) and merge, so that updates lost or missed in transit eventually reach every replica. D6 D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

Keeping copies of the club notebook in three towns means losing one notebook loses nothing, and members can look things up in their own town. But the copies have to be kept the same. Mapping: notebook copy = replica; the friend who writes the order = primary (leader); posting changes = replication messages; waiting for "copied" before saying "done" = synchronous replication; posting a bundle in the evening = asynchronous replication; Bao taking over after the flood = failover.

Copies are only as good as the way changes reach them. If you say "done" before the copies have the change, a crash can lose something you promised. If you wait, you are slower, and you still have to deal with questions that are asked twice. If everyone may write in their own copy, copies can disagree, and you need a rule for combining them that never loses anything.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why replicate, and the three architectures

Replication keeps copies of the same data on several nodes to keep the data available when nodes fail, to avoid losing it (durability), to serve more reads than one node can, and to place data near its users D1. If the data never changed, replication would be copying files. All the difficulty is in handling changes D1. Three architectures exist D1:

  • Single-leader (primary-backup). One primary orders and executes all writes and sends the changes to the backups in that order. Reads may go to the primary (fresh) or to backups (possibly stale). Simple, and the order of writes is defined by one node. The rest of this chapter's lab rows 5–7 and the course project use it.
  • Multi-leader. Several nodes accept writes (for example one per data centre) and exchange them. Concurrent writes to the same item on different leaders conflict and must be resolved.
  • Leaderless. Clients write to several replicas directly and read from several, using quorums (F5-13) and background repair. Conflicts are possible as in multi-leader systems.

Synchronous versus asynchronous

With synchronous replication, the primary acknowledges a write only after the backup has it; a failover to that backup therefore loses no acknowledged write (rows 6–7). The cost: every write waits for a round trip to the backup, and if the backup is slow or down, writes stall unless the system drops it from the synchronous set D1. With asynchronous replication, writes are fast and independent of backups, but every write still in flight when the primary fails is lost even though the client was told "done" (row 5) D1 R1. A common middle way makes one backup synchronous and the rest asynchronous, so that at least two nodes have every acknowledged write D1.

Retries cross the failover

Row 6 is subtle: synchronous replication guarantees the backup has op 71, but the client never heard "ok" and resends it to the new primary. The duplicate-detection state of F5-09 (op ids, reply caches) must itself be replicated with the data, so that the new primary recognises the resend (row 7) R1. "Exactly-once effects" survive a failover only if the memory of what was done moves with the data.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What to send: statements, operations or state

A primary can send backups different things D1: the client's request (re-executed on each backup, dangerous for anything non-deterministic: current time, random numbers, iteration order), a deterministic description of the change (an operation log: "key k: value v", "counter: +1 as op 71"), or the resulting state (whole objects or pages). An ordered log of deterministic changes, applied in the same order by every backup, is the usual choice: it makes backups identical to the primary at the same log position, and a backup's log position measures its lag. This is the replicated log of the registered analogy ("everyone's copy of the shared notebook: same entries in the same order"), and DS302 makes it fault-tolerant with consensus.

Failover, done carefully

A failover has four steps, each with its own trap D1:

  1. Detect that the primary failed: a timeout (F5-11), with its false suspicions.
  2. Choose the new primary: the replica with the longest log loses least; with asynchronous replication, whatever it lacks is lost.
  3. Reconfigure clients and other replicas to use it; the new primary must continue numbering (ticket numbers, op ids, log positions) after everything that was handed out, not after what it happens to have (the forensic lab).
  4. Fence the old primary, so that if it was only paused it cannot keep accepting writes (F5-11), and decide what happens to its unreplicated writes when it returns (usually: discard them, which must be reported, not silent).

Conflicts in multi-leader and leaderless systems

When two replicas accept writes to the same item concurrently, the system must resolve the conflict. "Last writer wins" by timestamp silently discards one write, and with skewed clocks (F5-10) it may discard the later one D1. Version vectors (F5-10) detect that two versions are concurrent, so that both can be kept and merged by the application D6. Conflict-free replicated data types go further: if the data type's merge is commutative, associative and idempotent, replicas can accept updates independently and always converge once they have exchanged states D7. The G-counter (row 4) is the smallest example; a counter that can also decrement keeps two G-counters (increments and decrements) and reads their difference. CRDTs do not remove every problem: a G-counter cannot enforce "never more than 500 tickets", because no replica knows the global count at the moment of an increment. Invariants that span replicas need coordination.

Replication lag and what readers see

Reading from asynchronous backups spreads read load, but each backup is somewhere behind the primary. A user can write and then fail to see the write; read a newer value and then an older one from a different backup; or see an answer before the question it replies to D1. These anomalies, and the guarantees that rule them out, are the subject of F5-13.

Not verified — check before relying on this

This chapter describes architectures, not products. Which replication modes a particular database or storage system offers, how it chooses a new primary, how it fences the old one and what it does with unreplicated writes are product facts that must be taken from that product's documentation for a recorded version; none was opened in this build. The 10 % loss and 5 % duplication rates of Listing 1 are exercise values, not measurements of a network (AH-22).

How the hardware actually does it

"The backup has the write" means different things depending on where the write is. In memory, it is lost if the backup loses power too. Written to a file, it may still sit in the operating system's page cache; it reaches the storage device only after an explicit flush (fsync on Linux, OS304, OS401), and even then the device's own write cache matters (HW205). A synchronous replication protocol must state which of these the acknowledgement promises; acknowledging from memory on two machines in different failure domains (F5-08) is a deliberate trade-off against flushing to disk on one.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on real hardware: every replica in this chapter is a variable in one process; no write was made durable on a storage device and no message crossed a network. The durability semantics of fsync and of device write caches must be checked in the POSIX specification, the Linux manual pages and the device's documentation (OS304 and OS401 cover crash consistency on one machine). The course project's failure injection runs real processes on one machine; running it across machines is untested in this build.

Worked example

Predict the asynchronous row (5) of counter.out by hand, then change the batch size.

  1. The primary acknowledges each op at once and ships its pending ops after ops 8, 16, 24, …, 64 (multiples of 8, line 153).
  2. The crash happens after op 70 was acknowledged. The last shipment was after op 64; ops 65–70 are pending: 70 − 64 = 6 ops.
  3. The backup has 64; after promotion the client sends ops 71–100 (30 ops) to it: 64 + 30 = 94. The recorded row says 94, and "6 increments were acknowledged … but had not reached the backup" R1.
  4. With batches of b ops and a crash after op n, the loss is n mod b (between 0 and b − 1). For b = 5: 70 mod 5 = 0, nothing lost in this run, by luck of timing, not by design; for b = 32: 70 mod 32 = 6; for b = 50: 20.
  5. Worst case: b − 1 acknowledged ops lost. Shipping more often reduces the expected loss but never makes it zero; only waiting for the backup before acknowledging does (rows 6–7).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A flooded notebook is plainly ruined. A crashed primary may come back with its unreplicated writes intact on disk, and then two histories exist that must be reconciled or one discarded.
    • 

  • Friends who disagree about a loan can talk it over. Replicas cannot "talk it over" unless the data type or the application defines how to merge; "keep the latest" by timestamp silently loses data.
    • 

  • A postcard "copied" means Bao wrote it in ink. A replica's acknowledgement may mean only "in memory": the analogy hides the durability question (How the hardware actually does it).
    • 

  • The shared notebook of the registered analogy has "the same entries in the same order" by agreement. Keeping that order when the leader fails is not automatic at all: it is the consensus problem of DS302.

Summary


    

  • Replicate for availability, durability, read capacity and locality; the difficulty is entirely in propagating changes.
    • 

  • Single-leader replication gives one order of writes; multi-leader and leaderless replication accept concurrent writes and must resolve conflicts.
    • 

  • Asynchronous replication can lose acknowledged writes in a failover (6 in the lab); synchronous replication does not, at the cost of waiting.
    • 

  • Retries cross failovers: the duplicate-detection state must be replicated with the data.
    • 

  • Read-modify-write of values loses updates; send operations, or use compare-and-set or a CRDT.
    • 

  • A G-counter's max-merge converges despite loss and duplication; invariants across replicas still need coordination.
    • 

  • Failover: detect, choose the most up-to-date replica, continue numbering safely, and fence the old primary.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. The client got "OK" for 1041–1054 from the primary (19:30:00.184–03.101) and for 1051–1053 again from the promoted backup (04.871–05.291). The primary shipped 1041–1045 and 1046–1050; the backup applied exactly those. Tickets 1051–1054 were acknowledged but never shipped: the primary's log ends at 03.167 with them pending R2.


Seats sold twice. The backup was promoted with "last applied ticket 1050" and continued numbering at 1051, re-issuing ticket numbers 1051–1053, which map to the same seats 281–283. Ticket 1054 (seat 284) is lost: its buyer has a ticket the system no longer knows about, and the seat will be sold again.


Hypotheses. (1) "The backup crashed or missed messages": ruled out, the backup's log shows both batches applied promptly, and no batch for 1051–1054 was ever sent (the primary's log has no "shipped" line after 1050). (2) "Asynchronous replication with batching": confirmed by the configuration (mode = async, ship_every_n_changes = 5, acknowledge_client_after = local_write) and by the gap between acknowledgements and shipments. Root cause: acknowledged writes lived only on the primary when it crashed; failover then reused their numbers.


Fixes. Acknowledge after the backup has the change (synchronous replication to at least one backup): every ticket the box office was told about survives; cost: each sale waits one round trip to the backup and stalls if it is unavailable. Failover procedure: before accepting sales, the new primary should determine the highest ticket number ever issued (for example from the clients' acknowledgements, or by reserving number ranges durably on both nodes) and refuse to reuse numbers; and the failover report should list "acknowledged but not replicated" writes as lost, rather than reporting success.


Sentence to the vendor. "The failover was fast, but in asynchronous mode it silently discarded four acknowledged sales and reissued three of their ticket numbers; a failover is only correct if every acknowledged write survives it or is reported as lost."


How the evidence was produced. failover.cpp: a scripted scenario (no random numbers): 14 sales on the primary, a shipment after every fifth ticket number, a crash 70 ms after the last sale, promotion of the backup after 1.5 s, three further sales on the backup. Output unedited.
