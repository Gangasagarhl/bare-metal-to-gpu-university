Story hook

Noor has a summer job as a bicycle courier. Before her first real delivery, the dispatcher makes her ride the route on a trainer in the depot, with the map on a screen, until she reaches every address without a wrong turn. Then a coach rides behind her through quiet streets for a week, with one rule: when the coach shouts "stop", she stops, at once, wherever she is. Only after that does she ride the route alone, and every evening she compares her delivery times and her wrong turns with what the trainer predicted.

This mega project is that summer, for a robot. You build its whole software stack, from the microcontroller that turns the wheels to the task that carries a cup from the kitchen to the bedroom. You prove it on a simulator first, then on a real kit robot with a supervisor's hand on the stop button, and finally you show, with logs, how far the real robot differs from its simulated twin and why.

What you will be able to do

  • Integrate the pieces you built in RB302, RB303, RB304, RB401 and RB403 into one robot software stack that completes a delivery task in simulation, measured against ground truth.
  • Meet a real-time requirement that you fixed in advance, on a Linux computer with PREEMPT_RT, and prove it with measurements, not with an argument alone.
  • Write microcontroller firmware on an RTOS that talks to the robot computer and keeps the motors safe when that link fails.
  • Build a reproducible OS image for the robot computer that boots into the stack and refuses to arm until its safety parts are ready.
  • Run the same task on a supervised kit robot after a readiness review, and explain each difference between the real and the simulated logs.
  • Argue that the robot is acceptably safe for its lab use in a safety case whose every claim points to a test you ran, and defend the whole project in front of reviewers.
Jargon box
Task success rate
Simple words: how many of the attempted deliveries really ended at the right place. Analogy: the parcels that reached the right door, counted by the customer, not by the courier. Precise: the number of task runs, out of a fixed number set in advance, in which every stop was reached within the task tolerance as measured by an independent reference (ground truth in simulation, a measured mark on the floor on hardware), with no collision and within the time limit; this handbook's definition. C1 R2
Absolute trajectory error (ATE)
Simple words: on average, how far the robot's belief about its position was from where it really was. Analogy: how far the dot on your phone's map was from where you actually stood, averaged over the whole ride. Precise: here, the root mean square of the distance between the estimated and the true position, sampled every 0.5 s after the first 5 s of a run, in the map frame; in this lab both come from the simulator, so no alignment step is needed; this handbook's definition, after the RMS error of F9-34. R1 R9
Log schema
Simple words: the fixed list of columns that every run's log has, whether it came from the simulator or the real robot. Analogy: the same delivery sheet used on the trainer and on the street, so the two can be laid side by side. Precise: a versioned contract that names each logged field, its unit, frame and rate; here t, leg, state, x_true, y_true, th_true, x_est, y_est, th_est, sd_x, sd_y, sd_th, v_cmd, w_cmd, v_true at 2 Hz, where the *_true fields come from the simulator or from an external reference on hardware. R1
Paired run
Simple words: the same task, with the same settings, run once in simulation and once on the real robot, so that differences can be blamed on the robot and not on a changed setting. Analogy: riding the same route on the trainer and on the street on the same day, with the same bicycle settings. Precise: two runs that share the software version, configuration file, task, start pose and log schema, differing only in the plant (simulator or kit robot); the unit of the sim-to-real analysis of milestone M5; this handbook's definition. C1
Stopping distance
Simple words: how far the robot still rolls after it is told to stop. Analogy: the length of road you need after squeezing the brakes. Precise: the distance the robot's reference point travels from the moment a stop is commanded until both wheels are still; for a drive that ramps each wheel down at a constant deceleration a from speed v, the ramp alone gives v²/(2a) per wheel; reaction delays add their own distance. W1 R5
Surviving mutant
Simple words: a deliberately planted fault that your tests did not notice. Analogy: a puncture the pre-ride check did not find. Precise: in mutation testing, a modified version of the program (here a removed safety mechanism or a wrong calibration value) for which every test still passes; it shows a property the suite does not check, and is either killed by a new test or recorded with a reason. R2

Terms you already know and will use constantly: extended Kalman filter, innovation gate, NIS and NEES (RB302); costmap, A*, pure pursuit, behaviour tree (RB401); jitter requirement, PREEMPT_RT, hardware interface, micro-ROS (RB304); robot OS image, software watchdog, latched safe state, sim-to-real gap (RB403); safety case, hazard log (SS402); readiness review, walking skeleton (SE501); mutation testing (DR401).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot that delivers a cup is a body with senses and muscles and a small brain that plans the route. In this project you build every part of that brain, check it on a pretend robot inside the computer, then let it drive a real robot slowly while a grown-up keeps a finger on the big red stop button. At the end you compare the pretend rides with the real rides and explain every difference.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What you build

The card names six milestones C1. Read together, they describe one robot stack, built bottom-up on paper and outside-in in practice:

  1. M1, simulated robot. Simulated drivers, EKF localisation, navigation and one manipulation or delivery task, measured against the simulator's ground truth.
  2. M2, real-time control loop. A control loop on Linux with PREEMPT_RT that meets a jitter requirement you measured, behind a ros2_control hardware interface.
  3. M3, firmware. micro-ROS firmware with an RTOS on the motor or sensor microcontroller.
  4. M4, OS image. Your own image for the robot computer, booting into the stack.
  5. M5, sim-to-real. The same task on the kit robot, supervised, with logs compared to simulation.
  6. M6, safety case. E-stop, watchdog and safe-state tests, and the argument that ties them together.

Nothing here is new in isolation; every piece was a chapter or a course project. The project is the integration: the EKF of F9-34 now steers the navigator of F9-56, whose commands pass through the supervisor of F9-69 into the control framework of F9-50, which talks to the firmware of F9-51 on a board started by the image of F9-67. Integration is where the interfaces you never had to agree on bite: units, frames, rates, timestamps, start-up order and who owns the motors when something fails.

M2 — Real-time control loop with PREEMPT_RT and a ros2_control hardware interface

Entry criteria. R2 passed; the jitter requirement written with numbers at R1, derived from what the control loop tolerates D9; the robot computer (or the faculty's reference PC) runs a PREEMPT_RT kernel whose configuration you checked at run time, as in F9-47.

Tasks.

  1. Write the hardware interface for the kit (start from the F9-50 framework and port it to ros2_control) with the read–update–write cycle, no allocation in the loop and the priorities of F9-49.
  2. Until R3, run it against a plant model on the microcontroller or on a second process (hardware-in-the-loop), with the motor drivers' power disconnected.
  3. Measure the loop's periods and its compute time under the declared load: navigation, localisation, logging and network traffic all running.
  4. Repeat the measurement with the load and without PREEMPT_RT (or without CPU isolation) to show what each measure buys.

Evidence. Latency histograms and the requirement table with PASS or FAIL for each line, labelled with the machine, kernel, tool version and date (AH-23); the configuration files; the load script. The F9-50 lab plan shows the form of such a requirement: at 1 kHz, at least 99.9 % of periods within 1000 ± 100 µs, no period longer than 2000 µs, and the loop's own work under 200 µs. On the build container, a virtual machine without PREEMPT_RT, the same loop failed the first two lines (96.74 % within the band; 9 periods over 2000 µs) and passed the third R6. That is why M2's evidence must come from the real robot computer, not from a laptop or a shared VM.

Builds on. F9-45 to F9-50; F9-66.

M3 — micro-ROS firmware with an RTOS on the motor or sensor microcontroller

Entry criteria. R2 passed; the kit's microcontroller board, its reference manual and a debug probe available; the RTOS (Zephyr or FreeRTOS, as OS305 taught) and the micro-ROS release chosen at R1, with the board on the documentation's supported list (checked, not assumed).

Safety

The software supervisor of the starter lab and of F9-69 is a teaching model, not a safety function. On the kit robot, the emergency stop is a hard-wired chain that removes motor power with no software in its path, and it is tested at the start of every session. Standards that govern emergency stops and machine safety (ISO 13850, IEC 60204-1, ISO 13849-1) are named only as reading references: none was opened in this build and no requirement of theirs is claimed D8. Electrical work on the e-stop chain or the battery wiring is done or checked by a qualified person named in the R3 record; no mains-voltage work at any level of this university.

How the hardware actually does it

Three pieces of hardware decide whether the stack works on the kit, and the simulator models none of them faithfully.

The robot computer and its timers. A PREEMPT_RT kernel makes most of the kernel preemptible and moves interrupt handlers into threads with priorities, so that a high-priority control thread can run soon after its timer fires D4. How soon depends on the specific processor, its firmware and its peripherals, which is why the requirement must be measured on that computer. RB304's measurements on the build container show the opposite case: without PREEMPT_RT, on a shared virtual machine, a 1 kHz loop missed its band in more than three periods per hundred R6.

The microcontroller. It runs the wheel speed loop next to the motor drivers with interrupt latencies set by its own core and RTOS, not by Linux. That is the reason for the split of Figure 1: the fast, simple loop sits where timing is easiest to guarantee, and it keeps a watchdog of its own C2.

The e-stop chain and the drive. On the real robot, pressing the e-stop opens a circuit that removes motor power; the motors then stop as their drivers and the robot's inertia allow, which is not the gentle ramp of the simulator. The kit maker's documents give the drivers' behaviour on loss of enable or power; the stopping distance on the floor must be measured, not computed from the simulator's 0.8 m/s² D7.

Untested on hardware

Nothing in this handbook ran on a robot computer, microcontroller, motor, battery or e-stop: the build container has none. The starter lab's robot is a C++ model; its numbers (wheel radius 0.050 m, track 0.300 m, 1024 counts per revolution, ramp 0.8 m/s², speed limit 0.30 m/s, watchdog 0.25 s) are the lab plan's choices and describe no kit. M2–M5 and the hardware goals of M6 need the kit, the robot computer and a supervisor; they are untested in this build.

Worked example

Question. In the suite's e-stop scenario the button is pressed at 20.00 s. Predict the time until both wheels are still and the stopping distance, and check the prediction against the run. Then predict the watchdog scenario's detection time.

Step 1 — the state at the press. The nominal log m1_log.csv (same seed, identical up to the press) has, at t = 20.00, v_cmd = 0.300 m/s and w_cmd = 0.573 rad/s: the robot is turning toward the kitchen door R1.

Step 2 — wheel speeds. With track b = 0.300 m, the wheels move at v ∓ ωb/2 = 0.300 ∓ 0.573 × 0.150, that is 0.214 and 0.386 m/s W1.

Step 3 — stop time. The supervisor commands zero in the same 10 ms cycle (check E1: 0.000 s). Each wheel ramps down at a = 0.8 m/s², so the faster one needs 0.386 / 0.8 = 0.482 s. The suite measured 0.48 s (E2). Had the robot been driving straight, 0.300 / 0.8 = 0.375 s R5.

Step 4 — stopping distance. Each wheel rolls v²/(2a); the centre travels the mean of the two: (0.214² + 0.386²)/(4 × 0.8) = 0.061 m with a continuous ramp, and 0.059 m when the ramp is applied in 10 ms steps as the simulator does. The suite measured 0.059 m (E3) R5 R2. Straight at 0.300 m/s it would have been 0.056 m: turning lengthens the stop, because the outer wheel is faster than the robot.

Step 5 — the watchdog. The navigator ticks every 50 ms; its last command before the stall at 30.00 s is stamped 29.95 s. The supervisor locks at the first 10 ms cycle in which the command is older than 0.25 s: at 30.21 s, which the run prints as "newest command is 0.26 s old"; the suite's W1 reports 0.21 s after the stall began. The worst case after the last good command is the watchdog plus one supervisor period, 0.26 s R2 R5.

What this shows. Every limit in the suite (E2 0.6 s, E3 0.12 m, W1 0.27 s) can be derived from the design's numbers with a margin; a limit you cannot derive is a guess. On the real robot the same arithmetic gives a prediction that the bench measurement in M5 must confirm, with the real drive instead of the simulator's ramp.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Noor can feel that she is lost; a robot cannot. Its filter can be confidently wrong, as the forensic lab shows: the estimate's standard deviation said 2 cm while the truth was 20 cm away. Success must be judged from outside the robot.
    • 

  • Noor's coach can shout "stop" and Noor decides to stop. The robot's e-stop must not depend on the robot deciding anything: it removes motor power through a circuit with no software in it.
    • 

  • Noor's trainer and street differ in ways she can name. A simulator's differences from the kit are partly unknown until measured; M5 exists to find them, and some (contact, friction, timing) cannot be named in advance.
    • 

  • Noor learns the route once. The stack must be re-verified after every change: a gate passed on an old build says nothing about a new one.
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. The navigator can only act on what the robot knows, its estimate; a success test that used the estimate would repeat the robot's own mistake (in the forensic run the robot "arrived" 0.285 m from the stop). The true pose comes from the simulator; on the kit robot, an independent reference plays its role: measured floor marks at each stop, or a tracking system if available.
    2. 

  2. The mutant changes only what happens after the e-stop is released. Pressing still locks at once (E1), the ramp still stops the wheels (E2, E3). Only E4 looks at the time between release (23 s) and the accepted reset (26 s), when the mutant drives off. Each check tests one property; this is why a scenario needs several.
    3. 

  3. (a) With posts in view at 5 Hz, the filter corrects the odometry continually; the estimate stays good and the stops are reached. (b) Not wrong, but blind to calibration: it checks the task and the safety mechanisms, not the odometry. (c) M5 must measure calibration with an external reference, and the mini-project adds checks (for example dead reckoning over a leg with updates off) that kill this mutant. A real robot may cross areas with few landmarks, where the hidden error appears.
    4. 

  4. Straight: 0.20/0.8 = 0.25 s and 0.20²/(2 × 0.8) = 0.025 m. Both are well inside E2 (0.6 s) and E3 (0.12 m), so the limits remain valid (they could be tightened, at a gate). The run takes longer, so check the time limit.
    5. 

  5. Prediction: the last command before the stall is stamped 29.95 s; the supervisor locks at the first cycle where the age exceeds 0.10 s, about 30.05–30.06 s, so W1 reports about 0.05–0.06 s; the wheels stop about 0.4 s later. The navigator publishes every 0.05 s, below 0.10 s, so the nominal seeds should still pass. A watchdog shorter than the publisher's period plus its delay would trip in nominal runs. Run it to check.
    6. 

  6. For a consistent filter, the NIS of a two-dimensional reading follows a chi-square distribution with two degrees of freedom, whose tail is P(NIS > g) = e−g/2; g = −2 ln 0.01 = 9.21 gives 1 % R5. In the fix run, 5 of 851 good readings were rejected, the same order as the 8.5 expected. A robot that is truly lost has large innovations for every reading, so the gate rejects all of them and the filter never recovers; it needs a monitor of the rejection rate and a recovery (relocalisation, stop and alarm).
