Story hook

At the school fair, Lina carries the tray and a small team keeps her going. Tomás refills glasses whenever one is empty. Aiko keeps the route clear and tells Lina where to turn. Kwame, at the table, takes money and writes the day's notes. None of them has one big job: each has a list of quick jobs and works through it in order, top to bottom, one at a time. Tomás's list is short and urgent ("glass 3 is empty"); Kwame's is long and can wait.

The system works well until someone, trying to help, adds "wash all the spare cups" to Tomás's list. It is a perfectly good job. But it takes ten minutes, and Tomás does his jobs one at a time. For ten minutes no glass is refilled, and Lina stands in the hall with an empty tray. Nobody broke anything; a job was simply put on the wrong list.

PX4 organises its software in the same way. It is built from many modules, and most of their regular work is done by a few shared threads, the work queues, each working through its list of quick jobs. In this chapter you draw PX4's architecture, then model work queues on one processor, measure what each job waits for, and find out what happens when a long job is put on the wrong list.

What you will be able to do

  • Draw PX4's architecture: modules communicating through uORB on NuttX, from sensor drivers to motor outputs, with the supporting modules (commander, navigator, logger, MAVLink).
  • Explain the two ways a PX4 module runs: as its own task, or as work items on a shared work queue, and the trade-offs between them.
  • Compute the worst waiting time of a work item from its queue, its priority and the items it shares the queue with, and check it against a simulation.
  • Choose a work queue for a new piece of work and justify the choice.
  • Diagnose a late control loop caused by a long item placed on a high-priority work queue.
Jargon box
Module (PX4)
Simple words: one self-contained part of the flight software, such as a sensor driver, the estimator or a controller. Analogy: one member of Lina's team with a clear role. Precise: a unit of PX4 software that is built, started, stopped and queried on its own (through a shell command with sub-commands such as start, stop and status) and that communicates with other modules only through uORB topics. D1
Work queue
Simple words: one thread that runs many small jobs, one after another. Analogy: one helper working down a list of quick jobs, in order. Precise: in PX4, a thread with a fixed priority and its own stack that runs the work items scheduled on it in first-in, first-out order, each to completion; several work queues exist with different priorities, so that urgent work is not delayed by less urgent work. D1
Work item
Simple words: a small job that is put on a work queue whenever it is due. Analogy: one quick job on a helper's list ("refill glass 3"). Precise: an object with a run function that is scheduled on a particular work queue, either on a fixed interval or when a uORB topic it watches is published; while it is already waiting in the queue, a new request does not queue it a second time. D1 R1
Run to completion
Simple words: once a job starts on a list, the helper finishes it before taking the next. Analogy: Tomás does not stop washing cups halfway to refill a glass. Precise: items on one work queue do not preempt each other; an item can only be preempted by threads of higher priority (other work queues, tasks, interrupts). Therefore an item can be blocked by any item of its own queue that is already running or queued ahead of it. D1 D4
Blocking term (work queue)
Simple words: the extra wait caused by a less urgent job that is in the way and cannot be interrupted. Analogy: the ten minutes of cup washing in front of "refill glass 3". Precise: in response-time analysis, the term B added to a job's response time for non-preemptible work of equal or lower urgency that can delay it; for a work item, the longest execution time among the other items of its own queue that can be ahead of it. D4
Merged request
Simple words: asking for a job that is already on the list does not add it twice. Analogy: "glass 3 is empty" shouted twice is still one refill. Precise: in this chapter's model (and, by the PX4 documentation's description, in PX4's work queues), scheduling an item that is already queued or running does not create a second entry; the request is lost as a separate run, which shows up as fewer runs than releases. R1 D1
Commander, navigator, logger
Simple words: PX4 modules that decide the flight mode and arming, plan where to go, and record the flight. Analogy: the team leader, the route planner, and Kwame writing the day's notes. Precise: PX4 modules described in the PX4 Autopilot User Guide: the commander handles vehicle state, arming and failsafes; the navigator executes missions and automatic modes; the logger writes selected uORB topics to a ULog file (F10-25). D1

Related terms: NuttX and task and thread (F10-20), blocking term (RB304), response-time analysis (F3-39), executor (ROS 2) and callback group (RB303), which solve a similar problem in ROS 2.

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

PX4 is not one big program. It is a team of small programs called modules. One reads the motion sensor, one works out which way the drone is tilted, one decides how hard each motor should push, one records everything, and so on. They never call each other directly: each one pins its results on a notice board, uORB, and reads what it needs from there.

Many modules only need a moment of work, many times a second. Giving each one its own helper would waste memory, so PX4 gives a few helpers a list of quick jobs each. A helper does its list in order. The most urgent helper can interrupt the others whenever it needs to.

The rule to remember: on one list, nothing interrupts a job that has started. So a slow job must never go on an urgent helper's list.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Modules and the uORB boundary

The PX4 Autopilot User Guide describes PX4 as made of two layers: a flight stack (estimation, control and navigation) and a middleware layer that moves data between modules and abstracts the hardware and operating system. Modules are reactive: they wait for new data on the topics they subscribe to, do their work, and publish results on their own topics. Because the only interface between modules is the set of topics and their message definitions, a module can be replaced, added or removed without changing the others, and the whole stack can run in simulation by replacing the drivers with a simulator interface (F10-23). D1

Figure 1 follows the data. Sensor drivers publish raw measurements. A sensors module checks and combines them. The estimator (F10-15) publishes the vehicle's attitude, velocity and position. The controllers form the cascade of F10-16: position to velocity to attitude to body rates. Control allocation (F10-18) turns the requested torques and thrust into per-motor commands, and output drivers send them to the ESCs. Beside the chain, the commander decides whether the vehicle may arm and which mode it is in, the navigator turns missions into setpoints, the MAVLink module talks to the ground station, and the logger records topics to a file. D1

How a module is started, stopped and queried

Each PX4 module is also a shell command. The start-up scripts start modules in order during boot with the command's start sub-command; on the console you can ask any module for its status and stop it. Most modules support the same set of sub-commands because they share a common base class in PX4's source that implements the command handling and the start-up and shut-down synchronisation. D1 D3 You will write such a module in F10-24 on this course's miniature runtime; the exact PX4 class names are in the unverified box below.

Tasks or work items

A module's periodic work can run in two ways. It can be its own task (F10-20) with its own stack and priority, looping and blocking on new data. Or it can be a work item on a shared work queue: a thread that runs the items scheduled on it, in order, each to completion. PX4's documentation explains the reason for work queues: fewer threads mean less RAM spent on stacks and fewer context switches, and an item can be scheduled to run whenever a topic it depends on is published, so that data flows through the chain without waiting for a timer. Several work queues exist at different priorities, and the drivers of devices on one bus are grouped on a queue for that bus. D1

One processor, four work-queue threads with fixed priorities, six items with a period and a cost; the IMU item runs every 1,000 µs, and its completion triggers the rate controller. The model runs for 200 ms of simulated time, one microsecond per step, and reports per item: how many times it ran, how many requests were merged, its worst waiting time (queued to started) and its worst response time (queued to finished). Configuration A puts each item on the queue that matches its urgency (Figure 2); configuration C gives every item its own thread: R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


Response-time analysis with a run-to-completion queue


Response-time analysis (F3-39) extends directly. For an item i on queue q:


Ri = Ci + Bi + Σj on higher-priority threads ⌈Ri / Tj⌉ · Cj (+ the items of queue q that are ahead of i in the FIFO when it is queued)


The new term is Bi, the blocking from items of the same queue that cannot be preempted: at most one item that is already running when i arrives (the longest one that can be running), plus whatever is queued ahead of it. A long item on an urgent queue therefore adds its whole execution time to every other item of that queue. That is the "slow job on the urgent list" of the story, and the reason PX4 keeps several queues at different priorities. D4 D1 The Worked example applies the formula and compares it with the model.



Merged requests: the silent loss


If an item is requested while it is still queued or running, the model does not queue it twice: the request is merged and counted. A merged request of a control loop is a lost control cycle. It does not crash anything and produces no error message; it only appears as "runs" smaller than "releases", or as a gap in the timestamps of the loop's output topic. Watch that counter. In PX4, the equivalent symptom is a loop rate below its nominal value in the module's status or in the log (F10-25). R1 D1



What a thread costs


Every thread needs its own stack, sized for its deepest call chain, and its own control block; on a flat build there is no virtual memory to share or grow stacks (F10-20). With n modules as tasks you pay n stacks; with n items on k queues you pay k stacks, each sized for the deepest of its items. On a microcontroller with a few hundred kibibytes of RAM (the exact amount is in the named part's datasheet), that difference decides whether a feature fits. The cost of sharing is the blocking term. The design question is always the same: group items that have similar urgency and similar, short execution times. D1 D4



Interval scheduling versus triggering on new data


An item can be scheduled on a fixed interval or when a topic it watches is published. Triggering on publication makes the chain data-driven: the rate controller runs as soon as a new gyro sample exists, so the delay from sample to motor command is the sum of the chain's execution and waiting times, not that sum plus up to one timer period. Interval scheduling is simpler and suits work that has no single input, such as housekeeping. In Listing 1, rate_ctrl is triggered by imu_driver's completion, the model's stand-in for "woken by the publication of a new sample". D1 R1 ROS 2's executors and callback groups face the same choices (RB303), with the same failure when one slow callback shares a thread with a fast one.



What the model leaves out


No interrupts, no context-switch cost, no locks, no multi-core processors, no variation in execution time, and releases that always fall on exact multiples of their periods. Each omission makes the real worst case larger. Use the model to reason about structure and to compare configurations, then measure the real system: PX4 provides commands that report per-thread processor load and per-work-queue information (unverified box). How the hardware actually does it


A work-queue thread is an ordinary NuttX thread: it sleeps on a semaphore or similar object until an item is queued, then the scheduler switches to it if it has the highest priority among ready threads (F10-20). Scheduling an item from a driver's interrupt handler or from another thread is a few memory operations to append to the queue plus a signal to the thread; the actual switch happens through the core's exception mechanism, as in F3-39. D2 On a single-core flight controller, "a higher thread preempts a lower one" means the core stops executing the lower thread's instructions, saves its registers on its stack, and continues the higher one; two work queues never run at the same instant. Bus queues exist because one SPI or I2C bus can only carry one transfer at a time: putting every driver of one bus on one thread serialises their transfers naturally, without a bus lock shared across priorities. Worked example


Task. In configuration B (the forensic lab's), mag_cal_step (period 50,000 µs, cost 2,500 µs) is moved to wq_rate, where it shares the queue with rate_ctrl. Predict the rate controller's worst waiting and response times, then check against the model.


    

  1. Worst case scenario. At t = 0 both imu_driver (on wq_sensor) and mag_cal_step (on wq_rate) are released. imu_driver runs first (higher thread). At t = 60 µs it finishes and queues rate_ctrl, but mag_cal_step is already ahead of it on wq_rate, and it starts at t = 60.
    2. 

  2. Blocking. B = 2,500 µs: rate_ctrl must wait for the whole calibration step, because items on one queue run to completion.
    3. 

  3. Interference from higher threads. While rate_ctrl waits and runs, imu_driver preempts at 1,000 µs and 2,000 µs: 2 × 60 = 120 µs. (The next IMU release, at 3,000 µs, comes after the window, see step 4.)
    4. 

  4. Response time. From its queueing at 60 µs: R = C + B + interference = 150 + 2,500 + 120 = 2,770 µs, so it finishes at 2,830 µs, and its waiting time is 2,770 − 150 = 2,620 µs. The window ends before 3,000 µs, so no third IMU job enters: the iteration has converged.
    5. 

  5. Lost cycles. During that window the IMU item completed at 1,060 and 2,060 µs and tried to queue rate_ctrl again; it was already queued, so both requests were merged: 2 lost control cycles per calibration step. Over 200 ms there are 4 calibration steps: 4 × 2 = 8 merged, so 200 − 8 = 192 runs.
    6. 

  6. Check. The model's forensic run reports worst wait 2,620 µs, worst response 2,770 µs, 192 runs and 8 merged (forensic lab evidence). The prediction is exact because the model has no overheads.
    7.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Tomás can decide to stop washing cups when he sees an empty glass; a work queue cannot. An item runs to completion no matter what is waiting behind it; only a higher-priority thread can interrupt it, and then the waiting item still waits.
    • 

  • Helpers work at the same time; work-queue threads on one core take turns. "Kwame writes notes while Tomás refills" is true for people; on a single-core flight controller the lower thread is frozen while the higher one runs.
    • 

  • Shouting "glass 3 is empty" twice gets you noticed; requesting a queued item twice gets you nothing. The second request is merged and the cycle is lost without a trace, unless you count it. R1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a control loop's rate drops and its latency spikes periodically. Cause: a long item on the same work queue (forensic lab). Find: the loop's timestamps in the log, the work-queue listing, and per-item execution times; look for an item whose period matches the spikes. Fix: move the long item to a low-priority queue, or split it into short steps.
    • 

  • Symptom: fewer runs than releases, no error message. Cause: merged requests: the item was still queued or running at its next release. Find: count runs per second, or gaps in the output topic's timestamps. Fix: reduce the item's waiting time or execution time; never assume every request runs.
    • 

  • Symptom: a new module makes the system run out of memory at start. Cause: it was written as a task with a large stack. Fix: if its work is periodic and short, make it a work item on an existing queue of the right priority.
    • 

  • Symptom: a driver occasionally reads corrupted data from a bus. Cause: two drivers on the same bus run on different threads and their transfers interleave. Fix: put all drivers of one bus on that bus's queue (or protect the bus properly).
    • 

  • Symptom: blocking calls (sleeping, waiting on a device) inside an item stall unrelated items. Cause: an item that blocks holds its whole queue. Fix: items must not sleep or wait; reschedule yourself later instead.
    •

Summary


    

  • PX4 is a set of modules that exchange data only through uORB topics, on NuttX (Figure 1).
    • 

  • Each module is also a shell command with start, stop and status; start-up scripts start them at boot.
    • 

  • Module work runs either in its own task or as work items on shared work-queue threads.
    • 

  • Items on one queue run in order, each to completion; only higher-priority threads preempt them.
    • 

  • Response time = own cost + blocking by same-queue items + interference from higher threads; one 2,500 µs item on the rate queue raised the rate controller's worst response from 150 µs to 2,770 µs.
    • 

  • Requests for an item already queued are merged: control cycles are lost silently (8 in 200 ms in the forensic run).
    • 

  • Group items of similar urgency and short execution time; put long work on low-priority queues.
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Sensor drivers → sensors (combine) → estimator → controllers (position, attitude, rate) → control allocation → output drivers to the ESCs. Supporting: commander (arming, modes, failsafes), navigator (missions), MAVLink (ground link), logger (records topics).
    2. 

  2. The topics and their message definitions are the only interface, so modules can be replaced, added or removed without changing others, and drivers can be swapped for a simulator interface. It also makes every exchange observable and loggable.
    3. 

  3. Once an item starts, the thread finishes it before starting the next item of that queue; only higher-priority threads can interrupt. Therefore every item on a queue can be delayed by the full execution time of any other item of that queue that is running or ahead of it: placing an item on a queue adds its cost to the others' worst case.
    4. 

  4. It is queued at t = 0 together with attitude_ctrl, which is ahead of it in the same FIFO. Before it starts: imu_driver 60 µs (higher thread), rate_ctrl 150 µs (higher thread), attitude_ctrl 300 µs (same queue, ahead): 60 + 150 + 300 = 510 µs.
    5. 

  5. Because in this task set the same-queue items are always queued in the order of their urgency, so no item waits for a less urgent one that started first. If position_ctrl could be running when attitude_ctrl is released (for example with a phase offset), A would block the attitude controller for up to 600 µs while C would preempt it.
    6. 

  6. The logger item is released at t = 0, at the same instant as the IMU item, so it is queued on wq_rate before the rate controller (queued at 60 µs, when the IMU item finishes). It starts at 60 µs and runs for 20 µs: the rate controller's worst wait becomes 20 µs and its worst response 170 µs (the model confirms both, and the attitude controller's worst wait grows from 210 to 230 µs). It meets its timing, but it puts non-control work on the control queue and adds to the rate loop's latency every cycle; it belongs on a low-priority queue.
    7. 

  7. Prediction: battery is released every 100 ms, at instants when attitude_ctrl and position_ctrl are released too; in this model items released at the same instant are queued in table order, so battery is behind both and the attitude controller's worst wait stays 210 µs. The model (run in this build's preparation with that one change) confirms: attitude 210 µs, position 510 µs, battery itself 1,320 µs. With another phase, battery could be running when the attitude controller is released, and the attitude controller would then wait up to 400 µs more: the placement is safe only by the accident of the phasing.
    8. 

  8. A request to run an item that is already queued or running; it does not add a run. You notice it as fewer runs than releases: a lower loop rate, gaps in output timestamps, or a counter like the model's.
    9. 

  9. Its work blocks or takes long (file writing, long computations, waiting for a device), so it would hold a shared queue; or it needs a priority or stack size different from every queue. (Either is acceptable.)

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. rate_ctrl: 192 runs instead of 200, 8 merged, worst wait 2,620 µs, worst response 2,770 µs (A: 200, 0, 0, 150). attitude_ctrl worst wait 2,830 µs, position_ctrl 3,340, battery 3,940. mag_cal_step is listed on wq_rate at priority 9. Processor use moved from wq_lowprio (5.4 % → 0.4 %) to wq_rate (15.0 % → 19.4 %); the total is unchanged.


Timeline. From 60 µs the calibration step (m) runs, interrupted only by the IMU item (ii) at 1,000 and 2,000 µs; the rate controller (rrr) starts only at 2,680 µs, followed by the attitude controller.


Hypotheses. (a) Overload: no; total processor use is unchanged and well below 100 %. (b) Late IMU: no; imu_driver's worst wait is 0 in both runs. (c) Wrong queue: yes; the calibration step now shares the rate controller's queue and runs to completion ahead of it.


Root cause. R = 150 + 2,500 (blocking) + 2 × 60 (IMU) = 2,770 µs, matching the table. Each calibration step blocks the queue for about 2.6 ms; the IMU completions at 1,060 and 2,060 µs request the rate controller, which is still queued: 2 merged per step, 4 steps in 200 ms, 8 merged. The 50 ms twitch is the calibration's period.


Fix and prevention. Run the calibration step on the low-priority queue (as in A), or split it into short steps. Prevention: a CI test in SITL that checks the rate controller's loop rate and worst latency from the log (F10-25), and a review rule that every item placed on a control queue states its worst execution time.


Alternative wrong answers. "Increase the rate controller's priority": it is already the highest work item after the IMU; the blocking comes from its own queue, which priority cannot fix. "The IMU driver is slow": its numbers are identical in A and B.


How the evidence was made. forensic_wq.cpp takes configuration A and changes one field: mag_cal_step's queue from wq_lowprio to wq_rate.
