Story hook

After the fair, Lina's teacher asks why the tray wobbled so much at half past two. Nobody remembers. But Mateus had filmed the whole afternoon from the balcony, and next to the film he had written down the settings of the day: Lina's route, the tray, the number of glasses, and the note that someone had glued a little flag to the tray "half a metre in front of the handle", to help the spotters see where she was.

The film shows something odd. Whenever Lina walks north, the spotters' marks on the floor plan agree with the film. Whenever she turns around and walks south, the spotters' marks jump a metre ahead of her, and the head spotter starts ignoring them: "the spotters must be wrong". Then the teacher looks at the settings again. The flag was not half a metre in front of the handle. It was fifteen centimetres. Everyone had been correcting for a flag position that was wrong, and the error flipped sides every time Lina turned.

A flight log is the film plus the settings. In this chapter you learn what PX4 writes into its logs, how such a log is laid out, and how to read it with tools. Then you solve the course forensic case: an estimator that rejected GNSS, and the one parameter that caused it.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a ULog contains (header, message formats, information, parameters, subscriptions, data, logged strings) and why it is self-describing.
  • Read a log with tools: list topics and record counts, print parameters and messages, extract a topic as a table.
  • Compare two logs by their parameters to find what changed.
  • Interpret estimator innovations and test ratios, and recognise the signature of a sensor position (lever arm) error.
  • Investigate "estimator rejected GPS" from a log alone and name the misconfigured parameter with evidence.
Jargon box
ULog
Simple words: the file format in which PX4 records a flight. Analogy: the film of the afternoon, with the settings written next to it. Precise: PX4's binary log format: a header, then a definitions section (message formats, information key-value pairs, parameters), then a data section (subscriptions, timestamped topic data, logged strings and parameter changes); written by the logger module, typically to the SD card. D1
Self-describing log
Simple words: a log that contains the description of its own records, so a reader needs no other file. Analogy: each film reel starts with a card saying what is on it. Precise: a format in which every record type is defined inside the file (field names, types, order) before records of that type appear; tools decode any topic without knowing the firmware version. D1 R1
Innovation (measurement residual)
Simple words: the difference between what a sensor says and what the estimator expected it to say. Analogy: the gap between where the spotter marks Lina and where the head spotter thought she was. Precise: y − h(x̂), the measurement minus the measurement predicted from the current state estimate; its covariance S = HPHᵀ + R (F10-14). D3
Innovation test ratio
Simple words: a number that says how surprising a measurement is, scaled so that above 1 means "too surprising, reject". Analogy: "that mark is more than four steps away from where I expected; ignore it". Precise: in this course's estimator, innovation² / (gate² · S), with gate = 4 standard deviations: a measurement with ratio > 1 lies outside the gate and is not fused. PX4's estimator logs test ratios defined in a similar spirit (unverified box). R1 D2
Innovation gate
Simple words: the limit beyond which a measurement is ignored. Analogy: the "four steps" rule. Precise: a threshold on the normalised innovation that protects the estimate from outliers; set too tight it rejects good data, too loose it accepts bad data. D3
Lever arm (sensor position offset)
Simple words: where a sensor is mounted relative to the point the estimator tracks. Analogy: the flag 15 cm in front of the handle. Precise: the body-frame vector from the estimator's reference point (usually the IMU or centre of gravity) to the sensor; the estimator rotates it by the attitude to predict the sensor's measurement. A wrong lever arm produces an innovation that changes with heading. D2 D3
Logged string message
Simple words: a line of text that the flight software writes into the log at a moment in time. Analogy: Mateus's spoken comment on the film: "she turned now". Precise: a log record with a level, a timestamp and text; in PX4, messages that modules print or send to the ground station are also logged. D1

Related terms: innovation, innovation covariance, normalised innovation squared, Kalman filter, extended Kalman filter; from this course uORB.

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

While a drone flies, it writes a diary, many times every second: what the sensors measured, where it thought it was, what it told the motors, and every message it printed. At the start of the diary it writes down all its settings. After the flight, people read the diary with programs that draw graphs and tables.

The diary explains itself: before the first entry about the gyroscope, it says "a gyroscope entry has these numbers, in this order". So any reader can open any diary, even from a newer drone.

In the detective case of this chapter, the drone ignored its GPS for a while. The diary shows that it happened only when the drone faced south. That clue, plus the settings at the start of the diary, leads to one wrong number: where the GPS antenna is mounted on the drone.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What gets logged

PX4's logger module subscribes to a configurable list of uORB topics (F10-22) and writes their messages, each at a chosen maximum rate, into a ULog file, together with the parameters and system information at the start, parameter changes during the flight, and printed messages. Because the logger records uORB topics, everything a module publishes can be logged, including your own module's topic (F10-24). Logging usually starts at arming and stops at disarming, to keep files small; the settings for this are parameters. D1 The format details and logger parameter names are in the unverified box of Layer 3.

A log you can open byte by byte

The build had no PX4 and no real flight logs, so this chapter's logs are written by the course's flight simulation (flight_sim.cc, Listing 1) in a ULog-style format (ulog_lite.h, Listing 2): its structure follows the ULog structure as described in the PX4 documentation, and it was written from memory. It shows how such a log works; whether PX4's tools would open it is not verified. R1 The first 48 bytes of a lab log: R2



Bytes 0–6 are the magic "ULog" and three more bytes, byte 7 is the version (1), bytes 8–15 the start time (0 µs). At byte 16 the first message begins: size 4e 00 (78, little-endian), type 46 (F, a format definition) and the text vehicle_state_model:uint64_t timestamp;…. A reader never needs to know the firmware: it learns each topic's fields from the F messages.



Reading a log with tools


The course's ulog_info (Listing 3) prints what is inside a log: message counts by type, every format with its record count, the information pairs, the parameters, and the logged strings. Run on the log of a hover with a yaw turn: R3




Read it from top to bottom like an investigator. Four topics: the vehicle state (heading) and the position estimate at 10 Hz (340 records in 34 s), GNSS measurements and estimator innovations at 5 Hz (170). Information: the log comes from the course simulation, not PX4. Eight parameters, all named UNI_…: these are this course's own names, not PX4's (the PX4 equivalents are in the unverified box). And then the logged messages: the estimator stopped fusing GNSS at 10.80 s, reset its position after a 5 s timeout, resumed, and stopped again several times between 22.8 and 28.2 s. That is the forensic case; Layer 3 explains the tools that reveal its cause, and the Forensic lab solves it.



Innovations: the estimator's surprise


Every time a GNSS measurement arrives, the estimator predicts where the antenna should be: its position estimate plus the antenna's mounting offset rotated by the current heading. The difference between measured and predicted antenna position is the innovation. Its expected size comes from the estimator's uncertainty plus the GNSS noise: S = P + R. The test ratio compares the two: ratio = innovation² / (gate² · S). With a gate of 4 standard deviations, a ratio above 1 means the innovation is more than 4σ: the measurement is rejected (Listing 1, lines 145–151). R1 D3 A healthy log has innovations that look like noise around zero and ratios well below 1. The innovation report (Listing 4) of a normal flight, one line per second: R4




Innovations of a few tenths of a metre in both directions, the largest ratio 0.50, every sample fused, through both turns. The last line fits the north innovation against the cosine of the heading; a slope near zero (−0.01 m) means the innovations do not depend on heading.



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


Why the format looks like this


A flight logger must write tens to hundreds of kilobytes per second to an SD card from a small processor without disturbing control. That rules out text formats and per-record field names. Defining formats once and then writing packed binary records keeps the data rate low and the writer simple (copy the uORB struct). The size-and-type prefix on every message lets a reader skip types it does not know, so old readers survive new message types. Storing the parameters at the start and every change during the flight makes each log a complete record of the configuration, which is exactly what the forensic case needs. Logging continues while the SD card is slow by buffering; if the buffer overflows, data is dropped, and a good log tool reports the dropouts. D1



Comparing logs


When a vehicle "suddenly" behaves differently, compare parameters first. The course tool prints the parameters that differ between two logs: R3




A parameter diff is cheap and often decisive, but it requires a reference log from a known-good flight of the same vehicle. In the Forensic lab you will first reach the answer from the bad log alone, and only then confirm it with this diff.



Reading an estimator's complaints


A rejected sensor is a symptom with several possible causes, each with its own signature in the log:

Sensor glitch (multipath, jamming)Sudden jumps, not related to vehicle motionGNSS quality fields (satellites, accuracy estimates) worsen at the same time

The second row is our case. The innovation is the measured antenna position minus the predicted one. With the true offset L and the configured offset Lcfg, along north: innovation = (L − Lcfg)·cos ψ − p̂, where p̂ is the estimator's position error. While the heading is constant, fusion drives the innovation to zero by moving p̂; when the heading changes, the term (L − Lcfg)·cos ψ changes and the innovation appears. The report's least-squares fit of innovation against cos ψ estimates a = L − Lcfg; because the estimator keeps absorbing part of the error into p̂ (and the timeout resets it), the fitted size underestimates the error, but its sign and its clear departure from zero are the evidence (Listing 4, lines 43–64). How the hardware actually does it


On a flight controller the logger runs as a low-priority task: other modules publish topics, the logger copies the subscribed messages into a RAM buffer, and a separate writer thread writes the buffer to a file on the SD card through NuttX's file system (F10-20). SD cards have unpredictable write latencies (internal erase and wear levelling can stall a write for many milliseconds), which is why the buffer exists and why dropouts appear in logs when the card is slow; the size of the buffer and the card's performance decide how much is lost. D1 Timestamps in the log are the flight controller's microsecond clock at the time the data was sampled or published, so log time is the vehicle's time, not the wall clock; in SITL it is the simulator's time (F10-23). GNSS receivers report positions of the antenna's phase centre, not of the vehicle, which is why the antenna position on the airframe must be configured; on a small multicopter the antenna is often on a mast above the centre, on a larger airframe it can sit a metre or more from the IMU, and then a wrong value matters. D2 No hardware numbers are given here; record your card's write behaviour from your own logs.



Worked example


Task. Using the course estimator (gate 4σ, GNSS noise σ = 0.30 m, steady-state position variance before a fusion P ≈ 0.0095 m², computed from the filter equations of Listing 1 for 0.15 m/s² acceleration noise), predict: (a) the innovation after a 180° turn if the configured antenna offset is 1.5 m and the true one 0.15 m; (b) its test ratio; (c) the largest offset error that would still be accepted after such a turn, ignoring noise.


    

  1. (a) Before the turn (heading north, cos ψ = 1) the estimator has absorbed the error: p̂ ≈ L − Lcfg = 0.15 − 1.5 = −1.35 m (the estimate sits 1.35 m south of the truth). After the turn (cos ψ = −1): innovation = (L − Lcfg)·(−1) − p̂ = 1.35 + 1.35 = 2.70 m.
    2. 

  2. (b) S = P + R = 0.0095 + 0.30² = 0.0995 m². Ratio = 2.70² / (4² · 0.0995) = 7.29 / 1.592 = 4.6. Rejected. The log shows per-second maxima between 3.5 and 5.9 while facing south (noise adds and subtracts).
    3. 

  3. (c) Accepted while |innovation| ≤ 4·√0.0995 = 1.26 m. The innovation after a 180° turn is twice the offset error: 2·|ΔL| ≤ 1.26 → |ΔL| ≤ 0.63 m. Smaller errors pass the gate, and that makes them harder to see: every sample is fused, the estimator follows the error, and the estimate is wrong by up to about ΔL after each turn. A run of the course model with 1.5 replaced by 0.6 m (scratch run, not a lab output) fused all 170 samples with a maximum ratio of 0.89, a heading fit of only a = 0.01 m, and a maximum position error of 0.84 m against 0.57 m with the correct offset.
    4. 



The useful conclusion: a lever-arm error stays invisible while the vehicle holds one heading, and appears, doubled, after a half turn. A log of a hover without turns cannot reveal it.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A film shows everything in view; a log shows only the topics on the logger's list, at the logged rate. If the topic you need was not logged, or logged at 5 Hz while the event lasted 20 ms, the evidence is not there.
    • 

  • Mateus wrote the settings once; a log also records every change. A parameter changed in flight appears in the data section with its time; read it.
    • 

  • Spotters ignoring a mark is a human judgement; an estimator's rejection is a fixed rule. The rule is right only if the noise and offset parameters it uses are right. R5
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: "GPS rejected" and the operator replaces the GNSS receiver; nothing changes. Cause: the problem is in the estimator's configuration, not the receiver. Find: innovations versus heading, velocity and time; parameters. Fix: the parameter, with evidence.
    • 

  • Symptom: the log analysis tool shows no estimator topics. Cause: the logger's profile did not include them. Fix: choose a logging profile that includes estimator topics before test flights.
    • 

  • Symptom: gaps in logged data. Cause: SD card too slow, buffer overflow. Find: the tool's dropout report. Fix: a faster card, fewer topics or lower rates.
    • 

  • Symptom: innovations look fine but the vehicle drifts. Cause: the estimator absorbed a constant error (as before the turn in our case). Find: fly a manoeuvre that changes the geometry (turns, climbs), then look again.
    • 

  • Symptom: after "fixing" by widening the gate, no more rejections, but positions are worse. Cause: the gate hid the symptom; the wrong offset still biases the estimate. Fix: correct the cause; keep the gate.
    •

Forensic lab — Estimator rejected GPS (course forensic)


Scenario. A multicopter of the course fleet has a new, larger frame with the GNSS antenna moved to a new mount. On its first hover test the operator saw "GNSS fusion stopped" messages on the ground station after the vehicle turned, and position hold felt "wandering". The operator replaced the GNSS receiver; the next flight showed the same messages. You receive the log of that flight, nothing else. The log comes from the course simulation; its parameter names are the course's (UNI_…), see the unverified box for PX4's.


Evidence pack. The summary of the log (shown in Layer 2: ulog_info bad.ulg) and the innovation report of the same log: R5

Summary


    

  • A ULog holds a header, definitions (formats, information, parameters) and data (subscriptions, records, logged strings, parameter changes); it describes itself.
    • 

  • The logger records uORB topics from a configured list at configured rates; what was not logged cannot be analysed.
    • 

  • Read logs top-down: messages and parameters first, then the topics the messages point to.
    • 

  • Innovation = measured − predicted; the course's test ratio = innovation² / (gate² · S); above 1 is rejected.
    • 

  • A lever-arm error is absorbed at a constant heading and appears, doubled, after a 180° turn (2.70 m, ratio 4.6 predicted; 3.5–5.9 logged).
    • 

  • In the course forensic case the antenna offset parameter was 1.5 m instead of 0.15 m; the heading fit gave a = −0.66 m (−0.01 m in the good log), and the parameter diff confirmed it.
    • 

  • The course log format and every PX4 log tool, topic and parameter name are unverified; real PX4 logs were not available in this build.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Seconds 1–10 (heading 0°): all samples fused, ratios ≤ 0.31. Second 11 (turning): 3 of 5 fused, ratio 1.13; the log says "GNSS fusion stopped: test ratio 1.10" at 10.80 s. Seconds 12–15 (heading 180°): none fused, north innovation +2.3 to +2.5 m, ratios 3.5–5.9. At 15.60 s "position reset to GNSS after timeout" (5 s after the last fusion); from second 16–17 on, fusion resumes and innovations are small again while the heading stays 180°. Seconds 23–28 (turning back and heading 0°): north innovation −1.7 to −2.2 m, few samples fused, the stop/resume messages from 22.80 to 28.20; from second 29 on mostly fused again as the estimate drifts back.


Hypotheses. (a) Receiver or reception: would not correlate with heading; would show a GNSS quality change; the operator already replaced the receiver with no change. Rejected. (b) Delay: would show innovations proportional to velocity; the vehicle hovers, velocity is near zero, and innovations persist during the hold at 180°. Rejected. (c) Gate or noise too tight: would show frequent ratios near 1 at all headings; at heading 0° ratios are ≤ 0.31 with the same settings. Rejected (and UNI_GATE 4, UNI_GPS_NOISE 0.3 are plausible). (d) Antenna position: predicts innovations that flip sign with a 180° turn and grow to twice the error; observed. Supported.


The fit. a = −0.66 m: the north innovation goes down by 0.66 m per unit of cos ψ, i.e. it is higher when facing south. With innovation = (L − Lcfg)·cos ψ − p̂, a negative a means Lcfg > L: the configured antenna position is too far forward. Its size understates the error because fusion and the reset keep absorbing part of it.


Root cause. UNI_GPS_OFF_X = 1.500 m in the log's parameters; the new mount puts the antenna much closer to the IMU. Using the heading-180° innovation of about 2.3–2.5 m ≈ 2·ΔL, ΔL ≈ 1.2–1.25 m (an underestimate; the true value, from the simulation, is 1.35 m, i.e. the antenna sits 0.15 m forward). Explanation of the messages: 10.80 s, the turn starts to expose the error and the ratio exceeds 1; 15.60 s, 5 s without fusion triggers the timeout reset, which moves the estimate to agree with GNSS at heading 180°; 15.80 s, fusion resumes because the estimate now absorbed the error for this heading; 22.80–28.20 s, turning back reverses the error and the cycle repeats until the estimate drifts back. The "wandering" position hold is the estimate moving by up to 2.7 m between headings (maximum position error 1.98 m in the simulation's summary). 


Check and PX4 parameter. Measure the antenna's position relative to the IMU (or the reference point the estimator uses) on the airframe, in the body frame and sign convention of the documentation, then set the parameter. In PX4 the corresponding parameter is believed to be EKF2_GPS_POS_X (with _Y, _Z), to be confirmed in the PX4 Parameter Reference (unverified box).


Confirmation. The good log, identical except UNI_GPS_OFF_X = 0.150 (see the parameter diff in Layer 3 and the flight summary in the Code walk-through), fuses all 170 samples with maximum ratio 0.50 and a heading fit of −0.01 m.


Prevention. A frame build checklist that includes measuring and entering sensor positions after any mount change; a first hover test that includes 180° turns, with an automatic heading-correlation check on the log before further flights.


How the evidence was made. run.sh runs flight_sim bad.ulg 1.5; the simulator's true offset is 0.15 m (Listing 1 line 17); everything else, including the random seed, is identical to good.ulg.
