Story hook

The school has a big clock in the hall, accurate and checked by the caretaker, but a long walk from the principal's office. On her desk the principal has a fast little desk counter that clicks very quickly; nobody told her how many clicks make a second. So once a morning she walks to the hall with a stopwatch, counts clicks while the hall clock moves 50 seconds, and writes the rate on a sticky note. From then on the desk counter tells the time.

She also has an alarm clock that she sets for the next appointment only, not every minute. When it rings she handles every appointment that is due and sets it again for the next one.

In the old building there was no hall clock, only the gym teacher's stopwatch, whose dial went up to 65,535. One day a new teacher asked it to time 119,318 ticks. The stopwatch quietly timed 53,782 instead, the principal wrote down a rate that was 2.6 times too small, and every lesson that day finished early by the hall clock.

This chapter builds the kernel's sense of time: a clock source (the hall clock or the desk counter), calibration (the stopwatch walk), a one-shot timer with a sorted queue of deadlines (the alarm clock), sleep functions, and a measurement of how accurate they are.

What you will be able to do

  • Read the HPET from its ACPI table: period, comparators, counter width; and fall back to the PIT when there is none.
  • Calibrate the TSC and the local APIC timer against a reference, and cancel fixed overheads with two windows.
  • Convert counts to nanoseconds exactly, without overflow, and prove it on the host against 128-bit arithmetic.
  • Choose a clock source by a stated policy, and build a monotonic now_ns() on it.
  • Implement a tickless one-shot timer with a sorted timer queue, periodic timers, and sleep_ns without a lost wake-up.
  • Measure a 10-second sleep against the host's clock and report the error with a tolerance, separately for TCG and KVM.
Jargon box
Clock source
Simple words: the counter the kernel reads to know what time it is. Analogy: the hall clock or the desk counter. Precise: a monotonically increasing hardware counter with a known rate, read by now_ns(); here the TSC or the HPET main counter. D5
Monotonic clock
Simple words: a clock that never goes backwards. Analogy: a clock nobody is allowed to set back. Precise: a time base whose successive reads are non-decreasing, unaffected by changes to wall-clock time; required for timeouts and intervals. D5
HPET
Simple words: a precise timer chip with one fast counter and a few alarms. Analogy: the accurate hall clock. Precise: the High Precision Event Timer: a memory-mapped block with a main counter of fixed period (at most 100 ns), and several comparators; described by the ACPI "HPET" table. D1
PIT (8254)
Simple words: the old timer chip of the PC, with 16-bit counters. Analogy: the gym teacher's stopwatch that only counts to 65,535. Precise: the Intel 8254 Programmable Interval Timer: three 16-bit down-counters at 1,193,182 Hz; channel 0 drives IRQ 0, channel 2 is gated by port 0x61 and readable through its output bit. D3
TSC (time-stamp counter)
Simple words: a counter inside the CPU that goes up very fast. Analogy: the desk counter. Precise: a 64-bit per-CPU counter read with rdtsc; its rate is not reported directly on many CPUs and must be measured. D2
Invariant TSC
Simple words: a TSC that ticks at the same rate whatever the CPU's speed or sleep state. Analogy: a desk counter that does not slow down when the principal does. Precise: a TSC that runs at a constant rate in all ACPI P-, C- and T-states, reported by CPUID leaf 0x80000007, EDX bit 8. D2
APIC timer
Simple words: the alarm built into each CPU's interrupt receiver. Analogy: the alarm clock on the principal's desk. Precise: the local APIC's timer: an initial-count register counting down at the bus or core crystal rate divided by a configurable divisor, raising the vector in its LVT entry in one-shot, periodic or TSC-deadline mode. D2
Tickless timer
Simple words: a timer set only for the next thing that must happen, instead of ringing regularly. Analogy: setting the alarm for the next appointment only. Precise: programming a one-shot timer for the earliest pending deadline; no interrupts arrive while nothing is due. D5
Timer queue
Simple words: the list of things waiting for a time, earliest first. Analogy: the principal's appointment book. Precise: a priority queue of timers keyed by deadline; the timer interrupt runs every expired timer, re-queues periodic ones and re-arms the hardware for the new head. R1

As in the whole course, "kernel" means the operating-system kernel; the "kernel (GPU)" of F6 is a function launched on a GPU.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A computer has several counters that go up by themselves. Some are slow but tell you exactly how fast they are (the PIT, the HPET). One is very fast but does not say how fast it is (the TSC). So the kernel watches the fast counter while the careful counter counts a known amount of time, and works out the fast counter's speed. After that it can tell the time by reading the fast counter.

To wake up later, the kernel writes down when, in a list sorted by time, and sets an alarm for the earliest one only. When the alarm rings, it does everything that is due and sets the alarm again.

Analogy

The hall clock is the HPET, the gym stopwatch is the PIT, the desk counter is the TSC, the sticky note with the rate is the calibration, and the alarm clock set for the next appointment is the tickless APIC timer.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The HPET

The ACPI "HPET" table (found in F3-24) contains a Generic Address Structure whose 64-bit address, at offset 44 of the table, is the HPET's register block D1D4. Register 0x000 holds the capabilities: bits 63:32 the period in femtoseconds (10−15 s), bits 12:8 the number of comparators minus one, bit 13 "64-bit counter". Register 0x010 is the configuration (bit 0 enables counting), register 0x0F0 the main counter. QEMU: base fed00000, capabilities 009896808086a201: period 0x00989680 = 10,000,000 fs = 10 ns, so 100 MHz, 3 comparators, 64-bit counter R2. The kernel stops the counter, zeroes it and starts it.

The PIT as a stopwatch

Without an HPET (QEMU's -machine pc,hpet=off), the reference is the 8254 PIT at 1,193,182 Hz. Channel 2 is convenient for measuring: its gate is bit 0 of port 0x61 and its output can be read as bit 5 of the same port. In mode 0 ("interrupt on terminal count") the output goes high when the count reaches zero D3. The kernel writes the count (low byte, then high byte), reads the TSC when the high byte is written, polls bit 5, and reads the TSC again. The counter is 16 bits: at most 65,535 ticks, about 55 ms per window.

Calibrating the TSC

With the HPET: read the HPET counter and the TSC, wait 50 ms of HPET counts (5,000,000), read both again; TSC rate = TSC difference × HPET rate / HPET difference. The run: 2,099,868,140 Hz over 5,004,551 HPET counts R2. With the PIT: each window includes a fixed cost (port writes and the first poll) that is large under emulation, where each port access is slow. Two windows of 11,931 and 59,659 ticks have the same fixed cost; the difference of their TSC counts is the TSC cycles of exactly 47,728 PIT ticks. The run: 2,098,933,108 Hz, within 0.05 % of the HPET-calibrated value R3. (An earlier single-window version of this lab gave a 10-second sleep that was 1.15 % off the host clock under TCG.)

Choosing the clock source

A good clock source is fast to read, has a known rate, and never changes speed. The TSC is the fastest to read (one instruction), but without the "invariant" flag its rate may change with power states. The HPET is slower to read (a device register access) but has a fixed, reported rate. The policy: an invariant TSC first; otherwise the HPET; otherwise the TSC with a warning. QEMU's TCG qemu64 CPU reports no invariant TSC, so the HPET run chose 'hpet', and the run without HPET chose 'tsc' and logged the warning R2R3. The cost difference is visible: 10 million reads took 3,978 ms with the HPET and 665 ms with the TSC under TCG.

The APIC timer, one-shot

The local APIC timer counts down from an initial count at a rate the kernel does not know: on real hardware the bus or crystal clock divided by the divide configuration (here 16, register 0x3E0 = 3). So it is calibrated too: masked, initial count 0xFFFFFFFF, wait 50 ms by now_ns(), read the current count. The run: 62,469,022 Hz R2. The LVT timer entry (0x320) is then set to vector 0x40, one-shot. Vector 0x40 has priority class 4, above the devices of F3-24 (class 3).

The timer queue and tickless operation

A timer is a deadline in nanoseconds, an optional period, and a function. The queue keeps up to 32 timers sorted by deadline (insertion into a sorted array: fine for 32; OS303 will want something better). arm() programs the APIC timer for the head's deadline only; an empty queue stops the timer. The interrupt handler runs all expired timers, re-queues periodic ones, re-arms and sends EOI. If a periodic timer falls behind by more than a period, the missed periods are skipped rather than run in a burst. A 100 Hz "scheduler tick" is one more periodic timer in the queue; when OS303 has nothing to run, it can cancel the tick and the CPU sleeps until the next real deadline.

Sleeping without losing the wake-up

sleep_ns queues a timer that sets a flag, then loops: disable interrupts, check the flag, and if it is not set, sti; hlt. The pair matters. If the kernel enabled interrupts, checked, and then halted, the timer interrupt could arrive between the check and the hlt, and the CPU would halt with nothing left to wake it. sti delays interrupt delivery until after the next instruction, so the interrupt can only arrive once the CPU is halted, and wakes it D2.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Exact conversion without overflow

Nanoseconds from counts: cycles × 109 / hz. The product overflows 64 bits when cycles exceed 264 / 109 ≈ 1.84 × 1010: about 8.8 seconds of a 2.1 GHz TSC. The lab's 10-second sleep would already break. clock.h splits: whole seconds (cycles / hz) times 109, plus the remainder (below hz) times 109 / hz. The remainder product fits as long as hz < 1.8 × 1010. The host test checks 1,128,426 random cases at six rates against 128-bit arithmetic and shows the naive formula's result for one hour of TSC: 7,452,083,202 ns instead of 3,600,000,000,000 R1. Linux avoids division altogether with a precomputed multiplier and shift (mult/shift), trading a tiny rounding error for speed D5.

How accurate, and how to know

The milestone's first acceptance test measures a 10-second kernel sleep against the host clock with a tolerance (for example 1 %) under KVM, and logs TCG separately. The host program hostclock.py stamps every serial line with the host's monotonic clock as it arrives and compares the interval between "sleep: start" and "sleep: end" with the kernel's own measurement. Individual sleeps finish late, never early: 1 ms took 1,235 µs, 10 ms took 10,126 µs, 100 ms took 100,212 µs in the HPET run R2. Lateness comes from interrupt latency, the handler's work, and under TCG from QEMU's translation and scheduling; how much of the 100–250 µs is TCG behaviour would need KVM to judge (an earlier run of the same lab showed 2.4 ms on 100 ms, so the numbers vary from run to run). During the 10 s sleep the 100 Hz tick fired 1,000 times in this run; an earlier run counted 996, when the catch-up rule dropped periods because the emulator fell behind.

Monotonic, on one CPU and later on many

On one CPU, a counter that only increases and an exact conversion give a monotonic clock: 10,000,000 reads, 0 went backwards, 0 repeated R2. The milestone asks for this "on all CPUs (after B11)": with several CPUs, each has its own TSC, which may be offset or drift between sockets unless the TSC is invariant and synchronised; a clock that is monotonic per CPU can still go backwards when a thread moves between CPUs. OS303 repeats this test across CPUs; this chapter can only test one. The largest step between two reads, 4,059 µs with the HPET, is the CPU being interrupted (or QEMU being descheduled), not the clock jumping.

Not verified — check before relying on this

From memory, pending D1–D4: the HPET register offsets (0x000, 0x010, 0x0F0), capability bits (period 63:32, comparators 12:8, 64-bit 13), the 100 ns period limit, and the ACPI HPET table's address field at offset 44; the PIT's ports (0x42, 0x43, 0x61), command byte 0xB0 and mode 0 behaviour, and its frequency 1,193,182 Hz; the APIC timer registers (LVT 0x320, initial 0x380, current 0x390, divide 0x3E0 with value 3 = divide by 16); the invariant-TSC bit (CPUID 0x80000007 EDX bit 8); the one-instruction interrupt shadow of sti. The 1 % KVM test is not run; nothing here is tested on hardware.

How the hardware actually does it

On current x86 processors the TSC is invariant and runs at a fixed reference rate, and newer CPUs report that rate through CPUID leaf 0x15 (crystal clock and ratio), so calibration can be skipped D2. The local APIC timer also offers TSC-deadline mode: the kernel writes an absolute TSC value to an MSR and the interrupt arrives when the TSC reaches it, with no separate calibration or rounding of counts. Many modern laptops disable the HPET or the PIT to save power; a kernel must cope with either being missing.

Under KVM, the guest usually sees an invariant TSC (when the host has one) and a paravirtual clock (kvmclock) that the host keeps consistent across migrations. TCG emulates everything in software: a PIT port access or an HPET read costs microseconds, and guest time follows QEMU's virtual clock. That is why this chapter reports TCG separately and why the KVM numbers are a decision for the owner.

Watch out

Untested on hardware, and untested under KVM. On real hardware the APIC timer may stop in deep C-states on older CPUs (the "ARAT" feature says it does not); a tickless kernel that sleeps with hlt must know this.

Worked example

How many APIC timer counts for a 10 ms deadline, and what does the forensic calibration do to a 10 s sleep?

  1. APIC rate 62,469,022 Hz (R2). ns_to_cycles(10,000,000, 62,469,022) = 0 × 62,469,022 + 10,000,000 × 62,469,022 / 109 = 624,690 counts (624,690.22 rounded down). That fits easily in the 32-bit initial count; the longest one-shot is 232 / 62.47 MHz ≈ 68.8 s, after which arm() wakes early and re-arms.
  2. Forensic calibration: windows of 11,931 and 53,782 ticks really timed, but the code assumes 119,318 − 11,931 = 107,387 ticks of difference, i.e. 90 ms, where 41,851 ticks (35.1 ms) passed. TSC rate = true rate × 41,851 / 107,387 = 2.1 GHz × 0.3897 ≈ 818 MHz; the run printed 817,626,796 Hz.
  3. A 10 s sleep waits until the TSC has advanced by 10 × 818 × 106 cycles, which at the true 2.1 GHz takes 10 × 0.3897 ≈ 3.9 s. The host measured 3.894 s R4.
  4. The kernel itself saw nothing wrong: by its own clock the sleep took 10.000643 s. Only an outside reference catches a calibration error.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The hall clock is far away but always right. The HPET is "far" (slow to read) and right only to the extent its reported period is; QEMU makes it exactly 10 ns, real hardware has a crystal tolerance.
    • 

  • The principal checks the desk counter once a day. The kernel calibrates once at boot; a TSC that changes speed afterwards (non-invariant) silently makes every later time wrong.
    • 

  • An alarm clock rings on time. The APIC timer's interrupt can be delayed by masked interrupts, higher-priority handlers and, under emulation, by the host.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • 16-bit overflow in PIT counts: the forensic lab. A static_assert on the constant prevents it.
    • 

  • Naive cycles × 109 / hz: wraps after about 8.8 s at 2.1 GHz.
    • 

  • One calibration window under emulation: the fixed overhead biases the rate (a 1.15 % sleep error here before the two-window fix).
    • 

  • Checking the flag with interrupts on, then hlt: a lost wake-up and a hang.
    • 

  • Changing the timer queue with interrupts on: the handler and the caller corrupt the array.
    • 

  • Forgetting the IRQ 0 → GSI 2 override when using the PIT's channel 0 for interrupts (not used here: channel 2 is polled).
    • 

  • Trusting the kernel's own clock to check the kernel's own clock: compare against an outside reference.
    •

Summary


    

  • References with known rates: the HPET (period from its capabilities) or the PIT (1,193,182 Hz, 16-bit counters).
    • 

  • The TSC and the APIC timer are calibrated against a reference; two PIT windows cancel fixed overheads.
    • 

  • Policy: invariant TSC, then HPET, then a plain TSC with a warning.
    • 

  • Counts become nanoseconds by splitting into seconds and remainder: exact and overflow-free.
    • 

  • Tickless: the APIC timer is armed for the earliest deadline in a sorted queue; a periodic tick is one more timer.
    • 

  • sti; hlt prevents a lost wake-up in sleep_ns.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. TSC 817,626,796 Hz against 2,098,933,108 Hz in pit.out: a ratio of 0.390. APIC timer 24,300,654 Hz against 62,434,288 Hz: 0.389. Both are wrong by the same factor, because the APIC timer was calibrated against the TSC-based now_ns(). The host saw 3.894 s for the kernel's "10.000643 s"; between "sleep: start" and "B8 ok" the kernel's timestamps advance 12.35 − 0.20 = 12.15 s while the host's advance 5.00 − 0.27 = 4.73 s, the same ratio.


The tell-tale line. "windows of 11931 and 53782 ticks (119318 requested)": the long window requested and the one programmed differ.


Hypotheses. A slow QEMU would make the kernel's clock lag, not run fast. A non-invariant TSC changing speed would drift over time, not be wrong by a constant factor from the start. The calibration line shows the cause directly.


Root cause. kLong = kPitHz / 10 = 119,318 does not fit in the PIT's 16-bit counter; the cast to uint16_t (line 154, and the parameter of pit_window_tsc) keeps 119,318 − 65,536 = 53,782. The arithmetic on line 155 uses the intended 119,318, so the TSC cycles of 41,851 real ticks are divided by the time of 107,387 ticks. The kernel cannot notice, because every later measurement uses the same wrong rate.


Fix and prevention. Use a window that fits (59,659 ticks, 50 ms, as the normal build does) or chain several windows for longer ones. Add static_assert(kLong <= 0xFFFF && kShort <= 0xFFFF), and make -Wconversion part of the kernel build so that narrowing casts are visible. Keep the host-clock comparison in the lab: it is the only test that sees calibration errors.
