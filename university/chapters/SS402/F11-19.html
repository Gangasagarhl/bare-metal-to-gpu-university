Story hook

Sana's uncle Farid works for a company that designs car seats. One evening he shows her a long list on his laptop. Each line is a way a passenger can be hurt: a crash from the front, from the side, from behind; a child who sits too close to the dashboard; a passenger who is very tall; a seat that slides forward when the car brakes hard. Next to each line are three small numbers.

"Before we design anything," Farid says, "we write this list. How badly could someone be hurt? How often is a passenger in that situation? Could they do anything to avoid it? The worst lines get the most protection, and the most testing." Sana points at a line with no numbers. "That one is new," he says. "A colleague found it last week: what happens when the seat is folded flat to carry boxes and someone sits on it anyway? Nobody had thought of the seat being used that way."

Sana thinks the list is the boring part. Farid thinks it is the most important part. Every airbag, every belt, every test in the car exists because a line on that list asked for it. And a danger that never made it onto the list gets no protection at all, however good the engineering is.

This chapter writes that list for the course robot, scores each line, and checks the list for the kind of gap Farid's colleague found: a way of using the machine that nobody analysed.

What you will be able to do

  • Identify hazards systematically, by operating mode and life phase, with guide words, so that the list does not depend on who happened to be in the room.
  • Estimate risk with a risk graph (severity, exposure, avoidability) and turn it into a target for risk reduction, using the course's own graph and knowing how the standards' graphs differ.
  • Maintain a hazard log and check it for completeness with the course's checker: modes without hazards, hazards without safety functions, safety functions without tests, exposure that contradicts usage data.
  • Build a fault tree, find its minimal cut sets, compute the probability of the top event, and explain why a basic event that appears twice makes simple gate arithmetic wrong.
  • Diagnose a hazard analysis that was reviewed and signed yet missed the hazard that caused an incident.
Jargon box
Hazard analysis
Simple words: listing everything about a machine that could hurt someone, in every way it is used, and judging how bad each one is. Analogy: Farid's list of ways a passenger can be hurt, written before any airbag is designed. Precise: the lifecycle activity that identifies the hazards, hazardous situations and hazardous events of the equipment under control in all reasonably foreseeable conditions (operating modes, life phases, foreseeable misuse), estimates the risk of each, and determines the risk reduction required; called hazard and risk analysis in IEC 61508 and hazard analysis and risk assessment (HARA) in ISO 26262. D1 D2
Operating mode and life phase
Simple words: the different ways a machine is used (working, being taught, charging) and the stages of its life (built, moved, repaired, scrapped). Analogy: a seat used for sitting, folded for boxes, or taken out at the garage. Precise: a mode is a defined state of operation with its own behaviour and people present (automatic, teach, maintenance); a life phase is a period from transport and installation through use, maintenance and decommissioning; hazards are identified for every combination that can occur. D4
Hazard log
Simple words: the list of hazards, kept up to date for the whole life of the machine, with what is done about each one. Analogy: Farid's list, with a column saying which airbag or belt answers each line. Precise: a controlled record of each identified hazard with its context (mode), risk estimate, required risk reduction, the safety functions and other measures that address it, their verification, and its status; versioned and updated on every change. D1
Severity, exposure, avoidability
Simple words: how badly someone could be hurt, how often someone is in the dangerous situation, and whether they could get out of the way. Analogy: Farid's three small numbers on each line. Precise: the parameters of a risk graph: severity (S) classes the worst credible harm; exposure (E) classes how often or how long people are in the hazardous situation; avoidability (A, called controllability C in ISO 26262) classes whether the people involved can avoid the harm once the event starts. D1 D2
Risk graph
Simple words: a fixed table that turns the three numbers into "how much protection this line needs". Analogy: the company rule that says which crashes need an airbag and which need only a belt. Precise: a qualitative method that maps combinations of risk parameters to a required risk-reduction class (an integrity level in a standard; a course target CT0 to CT3 in this course); calibrated by whoever defines it, and valid only with that calibration. D1
Course target (CT)
Simple words: this course's own label, from 0 to 3, for how much protection a hazard needs. Analogy: a practice grading scale, not the real exam's. Precise: SS402's risk-reduction class: points = 2·S + E + A; CT0 for at most 7 points, CT1 for 8–9, CT2 for 10–11, CT3 for 12–13; defined by this course for teaching, deliberately not equal to any standard's integrity levels (F11-20). R1
FMEA (failure modes and effects analysis)
Simple words: go part by part and ask "how can this part fail, and what happens then?". Analogy: checking each piece of the seat in turn: the rail, the lock, the belt buckle. Precise: a bottom-up (inductive) analysis that lists, for each component or function, its failure modes, their local and system-level effects, and how each is detected and mitigated; finds single points of failure; complements top-down analysis. D5
Fault tree analysis (FTA)
Simple words: start from one bad outcome and ask "what would have to go wrong for this to happen?", again and again, down to single parts. Analogy: starting from "the seat slid forward" and working back to the worn lock and the missing check. Precise: a top-down (deductive) analysis that decomposes an undesired top event through AND and OR gates into basic events; yields minimal cut sets and, with probabilities for the basic events, a probability for the top event. D5
Minimal cut set
Simple words: a smallest group of failures that, together, is enough to cause the bad outcome. Analogy: "the lock is worn AND nobody checked it": both together, and nothing less, make the seat slide. Precise: a set of basic events whose joint occurrence causes the top event, such that no proper subset does; a cut set of size one is a single point of failure. D5

Terms from F11-18: harm, hazard, risk, safety function, safe state, common-cause failure. "Hazard" is the functional-safety term, not the pipeline hazard of F1-26. The parallel with threat modelling (F11-14) is close and useful: a threat model asks what an attacker could do; a hazard analysis asks what could go wrong without anyone wanting it to.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Before you build a protection, you need to know what it protects against. So you sit down with the robot and ask: how could this hurt someone? You ask it for every way the robot is used. When it works by itself. When a person teaches it by moving its arm. When it is charging. When someone repairs it.

For each danger you write three things. How badly could someone be hurt? How often is someone close by? Could they step out of the way? A bad injury, near people every day, with no time to step away, gets the most protection. A small bump that happens rarely gets less.

The list is never finished. Each time you change the robot, you read the list again. And the most dangerous line is the one that is not on the list at all, because nobody protects against a danger they never wrote down.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Step 1: describe the system. A hazard analysis is only as good as its description of the system. Write down the boundary (the robot with its base and arm, its charger, its e-stop chain; not the building), its functions (drive, fetch, grasp, carry, dock, be taught a pose), its operating modes and the people present in each (operate: learners walk past in the corridor; teach: a learner holds the arm; charge: nobody, or someone plugging in; maintenance: a technician with hands in the drive train), and its life phases (transport to the lab, first switch-on, normal use, repair, software update, scrapping with the battery). Most missed hazards come from a mode or phase nobody wrote down. D4

Step 2: identify hazards with guide words. Asking "what could go wrong?" in a meeting finds the hazards that the people present happen to think of. Guide words make the search systematic: apply each word to each function in each mode. For a motion function: no motion when commanded (a stuck arm in a doorway), more (overspeed, too much force), less (a weak grip drops the object), reverse (a sign error), early or late (moves before the person has let go), other than (a different joint moves), after (moves after a stop command, the course forensic case of F11-21). This is the idea of a HAZOP study, adapted for functions. Checklists of energy sources (kinetic, gravity on the arm, stored energy in the battery and springs, electrical, heat) catch hazards that are not functions at all. D4 D5

Bottom up and top down. Two analyses complement the guide words. An FMEA goes part by part: encoder A can freeze, drift or reverse; the relay can weld or fail open; each failure mode gets a local effect, a system effect and a detection. It finds single points of failure that a functional view misses. A fault tree goes the other way: from one hazardous event down through AND and OR gates to basic events. It shows which combinations of failures matter (minimal cut sets) and, with probabilities, how likely the top event is. A good analysis uses both and checks that every FMEA effect that reaches a hazard appears in the matching fault tree. D5

Step 3: hazardous events. Write each line as a short scenario: who, where, doing what, and what the machine does. "Fingers pinched between arm links while a learner guides the arm by hand in teach mode" is analysable; "pinch hazard" is not, because severity, exposure and avoidability depend on the situation. D1

Steps 4 and 5: estimate the risk. For each hazardous event, choose three classes, using the course's definitions. These classes are the university's own, made for a small lab robot; they are not taken from a standard:

Step 6: decide the risk reduction. For each hazard with CT1 or higher, the first question is whether the design can remove or reduce it (lower the arm's maximum torque so a pinch is a bruise, not a fracture: S drops). Only what remains gets a safety function, and the course target becomes that function's integrity requirement (F11-20 maps it to the standards' ideas). H8, "robot drops the fetched object on a foot", is CT0 because the course robot's objects are light (S1); if the robot carried heavy tools, S would rise and the line would need a measure. Every number in a hazard log is an assumption about the robot and its use, and the assumptions must be written next to the numbers. R1


The hazard log as a living record. The log is versioned (this is v1.2) and every change to the robot, its software, its environment or its use triggers a review of it. The checker in Listing 1 automates four completeness checks the course requires: every declared mode has at least one hazard; every hazard with CT1 or higher has a safety function; every safety function listed for a hazard has a test; and the exposure class declared for a hazard is not lower than the class implied by the measured share of operating time in its mode (the USAGE line, from the robot's logs). The last check is the one people skip: exposure is a guess until someone measures how the robot is used. R1

How the standards estimate risk, at reading level. Both IEC 61508 and ISO 26262 start from hazard analysis and turn risk parameters into a required level, but they do it differently, and the course's graph is a simplification of neither. The box records what the author recalls, for you to check. D1 D2


Not verified — check before relying on this
Risk parameters in the standards. From the author's memory, not checked in this build: ISO 26262 Part 3 (concept phase) defines a hazard analysis and risk assessment in which each hazardous event of a vehicle function is classified by severity (classes S0 to S3), probability of exposure (E0 to E4) and controllability by the driver or others (C0 to C3), and a table maps the combination to an ASIL (A to D) or to QM (quality management, no ASIL); the results are safety goals with an ASIL each. IEC 61508 Part 5 gives examples of methods for determining safety integrity levels, among them a risk graph with consequence, frequency of exposure, possibility of avoiding the hazard and probability of the unwanted occurrence, and a layer-of-protection analysis; the user calibrates the method for the application. Machinery standards (ISO 12100 for risk assessment, ISO 13849-1 for performance levels) use their own parameters. Read the cited parts of the current editions before using any table; never copy a table from memory into a safety document. D1 D2 D4


Why calibration matters. A risk graph is a function from a few classes to a level. Its output means something only because someone chose the class boundaries and the mapping so that each level corresponds to a tolerable risk for a given population and use. The course graph was chosen for teaching: it is monotonic (raising any parameter never lowers the target), it weighs severity more than the other two, and its extremes are sensible for a lab robot. It has not been calibrated against injury data, because the course has none. That is why its classes are called CT, not SIL or ASIL: using a standard's name for an uncalibrated graph would claim something the course cannot support. R1


Exposure from data, not from opinion. In the forensic lab, a team declared exposure E1 ("rare") for a pinch hazard while their own logs showed the robot spending 30 % of its time in the mode where it occurs. The checker's exposure rule turns that disagreement into a finding. The general lesson is that each parameter of a risk graph should be backed by an argument or a measurement written in the log: a usage log for exposure, an injury mechanism for severity (force, speed, mass, pinch geometry), a reaction-time argument for avoidability (can a person withdraw a hand from a joint moving at 150 deg/s?). R3


Fault trees and repeated events. Listing 2 evaluates the tree of Figure 2 three ways:






The minimal cut sets say what the tree means. Three single points of failure: the button's contacts, the relay's logic, and an overcurrent that welds both contactors at once. One pair: K1 and K2 welded independently. The pair contributes 1 × 10−6; the common cause contributes 5 × 10−4, five hundred times more. The redundancy of two contactors is nearly worthless against the shared cause, and the cut sets show it at a glance. R2


The three probabilities show a classic mistake. "Gate arithmetic" combines each gate's inputs as if they were independent (OR: 1 − Π(1 − p); AND: Π p). It gives 3.0 × 10−4, but the exact value, from enumerating all 25 combinations of the basic events, is 8.0 × 10−4. The gate arithmetic is wrong because the two inputs of the AND gate both contain overcurrent_welds_both; multiplying their probabilities squares that event's probability, as if the overcurrent had to happen twice. Repeated events are exactly the common causes a safety analysis cares about, so a tool or a hand calculation that ignores them is optimistic in the most dangerous place. The sum over minimal cut sets (the rare-event approximation) is close to exact here because every probability is small. R2


Where the probabilities come from. The numbers in fault_tree.in are illustrative. In a real analysis, basic-event probabilities come from the component makers' reliability data, from recognised failure-rate handbooks, or from field data, with the conditions of use (load, temperature, number of operations) stated, and they are combined with the time between proof tests (F11-20). Many analyses of software-intensive systems stay qualitative for the software parts, because software failures are systematic and have no meaningful rate. D1 D5

Analysis techniques and their standards. From the author's memory, not checked in this build: FMEA is described in IEC 60812, fault tree analysis in IEC 61025, and HAZOP studies in IEC 61882. FMEA practice sometimes ranks failure modes with a risk priority number (the product of severity, occurrence and detection ratings), and that practice has known weaknesses (different combinations give the same number; the ratings are ordinal); some current guidance prefers other ranking schemes. Systems-theoretic process analysis (STPA), from Nancy Leveson's work, analyses unsafe control actions in a control structure rather than component failures, and is used alongside FMEA and FTA for software-intensive systems. Check the current editions and texts before relying on any of this. D5



How the hardware actually does it


Hazards are physical. Each line of the hazard log ends in energy reaching a person: the kinetic energy of the base and the arm, gravity acting on the arm and on whatever it holds, the clamping force of the gripper and of two links closing on each other, stored electrical energy in the battery (heat, fire) and in the charger contacts, and stored mechanical energy in springs and in a brake that is released. Severity follows from how much energy can be delivered and how it is concentrated (a pinch point concentrates force on a finger). The parameters that a hardware engineer can change are the strongest risk reduction available: lower drive current limits, smaller gear ratios, rounded links and pinch-point covers, a brake that holds the arm when power is lost, a charger that keeps its contacts dead until the robot is docked. Every such change is a design measure (step 6's first choice) and must be recorded in the log so that nobody later "optimises" it away. The real values of a kit's motor torque, battery energy or brake holding force come only from that kit's datasheets (dossier part F); none are given here because no kit was available in this build. D4


Safety
Hazard analysis of a real robot is done with the robot switched off, or with its drives disabled at the hard-wired chain. To observe a mode (teach, maintenance), have the supervisor present, keep hands out of pinch points while the drives are enabled, and follow the battery maker's instructions for any work near the battery. Do not create a hazardous situation on purpose to "measure exposure": use the robot's logs. C1



Worked example


A. Score H5 by hand. "Arm starts moving when switching from teach to operate." The learner's hands are on the arm in teach mode, so the worst credible harm is a serious injury to a hand: S3. Teach mode is 18 % of operating time: E3 (10 % or more). The motion starts while the hands are engaged: A2. Points = 2 × 3 + 3 + 2 = 11, which is CT2 (10–11). The log line in hazard_log.out shows CT2. R1


B. What one class changes. If a design change made the arm unable to move at more than a slow, warned speed when leaving teach mode, A would drop to 1: points 10, still CT2. If the drives' torque limit in teach mode made the worst harm a bruise, S would drop to 1: points 2 + 3 + 2 = 7, CT0. Severity counts twice in the course graph, so reducing it is the strongest lever, as Layer 2 said.


C. The fault tree, exactly, by hand. Let pb = 0.0001, pr = 0.0002, k = 0.001 for each contactor, c = 0.0005 for the overcurrent. Both contactors stay closed if the overcurrent happens, or if it does not and both weld independently: c + (1 − c)·k2 = 0.0005 + 0.9995 × 0.000001 = 0.0005010. The top event is the OR of three independent events: 1 − (1 − 0.0001)(1 − 0.0002)(1 − 0.0005010) = 0.0008008, matching the program's exact value. Gate arithmetic instead computes each contactor's OR as 1 − (1 − 0.001)(1 − 0.0005) = 0.0014995 and squares it: 0.0000022, about 225 times too small for that branch.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Farid's company has years of crash data to set its numbers; the course robot has none. The analogy makes the three small numbers look more certain than they are for a new machine.
    • 

  • A car seat is used in a few well-known ways. A robot can be reprogrammed into new behaviours, so its "foreseeable uses" change with every software release, and the hazard log must follow.
    • 

  • In the story, the list is written before the design. In practice, hazard analysis and design go back and forth: every protection you add (a teach mode, a charger) brings its own new hazards.
    • 

  • Crash protections act on the car's occupants, who chose to ride. Many robot hazards affect bystanders who never chose to be near the robot, which changes what risk is tolerable.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Analysing only the main mode. Symptom: every hazard is in "operate". Cause: the analysis followed the demo, not the robot's life. Find it: the checker's "mode has no hazards" finding; a walk through every life phase. Fix: one session per mode and phase, with someone who actually does that work (the technician for maintenance).
    • 

  • Exposure by opinion. Symptom: E1 for a mode that is used every day. Find it: the exposure check against the usage log (forensic lab). Fix: measure usage from logs; write the source next to the number.
    • 

  • Severity of the typical case. Symptom: "the arm usually only bumps" (S1). Fix: severity is the worst credible harm in that situation, not the most frequent one.
    • 

  • Counting the safety function before it exists. Symptom: risk is scored low "because the monitor will stop it". Cause: scoring the residual risk instead of the risk without the safety function. Fix: score the hazard without the safety function; the function's target is what the score demands.
    • 

  • Gate arithmetic on a tree with repeated events. Symptom: a probability far below the cut-set sum. Find it: look for an event name that appears twice in the tree. Fix: minimal cut sets and exact or cut-set-based quantification (Listing 2).
    • 

  • A hazard log that never changes. Symptom: version 1.0, dated at the start of the project. Fix: every change request asks "does this change the hazard log?", and the answer is recorded.
    •

Summary


    

  • A hazard analysis describes the system, identifies hazards in every mode and life phase, describes hazardous events, estimates their risk and decides the risk reduction; then it is repeated after every change.
    • 

  • Guide words, energy checklists, FMEA (bottom up) and fault trees (top down) make hazard identification systematic.
    • 

  • Risk graphs map severity, exposure and avoidability to a required level; they are valid only with their calibration. SS402's graph (points = 2S + E + A, CT0 to CT3) is a teaching graph, not a standard's.
    • 

  • Every parameter needs a source; exposure should come from usage data.
    • 

  • Score hazards without the safety function you are about to add; the score sets that function's target.
    • 

  • Minimal cut sets reveal single points of failure and common causes; gate arithmetic is wrong when an event repeats.
    • 

  • The hazard log is versioned, checked for completeness, and cited as evidence in the safety case.
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Hazards depend on who is present and what the machine does, and both change between modes and life phases: a technician's hands are in the drive train only in maintenance; a learner holds the arm only in teach mode. An analysis of normal operation alone misses exactly those situations, and incidents often happen in them.
    2. 

  2. Severity: a foot under a slow base is a reversible injury at worst, S2. Exposure: 19 % is E3. Avoidability: slow and audible, A1. Points 2 × 2 + 3 + 1 = 8, CT1. (A heavier or faster base would raise S or A.)
    3. 

  3. Because the course graph is not calibrated against injury data or a standard's tables. Using SIL or ASIL names would suggest a correspondence and a validity it does not have; CT is honest about being a teaching scale.
    4.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. Listing 1 run on forensic.in, a log written for this lab with three planted defects: no teach-mode hazards although teach mode is declared and used 30 % of the time; the pinch hazard placed in operate mode with E1; H7 with safety functions but no test.


    

  1. "FINDING mode 'teach' has no hazards analysed". The incident happened in teach mode, which the log declared and the usage line shows at 30 % of operating time.
    2. 

  2. H3 was scored S2, E1, A2: 4 + 1 + 2 = 7 points, CT0, so no safety function was required. E1 contradicts the usage data (operate is 55 %, E3), and the situation was analysed in the wrong mode.
    3. 

  3. With E3 (as the checker says): 4 + 3 + 2 = 9, CT1, which requires a safety function. For the incident's situation, teach mode with hands on the arm: a crushed finger can be an irreversible injury (S3), teach mode is 30 % (E3), and with hands engaged the learner can hardly avoid it (A2): 6 + 3 + 2 = 11, CT2. The line should have been a teach-mode hazard "fingers pinched between arm links while guiding by hand", CT2, with safety functions such as reduced speed and force in teach mode and an enabling switch.
    4. 

  4. H4 (e-stop) declares E2 in a mode used 55 % of the time (E3). Its course target stays CT2 (10 points become 11), but the exposure was not taken from the data, which says the review did not check any exposure against usage. H7 (maintenance) has safety functions SF4 and SF9 and no test, so nobody knows whether they work. Both are the root causes of a later incident in waiting.
    5. 

  5. The panel checked the lines that were there, not the lines that were missing, and accepted exposure classes without data. Changes: run the completeness checker (or an equivalent checklist) before any review and refuse to sign with open findings; require a usage source for every exposure class and one guide-word session per declared mode, attended by someone who works in that mode.
    6. 



Wrong answers and why. "The learner should have been more careful": avoidability was part of the analysis and was scored A2 by the team itself. "The checker would have prevented it": the checker finds gaps in a log; it cannot find a hazard in a mode that is not declared at all, which is why the system description of step 1 is reviewed too.
