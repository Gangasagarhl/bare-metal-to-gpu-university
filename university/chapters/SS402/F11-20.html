Story hook

Ifeoma's class visits a bus depot. A mechanic named Rui shows them two machines side by side: a go-kart from the park next door and a school bus. Both have brakes. The go-kart's brake is a single cable. Someone looks at it every few weeks, and if it feels soft, they tighten it.

The school bus is different. It has two separate brake circuits, so that one leak does not leave it without brakes. A warning lamp on the dashboard tells the driver when the pressure in a circuit drops. Every so often a mechanic tests both circuits on a machine and signs a sheet. A second mechanic checks the first one's work and signs too. "Why so much fuss?" asks Ifeoma's friend Daniel. "A brake is a brake."

Rui laughs. "Count the children who ride in each one," he says. "The go-kart carries one person, slowly, on a closed track. The bus carries fifty children on a motorway. Same idea, same job, but the rules for how carefully it is built, how often it is tested and who checks the tester are much stricter for the bus. The more depends on the brake, the more we must be able to prove that it works."

That grading, from go-kart to school bus, is what the safety standards call an integrity level. This chapter looks at how three standards grade it, why the grade contains two very different things, and how to calculate one of them.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what an integrity level grades: a target for random hardware failures and a degree of rigour against systematic failures, and why software gets only the second.
  • Compare, at reading level, the safety integrity levels of IEC 61508, the ASILs of ISO 26262 and the software levels of DO-178C, and say why there is no exact conversion between them.
  • Derive the average probability of failure on demand of one channel and of two channels with a common cause, and check the derivation with a Monte Carlo simulation.
  • Show, with numbers, how proof-test intervals and common causes dominate the benefit of redundancy.
  • Apply the course's own requirements for each course target (CT1 to CT3) to the course robot's safety functions.
Jargon box
Integrity level
Simple words: a grade that says how reliable a protection must be, and so how carefully it must be built and checked. Analogy: the go-kart's brake rules versus the school bus's brake rules. Precise: a discrete level assigned to a safety function (or to the system or software element implementing it) from the risk reduction it must provide; it sets both quantitative targets for random hardware failures (where the standard has them) and the rigour of the measures against systematic failures. D1
SIL (safety integrity level)
Simple words: IEC 61508's grade, from 1 (lowest) to 4 (highest). Analogy: four classes of vehicle, each with stricter brake rules. Precise: one of four discrete levels in IEC 61508 for specifying the safety integrity requirements of safety functions allocated to safety-related systems; SIL 4 is the highest; each SIL has a band of target failure measures for low-demand and for high-demand or continuous mode. D1
ASIL (automotive safety integrity level)
Simple words: the car industry's grade, from A (lowest) to D (highest), with QM for "normal quality is enough". Analogy: the same idea, written for cars. Precise: one of four levels in ISO 26262 (A to D, D the most stringent) assigned to safety goals from the severity, exposure and controllability of hazardous events; QM denotes no ASIL requirement; requirements and their ASILs are inherited by the elements that implement them. D2
Software level (DAL)
Simple words: aviation's grade for software, from A (most critical) to E (no safety effect). Analogy: the rule book for the software inside the bus's brake controller. Precise: in DO-178C, levels A to E assigned to software from the most severe failure condition its anomalous behaviour can cause (determined by the system safety assessment); the level sets which objectives apply and with what independence; often called a development assurance level (DAL). D3
Low-demand and high-demand mode
Simple words: is the protection needed rarely (and must work when asked), or is it working all the time? Analogy: an airbag (rare) versus the brakes (every stop). Precise: in IEC 61508, a safety function in low-demand mode is demanded rarely (the standard states a frequency boundary) and its target is the average probability of dangerous failure on demand (PFDavg); in high-demand or continuous mode its target is the average frequency of dangerous failure per hour (PFH). D1
PFDavg
Simple words: the chance that the protection is broken at the moment it is needed, averaged over time. Analogy: the chance that the bus's brake has an unnoticed leak on the day a child runs into the road. Precise: the time-averaged probability that a safety function is in a dangerous failed state, and therefore cannot act, when a demand occurs; for a channel with dangerous undetected failure rate λ and perfect proof tests every T hours, approximately λT/2 when λT is small (derived in Layer 3). D1 R1
Proof test
Simple words: a full, periodic test that finds hidden faults in a protection that is not used day to day. Analogy: the mechanic testing both brake circuits on the machine and signing the sheet. Precise: a periodic test that detects dangerous undetected failures of a safety-related system so that they can be repaired, returning it (ideally) to an "as new" condition; the proof-test interval T bounds how long such a failure can stay hidden. D1
Dangerous undetected failure
Simple words: a fault that stops the protection from working and that nothing notices. Analogy: a slow leak in a brake circuit with a broken warning lamp. Precise: a failure that prevents the safety function from acting on demand and is not revealed by diagnostics; revealed only by a demand or by a proof test; its rate is written λDU. D1
MooN architecture (1oo1, 1oo2)
Simple words: how many channels there are, and how many must agree to act. Analogy: a bus with one brake circuit, or two where either one stops the bus. Precise: "M out of N": N independent channels, of which M must act for the safety function to act; 1oo1 is a single channel; in 1oo2 either of two channels can bring the safe state, so both must fail for the function to fail. D1
Beta factor
Simple words: the share of failures that hit both channels at once. Analogy: the stone that cuts both brake lines because they run side by side. Precise: in the beta-factor model of common-cause failure, the fraction β of each channel's dangerous failure rate that is assumed to fail all channels simultaneously; the rest, (1 − β)λ, fails channels independently. D1

Terms from F11-18 and F11-19: random hardware failure, systematic failure, common-cause failure, risk graph, course target (CT). "Integrity" here is about how reliably a safety function works; it is not data integrity (F11-01).

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 puts the three standards' scales side by side. The arrow is the only thing they share for certain: up means more risk reduction required, more rigour, more independence. The dashed lines between columns are deliberately absent: Layer 3 explains why.

Figure 2 shows why a hidden failure matters and why proof tests help. After each proof test the channel is as good as new; then the probability that it has failed without anyone noticing grows until the next test resets it. PFDavg is the average height of this sawtooth.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Some protections matter more than others. A brake on a go-kart and a brake on a school bus do the same job. But many more people depend on the bus. So the rules for the bus are stricter: two brakes instead of one, more tests, and a second person who checks the tests.

Safety rule books give each protection a grade. A low grade means "build it carefully". A high grade means "build it very carefully, test it often, have someone else check, and show your proof". The grade comes from the list of dangers in the last chapter: the worse and more frequent the danger, the higher the grade.

There is one surprise. A brake part can wear out, so you can count how often it breaks. A computer program does not wear out. If it is wrong, it is wrong every time. So for programs, the grade cannot be a number of failures. It says how carefully the program must be written and checked.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What a level grades. In F11-19, the risk graph turned each hazardous event into a required amount of risk reduction. An integrity level is that requirement attached to the safety function (or to the goal it serves). It controls two different things, because a safety function can fail in the two ways of F11-18. For random hardware failures, a level can set a quantitative target: how probable may it be that the function fails when needed, or how often may it fail dangerously per hour of operation. For systematic failures, which have no meaningful rate, a level sets the rigour of the measures that prevent and remove them: which analyses, which review and test methods, how much independence between the people who build and the people who check, how strict the configuration control. Software, being purely systematic, gets only the second kind. D1 D2 D3

Rarely needed or always working. The right quantitative measure depends on how the function is used. The arm's e-stop path is demanded rarely: most days nobody presses the button. What matters is the probability that it is broken when somebody does: PFDavg. The speed monitor of F11-18 is different: it is effectively watching all the time, and a dangerous failure of it in combination with a controller fault can happen at any moment; the measure is a dangerous failure frequency per hour (PFH). This chapter's calculation is for the low-demand case, where the arithmetic is clean and shows the main effects. D1

Hidden failures and proof tests. A protection that is not used every day can fail without anyone noticing: a relay contact welds, a sensor wire breaks behind a connector. That is a dangerous undetected failure. Two things limit how long it can stay hidden: diagnostics (the channel tests itself, as the bus's pressure lamp does, and turns a hidden failure into a detected one that leads to the safe state) and proof tests (someone exercises the whole function periodically, as the bus mechanic does). Between tests the probability of being failed grows (Figure 2); the longer the interval, the larger the average. D1

Redundancy and its limit. Two channels where either one can stop the arm (1oo2) fail only if both fail. If they fail independently, the probability is roughly the square of one channel's, which is much smaller. But any common cause, a shared power supply or one firmware image, fails both at once, and that part is not squared. The lab run shows that even a modest common-cause share erases most of the benefit. R1

Integrity levels as the author recalls them. IEC 61508: four SILs; Part 1 gives, for each SIL, a band of PFDavg for low-demand mode and a band of PFH for high-demand or continuous mode (the band boundaries are powers of ten; this chapter deliberately does not write them, read the tables); Part 2 adds hardware requirements (architectural constraints expressed through hardware fault tolerance and safe failure fraction, or through field-proven data) and requirements for diagnostics; Part 3 gives tables of software techniques and measures with recommendations that strengthen with the SIL; the "systematic capability" of an element states up to which SIL its development process is adequate. ISO 26262: ASILs A to D and QM from the hazard analysis of Part 3; hardware metrics (single-point fault metric, latent fault metric, and an evaluation of the probability of violating a safety goal) in Part 5; software requirements with method tables per ASIL in Part 6; ASIL decomposition in Part 9 lets a requirement be split over two sufficiently independent elements with lower ASILs. DO-178C: software levels A to E from the failure condition categories (catastrophic, hazardous or severe-major, major, minor, no safety effect) determined at system level; Annex A tables list the objectives per level, which of them must be met with independence, and the data to produce; structural coverage to MC/DC is an objective at level A; no failure probabilities are assigned to software. Check every item against the current edition of each standard. D1 D2 D3

Why there is no exact conversion. Tables that put "SIL 2 ≈ ASIL B ≈ DAL C" in one row circulate widely, and they mislead. The scales are derived from different risk parameters with different calibrations (a car's driver can sometimes control a hazard; an aircraft's software level comes from a system safety assessment of failure conditions; IEC 61508's levels come from a required risk reduction), they attach to different things (a safety function, a safety goal, a software item), and they impose different sets of requirements. An element developed to one standard can be argued into a system under another, but the argument is about which requirements were met, not about a conversion of labels. D1 D2 D3

Deriving PFDavg. This derivation is arithmetic on a stated model, so it can be checked here, unlike the standards' tables. Model: a channel fails dangerously and undetected at a constant rate λ (per hour), so the probability that it has failed by time t after a perfect proof test is F(t) = 1 − e−λt, which is close to λt when λt is small. Demands arrive at random times, independent of the channel's state, so the probability that the function fails on demand is the time average of F over one proof-test interval T:

  • 1oo1: PFDavg = (1/T) ∫0T λt dt = λT/2.
  • 1oo2, independent channels: both must have failed, (λt)2, so PFDavg = (1/T) ∫0T (λt)2 dt = (λT)2/3.
  • 1oo2 with a common cause (beta-factor model): the independent part has rate (1 − β)λ per channel and the common part βλ fails both. To first order the two ways to fail add: PFDavg ≈ ((1 − β)λT)2/3 + βλT/2.

Listing 1 prints these formulas next to a Monte Carlo estimate that does not use them: for each of a million proof-test intervals it draws exponential failure times for the channels (and the common cause), finds when the function stopped being able to act, and adds up the hours it spent failed before the next test. The rates are illustrative: λ = 2 × 10−6 per hour, not the rate of any product. R1





Read the output in three steps. First, the model is right: the simulated values agree with the formulas to within a few per cent. For one channel the simulation (8.704 × 10−3) sits slightly below λT/2 = 8.760 × 10−3 because the formula is first order; the exact average of 1 − e−λt is 8.709 × 10−3 (worked example A). For the two-channel cases, the difference is mostly sampling noise: with independent channels, both fail within a year in only a few hundred of the million simulated intervals. R1


Second, redundancy works only with independence. Two independent channels give 1.02 × 10−4, about 86 times better than one. With β = 0.05 the value is 5.3 × 10−4, and with β = 0.10 it is 9.6 × 10−4: the second channel now buys a factor of about 9, and almost all of what remains is the common-cause term βλT/2 = 8.76 × 10−4. Every effort that reduces β (separate supplies, separate routing, diverse designs, staggered maintenance) is worth more than a better component. R1


Third, testing more often is a strong lever. Quarterly proof tests (T = 2190 h) cut the single channel to 2.19 × 10−3 (a factor of 4, linear in T) and the β = 0.10 pair to 2.2 × 10−4. A proof test, however, is only as good as its coverage: a test that does not exercise the part that failed does not reset its sawtooth. The forensic lab shows a proof test that was not done at all. R1


What this model leaves out. Real assessments also count diagnostics (detected failures lead to the safe state, so only the undetected rate λDU enters the formulas), repair time, imperfect proof tests, failures of the proof test itself, demand rates that are not small, and the probability of spurious trips (a safety function that stops the robot too often gets bypassed, which is a systematic failure of the organisation). The standards give formulas or methods that include several of these effects; do not use the three formulas above for a real system without checking the method your standard prescribes. D1

Decomposition and its trap. ISO 26262 lets a high-ASIL requirement be met by two lower-ASIL elements, if they are sufficiently independent. The lab numbers show why the condition matters: the benefit of two channels depends almost entirely on β, and β is not a property of either element but of how they are built, powered, programmed and maintained together. A decomposition argument is therefore mostly an independence argument, and it fails silently if a later change introduces a shared part (the forensic lab of F11-18). D2 R1


Software levels in practice. For software, the level decides the process: what must be specified, reviewed, analysed and tested, to what coverage, with what independence, and how the tools that produce or check the software are trusted. F11-22 runs one of those objectives, MC/DC coverage of a decision, on the course robot's drive-enable rule. The course's CT2 row asks for it because it is a concrete, checkable stand-in for "more rigour"; it does not make the software level A or anything else. D3



How the hardware actually does it


The random-failure side of an integrity level is decided by hardware facts: the dangerous failure rates of each component (from the maker's reliability data, recognised handbooks or field data, under stated conditions), the fraction of them the diagnostics detect, how the channels are separated, and how the proof test reaches each part. A relay's contact can weld; a read-back contact (a mechanically linked auxiliary contact) lets the logic see the weld, turning a dangerous undetected failure into a detected one. An encoder can freeze; comparing two encoders detects it. A processor can hang; a watchdog detects it (F11-21). Separate power supplies, separate cable routes and separate connectors reduce β. None of these values can be given here: AH-21 forbids hardware numbers that are not from a named product's datasheet, and no product was used. The λ of the lab is an illustrative number chosen to make the arithmetic visible. D1

Worked example


A. One channel, exactly. λT = 2 × 10−6 × 8760 = 0.01752. The first-order formula gives 0.01752 / 2 = 0.008760. The exact average of 1 − e−λt over one interval is 1 − (1 − e−λT)/(λT); expanding, it is λT/2 − (λT)2/6 + …; the second term is 0.017522/6 = 0.0000512, so the exact value is 0.008709. The simulation printed 8.704 × 10−3, within its sampling noise of the exact value. R1


B. Two channels with β = 0.10. Independent part: (0.9 × 0.01752)2/3 = 0.015772/3 = 0.0000829. Common part: 0.1 × 0.01752 / 2 = 0.000876. Sum: 0.000959, the formula column's 9.589 × 10−4. The common part is 91 % of the total.


C. Which proof-test interval meets a target? Suppose a course reviewer asks for PFDavg below 1 × 10−3 for the single-channel e-stop path (an illustrative requirement). With one channel: λT/2 ≤ 10−3 gives T ≤ 2 × 10−3/λ = 1000 h, so roughly a test every six weeks of continuous operation. With the β = 0.10 pair, the common part alone is 10−7 × T, and the target is met with a yearly test (9.6 × 10−4 from the table). The decision is then about which is more credible: a test every six weeks that people actually do, or a β of 0.10 that someone must argue.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A bus carries fifty children, so its brakes matter more. An integrity level is not set by the number of people alone but by severity, exposure, avoidability and the other protections that exist; a protection with a good one behind it may need a lower level.
    • 

  • The bus's second brake circuit is an independent copy that wears independently. Two copies of the same software are not independent at all; the analogy makes redundancy look like an answer to bugs.
    • 

  • The bus inspection is the same every year. A real proof test must be designed to reach each hidden failure, and a test that misses a part leaves that part's sawtooth growing (Figure 2).
    • 

  • Rui can say "the bus is stricter" without numbers. The standards attach specific quantitative bands and specific process requirements to each level, which the analogy cannot carry.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • "SIL 2 means the software has a failure rate of …". Cause: applying the quantitative part of a level to software. Fix: software gets the process requirements of its level; numbers apply to random hardware failures.
    • 

  • Converting levels with a table. Symptom: a report calls an ASIL B component "SIL 2 certified". Fix: argue which requirements of the target standard were met, with evidence.
    • 

  • Redundancy without β. Symptom: a 1oo2 calculation with β = 0 (forensic lab). Find it: look for any shared supply, firmware, sensor, cable or maintenance step. Fix: estimate β with an argument, and show the result is dominated by it.
    • 

  • Proof tests that are planned but not done. Symptom: the calculation uses one year; the maintenance log shows three. Fix: put the proof test in the operating procedure, record it, and use the real interval in the case.
    • 

  • Diagnostics counted twice. Symptom: a failure counted as detected in the rate and again as reducing β. Fix: one model, written down, with each failure mode assigned once.
    • 

  • Monte Carlo with too few samples. Symptom: a simulated PFD of zero for a good 1oo2 design. Fix: estimate how many failure events you expect (here a few hundred per million intervals) and use enough intervals or importance sampling.

Summary


    

  • An integrity level grades a safety function by the risk reduction it must provide; it sets targets for random hardware failures and rigour against systematic failures.
    • 

  • IEC 61508 uses SIL 1 to 4, ISO 26262 ASIL A to D (and QM), DO-178C software levels A to E; the directions agree, the scales do not convert.
    • 

  • Software gets only process requirements, because its failures are systematic.
    • 

  • Low-demand functions are measured by PFDavg; for one channel PFDavg ≈ λT/2, for two independent channels (λT)²/3.
    • 

  • Common causes are not squared: with β = 0.10 the second channel buys about a factor of 9 instead of 86 in the course's example.
    • 

  • Proof tests reset hidden failures; the real interval, not the planned one, belongs in the calculation.
    •

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Software does not wear or age; a software failure happens deterministically whenever a particular input and state occur, in every copy. There is no population of independent failures to count, so a failure rate has no meaning. The level instead prescribes how carefully the software is specified, built, reviewed and tested.
    2. 

  2. Low-demand: the function is called on rarely (an e-stop path); the measure is the probability it is failed when called, PFDavg. High-demand or continuous: the function is called often or acts all the time (a continuously active monitor); the measure is the dangerous failure frequency per hour, PFH.
    3. 

  3. F(t) = 1 − e−λt ≈ λt for small λt; the time average over [0, T] is (1/T)∫λt dt = λT/2. Assumptions: constant rate, perfect proof test that restores "as new", demands independent of the channel's state, and λT small.
    4. 

  4. The independent part is squared, (0.9 × 0.01752)²/3 ≈ 8.3 × 10−5, while the common part is linear, 0.1 × 0.01752/2 ≈ 8.8 × 10−4. A tenth of a linear term is still far larger than a squared small number.
    5. 

  5. Independent part (0.98 × 0.01752)²/3 ≈ 9.8 × 10−5; common part 0.02 × 0.01752/2 ≈ 1.75 × 10−4; total ≈ 2.7 × 10−4. The common part still dominates, even at β = 0.02.
    6. 

  6. That the two scales come from different standards with different parameters and requirements; there is no exact conversion. If the sensor is to be used in an IEC 61508 system, someone must show which IEC 61508 requirements its development and data meet.
    7. 

  7. PFH: the check is active continuously during flight, so a dangerous failure matters whenever it happens, not only on rare demands.
