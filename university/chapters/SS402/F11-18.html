Story hook

Kofi rides to the market with his aunt Mariam. Before she starts the engine, she checks that his seatbelt is fastened. When the engine starts, a small light with a drawing of an airbag glows on the dashboard for a moment and then goes out. "That is the car checking its airbag," she says. "If the light stays on, something is wrong, and I take the car to the garage."

Kofi thinks about it on the way. The seatbelt and the airbag do nothing at all on a good day. The car drives, brakes and turns without them. They exist only for the one second when something goes wrong, and in that second they must work the first time, with nobody there to fix them.

At the market, Mariam's friend Jonas, who repairs cars, adds something Kofi had not thought of. "An airbag that opens when it should not is dangerous too. And nobody can test the real thing on every car. So the people who build it must prove, with drawings, calculations and tests on other cars, that it will work. The proof is written down before the car is ever sold."

A robot arm in a lab is in the same position. Most of its software makes it useful. A small part of it, and some hardware around it, exists only to keep people from being hurt when the useful part fails. This course is about that small part: how to decide what it must do, how to build it, and how to show, with evidence, that it does.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what functional safety is and how it differs from other kinds of safety (mechanical design, electrical safety, security), using the terms hazard, harm, risk, safety function and safe state.
  • Separate a control function from a safety function in a design, and state what makes them independent.
  • Classify failures as random hardware failures or systematic failures, and say which kinds of protection work against each.
  • Run and interpret the course's arm-joint simulator: show a safety function catching a controller bug and a sensor fault, and show it failing when it shares a sensor with the controller.
  • State honestly what this course can and cannot give you: concepts and practice, not certification under any standard.
Jargon box
Harm
Simple words: someone gets hurt, or something important is damaged. Analogy: the bruise or the broken arm after a car crash. Precise: physical injury or damage to the health of people (some standards also include damage to property or the environment); the thing a safety analysis is ultimately about. D1
Hazard
Simple words: something that could hurt someone if things go wrong. Analogy: a sharp corner of the dashboard in front of a passenger. Precise: a potential source of harm; for a robot, for example, a moving arm link that can strike or pinch a person; a hazard leads to harm only in a hazardous situation (a person within reach) followed by a hazardous event (the arm moves unexpectedly). D1
Risk
Simple words: how bad the harm could be, combined with how likely it is. Analogy: a short slow trip on a quiet road against a long fast trip on a busy one. Precise: the combination of the probability of occurrence of harm and the severity of that harm; reduced by design, by safety functions and by other measures until it is judged tolerable. D1
Functional safety
Simple words: the part of safety that depends on a machine's own systems doing the right thing at the right moment. Analogy: seatbelts and airbags that act when something goes wrong, designed and tested to rules. Precise: the part of the overall safety of a system that depends on a safety-related system (electrical, electronic or programmable electronic, plus other technologies) operating correctly in response to its inputs; contrasted with safety achieved by passive means (padding, guards, low force by design). D1 D2
Equipment under control (EUC)
Simple words: the machine whose dangers you are protecting people from. Analogy: the car itself, with its engine and wheels. Precise: the equipment, machinery or plant that gives rise to the hazards, together with its control system; the safety-related system acts on it to reach or keep a safe state. D1
Safety function
Simple words: one specific protective job, like "stop the arm if it goes too fast". Analogy: the airbag: one device, one job, for one kind of crash. Precise: a function, implemented by a safety-related system or by other risk-reduction measures, intended to achieve or maintain a safe state of the EUC with respect to a specific hazardous event; specified by what it detects, what it does, how fast, and how reliably. D1
Safety-related system
Simple words: the parts (sensors, logic, outputs, software) that carry out the safety functions. Analogy: the crash sensor, the airbag controller and the airbag together. Precise: a system that implements the required safety functions and is intended to achieve the necessary safety integrity for them; it may be separate from the control system or share parts with it, and every shared part must then meet the safety requirements. D1
Safe state
Simple words: the condition the machine goes to when something is wrong, chosen so that it cannot hurt anyone while it stays there. Analogy: the car stopped at the side of the road with the engine off. Precise: a defined state of the EUC in which the hazard under consideration is absent; entered by a safety function and left only by a defined reset; it depends on the hazard (for an arm holding a load, "power off" may drop the load). Same meaning as in F9-69. D1
Random hardware failure
Simple words: a part breaks at an unpredictable moment, the way a light bulb burns out. Analogy: a tyre that goes flat on one day out of thousands. Precise: a failure occurring at a random time from one or more degradation mechanisms in the hardware (wear, ageing, stress, radiation); can be described by failure rates and reduced by redundancy, diagnostics and proof tests. D1
Systematic failure
Simple words: a failure built into the design, so it happens every time the same conditions occur. Analogy: a seatbelt that was designed too short for tall passengers: every one of those cars has the problem. Precise: a failure related in a deterministic way to a cause in the specification, design, manufacture, installation, operation or maintenance, which can be removed only by changing that cause; all software failures are systematic. Reduced by process (reviews, analysis, testing), not by identical redundancy. D1
Common-cause failure
Simple words: one cause that breaks two protections at the same time. Analogy: two brake lines that run side by side, cut by the same stone. Precise: a failure of two or more channels or functions from a single shared cause (a shared sensor, power supply, design error, environment or maintenance action), which defeats redundancy that assumed independence. D1
Safety lifecycle
Simple words: the list of steps, from the first idea to the scrapping of the machine, in which safety is planned, built, checked and kept. Analogy: the car's life from design drawings to crash tests to yearly inspections. Precise: the necessary activities for safety-related systems over the life of a product, from concept and hazard analysis through requirements, design, verification, validation, operation, modification and decommissioning, each producing documented outputs. D1 D2

Terms used from earlier courses: emergency stop and latched (F9-07); watchdog timer (F1-48); fault and failure (F5-08); real-time system (F9-45). "Hazard" here is the functional-safety term; it is not the pipeline hazard of a processor (F1-26). The word "integrity" here means "how reliably a safety function works"; it is not data integrity in the security sense (F11-01) or the integrity property of consensus.

Note

The honest scope of this course. SS402 teaches the concepts of functional safety and gives you practice with them on the university's own simulated robot. It does not make anything certified, and it cannot. Certifying a product under IEC 61508, ISO 26262, DO-178C or any other safety standard requires the purchased standards themselves, qualified and independent assessors, and an organisation with documented processes for safety management, competence and configuration control. None of those exists in this build: the standards are sold documents that were not opened while this course was written, so every statement about their content is marked as unverified and names the part of the standard to read. When you work on a real safety-related product, your organisation's safety manager and the current editions of the standards decide; this course prepares you to understand them. C1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A car has two kinds of parts. Some parts make it go where you want: the engine, the wheels, the steering. Other parts only matter when something goes wrong: the seatbelt and the airbag. On a normal day they do nothing. On a bad day they must work at once, the first time.

A robot arm is the same. Most of its program moves the arm to pick things up. A small, separate part watches for danger. If the arm moves too fast near a person, that small part stops it, even if the main program wants it to keep going. Engineers call this small part a safety function.

There is one big rule. The safety part must not use the same eyes as the main part. If both look through the same broken sensor, both are fooled at the same moment. That is why a careful robot has a second sensor that only the safety part reads.

And there is a second rule, the one Jonas told Kofi. It is not enough to build the airbag. You must show, with tests and written reasons, that it works. Collecting that proof is a big part of this whole course.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Safety as risk, not as perfection. In functional safety, "safe" does not mean "nothing can ever go wrong". It means that the remaining risk, after everything that has been done to reduce it, is judged tolerable for the people exposed. Risk combines the severity of a possible harm with its probability. So the work starts with harm (who could be hurt, how badly), goes back to hazards (what could hurt them) and to hazardous events (what would have to happen), and then asks how much each risk must be reduced and by what means. Chapter F11-19 does this for the course robot. D1

Three ways to reduce risk, in order of preference. First, design the hazard away or make it smaller: a lighter arm, lower motor torque, rounded links, a slower maximum speed in the drive's own configuration. Second, add protective measures that do not depend on anything working correctly: a fixed guard, padding, a fence. Third, and only for what remains, add safety functions: systems that detect a dangerous condition and act on it. Functional safety is the discipline of that third kind. It is the least preferred because it depends on things working, and things fail. D4

Other kinds of safety. Electrical safety (no shock from the robot's power supply), mechanical safety (a link that does not snap), fire safety of batteries and the intrinsic safety of equipment in explosive atmospheres are separate disciplines with their own standards. Security, the subject of SS301 to SS401 (F11-13), is related but different: it protects against people who cause harm on purpose. A safety function can be defeated by an attacker; a security control can create a hazard (a locked-out operator who cannot stop a machine). Real projects treat both and record where they interact. D2

Control function and safety function. Figure 1 shows the separation. The control function makes the arm do its job: a speed controller reads encoder A and commands the drive. The safety function has one job: if the joint speed stays above 200 deg/s for 3 ms, take over the drive, ramp it to zero, disable it and latch. Three things make it a separate function and not "more control code". It has its own input (encoder B, on the joint side). It has its own code and state. And it has authority over the output: when it trips, the controller's command no longer reaches the drive. In a real robot the separation goes further: separate processor, separate power, often separate hardware such as a drive's safe torque off input (F9-69). R1

What a safety function specification says. A safety function is specified, not only implemented. For the speed monitor: the hazardous event it addresses (arm overspeed near a person, hazard H2 in F11-19); what it detects (joint speed above 200 deg/s for 3 ms); what it does (controlled stop, then drive disable, latched); the safe state; how quickly it must act (its response time, here the 3 ms debounce plus the ramp); and how reliably it must work (its integrity, the subject of F11-20). If any of these is missing, nobody can test whether the function meets it. D1

Random and systematic failures. Figure 2 is the most useful idea in this chapter. Hardware wears and breaks at unpredictable times; a population of identical parts shows a failure rate, and a second, independent channel makes it unlikely that both break at once. Software does not wear. A software failure happens because the code (or its requirement) is wrong for some input, and it happens every time that input arrives, in every copy. The same is true of a wrong specification, a wrong configuration file or a wrong maintenance procedure. For these systematic failures, the protection is a disciplined process that finds and removes the errors: careful requirements, reviews, analysis, testing against the requirements, and controlled changes. Diversity (two different designs) helps against some of them; identical copies help against none. D1

Common cause. Redundancy assumes that the channels fail independently. A shared cause breaks that assumption: one power supply for both channels, one sensor read by both, one compiler bug in both builds, one technician who calibrates both wrongly on the same day. The forensic lab of this chapter is a common-cause failure in its purest form: the safety function and the control function read the same encoder. R2

Safe states and when there is none. For the course arm, the safe state is "stopped, drive disabled, brake holding". Some systems have no such state. A drone in flight cannot stop; its "safe" reaction is a controlled landing or a return, which requires the flight system to keep working (fail-operational rather than fail-safe). A car on a motorway cannot brake hard at once without creating a new hazard. The choice of safe state is a design decision made per hazard, and a wrong choice is itself a systematic failure. MP7 (drones) meets this problem in full; MP6 (the robot) mostly has a true safe state. D2 C1

How the standards relate. From the author's memory, not checked in this build: IEC 61508 is a generic "basic" functional-safety standard in several parts (general requirements, hardware, software, definitions, examples of methods for determining integrity levels, guidelines, and an overview of techniques), and several sector standards derive from it, among them IEC 61511 (process industry), IEC 62061 (machinery), EN 50128 and related railway standards, and ISO 26262 (road vehicles), which adapts its ideas to series-produced vehicles. ISO 13849-1 (machinery) uses its own "performance levels" next to IEC 62061's integrity levels. DO-178C is not derived from IEC 61508: it comes from the aviation community (RTCA in the United States, published in Europe by EUROCAE as ED-12C) and is used together with system-level guidance (ARP4754A for development, ARP4761 for safety assessment). Read the scope clause and the introduction of each standard's current edition before relying on any of these relationships. D1 D2 D3 D4

What the standards have in common. Read side by side (at the level this course can claim), they share a small set of ideas, and the rest of SS402 is organised around them. (1) Start from hazards and risk, not from the design (F11-19). (2) Grade the rigour of everything that follows by how much risk reduction a function must provide: an integrity level, assurance level or software level (F11-20). (3) Treat random hardware failures quantitatively and systematic failures by process. (4) Build safety mechanisms with known detection and reaction times and verified independence (F11-21). (5) Trace every requirement to its design and to its verification, and keep the evidence under configuration control (F11-22). (6) Argue, in writing, why the system is acceptably safe, and have that argument assessed by someone independent of the developers (F11-23). D1 D2 D3

Management of functional safety. Each standard also asks for an organisation around the technical work: a safety plan, named responsibilities, competence of the people involved, independence of the reviewers and assessors (graded by level), configuration management, and handling of changes. This part does not show up in code, and it is the part a student project most often lacks. SS402 imitates it in small ways: the hazard log has versions (F11-19), the evidence is tied to a build id (F11-22), and the safety case is reviewed by another learner (F11-23). D1

Read it line by line. In normal, the controller overshoots a little (157.6 deg/s, because of the integral term) and the monitor never trips. In controller_bug, the controller asks for twice the speed: the joint accelerates, passes 200 deg/s, and the monitor trips at 69 ms; the joint peaks at 207 deg/s and is brought to rest and locked. No hazardous speed is reached. A systematic fault in the control function was caught by a separate function that does not share its code. In stuck_encoder_A, encoder A's count freezes at 500 ms, so the speed computed from it reads 0. The controller sees an arm that has stopped, and its integral term winds up: the command climbs by about 30 deg/s every 100 ms. The monitor reads encoder B, sees the true speed cross 200 deg/s, and trips at 659 ms with a peak of 200.8 deg/s. A random hardware fault in the control function's sensor was caught because the safety function does not use that sensor. R1


What it does not show. The simulation assumes the monitor itself works: encoder B reads true, the monitor code is correct, the stop ramp is followed. Each of those is a new place where failures can hide, and the safety function needs its own analysis: what happens if encoder B fails? (Nothing, until the day the controller also fails: a latent fault, found only by a diagnostic or a proof test; F11-20.) What if the monitor's processor hangs? (A watchdog; F11-21.) What if the limit was configured as 2000 instead of 200? (Verification of the configuration; F11-22.) Functional safety is the habit of asking these questions about the protection, not only about the thing protected. R1


Software in a safety function. Because every software failure is systematic, the standards do not ask for a failure rate of software. They ask for a development process whose rigour rises with the integrity level: precise requirements, a design that can be analysed, restricted language subsets and coding rules, defensive checks, reviews, testing to stated coverage, and tools whose errors cannot silently enter the product. DO-178C is almost entirely about this; IEC 61508 has a whole part for it. The habits from SS302 (F11-07: memory safety, sanitizers, coding standards) and SP202 (F2-30: unit tests) are the beginning of that process, not a replacement for it. D1 D3

How the hardware actually does it


Independence is made of hardware. On a real arm, encoder B is a separate sensor on the output side of the gearbox, wired to a separate input, often of a separate processor (a safety controller, or a second core with its own memory protection). The safety function's output does not go through the control processor: it acts on the drive's enable, on a safe torque off input, or on a contactor in the motor supply, so that a hung control computer cannot keep the motor powered. Power for the safety channel is separate or monitored. A random hardware failure inside the safety channel itself is addressed by diagnostics (the channel checks its own sensor for plausibility against encoder A, checks its outputs by reading them back, and tests its memory and processor) and by periodic proof tests that exercise the function end to end. The airbag light in the story is that idea seen from the driver's seat: a self-test at start-up, and a warning when it fails. None of this hardware was available in the build container; the simulation models only the logic. D1 D4

Worked example


A. The trip time of controller_bug, by hand. With gain 2.0 the controller commands 300 deg/s from the start, and the drive adds 3 deg/s per step. The speed after step t is 3(t + 1). The monitor reads the speed at the start of a step, so at step 67 it reads 3 × 67 = 201 deg/s, the first value above 200. It must see the limit exceeded 3 times in a row: steps 67, 68, 69. It trips at step 69, as the output says. Meanwhile the drive kept accelerating during steps 67 and 68, so the speed at the start of step 69 is 3 × 69 = 207 deg/s, the peak in the output. The stop then ramps down at 3 deg/s per ms. The lesson in numbers: the debounce of 3 ms costs 6 deg/s of overshoot here, and a real response-time budget must count every such delay. R1

C. Does this need a safety function at all? The arm's drive is configured with a maximum speed of 150 deg/s in the drive's own firmware, which the robot computer cannot change. Where the analogy breaks


    

  • An airbag is a finished product, tested by its maker on many cars. Your robot's safety function is designed by you, for one robot, and nobody else has tested it. The analogy hides how much of the evidence you must produce yourself.
    • 

  • Seatbelts work without any computer. Many safety functions of a robot are software running on a processor, which brings systematic failures (bugs) that a seatbelt does not have.
    • 

  • A car always has a safe state nearby: stop at the side of the road. A drone in flight or an arm holding a heavy part does not; "stop everything" can itself be the hazard.
    • 

  • The registered mapping says that certification needs evidence, not only mechanisms. The car story makes it sound as if certification is someone else's job. For a real product it is the developer's job to produce the evidence; the assessor only judges it.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • "The software is tested, so it is safe." Symptom: no hazard analysis, no safety requirements, a green test suite. Cause: confusing correctness with safety; tests check what someone thought of. Find it: ask "which hazard does this test address?" for each test. Fix: start from hazards (F11-19), derive safety requirements, then test those.
    • 

  • The protection shares a part with the thing it protects. Symptom: a fault defeats both at once (the forensic lab). Find it: draw Figure 1 for your design and mark every shared sensor, power supply, processor, library and configuration file. Fix: separate them, or show why the shared part cannot fail dangerously.
    • 

  • Identical redundancy against software bugs. Symptom: two channels running the same code disagree never and fail together. Fix: diversity, or better, process (F11-21).
    • 

  • No specification of the safety function. Symptom: nobody can say whether a 50 ms reaction is acceptable. Fix: write what it detects, what it does, how fast, which safe state, how reliably.
    • 

  • Claiming compliance. Symptom: a report says "designed to IEC 61508" for a student project. Cause: wishing. Fix: say "uses concepts from IEC 61508; not assessed", which is what this course can support.
    •

Summary


    

  • Functional safety is the part of safety that depends on a system working correctly in response to its inputs; it is the last resort after designing hazards away and adding passive protection.
    • 

  • Risk combines the severity of harm and its probability; "safe" means the remaining risk is tolerable, not zero.
    • 

  • A safety function is specified by its hazardous event, detection, reaction, safe state, response time and integrity, and it needs authority over the output.
    • 

  • Random hardware failures are fought with redundancy, diagnostics and proof tests; systematic failures (including all software failures) with process and diversity.
    • 

  • Independence must be shown: a shared sensor, power supply, design or configuration turns two protections into one.
    • 

  • The safe state is a design decision per hazard; some systems (drones in flight) have none and must fail operational.
    • 

  • IEC 61508, ISO 26262 and DO-178C are named reading references; this course teaches their shared concepts, not certification.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. Listing 1 run on forensic.in: gain 1.0, encoder A frozen at 500 ms, monitor sensor A (the release 0.3 change), trace on.


    

  1. At 500 ms, encA drops to 0.0 while encB(true) reads 153.1 deg/s. From then on the command (cmd) climbs: 183.4 at 600 ms, 303.4 at 1000 ms.
    2. 

  2. The monitor read encoder A, which said 0.0 deg/s at 900 ms while the true speed was 273.1. It never saw a speed above 200, so it never tripped; the summary line says "trip never" and "HAZARD: speed above 300 deg/s", with a peak of 400 deg/s (the drive's maximum).
    3. 

  3. Hypothesis 1: the monitor's code was broken in release 0.3. Hypothesis 2: the monitor worked but was blind because it read the failed sensor. The evidence rules out hypothesis 1 as the cause: the monitor's input (encoder A) never exceeded the limit, so a correct monitor would not trip either; the same program trips correctly in safety_function.out when it reads encoder B.
    4. 

  4. Root cause: a common-cause failure introduced by design. The change made the safety function share its sensor with the control function, so a single random fault (the frozen encoder) disabled the control function's feedback and blinded the protection at the same moment. "A and B read the same" was true only while both were healthy.
    5. 

  5. Fix: move the monitor back to encoder B (and keep it there by a requirement, not a habit). Test: the stuck_encoder_A scenario with the expected result "trip before 300 deg/s"; on release 0.3 it reports a hazard. Better still, add the A/B plausibility check of the lab extension.
    6. 

  6. A change to a safety-related part must go back through the hazard analysis and the independence argument (Figure 3's return arrow), be reviewed by someone other than the author, and be verified by re-running the safety function's tests. "Saving a pin" was a safety-relevant change made as if it were a wiring detail.
    7. 



Wrong answers and why. "Encoder A failed, so it was bad luck": the encoder failure was the trigger, but the design turned a single fault into a hazard. "The monitor's limit was too high": no limit helps a monitor that reads 0.
