Story hook

Mei spends a day in the cab of a freight train with her aunt Ngozi, who drives it. Every minute or so a soft tone sounds and Ngozi taps a button by her knee. "If I do not press it in time," she says, "the train assumes something has happened to me and starts to brake by itself." The button does not care whether she is asleep, ill or looking at a map. It only cares that the answer did not come.

Later, Ngozi shows Mei how the brakes work. Air pressure holds them off. If a hose bursts, the pressure drops and the brakes go on. "A broken brake system stops the train," she says. "That is the whole idea. Whatever breaks, the train ends up standing still."

At the end of the day, Mei asks about the screen in the cab that shows messages from the control centre. "If the centre sends 'stop', does the train stop at once?" Ngozi thinks. "The message goes into the computer with everything else: the timetable, the logs, the weather. On a busy day it can take a moment before the computer gets to it. That is why the stop button in my cab is a wire to the brakes, not a message to the computer."

Three protections, three ideas: something that notices when an answer stops coming, a design in which breaking leads to stopping, and a stop that does not wait in a queue. This chapter builds each one for the course robot, and then examines a robot whose stop did wait in a queue.

What you will be able to do

  • Design a window watchdog (what kicks it, its window, its check period, latching and reset rules) and test it with a suite that is shown to fail on deliberately wrong watchdogs.
  • Choose a redundancy architecture (1oo2, 2oo2, 2oo3) for detection or for masking, and show with a fault-injection run why identical channels do not tolerate a systematic fault and diverse ones can.
  • Build a response-time budget from fault to safe state and compare it with the time the hazard allows.
  • Place safety reactions on the real-time path and explain what happens when they are not.
  • Diagnose "the arm moved after e-stop" from a timeline that shows the stop handled in a non-real-time thread, and verify the fix.
Jargon box
Safety mechanism
Simple words: a part of the design whose job is to notice a fault and react before it can hurt anyone. Analogy: the vigilance button, the brakes that clamp when pressure is lost. Precise: a technical solution implemented by electrical, electronic or programmable elements (or other technologies) to detect, indicate, mask or control faults or failures, in order to maintain intended functionality or to reach or keep a safe state; characterised by what it detects, its detection and reaction times, and its coverage. D2
Window watchdog
Simple words: a watchdog that is upset not only when a sign of life comes too late, but also when it comes too early. Analogy: a vigilance button that also complains if it is pressed twenty times a minute, because that is not a driver paying attention either. Precise: a watchdog with a minimum and a maximum time between kicks; a kick before the window opens or no kick before it closes trips it; catches a monitored loop that runs too fast (a timer misconfiguration, a loop that skips its work) as well as one that stalls. Builds on the software watchdog and kick source of F9-69. R2
Fault detection and fault masking
Simple words: noticing that something is wrong (and stopping), versus carrying on correctly in spite of it. Analogy: a warning lamp that says "a brake circuit has failed" versus a second circuit that keeps braking. Precise: a detecting architecture (such as 1oo2 comparison) recognises a disagreement and moves to the safe state; a masking or fault-tolerant architecture (such as 2oo3 voting) continues to deliver a correct output with one channel failed, at the cost of more hardware and a voter that must itself be trusted. D1
Voter (2oo3, mid-value select)
Simple words: three helpers compute the same number; the one in the middle is used, so one wrong helper cannot change the result. Analogy: three passengers read the speed sign and the middle answer wins. Precise: a two-out-of-three architecture combines three channel outputs; for continuous values, mid-value selection (the median) tolerates any single wrong channel, and the spread between highest and lowest flags the disagreement for diagnosis. R1
Diversity (diverse redundancy)
Simple words: building the backup differently from the original, so the same mistake is not in both. Analogy: one brake that works with air and a second that works with a spring. Precise: the use of different means (algorithms, code written by different people, different sensors, different processors or compilers) to perform the same function, so that a systematic fault in one channel is unlikely to be present in another; reduces, but does not remove, common-cause failure. D1
Fault-tolerant time interval
Simple words: how long after a fault the hazard can start, so how fast the protection must act. Analogy: the distance to the obstacle when the driver stops responding. Precise: the minimum time from the occurrence of a fault in a system to the possible occurrence of a hazardous event if the safety mechanisms are not activated; the sum of detection time and reaction time to the safe state must be shorter (ISO 26262 uses this term; IEC 61508 speaks of the process safety time). D2 D1
Fail-safe and fail-operational
Simple words: when something breaks, either stop safely, or keep working well enough to get to safety. Analogy: the train's air brakes clamp (fail-safe); an aircraft must keep flying (fail-operational). Precise: a fail-safe design reaches a safe state on a failure and accepts the loss of function; a fail-operational design keeps a (possibly degraded) function after a failure because no immediate safe state exists, which requires fault tolerance, not only detection. D2
Plausibility check
Simple words: comparing a reading with something else you know, to catch a sensor that lies. Analogy: the speedometer says zero but the trees are moving past. Precise: a diagnostic that compares a signal with an independent signal or with a physical model (two encoders, commanded versus measured motion, rate-of-change limits) and declares a fault when they disagree beyond a tolerance for longer than a set time. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A safety mechanism is a part of the robot that watches for trouble and acts fast. You met three of them in the train. A watchdog notices when the robot's brain stops answering. A design that falls into safety stops the robot when a part breaks. And a stop that never waits in line.

Sometimes engineers use three helpers instead of one. All three work out the same number, and the robot uses the middle answer. If one helper makes a mistake, the other two outvote it. But if all three learned the same wrong rule, they all make the same mistake at the same time, and the vote does not help. So the helpers must be different, not copies.

The last idea is about time. A stop that comes too late is not a stop. If the robot's stop message waits behind a big, slow job, the arm keeps moving. So the stop must go to the part of the robot that is always on time.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Every mechanism has a budget. Figure 1 is the first thing to draw for any safety mechanism. The fault-tolerant time interval comes from the hazard analysis: how long after a fault the hazardous event can occur (how fast the arm reaches a person, how far the base travels on a stale command). The detection time comes from the mechanism: a watchdog's timeout plus its check period plus its own scheduling delay; a monitor's debounce. The reaction time comes from physics and from the reaction path: the stop ramp and the time for the drive to be disabled. Only when detection plus reaction is shorter than the interval, with a margin, does the mechanism protect. D2

Watchdogs, one level up. F9-69 built a watchdog with a timeout, latching and reset rules, and showed that the kick source is the most important design choice. Two additions matter in functional safety. A window watchdog also trips on kicks that come too early: a loop that runs ten times too fast, because a timer was misconfigured or because it skips its real work and only kicks, is as broken as one that stalls. And the watchdog's own correctness is a safety requirement, so its tests must be able to fail: Listing 2 runs the same five tests against three deliberately wrong watchdogs (mutants) and requires each mutant to fail at least one test. A test suite that passes on a broken watchdog is evidence of nothing. R2

Redundancy: detect or mask. Two channels that compare their results (1oo2 for the safety reaction: either can trip) detect a single fault and go to the safe state; they cannot tell which channel is right, so they stop. Two channels that must both agree before acting (2oo2) avoid spurious trips but are less safe, since either channel failing silent prevents the trip. Three channels with a voter (2oo3) can mask a single fault: the median of three continuous values is correct if at most one channel is wrong, and the spread tells you that one is. The choice follows from the safe state: a robot arm, which can stop, needs detection; a system that must keep working (a flight controller) needs masking. D1 R1

Identical copies and the systematic fault. Listing 1 makes F11-18's Figure 2 concrete. Implementation X computes a speed from a 16-bit counter as "now − previous" in plain integers and forgets that the counter wraps from 65535 to 0. Every copy of X makes the same mistake at the same step. In the run, three identical X channels produce a wrong output at the wrap with zero disagreements: the voter sees three equal wrong values and uses one. The same voter with three diverse implementations (X, Y and Z) uses the correct median and flags the disagreement. A random fault (one bit flipped in channel 2's copy of the counter) is masked by both, because it hits only one channel. R1

Safe states and the reaction path. A safe state is useful only if the machine can reach it whatever has failed. That shapes the hardware: brakes held off by power, contactors held closed by a coil, enable signals that are active-high so a broken wire means "disabled", relays with a fixed delay that cut power whatever the software did. It also shapes the software: the reaction to a safety event must run on a path whose timing is guaranteed. On the course robot that means the real-time control thread or, better, the motor microcontroller (F9-65), not a general-purpose thread that also writes logs. C1

Diagnostics. Many failures can be found before a demand: a plausibility check between two encoders, a read-back of an output, a test pulse on a relay, a start-up self-test of RAM and of the watchdog itself. Each diagnostic turns some dangerous undetected failures into detected ones, and F11-20 showed that only the undetected part enters the probability. A diagnostic also needs its own reaction (usually the safe state) and its own test. D1

The watchdog test suite. Listing 2 is the SS402 lab "watchdog and safe-state tests". The watchdog under test has a window of 5 to 50 ms, latches every trip, refuses a reset while the e-stop is pressed, and restarts motion from zero. The five tests encode those requirements; the three mutants each break one of them:



The real watchdog passes all five tests. Mutant A (no early-kick window) fails only T3; mutant B (not latched) fails only T4; mutant C (reset ignores the e-stop) fails only T5. Each test is therefore shown to be the one that protects its requirement, and the final line, "ALL TESTS PASSED", requires both that the real watchdog passes and that no mutant survives. If a future change makes a mutant survive, the program exits with 1 and the lab fails: the test suite has lost its power to see that flaw. Note also what T2 checks: not only that the watchdog trips, but that it trips within the bound of 50 + 5 ms after the last kick and that the drive command is 0 afterwards. A safety test checks the timing and the resulting state, not only the event. R2


Redundancy under fault injection. Listing 1 runs six configurations. The 16-bit counter starts at 64000 and advances 37 counts per millisecond, so it wraps between step 41 and step 42; in some scenarios a bit of channel 2's copy of the counter flips at step 100:




Line by line: identical X channels with the flip mask the flip (one disagreement at step 100) but use a wrong value at step 42 with no warning; identical X channels without the flip show the systematic failure in its purest form, a wrong output and zero disagreements. Diverse channels (X, Y, Z) mask the wrap and the flip and flag both. A 1oo2 pair of identical correct channels (Y, Y) detects the flip and goes to the safe state at step 100; a diverse 1oo2 pair (X, Y) detects the wrap at step 42 and goes to the safe state. Both 1oo2 pairs are safe; neither can continue. That is the trade-off between detecting and masking, in two lines. R1


Diversity is not free, and not complete. Implementations Y and Z were written differently for this lab, but they were written by the same author, from the same requirement, on the same day. In practice that is the weak spot: independently written versions of a program tend to fail on the same difficult inputs more often than independence would predict, because the difficulty is in the requirement, not in the typing. Diversity therefore complements, and does not replace, the process measures against systematic faults (requirements review, boundary-value tests such as "the counter wraps", static analysis). In this example, a single test with the counter near 65535 would have found the bug in X before any voter was needed. D1 R1

The course forensic case: where the stop runs. Listing 3 models one CPU shared by two threads in 10 µs steps of virtual time. The control thread is real-time: released every 1 ms, it runs first and needs 200 µs. The comms thread is not real-time: it works through a first-in, first-out queue of jobs. At 1000 ms a log flush of 150 ms of work is queued; at 1020 ms the e-stop is pressed. The hard-wired chain's safety relay removes drive power 200 ms after a press, giving the software time for a controlled stop. In the design under investigation, the e-stop input reaches the software as a message to the comms thread, which sets a flag that the control thread reads. Here is the run:






The e-stop message waited behind the log flush. The flush needed 150 ms of CPU, but the comms thread gets only the 0.8 ms per millisecond that the control thread leaves, so it finished at 1187.6 ms; the stop flag was set at 1187.65 ms, 167.65 ms after the press. During those 168 ms the control thread did exactly what it was told: it followed the trajectory at 60 deg/s. The ramp started at the next cycle, and at 1220 ms the relay cut power with the arm still moving at 40.8 deg/s, so the brake had to stop it. The arm moved 11.683 degrees after the e-stop. R3


The fix moves the reading of the e-stop input into the control thread. Same flush, same press, same relay:




The control thread sees the press in its 1020 ms cycle and starts the ramp at once; the arm is at rest at 1119 ms, 101 ms before the relay opens, after moving 2.970 degrees. The comms thread is as busy as before; it no longer matters. R4


Why this keeps happening. Robot stacks are built from messages (F9-49): it is natural to publish "e-stop pressed" as one more topic and handle it in whichever callback is convenient. The bug is invisible in testing on an idle system, where the comms thread is never busy, and appears under load, when it matters. Three rules prevent it. Safety inputs are read by the real-time path itself (or by the microcontroller), not delivered through a general-purpose queue. Safety reactions do not depend on any thread whose timing is not analysed. And the response-time budget of Figure 1 is tested under worst-case load: a log flush, a map save, a burst of camera frames, all at once. On the real robot the hard-wired chain is the last line; the forensic case shows why the software's controlled stop still matters, since the relay alone stopped an arm moving at 40.8 deg/s. R3 R4



How the hardware actually does it


Most of the mechanisms in this chapter have hardware counterparts that are more trustworthy than software. A hardware watchdog timer (F1-48) is a counter with its own clock that resets the processor if it is not reloaded in time; some have a window mode and a separate oscillator so that a failed main clock cannot freeze the watchdog with it. Lockstep processors run two cores on the same instructions and compare their outputs in hardware every cycle, detecting random faults in one core (not software bugs, which both cores execute identically). Safety relays use forcibly guided contacts so that a welded contact is visible on a read-back contact, and they cut power after a fixed, adjustable delay. Drives with safe torque off remove the motor's ability to produce torque without software. Brakes are spring-applied and electrically released, so loss of power applies them: the train's air brakes, in electrical form. None of these was available in the build container; the e-stop timeline is a model on a simulated CPU with virtual time. D1 C1


Safety
When you test watchdogs and safe states on a real robot, induce each fault (a stalled control thread, a killed process, an unplugged encoder) first with the wheels off the ground or with motor power off at the hard-wired chain, and only then on the floor in a clear area with the supervisor holding the e-stop. Never disable the hard-wired chain or lengthen its relay delay to "give the software more time". Electrical work on the chain is for qualified people. C1

Worked example


A. The wrap, by hand. At step 41 the counter is 64000 + 37 × 41 = 65517. At step 42 it is 65554 − 65536 = 18. Implementation X computes 18 − 65517 = −65499. Implementation Y converts the unsigned difference 18 − 65517 to 16 bits (which is 37) and reads it as signed: 37. Implementation Z computes −65499, sees that it is below −32768 and adds 65536: 37. The median of (−65499, 37, 37) is 37. R1


B. The comms delay, by hand. The flush is queued at 1000 ms, after the control thread's 200 µs, so the comms thread starts at 1000.2 ms. It gets 0.8 ms of CPU in each 1 ms period, so 150 ms of work need 187.5 periods: it finishes at 1000.2 + 187 + 0.4 = 1187.6 ms (in the last period it needs 0.4 ms after the control thread's 0.2 ms). The 50 µs e-stop job ends at 1187.65 ms, as the run says. R3


C. The budget of the fixed design. Detection: at most one control period, 1 ms. Reaction: from 60 deg/s at 0.6 deg/s per cycle, 100 cycles, so 100 ms. Total about 101 ms against the relay's 200 ms: margin about 99 ms. The run shows the arm at rest at 1119 ms (99 ms after the press, because the first decrement happens in the 1020 ms cycle itself). Distance during the ramp: average speed 30 deg/s for 0.1 s, about 3 degrees; the run says 2.970. R4


D. The window, from the loop. The control loop kicks every 10 ms with a measured jitter (from your own run of F9-48's method) of, say, ±2 ms. A window of 5 to 50 ms leaves room for the jitter and for a few missed cycles, and catches a loop that runs at least twice too fast. A window of 9 to 11 ms would trip on ordinary jitter, and nuisance trips lead people to widen or disable the watchdog.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Ngozi's vigilance button is pressed by a person who can be asked why she missed it. A watchdog's kick comes from code, and the code that kicks may keep kicking while the useful work has stopped, which is why the kick source matters so much.
    • 

  • Air brakes are fail-safe because physics makes them so: no pressure, brakes on. Software has no such physics; "fail-safe" software must be designed and tested to reach the safe state on each failure, one failure at a time.
    • 

  • Two train brake circuits wear independently. Two copies of a program do not fail independently; the voter run shows them failing together.
    • 

  • Ngozi's stop button is a wire. On the course robot the hard-wired chain is that wire too, but the controlled stop before it is software; the analogy hides that a software path is still needed to stop well, not only to stop.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Safety input delivered as a message. Symptom: the stop works on the bench and is late under load (the forensic case). Find it: trace the e-stop from the input pin to the drive and list every queue, thread and lock it passes through. Fix: read it in the real-time path; test under worst-case load.
    • 

  • Watchdog kicked from a timer or an idle loop. Symptom: it never trips. Fix: kick at the end of a cycle that did its work (F9-69); add a window to catch a loop that only kicks.
    • 

  • Tests that cannot fail. Symptom: 100 % pass rate forever. Find it: mutation testing (Listing 2). Fix: one test per requirement, each shown to kill a mutant.
    • 

  • Identical redundancy against software faults. Symptom: three channels, zero disagreements, wrong output. Fix: diversity and, first, boundary tests.
    • 

  • Voting where detection is needed (or the reverse). Symptom: a 2oo2 arrangement that never trips when one channel fails silent. Fix: choose by safe state: stop on disagreement (1oo2) for an arm; vote (2oo3) only where you must keep going.
    • 

  • A budget never measured. Symptom: "the stop takes a few milliseconds" with no test. Fix: measure press-to-rest under load; compare with the fault-tolerant time interval.
    •

Summary


    

  • Every safety mechanism has a budget: detection time plus reaction time must be shorter than the hazard's fault-tolerant time interval.
    • 

  • A window watchdog catches loops that stall and loops that run too fast; it latches and has explicit reset rules.
    • 

  • Safety tests must be shown to fail: mutation testing kills deliberately wrong watchdogs.
    • 

  • 1oo2 detects and stops; 2oo3 masks and continues; the safe state decides which you need.
    • 

  • Identical channels do not tolerate a systematic fault; diverse ones can, and boundary tests are cheaper still.
    • 

  • Safe states are reached by designs where failure leads to stopping: spring brakes, active-high enables, timed relays.
    • 

  • Safety inputs and reactions belong on the real-time path; the course forensic case shows a stop that waited 168 ms in a non-real-time queue.
    •

  1. The e-stop message waited in its queue for 167.65 ms; until then the control thread, correctly following its inputs, kept the arm at 60 deg/s.

  2. Mutant A (no window) is killed by T3; mutant B (not latched) by T4; mutant C (reset ignores the e-stop) by T5. If B survived, no test would check that a trip stays latched, so a regression that lets a late kick clear a trip could ship with a green test suite; the lab would also exit with 1.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. Listing 3 run on estop_timeline.in (comms 150 200): e-stop delivered to the comms thread, a 150 ms log flush queued at 1000 ms, relay delay 200 ms. The fix is estop_fixed.in (control 150 200).


    

  1. Pressed at 1020.000 ms (and the relay's 200 ms delay starts); the message is queued at 1020.000 ms behind the flush (queue length 2); the flush ends at 1187.600 ms; the comms thread handles the message and sets the stop flag at 1187.650 ms (167.650 ms after the press); the control thread's 1188 ms cycle starts the ramp (the 1200 ms line shows 52.2 deg/s, "stopping"); at 1220.000 ms the relay cuts power at 40.8 deg/s; the arm moved 11.683 degrees after the press.
    2. 

  2. Because the control thread did not read the e-stop: it waited for a flag from a non-real-time thread whose latency is unbounded under load. The robot's response time is the sum along the whole path, and the slowest element was a FIFO queue in the comms thread.
    3. 

  3. Hypothesis 1: the control thread missed deadlines (overload of the real-time path). Hypothesis 2: the e-stop information was delayed before reaching the control thread. The control lines every 20 ms between 1020 and 1180 ms are on time and show 60.0 deg/s, so the control thread was running normally (rules out 1); the gap between "job queued: estop_msg" at 1020 ms and "stop_flag set" at 1187.65 ms, right after "job done: log_flush", locates the delay in the comms queue (confirms 2).
    4. 

  4. The relay removed drive power while the arm still moved at 40.8 deg/s, so the arm was stopped by its brake instead of by the controlled ramp.
    5. 

  5. Read the e-stop input in the control thread (or on the motor microcontroller) at the start of every cycle. The run of estop_fixed.in under the same flush shows the ramp starting at 1020 ms, the arm at rest at 1119 ms after 2.970 degrees, and the relay opening at 1220 ms with the arm at 0.0 deg/s.
    6. 

  6. An end-to-end e-stop test under worst-case load: press while the robot rotates its logs, saves a map and streams camera frames, measuring press-to-rest against the relay delay. On an idle robot the comms queue is always empty and the bug cannot appear.
    7. 



Wrong answers and why. "The relay delay is too long": the delay is what allows a controlled stop; shortening it would make every stop a jolt and does not fix the late software reaction. "Give the comms thread a higher priority": it would still process the flush before the message (FIFO), and raising a logging thread's priority takes time from everything else; the e-stop does not belong in that queue at all.
