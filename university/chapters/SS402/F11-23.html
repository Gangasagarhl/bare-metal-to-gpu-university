Story hook

Tomás's sister Lucía buys an old car for her first job. Before she may drive it on the road, it must pass an inspection. The seller gives her a thick folder: receipts for new brakes, a note about the airbag being replaced, the last inspection report. Lucía is proud of the folder. The inspector, an older woman called Amaka, is less impressed.

"This receipt says brakes were fitted," Amaka says. "It does not say they work. And this airbag note is for a different car: look at the number." She does not throw the folder away. She uses it. For each part she asks one question: what is the claim, and what here proves it? Where the folder proves something, she ticks it. Where it does not, she tests the car herself or writes "not shown".

At the end she gives Lucía a sheet with three lines still open. "The car may be fine," she says. "But I cannot say it is, and neither can you, until those three are answered." Lucía is annoyed for a day. Then she realises that the sheet is the most useful thing she owns about the car: it says exactly what is known, what is not, and why.

A safety case is that sheet, written by the people who built the machine before anyone else asks: the claim that the robot is acceptably safe for a stated use, the reasons, the evidence for each reason, and an honest list of what is still open. This chapter writes one for the course robot, checks it with a program, and then reviews someone else's.

What you will be able to do

  • Structure a safety case as claims, argument strategies, evidence, context and assumptions, in a goal-structured form.
  • Write the course robot's safety case, citing the evidence files of the earlier labs, and keep its open items visible.
  • Check a case mechanically (unsupported goals, missing or contradicting evidence, simulation evidence for hardware claims) with the course's checker.
  • Review a safety case as an independent reviewer, with the checklist of the SS402 practical exam, and write findings the authors can act on.
  • Plan the safety case for your MP6 robot or MP7 drone (the course project).
Jargon box
Safety case
Simple words: a written explanation of why a machine is safe enough for a stated use, with proof for every reason. Analogy: the folder for the inspector, plus the sheet that says what it proves. Precise: a structured argument, supported by a body of evidence, that provides a compelling, comprehensible and valid case that a system is acceptably safe for a given application in a given operating environment; maintained over the system's life. D6 D2
Claim (goal)
Simple words: a sentence the case says is true, like "the arm stops before power is cut". Analogy: "the brakes work". Precise: a proposition in the argument, stated so that it can be true or false, with an explicit scope; the top claim is the overall safety claim; sub-claims refine it. D6
Argument strategy
Simple words: how you break a big claim into smaller ones. Analogy: "I will show the car is safe by checking brakes, lights, tyres and airbags in turn". Precise: the inference that connects a claim to its sub-claims, for example argument over each hazard of the hazard log, over each operating mode, or over each safety function; must be complete (the sub-claims together imply the claim) under stated context. D6
Evidence (solution)
Simple words: the actual proof: a test result, an analysis, a review record. Analogy: the inspector's brake-test printout, not the receipt. Precise: an item (test result, analysis, review record, field data) that supports a claim directly; it must exist, apply to the claimed configuration, be of a kind that can support the claim (a simulation cannot alone support a claim about hardware), and show what is claimed. D6
Context and assumption
Simple words: what the claim is about and what you take for granted without proving it. Analogy: "this car, driven on roads, by a licensed driver" and "the driver is not drunk". Precise: context defines the terms and scope of a claim (the system, the environment, what "acceptably safe" means); assumptions are statements accepted without support in this argument, which must be listed so that reviewers and operators can check them. D6
Undeveloped goal (open item)
Simple words: a claim the case needs but cannot support yet, marked openly. Analogy: the three open lines on Amaka's sheet. Precise: a claim deliberately left without support, marked as such with the reason and what is needed to close it; the top claim does not hold until every undeveloped goal below it is closed. D6
Safety-case review
Simple words: someone who did not write the case reads it to find where it does not prove what it says. Analogy: Amaka with the folder. Precise: an independent examination of a safety case for validity (does each argument step hold?), sufficiency (is the evidence enough and of the right kind?), completeness (are all hazards, modes and assumptions covered?) and currency (does the evidence match the configuration?), producing findings that the authors must resolve. D1 D6

Terms from earlier chapters: hazard log and course target (F11-19, F11-20), safety mechanism (F11-21), traceability, configuration of evidence, verification and validation (F11-22).

What this safety case can claim. The course robot's case claims only that the robot's arm is acceptably safe to operate in the university lab under supervision, against the course's own hazard log and course targets. It is a learning artefact: it has not been assessed by anyone qualified, it does not meet any standard's requirements for a safety case, and it does not make the robot safe to use anywhere else. The supervision rules of the faculty F9 safety box remain in force whatever the case says. C1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the course robot's safety case in goal-structured form, as written in case_check.in. Rectangles are claims, parallelograms strategies, rounded boxes evidence; the two boxes marked OPEN are undeveloped goals.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

At the end of building a robot, you write down one big sentence: "this robot's arm is safe enough to use in our lab, with a grown-up watching". Then you show why. You break the big sentence into smaller ones, one for each danger on your list, and for each small sentence you point to the test or calculation that proves it.

Some small sentences you cannot prove yet. You do not hide them. You mark them "open" and write what you would need. Then someone who did not build the robot reads the whole thing and looks for holes, the way an inspector checks a car.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Why an argument, and not only a folder. The labs of this course produced a hazard log, safety functions, test results, a probability model, a trace and coverage. A pile of evidence does not say what it proves. A safety case connects them: it states the top claim with its scope, chooses a strategy to split it (here: one sub-claim per hazard, plus claims about the analysis and the evidence itself), and attaches each piece of evidence to the claim it supports. Writing the argument is where the gaps show: a hazard with no claim, a claim with no evidence, evidence that supports a different claim than the one it is attached to. D6

Scope first. The top claim of the course robot's case is narrow on purpose: the arm, in the university lab, operated by trained learners, with a supervisor present, against the course's hazard log. Each of those words is a context or an assumption (Figure 1). Widen any of them (a public exhibition, an untrained operator, a heavier payload) and the case no longer applies: the hazard analysis must be redone for the new use. A safety case that claims "the robot is safe" without scope claims something no evidence can support. C1

Choosing strategies. Arguing over hazards is the most common top-level strategy, because it ties the case to the hazard log and makes completeness checkable (every hazard of CT1 or more has a claim). Other strategies refine individual claims: over the parts of a mechanism (G3 is split into the software ramp, G31, and the hard-wired chain, G32), over operating modes, or over the lifecycle (the claim that the hazard log is complete, G1, and that the evidence is traced and current, G5). Each strategy needs a short justification of why the sub-claims together are enough. D6

Evidence that fits. For each evidence item, four questions: does it exist; does it apply to the claimed configuration (F11-22); is it of a kind that can support the claim; and does it actually show what is claimed? The course's checker answers three of them mechanically: it opens each evidence file, looks for the text that the claim relies on (for example "ALL TESTS PASSED" and the lines for T4 and T5), and refuses simulation evidence for a claim scoped to hardware. The fourth question, configuration, comes from the trace checker's build ids. What no program answers is whether the argument is valid: that is what the review is for. R1

Open items are part of the case. The course robot's case has two undeveloped goals: G32 (the hard-wired chain timed on the real robot; there is no robot in this build) and G7 (the teach-mode pinch hazard, whose safety functions are this chapter's mini-project). Both are marked, with the reason and what would close them. The checker's verdict reflects that: no defects, two open goals, and the top claim does not yet hold. A case with no open items for a student robot is more suspicious than one with honest ones. R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The course robot's case, checked. The case is a text file in a small goal-structured format (claims G, strategies S, evidence E with the file and the texts it must show, contexts C, assumptions A, undeveloped goals U). The checker walks the tree from the top claim, opens every evidence file from the earlier labs, and prints the tree with a status for each node:





Every evidence item is "ok": each file exists in the repository and shows the texts the claim depends on, for example E5 requires the lines "T2 PASS@ss402-lab-1" to "T5 PASS@ss402-lab-1" and the count "1 gaps" in the trace output, so that the case cites the trace exactly as it is, gap included. The verdict is "the argument is sound so far; open goals must be closed before the claim holds". That sentence is the honest state of the course robot: the software side of its arm safety is argued and evidenced in simulation; the hardware side and one hazard are not. R1


What the checker cannot see. A program can find unsupported claims, missing files and scope mismatches. It cannot judge whether "argue over every hazard" is complete for this robot (only a comparison with the hazard log and a reviewer's knowledge can), whether a simulation's model is faithful enough to support a claim scoped "sim" (F11-21's model ignores interrupts and caches), whether an assumption is true in practice (A0 says a supervisor is always present: is that enforced, or only hoped?), or whether there is counter-evidence the authors did not cite (an incident report, a failing run they deleted). These are the reviewer's questions, and the practical exam is built around them. D6


Confidence and counter-evidence. Each evidence link carries an implicit claim: "this evidence is trustworthy". For the course robot that rests on the earlier chapters: deterministic runs with recorded toolchains, tests shown to kill mutants, a trace tied to a build id. A strong case says so, in a short confidence argument per evidence kind, and actively looks for evidence against its claims: the forensic runs of this course (the shared encoder of F11-18, the comms-thread e-stop of F11-21) are counter-evidence for earlier designs, and the case should cite them as reasons for the current design, not leave them out. D6


A living document. The case is valid for one configuration: one hazard log version, one build, one hardware revision, one set of operating rules. Every change re-opens the claims it touches (Figure 3 of F11-18: the return arrow), and operating experience (near misses, incidents, logs) feeds back into the hazard log and the case. MP6's R3 gate, "safety and readiness review before any real-robot run", is the moment the course robot's case is reviewed against its real hardware; the open goal G32 must be closed there. C1


Not verified — check before relying on this
Safety cases in the standards and notations. From the author's memory, not checked in this build: ISO 26262 requires a safety case as a work product (Part 2), progressively compiled from the work products of the safety lifecycle, and a functional safety assessment that judges it; IEC 61508 requires a functional safety assessment and documented evidence but, as far as the author recalls, does not use the term "safety case" as its central requirement; DO-178C does not use the term, and its counterpart is the Plan for Software Aspects of Certification together with the Software Accomplishment Summary; safety cases are also central in some national regulatory regimes (for example UK defence standards) and in UL 4600 for autonomous products. Goal Structuring Notation (GSN) is published as a community standard by the Assurance Case Working Group (originating in work by Tim Kelly at the University of York), and the Object Management Group publishes a structured assurance case metamodel (SACM). The definition of a safety case in this chapter's Jargon box paraphrases a commonly cited one whose origin the author recalls as a UK defence standard. Check each of these against the documents before citing them. D1 D2 D3 D6



How the hardware actually does it


The hardware claims of a robot's safety case are closed by measurements on the robot itself, made in a planned and supervised way and recorded like any other evidence. For the course robot's G32: with the arm unloaded and the supervisor at the e-stop, press the button while the arm moves, and measure with a timer or oscilloscope on the drive-enable line (or the contactor's auxiliary contact) the time from the contact opening to power removal, and with the arm's encoder log the time to rest; repeat under the worst-case software load of F11-21; record the board revision, firmware build, instrument and date. The evidence item for G32 then has kind "hw", and the checker accepts it under a hardware-scoped claim. Until then, the claim stays open, and the case's operating restrictions (A0: supervision, lab only) are what keep people safe. C1


Safety
Closing a hardware claim means testing a safety function on a real machine, which is the moment it is most likely to fail. Test with the arm unloaded and at reduced speed first, with the supervisor holding the e-stop, with nobody inside the arm's reach, and with the hard-wired chain verified before the test. Never weaken a protection to observe a failure. Follow the faculty F9 safety rules and the MP6 R3 gate. C1

Worked example


A. Closing G7 on paper. G7 (teach-mode pinch, CT1 in the course log as H4, scored S2 E3 A2 = 9 points) needs safety functions: SF5, "in teach mode, joint speed is limited to a slow value and the joint torque to a value at which a pinch cannot cause more than a minor injury"; SF6, "in teach mode, motion is possible only while the learner holds a three-position enabling switch in its middle position". The case would refine G7 by a strategy over the two functions, with claims for each (requirement met in the configuration), evidence for each (a test of the speed and torque limits in teach mode, a test that releasing or squeezing the switch stops motion, both on the robot: kind "hw"), and context naming the slow value and torque value with their source (the torque that keeps a pinch at S1 must come from an injury argument, not from memory). The checker would keep G7 open until those evidence files exist and show their texts.


B. A review finding, written well. Bad: "E31 is weak." Good: "E31 (estop_fixed.out) supports G31 for the model of F11-21, which has one CPU, no interrupts and a single 150 ms flush. G31's text claims the property 'under worst-case load'. Either narrow G31 to the modelled load and add a hardware claim for the real worst case, or add evidence from a load test on the robot. Severity: major (claim wider than evidence)." A finding names the node, the problem, why it matters and what would resolve it.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Amaka checks a car against a known, published list of inspection items. A robot's safety case has no fixed list: its authors must argue that their own list (the hazard log) is complete.
    • 

  • A car inspection happens once a year. A robot's case must be revisited on every change of software, hardware or use, which may be weekly.
    • 

  • Lucía did not write the folder; the seller did. In a safety case the authors are responsible for the argument, and the reviewer is responsible only for judging it.
    • 

  • Amaka can test the car herself. A reviewer of a safety case usually cannot re-run everything and must judge the evidence's trustworthiness from how it was produced.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • No scope. Symptom: "the robot is safe". Fix: state system, use, environment, operators and the definition of acceptably safe as context.
    • 

  • Evidence that shows a different thing. Symptom: a test log attached to a claim it does not mention. Find it: name the texts the claim relies on and let the checker look for them. Fix: attach the right evidence, or narrow the claim.
    • 

  • Simulation for hardware. Symptom: a hardware claim supported only by a simulator log (forensic lab). Fix: scope claims, add hardware evidence, keep the claim open until then.
    • 

  • Hidden open items. Symptom: every claim supported, nothing open, on a robot never run with its safety functions. Fix: mark undeveloped goals with reasons.
    • 

  • Assumptions that carry the case. Symptom: an assumption that, if false, defeats a claim (forensic lab's A0). Fix: turn critical assumptions into claims with evidence, or into operating rules that are enforced and checked.
    • 

  • A case that is never updated. Symptom: the case cites evidence from builds that no longer exist. Fix: the trace checker's build ids, and a case review on every release.
    •

Summary


    

  • A safety case is a scoped claim, an argument that splits it, and evidence for every leaf, with context and assumptions written down.
    • 

  • Arguing over the hazards of the hazard log ties the case to the analysis and makes completeness checkable.

  • Evidence must exist, fit the claim's scope, show what is claimed, and apply to the released configuration.
    • 

  • Open items are marked as undeveloped goals; the top claim does not hold until they are closed.

  • A checker finds unsupported claims, missing or contradicting evidence and scope mismatches; a reviewer judges validity, completeness, assumptions and counter-evidence.

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. Evidence alone does not say what it proves or whether anything is missing. A safety case states a scoped claim, shows how it splits into sub-claims that together imply it, and attaches each piece of evidence to the claim it supports, which makes gaps, misfits and open items visible to a reviewer.
    2. 

  2. Because the hazard analysis, the course targets and the evidence were produced for that use only: lab, trained learners, supervisor. A public open day changes exposure (visitors near the robot), avoidability (untrained people) and the operators; the hazard log must be redone for it, the new hazards argued and evidenced, and the case reviewed again before the event.
    3. 

  3. It says the argument and its cited evidence have no defects, so work can continue and the case can be reviewed. It does not allow the top claim to be stated as true: the open goals (the hardware e-stop chain and the teach-mode hazard) must be closed first, and until then only the case's operating restrictions and the faculty rules protect people.
    4. 

  4. A simulation shows the behaviour of a model; a hardware-scoped claim is about the real machine, so a model alone cannot support it. Under "sim" the claim is explicitly about the model; "any" is permissive. For G2 (overspeed), "any" is too weak: the speed monitor's real timing and encoder independence should be shown on hardware, so G2 should be split into a sim claim (supported) and a hardware claim (open).
    5. 

  5. E4 shows "DOES NOT SHOW" for that text, a defect, and the verdict becomes "NOT acceptable". That is right: the claim would rely on a statement that the evidence does not contain; the checker reads the evidence instead of trusting the claim. (Run it to see the exact line.)
    6. 

  6. Top claim: "the drone is acceptably safe for supervised flights at the university's approved test site, within line of sight, by trained pilots, under the national aviation authority's current rules (cited from the authority's document)". Context: the airframe and software configuration, the site, the definition of acceptably safe. Strategies: argue over each hazard of the drone's hazard log (including loss of link, low battery, fly-away, propeller contact on the ground), and argue over the lifecycle (SITL, HITL, bench tests with propellers off, field tests with the checklist).
    7. 

  7. For example A0 of team 4 ("the comms thread is never busy for more than 10 ms"), or the course case's "a supervisor is always present": both carry safety claims, so they need evidence (a load test; an enforced procedure with a sign-in record) or must become claims of the case.
    8. 



Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was produced. Listing 1 on forensic.in, a case written for this lab: E1 cites team 4's hazard log run (F11-19/forensic.out, 4 findings); E3 cites the comms-thread e-stop run (F11-21/estop_timeline.out) under a hardware-scoped claim; E4 cites a file that does not exist; G5 has no support; A0 is contradicted by the F11-21 evidence.


    

  1. E1: G1 claims the hazard log is complete; the evidence's verdict line reads "4 findings: the log is NOT ready for review", so it does not show "0 findings". E3: G3 claims the arm stops within 20 ms on the robot; the evidence is a simulation (wrong kind for a hardware claim) and it shows the arm moving 11.683 degrees with the relay cutting power while moving, not "software brought it to rest before the relay". E4: the file watchdog_rc7.out does not exist, so G4 is unsupported. G5: nothing supports the claim that the speed monitor is independent; after F11-18's forensic case that claim needs evidence most of all.
    2. 

  2. The scope problem: estop_fixed.out would show the text, but it is still simulation evidence for a hardware-scoped claim. The claim would also still say 20 ms, while the fixed run shows the arm at rest 99 ms after the press: the claim itself is wrong and must be restated.
    3. 

  3. A0 asserts the condition whose failure caused team 4's earlier incident: the comms thread was busy for 187.6 ms with a log flush (F11-21). An assumption contradicted by the team's own counter-evidence makes every claim that depends on it invalid; the design should make it irrelevant (e-stop read on the real-time path) rather than assume it.
    4. 

  4. Comparing with the course log: H2 (overspeed) appears only indirectly through G5; there is no claim for H5 (unexpected start after a teach-to-operate switch) as such, none for teach-mode pinch (the cause of the earlier incident), and none for H7 (maintenance). The case argues over "hazard log v0.9", which was itself incomplete.
    5. 

  5. Verdict: "Not accepted; no supervised run with visitors." Findings, for example: "G1/E1: the cited hazard log has 4 open findings including no teach-mode analysis; complete the log and re-cite (major)." "G3/E3: simulation evidence for a hardware claim, and the simulation contradicts the claim; restate G3 (at rest before the relay), move the e-stop to the real-time path, provide sim evidence (estop_fixed) for a sim claim and a hardware measurement for a hardware claim (major)." "G4/E4: evidence file missing; provide the rc7 watchdog test output with mutants (major)." "G5: unsupported; provide the design evidence that the monitor uses its own encoder and a test with encoder A frozen (major)." "A0: contradicted by the team's own F11-21 run; remove the dependency on it (major)." "Completeness: add claims for every CT1+ hazard in the corrected log, including teach mode (major)."
    6. 



Wrong answers and why. "Only E4 matters, the others are wording": E1 and E3 are evidence that contradicts its own claim, which is worse than missing evidence. "Accept with conditions": a case with contradicting evidence and an unsupported independence claim cannot be accepted conditionally before a run with visitors; the open items are exactly the hazards that already caused incidents.
