Story hook

Before a long tour, Joon's club checks every bicycle against a list: tyre pressure within the numbers printed on the tyre's side, brake pads within the wear mark, the chain the right width for the gears. Nobody trusts "it looks fine"; each line on the list says where the number came from.

One year a rider fits new, wider tyres a day before the tour. They fit the rims, they look fine, and he rides off. Halfway down the first long hill, the brakes rub the new tyres: the frame's clearance, printed in the frame's manual, was a few millimetres too small. Nothing was broken. Two parts that were each fine did not fit together, and nobody had compared their numbers.

Robots fail like this too. This last chapter of RB201 turns the motors, sensors, power and buses of F9-08 to F9-14 into one plan that compares every pair of connected parts using their datasheets, and checks it before the robot is built.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a wiring and power plan in which every connection, rail and address is backed by a cited datasheet value.
  • Check a logic connection with VOH, VOL, VIH, VIL and absolute maximum ratings, using the guaranteed (minimum or maximum) values, never the typical ones.
  • Check each part's supply against its operating range and its rail's current against a design rule.
  • Find I2C address conflicts and choose a fix.
  • Diagnose intermittent and damaging faults on a robot from its plan and a plan checker.
Jargon box
Wiring and power plan
Simple words: a written list of every part, every wire and every power line of the robot, with the numbers that show it works. Analogy: the club's check list for each bicycle. Precise: a design document listing parts, rails, connections (nets), buses and addresses, each with the datasheet values that justify it and their citations, together with the checks performed; it sits between the block diagram and the schematic (F1-79). S1
Logic-level compatibility
Simple words: whether the "high" and "low" of one chip are understood as "high" and "low" by the next. Analogy: whether one rider's hand signals are big enough for the next rider to read. Precise: a driver's guaranteed output levels must fall inside the receiver's guaranteed input levels: VOH(min) ≥ VIH(min) and VOL(max) ≤ VIL(max), with margin for noise and ground offset; and the driver's highest output must not exceed the receiver's absolute maximum input. D1
Level shifter
Simple words: a small circuit that translates a signal from one voltage to another. Analogy: an interpreter between two riders who signal differently. Precise: a translator IC, a transistor circuit or (for slow, one-way signals into a lower-voltage input) a resistor divider, chosen for direction, speed and the voltages on both sides from its datasheet. D1 D2
I2C address conflict
Simple words: two devices on one I2C bus answering to the same number. Analogy: two riders with the same name on the team radio. Precise: two targets with the same 7-bit address on one bus both respond and their answers collide; fixed by an address-select pin (if the part has one), a second bus, or a bus multiplexer. D3
Design rule (derating)
Simple words: a team's own rule for keeping well below a limit. Analogy: the club's rule to ride at most 80 % of your top speed downhill. Precise: a stated margin applied to datasheet limits, such as loading a rail to at most 80 % of its regulator's maximum continuous current or a bus to at most 70 % (F9-14); it is a team decision written into the plan, not a property of any part. S1
Datasheet citation
Simple words: writing down exactly where a number came from. Analogy: "tyre pressure from the tyre's sidewall", not "about right". Precise: for each value in the plan, the document (part number, revision) and the table or section it was copied from, so that a reviewer can check it and the plan can be updated when a part changes. S1

Terms from earlier chapters used here: datasheet, absolute maximum ratings, electrical characteristics (min/typ/max), schematic (HW303); I2C, open-drain (F1-47); power rail, power tree (F9-13); bus load (F9-14).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every part of a robot comes with a booklet from its maker that tells you its numbers: how much voltage it needs, how much it can take before it breaks, what it counts as "on" and "off".

When you connect two parts, their numbers have to fit together. If one part says "on" with 5 volts and the other breaks above 4 volts, connecting them breaks it. If one says "on" with 3 volts but the other only listens above 3.5 volts, it will sometimes hear "on" and sometimes not.

So before you build, you write a plan: every part, every wire, every number, and the page each number came from. Then you check every pair. A computer program can help check, but only if the numbers you typed in are right.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What goes into the plan

Like the club's list, the plan has one line per thing to check, and each line names its source. In this chapter's format (Listing 1): part lines give a part's supply range and absolute maximum; rail lines give a rail's voltage and maximum current; power lines connect a part to a rail with its supply current; signal lines give a net's driver output levels and receiver input levels; i2c lines give each device's bus and address. Every line ends with cite= and the datasheet section. S1 The motors, drivers and their peak currents come from F9-08, the power tree and budget from F9-13, the buses and loads from F9-14.

Checking a logic connection

A datasheet gives a digital output's guaranteed levels (VOH minimum while sourcing a stated current, VOL maximum while sinking one) and a digital input's thresholds (VIH minimum, VIL maximum), plus the input's absolute maximum voltage (F1-79). D1 A connection works for every part from every batch only if VOH(min) ≥ VIH(min) and VOL(max) ≤ VIL(max); typical values describe one average part and prove nothing. If the driver can output more than the receiver's absolute maximum, the connection can damage the receiver, even if it seems to work for a while. D1 D2

Checking supplies and rails

Each part's rail must lie inside its recommended operating range, which is narrower than its absolute maximum; operating between the two is not damage, but nothing in the datasheet is guaranteed there (F1-79). D2 Each rail's total load must stay under the team's design rule, here 80 % of the regulator's maximum continuous current, with peak currents checked as in F9-13.

Checking buses and addresses

I2C devices have fixed addresses, often with one or two pins to choose among a few values; two devices at the same address on the same bus cannot both work. D3 The plan lists every address per bus, and the bus loads of F9-14. Listing 1 checks the course robot's plan, revision A: no errors and one warning, because the LiDAR's line has no citation. R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Conditions behind each number. VOH(min) is guaranteed at a stated output current, supply voltage and temperature; a pin loaded harder, or a supply at its lowest, gives less. A thorough plan copies the conditions too. D2
  • Power sequencing and back-powering. A 5 V signal reaching a 3.3 V part that is not yet powered can feed current into the part through its input protection and partly power it; datasheets specify input limits relative to the part's own supply, which is why the absolute maximum is often given as "VDD + 0.3 V" rather than a fixed number. D2
  • Noise margins and ground offset. On long wires and near motors, the receiver's ground can differ from the driver's by tenths of a volt (F1-72); signals that cross the robot are better carried differentially (CAN, RS-485) than as single-ended logic. D1
  • Plans as code. Keeping the plan as text in version control, checked by a program on every change, works like unit tests for wiring: a part change that breaks a rule fails before any board is ordered. The checker cannot know whether a copied number is right; review against the cited table does that. S1

How the hardware actually does it

A CMOS input decides "high" or "low" by comparing the pin voltage with thresholds set by its transistors and supply; between VIL(max) and VIH(min) its answer is not guaranteed and can differ between parts and temperatures. Inputs have protection diodes to their supply and ground; a voltage beyond the supply forces current through them, which is what the absolute maximum rating limits. D1 D2 An encoder output stage, a motor driver's logic inputs and a microcontroller's GPIO (F1-76) each have such levels in their datasheets' electrical-characteristics tables.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The parts in both plans (microcontroller, IMU, magnetometer, motor drivers, encoder, LiDAR, regulators) are not real parts, and every voltage, current, level and address is pretend; citations such as "D6:output" are placeholders for real datasheet sections. The course kit has not been chosen, so the course project's plan cannot yet be checked against real datasheets. The statements about input protection diodes and "VDD + 0.3 V" style limits describe a common pattern and must be checked in each real part's datasheet.

Safety

Plan checks happen on paper and in the program, before power is applied. When a real robot is built from the plan, power it first with the motors disconnected or the emergency stop pressed, from a current-limited bench supply if available, under adult supervision for L2 learners. S1

Worked example

Task. The left encoder of revision C runs from 5 V, and its output's guaranteed high is 4.4–5.0 V. The 3.3 V microcontroller's input needs VIH(min) 2.3 V and VIL(max) 1.0 V, and its absolute maximum is 4.0 V. Design a resistor divider for this slow signal and check it.

  1. Target. The divided high must be ≥ 2.3 V at the lowest VOH (4.4 V) and ≤ the microcontroller's supply (3.3 V, staying under 4.0 V with margin) at the highest (5.0 V).
  2. Ratio. With R1 on top and R2 at the bottom, k = R2/(R1 + R2). Choose R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 20 kΩ: k = 0.667.
  3. High level. 4.4 × 0.667 = 2.93 V ≥ 2.3 V; 5.0 × 0.667 = 3.33 V. Within a few hundredths of the supply: acceptable only if the datasheet allows inputs up to its supply plus a stated margin. A safer choice is R1 = 12 kΩ, R2 = 20 kΩ: k = 0.625, giving 2.75–3.13 V.
  4. Low level. The encoder's VOL(max) 0.4 V × 0.625 = 0.25 V ≤ 1.0 V.
  5. Speed. 12 kΩ ∥ 20 kΩ = 7.5 kΩ with, say, 10 pF of input and wiring gives a time constant of 75 ns: fine for an encoder at a few kHz, too slow for a fast SPI clock. The plan's signal line is updated with the divided levels and the divider's citation (your calculation).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A bicycle check finds a rubbing tyre by riding slowly; a robot's overvoltage may not show at once. An input above its absolute maximum can work for days and then fail. D2
    • 

  • Bicycle numbers are few; a robot's are many. Dozens of nets and rails make a program check worth writing, where a club uses a paper list.
    • 

  • Riders can explain a misunderstanding; logic inputs cannot. A voltage between VIL and VIH is read differently by different parts, with no error reported.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a signal works on one robot and not on another. Cause: the design relied on typical values. How to find it: compare guaranteed levels (Listing 1). Fix: level shifter, or a part whose levels match.
    • 

  • Symptom: an input pin stops working after some days. Cause: driven above its absolute maximum. Fix: divider or translator; replace the damaged part.
    • 

  • Symptom: one of two identical sensors is missing on I2C. Cause: same address. Fix: address-select pin, second bus or multiplexer.
    • 

  • Symptom: the checker passes but the robot fails. Cause: a wrongly copied number or a missing line. Fix: review each line against its cited table; add the missing connection.
    • 

  • Symptom: after a part change, the plan is out of date. Cause: the plan was not updated with the part. Fix: no part change without a plan change and a checker run.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. plan_forensic.in was written with three injected faults against the shared checker: a 5 V encoder output into a 3.3 V input with a 4.0 V absolute maximum; a motor driver whose VIH(min) of 3.5 V is above the microcontroller's VOH(min) of 2.9 V; and a second IMU at the same address 0x68 on i2c1. All values pretend.


Root cause. Line 14 (DAMAGE): the encoder drives up to 5.0 V into an input rated to 4.0 V; current flows through the input's protection, the readings are noisy, and after a week the input is damaged. Line 15 (ERROR): the enable signal's high is not guaranteed; the result depends on each driver's actual threshold, hence "two robots of three". Line 17 (ERROR): both IMUs answer at 0x68; their replies collide or one is never addressed, so only one appears to answer. All three were absent in revision A, which is why the bench prototype worked.


Wrong answers. "Software bug in the enable code": it would not depend on the board. "Bad encoder batch": a new encoder will damage the next input too. "Broken second IMU": it is fine, just unreachable at its address.


Fixes. First the damage, because it destroys parts with every hour of running: a divider or translator on ENC_L_A (worked example), and replace the microcontroller if its pin is damaged. Then a 3.3 V-to-5 V translator on MOT_EN, or a driver whose inputs accept 3.3 V logic. Then the second IMU's address-select pin set for another address: with imu2 at 0x69 the checker reports 2 errors instead of 3 (R3), the remaining two needing the fixes above.


Prevention. No part change without updating the plan (with citations) and running the checker; the checker runs automatically on every change of the plan file, and a reviewer compares each changed line with its cited datasheet table.
