Story hook

Amara rides her bicycle to school every day. On the flat road she uses a high gear: every turn of the pedals pushes her a long way, and her legs turn slowly. At the foot of the hill she clicks down to a low gear. Now her legs spin fast, the bicycle creeps forward, and the climb that would have stopped her becomes possible.

Her legs did not get stronger. The gears changed the deal between how hard she pushes and how fast she goes. Her muscles also have limits of their own: she can push very hard for a few seconds to get up a kerb, but on a long hill she must stay below the effort she can keep up, or she has to stop and rest.

A robot's wheels are driven by motors, its muscles. Choosing a motor and a gear ratio is exactly Amara's problem, solved once at design time: hard enough for the steepest ramp, fast enough for the flat corridor, and never above the effort the motor can keep up without overheating.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what you command and what you get back from a brushed DC gearmotor, a brushless motor with its controller, a hobby servo and a stepper motor.
  • Compute the force, wheel torque and wheel speed a mobile robot needs from its mass, wheel radius, acceleration and steepest slope.
  • Choose a window of usable gear ratios by checking speed, peak torque and continuous torque against a motor's torque–speed line.
  • Convert a stepper's steps, microsteps and lead-screw lead into resolution and step rates.
  • Diagnose lost steps on a stepper axis from a log of moves and home-switch errors.
Jargon box
Actuator
Simple words: the part of a robot that makes something move. Analogy: a muscle. Precise: a device that turns a command (a voltage, a current, a pulse train, a message) into motion or force; defined for kids in F9-03. D1
Torque
Simple words: how hard something twists. Analogy: how hard Amara presses on the pedal. Precise: the turning effect of a force about an axis, force times lever arm, in newton metres (N m). A wheel of radius r pushing the ground with force F needs torque F × r. D1
Gear ratio (gearhead)
Simple words: how many turns of the motor make one turn of the wheel. Analogy: the bicycle's gears, used the other way round: a robot uses a large ratio to turn a fast, weak motor into a slow, strong wheel. Precise: for a ratio G, wheel speed = motor speed / G and wheel torque = G × motor torque × gearbox efficiency (efficiency below 1 because of friction in the gears). D1
Continuous (rated) torque
Simple words: the twist the motor can keep up without getting too hot. Analogy: the effort Amara can keep up on a long hill. Precise: the torque at which the motor's temperature stays within its limit in continuous running, given in the motor's datasheet; brief peaks above it are allowed only for times the datasheet states. D1 D2
Stepper motor
Simple words: a motor that moves in small fixed steps, one step per pulse. Analogy: climbing stairs one step at a time instead of walking up a ramp. Precise: a motor whose rotor lines up with the magnetic field of whichever coils are energised; switching the coil currents in a fixed sequence turns it by one step per change, so position is known by counting steps, as long as no step is lost. D1
Microstepping
Simple words: making smaller steps between the big ones. Analogy: taking half or quarter stairs. Precise: a driver mode that sets the two coil currents to intermediate values so the rotor rests between full-step positions; the driver's datasheet lists the available divisions. D2
Lost steps (stall of a stepper)
Simple words: the motor misses steps it was told to make, and nobody notices. Analogy: tripping on the stairs while still counting steps out loud. Precise: when the torque needed to follow the step sequence exceeds what the motor can produce at that step rate, the rotor falls out of step with the field; an open-loop controller keeps counting, so its position belief becomes wrong. D1

Terms from earlier chapters used here: brushed DC motor, torque–speed curve, brushless DC motor (BLDC), hobby servo, ESC, PWM (all defined in HW302).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot's motors are its muscles. Some muscles are fast but weak, like a small motor that spins very quickly but can hardly push anything. A gearbox turns that into a slow, strong twist, the way a low bicycle gear lets you climb a hill.

Different motors are told what to do in different ways. A plain motor is told "this much power". A servo is told "go to this angle", and it gets there by itself. A stepper is told "one step, one step, one step", and it moves one small step each time. A brushless motor needs a little helper computer that switches its coils for it.

Before you buy a motor, you work out what the robot needs: how heavy it is, how fast it must go, and how steep a ramp it must climb. Then you check that the motor can do it without getting too hot.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What the robot needs at the wheels

Like Amara on her hill, the robot needs force to speed up and force to climb. For a robot of mass m on a slope of angle θ, accelerating at a, the wheels together must push with

F = m·a + m·g·sin θ + Crr·m·g·cos θ

where the last term is rolling resistance with a coefficient Crr that depends on the tyres and the floor. D1 Shared between n driven wheels of radius r, each wheel needs torque F·r/n, and to roll at speed v it must turn at v/r radians per second. D1 Listing 1 does this for a pretend 4.0 kg robot with 35 mm wheels: accelerating at 1.0 m/s² on the flat needs 4.785 N in total (0.0837 N m per wheel); climbing a 10° ramp at 0.6 m/s needs 7.587 N (0.1328 N m per wheel) at 17.14 rad/s. R1

Gearing: trading speed for torque

A gearhead of ratio G makes the motor turn G times faster than the wheel and needs only 1/(G·η) of the wheel torque from the motor, where η is the gearbox efficiency. D1 A brushed DC motor at a fixed supply voltage gives a torque that falls in a straight line from the stall torque at zero speed to zero at the no-load speed (F1-69). D1 Three checks decide whether a ratio works:

  1. Speed: G × wheel speed must stay below the no-load speed, or the robot never reaches its top speed.
  2. Peak torque: at that motor speed, the torque needed on the ramp must be below the line, or the motor stalls.
  3. Continuous torque: if the ramp is long, the torque needed must also stay below the motor's continuous rating, or it overheats. D2

A small G fails check 3 (too much torque for too long); a large G fails checks 1 and 2 (too fast for the motor). The usable ratios form a window. For Listing 1's pretend motor the window runs from 15 to 40 among the ratios tried; 10 overheats, 45 stalls, 50 and above cannot reach top speed. R1 A sensible pick sits in the middle, with margin on every check, because real loads, floors and batteries differ from the plan (F9-13).

Which muscle for which job

  • Brushed DC gearmotors drive wheels on small robots: simple to drive from an H-bridge with PWM (F1-70), cheap, and with an encoder on the shaft their speed can be controlled (RB202). Their brushes wear and make electrical noise. D1
  • Brushless motors have no brushes; an electronic controller switches three phases in step with the rotor's position, sensed by Hall sensors or estimated from the back-EMF. D1 You talk to the controller, not the motor: by PWM, by a serial protocol or over CAN (F9-14), as its documentation says. D2
  • Hobby servos contain a small motor, a gearbox, a potentiometer on the output and a controller: you send a pulse whose width means an angle, and the inner loop holds it there (F1-69). Good for steering linkages, grippers and pan-tilt heads; the pulse widths and angle range come from the servo's datasheet. D2
  • Steppers move a fixed angle per step without any feedback, which makes them natural for positioning axes (a lifting platform, a lead-screw slider). The price is that a lost step is invisible to the controller. D1

Stepper arithmetic

A two-phase stepper in full-step mode with one phase on at a time runs through the pattern A+, B+, A−, B−, which then repeats every four steps (Listing 2). D1 With a pretend 200 full steps per revolution, a full step is 1.8°; with the driver set to 8 microsteps per step and a lead screw that moves 8 mm per revolution, one microstep moves the carriage 0.005 mm, and 50 mm/s needs 10,000 pulses per second. R2 Microsteps are smaller, but they are not equally strong or equally accurate: the holding torque between full-step positions and the accuracy of each microstep are in the motor's and driver's datasheets. D2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Reflected inertia. A gearbox multiplies the motor's own rotor inertia by G² as seen from the wheel. With large ratios the motor's inertia, not the robot's mass, can dominate acceleration, and a wheel cannot be back-driven by hand. D1
  • The line moves with the supply. The no-load speed of a brushed DC motor is proportional to the voltage at its terminals; when the battery sags under load (F9-13), the whole line in Figure 2 moves down and to the left. Design with the lowest battery voltage you will run at, not the nominal one. D1
  • Thermal time constants. A motor may exceed its continuous torque for seconds while it warms up; the datasheet's thermal resistance and time constant (or a peak-torque duty table) say for how long. A ramp that takes two seconds is a peak; one that takes two minutes is continuous. D2
  • Stepper pull-out torque. The torque a stepper can deliver while keeping step falls as the step rate rises; datasheets give it as a pull-out curve measured with a named driver and supply voltage. Acceleration profiles must keep inertial plus friction torque under that curve at every speed, with margin. D1 D2
  • Field-oriented control. Better brushless controllers regulate the motor current as a vector aligned with the rotor (field-oriented control), which gives smooth torque at low speed; how a given controller does it, and what it reports back, is in its documentation. D1

How the hardware actually does it

Every actuator in Figure 1 has power electronics between your logic pin and the coils. A brushed motor's H-bridge switches the battery across the winding with PWM; a brushless controller has three half-bridges and a small processor that decides when to switch them; a stepper driver regulates the current in each coil by chopping it, which is how it can microstep. D1 Your microcontroller only produces a few milliamps at logic level; the driver's datasheet says which input levels it accepts (F9-15), how much current it can deliver, and what happens when it overheats or sees a short. D2 This is why "select an actuator" always means "select an actuator and its driver".

Worked example

Task. Check gear ratio 20 for Listing 1's robot and motor by hand, then decide whether the robot can also accelerate at 1.0 m/s² while already on the ramp.

  1. Motor speed at top speed. Wheel speed = 0.6 / 0.035 = 17.14 rad/s; × 20 = 342.9 rad/s, below the no-load speed of 800 rad/s. ✓
  2. Available torque there. 0.050 × (1 − 342.9/800) = 0.02857 N m.
  3. Torque needed on the ramp. 0.1328 N m per wheel / (20 × 0.80) = 0.00830 N m: below the line ✓ and below the continuous 0.012 N m ✓. These are the numbers of run R1's row for ratio 20. R1
  4. Ramp plus acceleration. Extra force m·a = 4.0 × 1.0 = 4.0 N; extra wheel torque 4.0 × 0.035 / 2 = 0.070 N m; total 0.1328 + 0.070 = 0.2028 N m per wheel; motor torque 0.2028 / 16 = 0.01268 N m. Accelerating at low speed: well under the stall torque (0.050) ✓. Near top speed: under 0.02857 ✓. Above the continuous rating, but only while accelerating, which lasts 0.6 s: a peak, allowed if the datasheet's peak rating agrees.
  5. Verdict. Ratio 20 works with margin: about 3.4 × on the ramp's peak torque and about 1.4 × on the continuous rating.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Muscles feel; most motors do not. Amara knows how hard she is pushing and where her feet are. A brushed motor or a stepper reports nothing: a robot knows its wheel turned only if an encoder tells it (F9-09), and a stepper's controller can believe in steps that never happened. D1
    • 

  • Muscles tire slowly and recover; motor windings overheat and can be damaged. Above the continuous rating a winding's insulation is at risk, and a stalled motor at full voltage draws its stall current until something gives way. Limits come from the datasheet, not from how it feels. D2
    • 

  • A bicycle's gears change while riding; a robot's gear ratio is usually fixed when the robot is built. The design must cover the whole range of tasks with one ratio, which is why the window and the margins matter.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot is fine on the flat and stops on the ramp. Cause: gear ratio too small or too large for the ramp, or the battery sags so the line moves (Layer 3). How to find it: log motor current and speed on the ramp; compare with Figure 2. Fix: pick a ratio in the window with margin, at the lowest battery voltage.
    • 

  • Symptom: the motors get too hot to touch after a long run. Cause: continuous torque above the rating. How to find it: compute the torque of the longest steady task (Listing 1's "overheats" row). Fix: a larger ratio, a larger motor, or a lighter robot.
    • 

  • Symptom: a stepper axis ends up short of its target after fast moves. Cause: acceleration needing more torque than the pull-out curve gives. How to find it: home after every move and log the error (forensic lab). Fix: gentler acceleration, a lower top speed, a higher driver voltage within the datasheet's limits, or an encoder to detect the loss.
    • 

  • Symptom: a servo buzzes and gets warm while holding still. Cause: it is pushing against a mechanical stop or a load it cannot hold. Fix: limit the commanded angle to the free range; reduce the load.
    • 

  • Symptom: the gearmotor's speed is half what you computed. Cause: the datasheet's speed is for the motor before the gearhead, or for another voltage. Fix: read which shaft and which voltage each number refers to (F9-15).
    • 




Transition box

Where you came from: F9-03 treated motors as muscles that turn wheels when told to; F1-69 and F1-70 (HW302) modelled the motor and its driver as circuits. What carries over: the torque–speed line, PWM through an H-bridge, and the rule that every number comes from a datasheet. D1 What changes: most dangerous first: you now choose parts for a whole robot, so a wrong choice is built into the machine; steady loads (ramps) and peaks (accelerations) must be checked separately; and steppers and servos hide their control loops, so their failures are silent. D1 What comes next: F9-09 adds the encoder that tells the robot how far its wheels really turned, and turns those counts into position.

Summary


    

  • Actuators differ in what you send them and what they report back: DC (power), brushless (via a controller), servo (an angle), stepper (steps, no feedback).
    • 

  • Wheel force = m·a + m·g·sin θ + rolling resistance; wheel torque = F·r/n; wheel speed = v/r.
    • 

  • A gear ratio G multiplies speed demand by G and divides torque demand by G·η.
    • 

  • Usable ratios form a window: speed and peak-torque checks bound it from above, the continuous-torque check from below.
    • 

  • Design at the lowest battery voltage, with margins on every check.
    • 

  • Steppers count steps; when the torque needed exceeds the pull-out curve, steps are lost silently.
    • 

  • Every number for a real design comes from the chosen part's datasheet.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. stepper_forensic.cpp simulates each move step by step with a trapezoidal profile (cruise 2000 steps/s). The torque available falls linearly from 0.40 N m at rest to zero at 3000 steps/s (a simplified pull-out curve); the torque needed is inertia × angular acceleration + friction. Empty: inertia 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ kg m², friction 0.03 N m. Carrying a part: 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ kg m², 0.05 N m. If the need exceeds the available torque at any step, the model stalls and all remaining steps of that move are lost (real steppers may lose fewer steps and keep going; the simplification makes the evidence clearer). All values pretend.


Root cause. Only cycles with v1.3 (acceleration raised from 2000 to 6000 steps/s²) and a part carried back fail (6 and 8); v1.3 without a part (4, 5, 7) and v1.2 with a part (2, 3) are fine. The error is always 3693 steps, so the stall happened 4000 − 3693 = 307 steps into the return move, at a rate of √(2 × 6000 × 307) ≈ 1919 steps/s: the point where, in the model, the loaded axis's need (5.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 6000 × 2π/200 + 0.05 ≈ 0.144 N m) meets the falling curve (0.40 × (1 − 1919/3000) ≈ 0.144 N m).


Wrong answers. "Broken switch": a broken switch would fail regardless of firmware and payload, and would not produce the same error each time. "Loose coupling": it would slip at random amounts, also when empty. "Random": the failures are perfectly predictable from two columns.


Fixes. Firmware: lower the acceleration when carrying a part (or back to 2000 steps/s²); lower the cruise rate so the accelerating demand stays under the curve. Hardware: a stepper or driver/voltage combination with more torque at speed (from the datasheets), or an encoder to detect and correct lost steps. Test: rerun the cycle log with each change and require home error 0 on every loaded cycle, plus a margin check like the lab's extension.


Prevention. The axis's design note states the pull-out curve used, the worst-case load, the acceleration limit and its margin; the firmware checks the home switch after every move (or compares with an encoder) and raises a clear "steps lost" error instead of "home not found".
