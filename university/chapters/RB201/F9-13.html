Story hook

Joon plans a long training ride: ten laps of a park loop, each with one steep hill. He ate a good breakfast and feels sure he has enough energy for all ten laps. On the flat parts of lap five he still feels fine. On the hill of lap five his legs suddenly will not push any more, and he has to get off and walk, even though he could ride on the flat for another hour.

His energy was not used up. What ran out was his ability to deliver a lot of power at once while already tired. On the flat it did not matter; on the hill it decided the ride.

Robots fail the same way. A battery with half its charge left can still switch the robot off, at the one moment the robot asks for its biggest current. This chapter is about planning a robot's power so that the plan matches what the battery, the regulators and the wires can actually deliver, and about the safety parts that every power system needs.

What you will be able to do

  • Draw a power tree for a small robot: battery, protection, emergency-stop path, regulators and rails, with each load on a rail.
  • Compute a power budget with regulator efficiencies and estimate runtime, stating what is datasheet, measured or pretend.
  • Choose between a linear and a switching regulator from the power it must waste as heat.
  • Size a fuse and check a wire's voltage drop for a given current.
  • Diagnose a battery-protection cut-off from a discharge log, separating "energy used up" from "voltage sag under peak current".
Jargon box
Power tree
Simple words: a drawing of where the electricity goes, from the battery to every part. Analogy: how the food Joon ate reaches his legs, heart and brain. Precise: a diagram of the power path from source to loads: protection, switches, the emergency-stop path, converters and rails, with the current each branch carries. D1
Power rail
Simple words: one supply voltage shared by several parts, such as "5 V". Analogy: one pipe in a house's water system that several taps hang on. Precise: a supply net at one nominal voltage, fed by one source or converter, with a maximum continuous current set by that source and by its wiring. D1
Linear regulator
Simple words: a part that makes a lower voltage by burning off the difference as heat. Analogy: braking on a downhill to keep a fixed speed: the extra energy becomes heat. Precise: a regulator that drops (Vin − Vout) across a pass transistor; it dissipates about (Vin − Vout) × Iout, so its efficiency is at most Vout/Vin; low noise, simple. D1
Switching regulator (buck, boost)
Simple words: a part that changes voltage by switching very quickly and storing energy in a coil, wasting little. Analogy: changing gear instead of braking. Precise: a converter that switches the input at a high frequency through an inductor and smooths the result; a buck converter steps down, a boost converter steps up; efficiency depends on load and is given as curves in its datasheet; it makes switching noise. D1 D4
Battery protection (cut-off)
Simple words: a circuit in or beside the battery that switches it off if something unsafe happens. Analogy: Joon's legs refusing to push when he is too tired. Precise: a protection circuit or battery management system (BMS) that disconnects the pack on under-voltage, over-voltage, over-current, short circuit or over-temperature, with thresholds and delays given by the pack maker. D3
Wire voltage drop
Simple words: voltage lost in the wires themselves. Analogy: pressure lost in a long, thin hose. Precise: ΔV = I × Rwire for the whole loop (out and back); Rwire = resistance per metre (from the wire's table) × loop length; it also heats the wire with I²R. D1
Motor power cut (emergency-stop path)
Simple words: the switch that takes power away from the motors when the red button is pressed. Analogy: Joon's hands on the brakes, which work even if his mind is elsewhere. Precise: a hardware path that removes energy from the actuators without depending on software (F9-07); which parts stay powered (for example the computer, to log the event) is a design decision written into the power tree. S1

Terms from earlier chapters used here: capacity and energy, C-rate, internal resistance and voltage sag, state of charge and cut-off voltage, power budget, brown-out (F1-71); star ground (F1-72); fuse (HW101); emergency stop (F9-07).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A robot's battery is like the food in a rider's body. It holds energy, and the robot uses it up as it drives, thinks and looks around.

Different parts need different voltages, like a house with big pipes and small pipes. Special parts called regulators make the smaller voltages. Some waste a lot of energy as heat; better ones waste little.

Every power system needs safety parts: fuses that melt before wires get too hot, and an emergency stop that cuts power to the motors. And it needs a plan: how much each part uses, so you know how long the robot can run. When the robot climbs a hill, it asks for a lot of power at once, and a tired battery may not manage that even though it still has energy left.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The power tree

Like the body sending food to its muscles and its brain, a robot's power tree sends battery energy to its actuators and its electronics, and it does that through two different kinds of branch (Figure 1). Motor branches carry large, changing currents and must be cut by the emergency stop in hardware (F9-07); logic branches carry small, steady currents at regulated voltages and must stay clean and stable (F1-72). D1 S1 Keeping them separate, each with its own fuse and with grounds joined at one point, keeps motor current from disturbing the computer's supply and lets the emergency stop remove motor power while the computer records what happened. D1

The budget with regulators

A power budget (F1-71) lists every load's average and peak current on its rail. Listing 1 adds one step: a load fed through a regulator draws its power divided by the regulator's efficiency from the battery. With seven pretend loads and an efficiency of 0.85, the robot draws 35.4 W on average (2.95 A from 12 V) and 113.7 W if every peak happened at once (9.48 A); using 80 % of 5 Ah gives about 81 minutes. R1 Each of these numbers needs a source: a datasheet value (typical or maximum, F1-79) or your own measurement. The motors' average depends on the route, so it is the least certain number in the budget. D2

Linear or switching

A linear regulator from 12 V to 5 V at 1.2 A takes 12 V × 1.2 A = 14.4 W and delivers 6 W: it turns 8.4 W into heat, an efficiency of 42 %, and needs a large heat sink. A switching regulator at 85 % takes 6/0.85 = 7.06 W and wastes 1.06 W. D1 A linear regulator still has its place: for small currents, or after a switching regulator to give a quiet supply to a sensitive sensor. Efficiency is not one number; a switching regulator's datasheet shows it against load current and input voltage. D4

Fuses and wires

A fuse protects the wire after it, not the device: it must open before that wire overheats, and it must not open at the normal peak current. So the order of choice is: find the branch's peak current (including motor start-up and stall, F9-08), choose a wire that carries it continuously, then a fuse rated between the two, checking the fuse's time–current curve for short peaks. D1 Long, thin wires also lose voltage: ΔV = I × R for the loop, which adds to the battery's own sag.

When the plan meets the battery

The battery's terminal voltage is its open-circuit voltage minus I × Rint (F1-71). D3 A protection circuit switches the pack off if the terminal voltage stays below its cut-off for a short time. So the highest current of the route, not the average, decides when the robot stops: the forensic lab shows a robot planned for over an hour that stops after 30 minutes with 62 % of its charge unused. R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Regeneration. A motor slowing down or driven downhill acts as a generator and pushes current back into the supply; with a battery whose protection blocks charging, or a supply that cannot absorb it, the motor rail's voltage rises and can exceed the drivers' absolute maximum (F1-79). Drivers and power trees handle it with a braking resistor or a clamp; the driver's datasheet says which. D2 D4
  • Inrush. Large capacitors at the input of drivers and converters draw a big current when power is switched on, which can trip a fuse or the pack's over-current protection, or weld a switch's contacts; pre-charge circuits or soft-start limit it. D1
  • Brown-out on the logic rail. A switching regulator keeps its output only while its input stays above a minimum; under motor sag the logic may reset even if the pack does not cut off (F1-71 forensic). The regulator's minimum input voltage belongs in the same check as the pack's cut-off. D4
  • Measuring instead of guessing. A current sensor on the battery line, logged with the robot's data, replaces the budget's guesses with measured averages and peaks; it is the fastest way to calibrate the model of Listing 2 for a real pack. D3

How the hardware actually does it

A robot battery pack is several cells in series (and sometimes in parallel), with a protection board that measures each cell's voltage, the pack current and the temperature, and opens power transistors when a limit is crossed; many packs also report their state through a bus such as SMBus or CAN (F9-14). D3 A buck converter switches its input through a transistor into an inductor at tens to thousands of kilohertz and regulates the output by changing the duty cycle, exactly like the PWM of F1-69 but controlled from the output voltage. D1 The motor-power cut is usually a contactor or relay (or a driver's dedicated safe-torque-off input) wired in series with the emergency-stop button so that pressing the button opens the circuit without software.

Not verified — check before relying on this

No battery pack, regulator, fuse or wire has been chosen for the course robot. Every current, efficiency (0.85), capacity (5 Ah), internal resistance (0.10 Ω), the 10.9 V cut-off and its 0.5 s delay, and the straight-line open-circuit voltage are pretend values: real packs have a discharge curve that is not a straight line, a resistance that rises with age and cold, and thresholds set by the pack maker. Take them from the pack's datasheet (D3), the regulator's datasheet (D4) and your own measurements. Emergency-stop circuits are covered by machinery-safety standards (for example ISO 13850, title only, not opened in this build); the course's rule of a hardware motor cut comes from F9-07, not from a reading of those standards.

Safety

Lithium packs can deliver very large currents into a short circuit and can catch fire if damaged, over-charged or over-discharged. Only use packs with protection, a fuse close to the pack, and the maker's charger; never cut or bypass a protection board. L2 learners work on robot power only with an adult who knows these packs, with the pack disconnected while wiring. S1 D3

Worked example

Task. The motor branch of Figure 1 carries 8 A at peak (both motors on the ramp) and 2 A on average. The wire from the battery to the drivers is 0.6 m long each way. A pretend wire table gives 0.013 Ω per metre for the wire size you would like to use, and says it carries 15 A continuously. (a) What is the wire's voltage drop at peak? (b) Choose a fuse rating. (c) What does the drop add to the pack's sag?

  1. Loop resistance. 2 × 0.6 m × 0.013 Ω/m = 0.0156 Ω.
  2. Drop at peak. 8 A × 0.0156 Ω = 0.125 V; heating 8² × 0.0156 = 1.0 W along the wire, spread over 1.2 m.
  3. Fuse. It must carry the 8 A peaks without opening and open well before the wire's 15 A limit is exceeded for long: a 10 A slow-blow fuse fits between them, if its time–current curve shows it will not open during the ramp's 8 s peaks. D1
  4. Total. The drivers see the pack's sag (8 A × 0.10 Ω = 0.8 V in the pretend pack) plus 0.125 V: 0.93 V below the resting voltage, which must stay above the drivers' minimum supply.
  5. Check against the budget. At 2 A average the wire wastes 2² × 0.0156 = 0.06 W: negligible for runtime, but not for the voltage at peaks.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Joon recovers after a rest; a protected pack may not switch back on by itself. Many protection circuits need a charger or a reset to reconnect. D3
    • 

  • Food is not delivered at fixed "voltages". The rail idea (many parts sharing one exact voltage, made by converters) has no good counterpart in the body.
    • 

  • Joon feels tiredness coming; a robot does not unless it measures. Without a logged battery voltage and current, the first sign is the cut-off itself.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: runtime far shorter than the budget. Cause: averages guessed, not measured; or a cut-off from sag (forensic lab). How to find it: log battery voltage and current. Fix: measured budget; check peak current × Rint against the cut-off margin.
    • 

  • Symptom: computer resets when the robot accelerates. Cause: brown-out on the logic rail from motor sag or shared ground wiring. Fix: separate branches, star ground, regulator with a lower minimum input (F1-71, F1-72).
    • 

  • Symptom: a regulator is too hot to touch. Cause: a linear regulator dropping a large voltage at a large current. Fix: switching regulator.
    • 

  • Symptom: the fuse blows at every start. Cause: inrush or start-up peaks above the rating. Fix: slow-blow fuse sized from the curve, or soft-start; never just a much larger fuse than the wire allows.
    • 

  • Symptom: the emergency stop stops the motors only after a delay, or not when the computer has crashed. Cause: the stop is a software message, not a power cut. Fix: hardware motor-power cut (F9-07).
    •

Summary


    

  • A power tree separates motor and logic branches, fuses each, and cuts motor power in hardware for the emergency stop.
    • 

  • Through a regulator, power is conserved, not current: battery power = load power / efficiency.
    • 

  • Linear regulators waste (Vin − Vout) × I; switching regulators waste little but make noise.
    • 

  • A fuse protects the wire: rated above the normal peaks, below what the wire carries.
    • 

  • Wires lose I × R of voltage on top of the battery's sag.
    • 

  • The route's peak current can end a run long before the energy is used: check OCV − Ipeak·Rint against the cut-off.

  • Log battery voltage and current; measured numbers replace the budget's guesses.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. discharge_log.cpp models a 5 Ah pack with an open-circuit voltage falling linearly from 12.6 V to 10.5 V, Rint = 0.10 Ω, and a protection that cuts if the terminal voltage stays under 10.9 V for 0.5 s; the route draws 3 A, and 9 A for 8 s of each 60 s loop. All values pretend.


Root cause. The gap between the open-circuit and lowest terminal voltage is 0.89–0.90 V every minute at a highest current of 9.0 A: Rint ≈ 0.9 / 9 = 0.10 Ω. The ramp's sag reaches the cut-off when the open-circuit voltage falls to 10.9 + 0.9 = 11.8 V; the log reaches it at 30.3 min with 1.92 of 5.0 Ah used (38 %). The pack is not faulty: its resting voltage of 11.79 V is exactly the open-circuit voltage at that charge. A second, smaller cause: the real average current is 3.80 A, not the budget's 2.95 A, so even without the cut-off the runtime would be 4.0 Ah / 3.80 A ≈ 63 min, not 81.


Wrong answers. "Faulty pack": the voltages behave exactly as the model of a healthy pack. "Empty battery": 62 % of the charge remains. "The label's cut-off is wrong": the cut-off works as labelled; it is the terminal voltage under load that reaches it.


Fixes. A pack with lower internal resistance (0.05 Ω: 47.3 min; R3), less current on the ramp (6 A: 46.3 min; R3), or a larger pack whose sag is smaller at the same current. Each is verified by a full patrol with the battery logged.


Prevention. The budget includes the route's peak current and checks OCV − Ipeak × Rint against the cut-off over the whole discharge; the robot logs battery voltage and current and returns to charge when the resting voltage nears the "peak-safe" level (11.8 V here), not when it nears the label's cut-off.
