Story hook

Kwame wants to know how far he rides to his grandmother's house, but his bicycle has no meter. So he ties a ribbon to a spoke of the front wheel and counts how often it comes round: 400 turns. He measures the wheel with a string: one turn rolls 2.1 metres. About 840 metres, he says.

On the way back it has rained. On the muddy patch by the river his back wheel spins without moving the bicycle forward, and on a steep corner his front wheel slides sideways. The count still says 400 turns. But now it is not quite the distance he rode.

A robot does what Kwame did, many times per second, with both wheels. From how far each wheel turned, it works out where it is and which way it faces. This is called odometry. It is wonderfully simple, it drifts a little with every metre, and when a wheel slips or a number in the calculation is wrong, it can drift a lot.

What you will be able to do

  • Convert encoder counts into distance rolled by each wheel, from the wheel diameter and the counts per revolution.
  • Compute the pose (x, y, heading) of a differential-drive robot from the two wheel distances, step by step.
  • Distinguish systematic odometry errors (a wrong diameter or track width) from non-systematic ones (slip, bumps) and say how each grows.
  • Calibrate wheel diameters and track width from measured test drives.
  • Diagnose "the robot drifts left" from encoder logs: slip versus a wrong wheel-diameter parameter.
Jargon box
Pose
Simple words: where the robot is and which way it faces. Analogy: "I am at the bakery, facing the river." Precise: for a robot on a floor, the triple (x, y, θ): position in a fixed frame and heading angle, measured counter-clockwise from the x axis. D1
Track width (wheel separation)
Simple words: how far apart the two drive wheels are. Analogy: the width of the bicycle's handlebar, if each hand were a wheel. Precise: the distance b between the contact points of the left and right wheels on the floor, used to turn the difference in wheel distances into a change of heading. D1
Dead reckoning
Simple words: working out where you are only from how you moved since the start. Analogy: Kwame's turn count, with no map and no signposts. Precise: estimating the current pose by adding up measured motion increments from a known start pose; errors are never corrected, so they accumulate. Wheel odometry is dead reckoning from wheel rotation. D2
Systematic odometry error
Simple words: an error that happens the same way every time, because a number is wrong. Analogy: Kwame measured the wheel with a stretched string. Precise: errors from imperfect kinematic parameters (unequal or mis-measured wheel diameters, wrong track width, misalignment); they push the estimate in the same direction on every run and grow steadily with distance. D3
Non-systematic odometry error (wheel slip)
Simple words: an error that happens now and then, when a wheel slides or bumps. Analogy: the back wheel spinning in the mud. Precise: errors from the interaction with the floor (slip while accelerating or turning, uneven floors, bumps); they depend on where and how the robot drove, not on a parameter. D3
Odometry calibration
Simple words: measuring the robot's real wheel sizes and wheel spacing by test drives. Analogy: Kwame rolling his bicycle along a measured 10 m line and counting turns. Precise: estimating the effective wheel diameters and track width from drives whose true result is measured (straight runs, turns in place, closed square paths in both directions), then using those values in the odometry. D3

Terms from earlier chapters used here: encoder (RB101), incremental encoder, counts per revolution (CPR), odometry (HW302, F1-66), differential drive (F9-03).

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows one odometry step for a differential-drive robot seen from above: each wheel rolls its own distance, the centre moves by their average, and the heading turns by their difference divided by the track width. Figure 2 shows the same idea over a whole run: the robot drove along a straight tape, but its odometry drew a path curving left.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Each wheel of the robot has an encoder that counts how much the wheel turns. If you know how far the wheel rolls in one turn, you can turn counts into metres. That is the first half of odometry.

The second half is about turning. If both wheels roll the same distance, the robot went straight. If the right wheel rolled further than the left, the robot turned left, like a car on a curve. The bigger the difference, the more it turned.

The robot adds up these little moves many times per second. The trouble is that every little mistake is added too. A wheel that slips, or a wheel size typed wrong, makes the robot believe it is somewhere it is not, and the mistake keeps growing.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From counts to wheel distance

Like Kwame counting ribbon turns, the robot counts encoder edges. With CPR counts per wheel revolution (after the gearbox and the x4 decoding of F1-66) and a wheel of diameter d, one count is π·d/CPR of rolled distance. D1 For the pretend robot of this chapter (d = 0.070 m, CPR = 1440) that is 0.1527 mm per count. R2 The counts must be read as differences since the last step, with the wrap-around care of F1-66, and both wheels must be read at the same moment.

One odometry step

For a differential-drive robot with track width b, if the left and right wheels rolled ΔsL and ΔsR since the last step, the centre moved Δs = (ΔsR + ΔsL)/2 and the heading changed by Δθ = (ΔsR − ΔsL)/b (Figure 1). D1 The position update uses the heading halfway through the step, θ + Δθ/2, which is more accurate than using the old heading when the robot turns during the step. D2 Listing 2 does exactly this.

Fed with the simulator's log of a 1 m square driven anticlockwise (Listing 1), Listing 2 ends at x = 0.0000 m, y = −0.0000 m with heading 360.008°: odometry with perfect parameters and no slip closes the square, apart from rounding counts to whole numbers. R2 Each 90° turn in place shows as the left count falling by 823 and the right rising by 823: both wheels roll an arc of (π/2) × (b/2) = 0.1257 m in opposite directions. R1

How odometry goes wrong

Odometry has no way to notice its own errors, so they accumulate (dead reckoning). Two families of error behave very differently: D3

  • Systematic errors come from wrong parameters. If the configured track width is 0.165 m instead of the true 0.160 m, every turn is under-estimated by about 3 %; after the square, the same log gives heading 349.1° and a final position 0.14 m from the start. R5 If both wheel diameters are configured 1 mm too large (0.071 m), every distance is about 1.4 % too long and the turns are over-estimated: heading 365.2°. R5 Systematic errors repeat on every run and grow steadily with distance.
  • Non-systematic errors come from the floor: a wheel that spins while accelerating, slides on a polished patch, or climbs over a cable. They appear only where and when the robot met those conditions. D3

Heading errors are the dangerous ones: a small error in θ turns every later metre of travel into a sideways error, so the position error grows faster than the distance driven. D2 That is why calibration concentrates on the two parameters that set the heading: the ratio of the two wheel diameters, and the track width.

Calibrating the parameters

Calibration replaces datasheet values with measured effective values: D3

  1. Each wheel's distance per count: push the robot by hand (motors off, so nothing slips) along a measured straight line, several metres long, and divide the length by each wheel's counts.
  2. Track width: turn the robot in place by a known number of full turns (for example against a mark on the floor) and solve Δθ = (ΔsR − ΔsL)/b for b.
  3. Check: drive closed square paths clockwise and anticlockwise and compare the end points with the start. The method of the paper D3 uses the two directions to separate the diameter error from the track-width error.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Odometry as a motion model. Probabilistic robotics treats the odometry increment as a noisy measurement of motion and grows the pose uncertainty with every step; the noise parameters grow with distance and with rotation. RB302 builds on this and fuses odometry with an IMU (F9-35). D2
  • Sampling and timing. Left and right counts must be latched at the same instant; reading them a millisecond apart while turning creates a fake heading change. Hardware counters latched by one timer event, or a timestamp per count pair, avoid this. D4
  • Where the encoder sits. An encoder on the motor shaft sees gearbox backlash (F1-66); an encoder on the wheel does not, but neither sees slip between tyre and floor. Only an external reference (a camera, a LiDAR scan match, a ground-truth system) sees slip. D1
  • Effective diameter is not the printed diameter. A rubber tyre compresses under the robot's weight, so the distance per turn depends on load and tyre pressure; that is why calibration measures it on the floor rather than with a ruler. D3

How the hardware actually does it

The quadrature signals from each wheel encoder go to a microcontroller timer in encoder mode or to interrupt pins (F1-66); a periodic control task, triggered by a hardware timer, reads both counters, computes the differences, and runs the odometry update of Figure 1. D4 On many robots this runs on the motor controller board, which then sends counts or pose over a bus to the main computer (F9-14). Whether counts or pose cross the bus is a design choice: counts let the main computer recompute odometry with new calibration values; pose saves bus bandwidth.

Worked example

Task. In one 0.1 s step the left encoder advanced 300 counts and the right 330 counts. The robot was at x = 1.000 m, y = 0.500 m, θ = 30°. Use d = 0.070 m, CPR = 1440, b = 0.160 m. Find the new pose.

  1. Distance per count. π × 0.070 / 1440 = 0.00015272 m.
  2. Wheel distances. ΔsL = 300 × 0.00015272 = 0.045815 m; ΔsR = 330 × 0.00015272 = 0.050397 m.
  3. Centre distance and heading change. Δs = 0.048106 m; Δθ = (0.050397 − 0.045815)/0.160 = 0.028634 rad = 1.641°.
  4. Midpoint heading. 30° + 0.820° = 30.820°; cos = 0.85878, sin = 0.51234.
  5. New pose. x = 1.000 + 0.048106 × 0.85878 = 1.04131 m; y = 0.500 + 0.048106 × 0.51234 = 0.52465 m; θ = 31.641°.
  6. Speeds. 0.048106 m in 0.1 s is 0.481 m/s forward; 1.641° in 0.1 s is 16.4 °/s to the left.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Kwame counts one wheel; a robot needs two. A bicycle's distance comes from one wheel, but its direction comes from the handlebar. A differential-drive robot has no handlebar sensor: its heading comes only from the difference between two wheel counts, so a tiny difference in the two wheel sizes becomes a steady turn. D1
    • 

  • Kwame feels the slip; the encoder does not. Kwame knows when his wheel spun in the mud. An encoder counts rotation, so slip shows in the counts only indirectly, as a disagreement with something else (the other wheel, an IMU, a LiDAR). D3
    • 

  • A wheel is not a perfect ruler. The analogy treats "one turn = 2.1 m" as exact. A rubber tyre's effective diameter changes with load and wear, so the conversion itself must be measured (Layer 3).
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot turns left but odometry says right. Cause: encoder channels swapped on one wheel, or left and right wheels swapped in software. How to find it: push the robot forward by hand: both counts must rise. Turn it left by hand: the right count must rise more. Fix: fix the sign or the mapping, then re-test.
    • 

  • Symptom: turns are always a few per cent too small or too large. Cause: wrong track width. How to find it: turn in place ten times; compare odometry heading with 3600°. Fix: calibrate b.
    • 

  • Symptom: a straight drive curves on the odometry plot, the same way every run. Cause: unequal effective wheel diameters, configured as equal (or the reverse). How to find it: hand-push along a measured line and compare each wheel's counts. Fix: a separate diameter for each wheel.
    • 

  • Symptom: odometry is fine on carpet and wrong on tiles, worst when starting. Cause: slip. How to find it: compare the count ratio during acceleration with the ratio at constant speed (forensic lab). Fix: gentler acceleration, better tyres, fusion with an IMU (RB302).
    • 

  • Symptom: a jump of thousands of counts. Cause: counter wrap-around handled wrongly (F1-66). Fix: signed difference of unsigned readings.
    • 




Transition box

D1 What changes: most dangerous first: two encoders are combined into a heading, so a small mismatch between them becomes a growing sideways error; the numbers in the calculation (diameters, track width) are now parameters that must be calibrated; and errors are never corrected by odometry itself. Summary


    

  • One count is π·d/CPR of wheel travel; odometry uses count differences per step.
    • 

  • Δs = (ΔsR + ΔsL)/2, Δθ = (ΔsR − ΔsL)/b; update x and y with the midpoint heading.
    • 

  • Odometry is dead reckoning: errors accumulate and are never corrected by it.
    • 

  • Systematic errors (wrong diameters, track width) repeat on every run and grow with distance; non-systematic errors (slip) depend on floor and motion.
    • 

  • Heading errors matter most: they turn later travel into sideways error.
    • 

  • Calibrate effective diameters by hand-pushing along a measured line, track width by turning in place, and check with squares in both directions.
    • 

  • A constant count ratio points to a parameter; a ratio that changes during acceleration or on one floor patch points to slip.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. drift_logs.cpp moves each robot exactly straight along the tape. Both robots' configuration says both wheels are 0.070 m. Robot A: the left wheel's true rolling diameter is 0.0714 m (a replaced tyre, 2 % larger), no slip. Robot B: both wheels 0.070 m, but the right wheel spins 4 % more than the ground distance while accelerating and 6 % more on the polished patch (2.0–2.6 m). All values pretend.


Root cause. Robot A: dL/dR is 0.980 from the first interval to the last (0.9792–0.9821 with count rounding), at every speed and on every part of the floor: a property of the robot. A larger left wheel needs fewer turns for the same distance; with the parameter saying 0.070 m, odometry thinks the left wheel travelled 2 % less and draws a steady left curve (28.05° after 4 m). Fix: set the left wheel's diameter to its calibrated value (0.070/0.9804 ≈ 0.0714 m) or replace the tyre; verify by hand-pushing along the tape and checking that both wheels give the same metres. Robot B: dL/dR = 1.0000 while cruising, 0.961 only during the first second (acceleration) and 0.943 only between 2.0 m and 2.6 m (the polished patch): a property of the moment. The right wheel's counts are higher there because it spun; the odometry heading jumps only in those intervals (4.48° after accelerating, 17.88° after the patch) and stays constant elsewhere. Fix: gentler acceleration, better tyres or cleaning the floor, and fusion with an IMU (RB302) to catch slip; verify by repeating the run and checking the ratio stays at 1.000 throughout.


Wrong answers. "Robot A slips": slip cannot be present at constant speed on carpet, tiles and the patch alike with exactly the same ratio. "Robot B has a wrong diameter": a wrong diameter would show in every interval, not only during acceleration and on one patch. "Both drift left, so it's the same fault": the same symptom has two different fingerprints.


Prevention. Log dL/dR (or the difference between odometry heading rate and gyro rate, F9-10) continuously: a constant offset means recalibrate; short excursions mean slip. Repeat the hand-push calibration whenever a wheel or tyre is changed.
