Story hook

Riding through a busy market, Joon looks ahead all the time. His eyes are excellent, but he still has to choose what to look at: far away for the turning he needs, close up for the child stepping out between stalls. When he rides fast past the stalls, the fruit on them is a blur. And because he sees with two eyes, he can tell that the child is closer than the stall behind her, even if they are the same size in his view.

Now imagine that Joon's eyes sent him pictures with a delay that kept growing: first a tenth of a second late, then half a second, then a whole second. He would steer around a child who had already moved. The pictures would be sharp and correct, only old.

A robot's cameras are its eyes. This chapter is about choosing a camera mode the robot can carry, about how far depth cameras can be trusted, and about the most common camera bug on robots: images that arrive later and later.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute the data rate of a camera mode from resolution, pixel format and frame rate, and check it against the link and the processing budget.
  • Compute the focal length in pixels from the field of view, and the motion blur from exposure time, distance and speed.
  • Explain how a stereo depth camera's error grows with the square of the distance, and why depth and colour images must be registered.
  • Measure latency from timestamps and explain why a queue between a fast camera and a slow consumer makes it grow without limit.
  • Diagnose growing image latency from a frame log, and fix it with a "keep latest" policy.
Jargon box
Camera mode (resolution, pixel format, frame rate)
Simple words: how big each picture is, how each dot is stored, and how many pictures per second. Analogy: how sharp, how colourful and how often Joon's eyes send pictures. Precise: a combination of width × height in pixels, an encoding of each pixel (for example 8-bit grey, 16-bit YUV-type, 24-bit RGB, or a compressed format) and frames per second; the camera supports a fixed list of modes, given in its datasheet. D3
Data rate (of a camera)
Simple words: how many bytes per second the camera sends. Analogy: how much the eyes ask the brain to look at each second. Precise: width × height × bytes per pixel × frames per second for an uncompressed mode; it must fit the link (with its protocol overhead) and the computer's processing budget. D1
Field of view (FOV)
Simple words: how wide the camera sees, as an angle. Analogy: how far to the side Joon can see without turning his head. Precise: the angle covered by the image across its width (horizontal FOV); in the pinhole model, f = (W/2) / tan(FOV/2), with f the focal length in pixels and W the width in pixels. D2
Motion blur
Simple words: smearing in a picture because something moved while the picture was taken. Analogy: the fruit stalls blurring as Joon rides past. Precise: the image of a point moving across the sensor during the exposure time t covers about (image speed in px/s) × t pixels; turning at ω rad/s gives about f·ω·t pixels near the centre. D1
Depth camera
Simple words: a camera whose picture says how far away each dot is. Analogy: Joon's two eyes telling him the child is nearer than the stall. Precise: a sensor that outputs a depth image (a distance per pixel), by stereo matching, structured light or time of flight (F1-67); for stereo, Z = f·B/d with baseline B and disparity d, so a fixed disparity error gives a depth error growing with Z². D2
Registration (depth to colour)
Simple words: lining up the depth picture with the colour picture, dot by dot. Analogy: knowing which thing you see in colour is the one you feel is close. Precise: mapping each depth pixel to a 3D point, transforming it by the extrinsic transform between the two cameras, and projecting it into the colour camera; because the cameras are apart, the shift depends on distance (parallax). D2
Latency (sensor-to-result)
Simple words: how old a picture is by the time the robot acts on it. Analogy: Joon's pictures arriving late. Precise: time from the moment of exposure to the moment a result computed from that image is available; measured with timestamps taken at capture and at result, on one clock. D1

Terms from earlier chapters used here: pinhole camera model, intrinsic parameters, rolling and global shutter, stereo disparity, structured light (HW302); mounting offset (F9-11); back-pressure, producer/consumer (SP203).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A camera takes many pictures every second. Each picture is made of many dots, and each dot is stored as one or more numbers. More dots, more colours and more pictures per second mean more numbers to send, and the cable and the computer can only take so many.

A depth camera also tells how far away each dot is. Like your two eyes, it compares two views. Close things it measures well. Far things it measures roughly, and the farther, the rougher.

The robot must act on new pictures, not old ones. If the computer is slower than the camera and saves every picture to look at later, the pile of waiting pictures grows. Then the robot is always looking at the past. It is better to throw old pictures away and always look at the newest one.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Choosing a mode the robot can carry

Like Joon deciding what to look at, a robot chooses a camera mode. An uncompressed mode's data rate is width × height × bytes per pixel × frame rate. D1 Listing 1 computes it for five modes: 640 × 480 grey at 30 fps is 9.22 MB/s; 1920 × 1080 RGB at 60 fps is 373.25 MB/s, forty times more. Against a pretend link that carries 40 MB/s of payload, only the two small modes fit. R1 The link is not the only limit: every byte must also be copied, converted and processed, so the processing budget (memory bandwidth, SP301) often decides before the link does. Compressed formats reduce the link's load but cost decoding time and add their own delay. D3

What the camera can see

The pinhole model (F1-68) gives the focal length in pixels from the horizontal field of view: for 640 pixels and 70°, f = 320 / tan 35° = 457.0 px, about 8 pixels per degree near the image centre. D2 R1 An object of width w at distance Z covers about f·w/Z pixels, which tells you how far away a detector can still find a person or a door handle. Wider lenses see more but give fewer pixels per degree and more distortion (F1-68).

During an exposure of t seconds, a robot turning at ω rad/s smears the image by about f·ω·t pixels: at 1.0 rad/s, 0.5 px at 1 ms but 9.1 px at 20 ms. D1 R1 Indoors, auto-exposure lengthens the exposure, so images get blurrier exactly where the light is poor; a robot that must see while turning needs a short exposure, more light, or a sensitive sensor.

How far depth can be trusted

A stereo depth camera computes Z = f·B/d from the disparity d between its two views (F1-67). D2 Differentiating gives ΔZ ≈ Z²/(f·B) · Δd: a fixed matching error Δd becomes a depth error that grows with the square of the distance. For a pretend camera with f = 450 px, B = 0.05 m and Δd = 0.25 px, Listing 2 gives 1 mm at 0.3 m, 11 mm at 1 m, 0.10 m at 3 m and 0.71 m at 8 m (Figure 2). R2 A wider baseline or a longer focal length reduces the error but increases the minimum distance at which both cameras still see the object. D2

The colour camera sits beside the depth camera, so the same object appears at different pixels in the two images, by about f·b/Z for a sideways offset b: 45 px at 0.3 m but 1.7 px at 8 m for a 0.03 m offset (Listing 2). R2 A constant pixel shift therefore cannot align them; registration must go through 3D, using the depth of each pixel and the extrinsic transform between the cameras. D2

Latency and queues

The age of a result is what matters to a moving robot: at 0.5 m/s, a result 0.5 s old describes the world from 0.25 m ago. D1 Latency is measured with two timestamps on one clock: at capture (from the driver, ideally the exposure time) and when the result is ready. If a consumer takes longer per frame than the frame period and frames wait in an unbounded first-in-first-out queue, the queue grows by a fixed number of frames per second and the latency grows without limit; this is the classic producer/consumer problem without back-pressure (SP203). The forensic lab shows it; Listing 3 fixes it by keeping only the newest frame. R3 R4

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Timestamps that mean something. The best timestamp is the middle of the exposure, set by the camera or driver; a timestamp taken when the frame arrives in your program includes transfer and buffering delay. Which one a driver provides is in its documentation; RB303's message headers carry it. D3
  • Synchronisation between sensors. Fusing a camera with a LiDAR or with odometry needs their timestamps on one clock; hardware triggers or synchronised clocks give that; software time of arrival does not. The same smearing as the LiDAR forensic lab of RB401 appears when it is missing. D1
  • Rolling shutter on a moving robot. A rolling-shutter sensor exposes rows one after another, so a turning robot sees leaning verticals (F1-68); for visual odometry or measurement while moving, a global-shutter sensor or a correction with the IMU's rates is needed. D1
  • Depth camera failure modes. Stereo fails on surfaces without texture; active projectors help indoors and can be washed out by sunlight; time-of-flight cameras suffer from multipath in corners and from other cameras of the same kind. Depth images therefore contain holes and invalid values whose encoding the driver documents. D3 D2

How the hardware actually does it

An image sensor converts light into charge in each pixel during the exposure, reads the pixels out row by row through ADCs, and sends the values over its interface: a parallel bus or MIPI CSI-2 lanes to a processor's camera interface, or through a bridge chip to USB (where cameras commonly follow the USB Video Class) or Ethernet. D1 D4 On the computer, a driver receives frames into a ring of buffers by DMA (F1-44) and hands them to user space; the number of buffers and what happens when all are full (drop the newest, drop the oldest, or block) is a driver setting, and it decides the latency behaviour of Figure 1. D3

Worked example

Task. A robot must detect a doorway 0.8 m wide at 6 m with at least 40 pixels across it, while driving at 1.0 m/s and turning at up to 0.5 rad/s with at most 2 pixels of blur. The camera candidate has a 90° horizontal field of view. (a) What image width is needed? (b) What is the longest exposure allowed? (c) Is 1280 × 720, 16-bit, 30 fps within a 40 MB/s link?

  1. Focal length needed. 40 px = f × 0.8 / 6 → f = 300 px.
  2. Width. f = (W/2) / tan 45° = W/2 → W = 600 px: the 640-pixel-wide mode just suffices (f = 320 px gives 42.7 px across the door).
  3. Exposure. Turning dominates: blur ≈ f·ω·t = 320 × 0.5 × t ≤ 2 → t ≤ 12.5 ms. (Driving forward at 1 m/s moves the doorway's image much less at 6 m.)
  4. Data rate. 1280 × 720 × 2 × 30 = 55.3 MB/s: does not fit 40 MB/s (Listing 1's third row). 640 × 480 × 2 × 30 = 18.4 MB/s fits. R1
  5. Verdict. 640 × 480 at a 90° field of view meets the pixel requirement with little margin; a 70° lens would give more pixels on the doorway (f = 457 px, 61 px) but a narrower view.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Joon's eyes adjust instantly; a camera's settings are chosen. Resolution, frame rate, exposure and pixel format are configured, and the wrong choice is not corrected by the camera. D3
    • 

  • Joon's brain drops old pictures by itself; software does not. A program keeps every frame it is given unless someone writes the policy that drops them (the forensic lab).
    • 

  • Two eyes judge depth with experience; a stereo camera only with geometry. The camera's depth error follows Z²/(f·B) exactly and fails completely on blank walls, where people still guess well from context. D2
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the camera works at low resolution but frames drop or the stream fails at high resolution. Cause: the link's data rate exceeded, or the computer cannot keep up. How to find it: compute the data rate (Listing 1); count frames per second received. Fix: smaller mode, compressed format, or a faster link.
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot reacts to obstacles late, and later the longer it runs. Cause: an unbounded queue in front of a slow consumer. How to find it: log capture and result timestamps (forensic lab). Fix: keep latest; make the consumer faster; lower the frame rate.
    • 

  • Symptom: depth values are noisy far away and fine close up. Cause: the Z² error law, not a broken camera. Fix: use depth only within the range where its error is acceptable; state that range in the design.
    • 

  • Symptom: objects in the colour image and their depth are offset, more so for near objects. Cause: depth and colour not registered, or registered with a constant shift. Fix: registration through 3D with the extrinsic calibration.
    • 

  • Symptom: blurry images only when turning, only indoors. Cause: long auto-exposure in low light. Fix: limit the exposure time; add light.
    •

Summary


    

  • Uncompressed data rate = width × height × bytes per pixel × fps; it must fit the link and the processing budget.
    • 

  • f = (W/2) / tan(FOV/2); an object of width w at distance Z covers about f·w/Z pixels.
    • 

  • Blur ≈ f·ω·t when turning: short exposures for robots that see while moving.

  • Stereo depth error grows as Z²/(f·B) × disparity error: trust depth only within a stated range.
    • 

  • Depth and colour images are aligned through 3D, because the shift depends on distance.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. frame_queue.cpp creates a frame every 1000/30 ms (33.3 ms) and gives it to a detector that needs 40 ms per frame, taking frames from an unbounded first-in-first-out queue. No other delay is modelled; the brakes are not involved. All timings pretend.


Root cause. Captures are 500 ms apart for every 15 frames (33.3 ms per frame); results are 600 ms apart for the same 15 frames (40 ms per frame). The detector falls behind by 6.7 ms per frame, so the age of the results grows by 100 ms every 500 ms of capture time, 20 % of elapsed time: 40 ms at the start, 1040 ms after five seconds; the queue grows by about 3 frames per 15 (from 0 to 30). A robot at 0.5 m/s acting on a 1 s old image is 0.5 m further on than the image shows.


Wrong answers. "Brakes": the result times themselves are late, before any braking starts. "Slow link": captures arrive on time every 33.3 ms. "Wrong detections": the results are correct, just old.


Fixes. Keep only the newest frame (Listing 3): the age of results stays between 40.0 and 66.7 ms for the whole run, with dropped frames counted (R4); or make the detector faster than the frame period, or lower the camera's frame rate to what the detector can sustain. Verify by logging age over a long run: its maximum must not grow with time.


Prevention. Monitor the age of every result against a limit from the robot's stopping budget (for example 100 ms, chosen by the team); if it is exceeded, slow down or stop and report "perception stale". Never put an unbounded queue between a sensor and a consumer on a robot.
