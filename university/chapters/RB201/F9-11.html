Story hook

Nadia cycles home after sunset with a lamp on her handlebar. She cannot see the whole street at once. She sees a bright spot wherever the lamp points, so she sweeps it slowly from side to side: the kerb, a parked trailer, the gap in the hedge where her path turns off. In her head the spots join into a picture of the street.

Two things can fool her. If she is turning the bicycle while she sweeps, the spots from the start of the sweep and the end of it were seen from different places, and the kerb looks bent. And if she forgets that the lamp sits on the handlebar, not on her nose, everything seems a little closer than it is.

A 2D LiDAR is a lamp that sweeps all the way round, hundreds of times per turn, and measures the distance to each spot it lights. This chapter reads one such sweep from a recording, draws it, and finds out what happens when the robot forgets where its lamp is mounted.

What you will be able to do

  • Read a recorded planar scan: header fields (angle of the first beam, angle increment, range limits) and one range per beam.
  • Convert ranges into points in the sensor frame, then in the robot frame using the LiDAR's mounting offset.
  • Visualise a scan from above and recognise walls, openings, objects and shadows behind them.
  • Explain invalid returns, angular resolution and motion distortion, and how each shows in a scan.
  • Diagnose a wrong mounting offset from scans taken before and after turning in place.
Jargon box
Planar scan (laser scan)
Simple words: one full sweep of distances, one per direction, in a flat slice around the robot. Analogy: Nadia's lamp sweeping once round. Precise: a list of ranges ri measured at beam angles θi = θmin + i·Δθ in the sensor's plane, with a timestamp, valid range limits and the sensor's frame. D1 D2
Angular resolution
Simple words: how many degrees lie between two neighbouring beams. Analogy: how far Nadia moves the lamp between two looks. Precise: the angle increment Δθ between consecutive beams; at range r the gap between neighbouring spots is about r·Δθ (Δθ in radians), so thin objects far away can fall between beams. D1
Mounting offset (sensor extrinsics)
Simple words: where the LiDAR sits on the robot and which way it faces. Analogy: the lamp is on the handlebar, not on Nadia's nose. Precise: the position (x, y) and yaw of the sensor frame in the robot's body frame, used to move every scan point into the body frame; part of the robot's description of its sensors (RB301, F9-29). D5
Recorded log (bag)
Simple words: a file that stores sensor messages with their times, so you can play them back later. Analogy: a diary of every sweep of the lamp. Precise: a time-stamped recording of sensor data in a defined format; ROS 2 records "bags" with its own tools (RB303); this course uses a small text format of its own, described in Listing 1, so that it can be read without ROS 2. D4
Motion distortion (scan skew)
Simple words: a scan looks bent because the robot moved while the sweep was going on. Analogy: the kerb looking bent because Nadia turned while sweeping. Precise: the beams of one scan are measured at different times during the sweep; if the robot moves or turns during it, treating them as simultaneous misplaces points; de-skewing corrects each beam with the robot's motion at its own time. D1

Terms from earlier chapters used here: LiDAR, time of flight, scan and point cloud, maximum range and invalid return, occlusion (HW302, F1-67); body frame, rotation matrix (F9-10); pose (F9-09).

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A LiDAR spins and shines an invisible light in one direction after another. For each direction it measures how far away the light bounced back. One full turn gives a list of distances, one per direction. That list is a scan.

To draw a scan, you go through the list. For each direction you walk out from the sensor by the measured distance and put a dot there. The dots show the walls around the robot. Where nothing came back, like through an open door, there is no dot.

Things hide what is behind them. A box near the robot blocks the light, so the wall behind it gets no dots. And the robot must know exactly where on its body the LiDAR sits, or it puts every dot in the wrong place.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What a recorded scan contains

Like a diary of Nadia's sweeps, a recorded scan needs enough information to rebuild the picture later: when it was taken, in which frame, the angle of the first beam, the step between beams, the valid range limits, and the ranges themselves. D1 ROS 2 defines a standard message for exactly this and records it in bags (RB303); the field names of that message are in the unverified box below. This course's lab uses its own plain-text log so it can run without ROS 2: a comment line, a mount line with the LiDAR's offset on the robot, a scan header and 360 ranges, ten per line, then end (Listing 1's output). R1

From ranges to points

Beam i points at θi = θmin + i·Δθ. A valid range r gives the point (r cos θ, r sin θ) in the sensor frame; the mounting offset (mx, my, yaw) then moves it into the robot frame with a 2D rotation and a shift (Figure 1). D5 Ranges outside [range_min, range_max] are not points at all: the recorded log uses 0 for "no return". Treating 0 as a real distance would draw a ring of fake obstacles on the sensor itself (the F1-67 forensic lab's phantom wall). D2

Listing 2 reads the log, converts every valid beam, draws a text map at 0.1 m per character, and reports 337 valid beams and 23 without return, with the nearest return 0.922 m away at −13°: the near corner of the box. R2 The 23 beams without return all point through the doorway, where the simulated room has nothing within range. R2

Reading a scan like an engineer

  • Walls appear as straight runs of points; their spacing grows with distance, because neighbouring beams spread apart by about r·Δθ: 1° at 2 m is 35 mm between points. D1
  • Openings appear as gaps, either because nothing is in range (the doorway) or because the surface does not return enough light (dark, shiny or transparent surfaces; the datasheet lists the conditions). D2
  • Objects show only the faces turned towards the sensor: the box appears as an L of two faces, never as a closed square.
  • Shadows (occlusion) are gaps behind objects. In Figure 2 the bottom wall and the right wall behind the box have no points. A map must not conclude that "no points" means "no wall". D1

Why the mounting offset matters

The LiDAR measures from its own centre, but the robot's pose (F9-09) is the pose of its body centre. Every point must be moved by the mounting offset before it is placed in the world: pworld = pose ⊕ mount ⊕ psensor, two rigid transforms in a chain. D5 If the offset is wrong, a robot driving straight sees every point shifted the same way, so successive scans still agree with each other; the error shows only when the robot turns, because the shift then points in a new direction. The forensic lab is built on this.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Motion distortion. A spinning LiDAR measures its beams one after another during a revolution. If the robot turns at ω during a revolution of period T, the last beam is measured from a heading rotated by ωT relative to the first; a straight wall crossing the start/end of the sweep shows a step or a bend. De-skewing interpolates the robot's pose at each beam's time (from odometry or the IMU, F9-10) and transforms each beam with its own pose. D1
  • Timestamps. Whether a scan's timestamp marks its first beam, its last or its middle, and whether per-beam times are provided, is defined by the driver; mixing conventions between LiDAR and odometry produces a smeared map (the RB401 forensic lab). D2
  • The measurement model. A range reading is the true distance plus noise most of the time, but also sometimes a short reading (something unexpected in the beam), a max-range reading (no return) or a random reading; probabilistic robotics models these four cases explicitly, which is why single points should never be trusted alone. D1
  • Mounting height and tilt. A 2D LiDAR sees a single horizontal slice: a table is seen by its legs, not its top; a small tilt makes the beam hit the floor at long range. Mount it level, at a height that sees the obstacles that matter, and record the height in the robot description. D1

How the hardware actually does it

A spinning 2D LiDAR has a laser emitter and a detector on a rotating head; for each beam it measures the time of flight or the phase of the returning light (F1-67), an encoder on the motor gives the angle, and a processor packs angle, range and often an intensity value into packets sent over a serial line, USB or Ethernet (F9-14). D2 The driver on the robot computer assembles the packets of one revolution into a scan. The rotation speed, number of beams, range limits, packet format and interface are specific to each product and come from its datasheet and protocol document. D2

Worked example

Task. In the lab's log (θmin = −180°, Δθ = 1°, LiDAR mounted 0.10 m ahead of the robot centre, facing forward), beam 167 reads 0.922 m. (a) Which angle is that? (b) Where is the point in the sensor frame and in the robot frame? (c) How far apart are neighbouring points on that face of the box? (d) What is the robot-frame position of the beam at index 180 if it reads 2.400 m?

  1. Angle. θ = −180° + 167 × 1° = −13°: Listing 2's "nearest return at −13°". R2
  2. Sensor frame. xs = 0.922 × cos(−13°) = 0.922 × 0.97437 = 0.8984 m; ys = 0.922 × sin(−13°) = −0.2074 m.
  3. Robot frame. Mount yaw 0: x = 0.10 + 0.8984 = 0.998 m; y = −0.207 m. In the simulated room the box's near corner is at (1.0, −0.2) m: the reading is that corner, within the ±5 mm noise.
  4. Spacing. 0.922 m × (1° = 0.01745 rad) = 16 mm between neighbouring points there; on the right wall, 2.4 m away, about 42 mm.
  5. Beam 180. θ = 0°: straight ahead; x = 0.10 + 2.400 = 2.500 m, y = 0: the right wall, which the simulated room has at x = 2.5 m.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nadia's lamp only lights things up; a LiDAR measures distance directly. Her brain guesses distance from what she sees; the LiDAR times the light's return and reports a number per beam. D2
    • 

  • Nadia sees colours and surfaces; a 2D LiDAR sees one thin flat slice. Anything above or below the slice, like a table top or a step down, is invisible to it (Layer 3).
    • 

  • Nadia knows she turned while sweeping; the scan does not. A scan arrives as one list with one timestamp; whether the robot moved during it must be supplied from odometry or the IMU. D1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the drawn scan is a mirror image of the room. Cause: angles increase clockwise in the sensor but counter-clockwise in your code, or the sensor is mounted upside down. How to find it: hold a box on the robot's left and see where it appears. Fix: follow the driver's angle convention; use the mounting yaw and roll.
    • 

  • Symptom: a ring of points around the robot. Cause: 0 or out-of-range values drawn as points. Fix: filter with range_min and range_max (Listing 2, line 51).
    • 

  • Symptom: the scan is rotated by a fixed angle. Cause: wrong mounting yaw, or θmin read wrongly. How to find it: drive towards a wall; it must appear straight ahead. Fix: measure and set the yaw.
    • 

  • Symptom: walls look doubled or smeared when the robot turns, sharp when it drives straight. Cause: wrong mounting offset (forensic lab), or motion distortion. How to find it: turn in place slowly and quickly; offset errors do not depend on speed, distortion does.
    • 

  • Symptom: a glass door is not in the scan. Cause: transparent or shiny surfaces return little light. Fix: add another sensor (bumper, ultrasonic, camera) for those cases; do not rely on one sense.
    •

Summary


    

  • A planar scan is a list of ranges at angles θmin + i·Δθ, with a timestamp, range limits and a frame.
    • 

  • A valid range becomes (r cos θ, r sin θ) in the sensor frame, then moves into the robot frame with the mounting offset.
    • 

  • Out-of-range values are not points; their encoding must be documented.
    • 

  • Walls are runs of points that spread apart with distance; objects show only their visible faces; occlusion leaves shadows.
    • 

  • A wrong mounting offset is invisible while driving straight and appears when turning.
    • 

  • A scan's beams are measured at different times; motion during a sweep bends it unless it is de-skewed.
    • 

  • Recording a scan with all its metadata lets anyone replay and check it later.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. mount_forensic.cpp places the LiDAR 0.20 m ahead of the robot centre (true mount), but the software transforms scans with a configured mount of 0.00 m (copied from a robot whose LiDAR sat at its centre). Odometry poses are exact. Scans 1 and 2 face +x (yaw 0); scan 3 faces +y (yaw 90°).


Root cause. Scans 1 and 2 put the right wall at 2.300 m, 0.200 m short of the true 2.50 m, and the top wall correctly at 1.800 m: the error points along the robot's forward axis. In scan 3 the robot faces +y, so the same forward error now points along world y: the top wall moves to 1.600 m (0.200 m short) and the right wall becomes correct (2.500 m). An error that is fixed in the robot frame and rotates with the robot is a mounting-offset error: the sensor is really 0.20 m ahead of where the configuration says.


Wrong answers. "Odometry heading": a heading error would rotate the walls, not shift them by exactly 0.20 m along the robot's forward axis; and odometry was checked. "Motion distortion": the scans are taken standing still, and distortion depends on turning speed. "Timing": with the robot still during each scan, timestamps do not matter.


Fix. Measure the LiDAR's position on the robot and set mount x = 0.20 m. Self-test without external equipment: take scans before and after turning in place by 90° and 180°; with the right offset, the wall estimates agree within the noise in all orientations (the test of this evidence, repeated).


Prevention. Keep the sensor mountings in one robot description file per robot (the URDF of RB301), measured from the robot's drawings and checked by the turn-in-place test at every configuration change; never copy mountings between robots of different shape.
