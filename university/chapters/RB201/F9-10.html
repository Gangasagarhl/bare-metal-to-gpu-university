Story hook

Close your eyes, stand on one leg, and a friend turns you slowly on a swivel chair. You can feel that you are turning, and roughly how fast, even without looking. That feeling comes from the balance sense in your inner ear. It also tells you which way is down.

Now imagine your inner ear were fitted sideways, by mistake. When you turned left you would feel "tipping forward". You would not be wrong about the motion; you would be wrong about which way your head points. And if you sat on the chair for an hour with your eyes closed, you would slowly lose track of which way you face.

A robot's inner ear is an IMU. This chapter is about the two things that go wrong when it is mounted on a real robot: it can be fitted turned, and it drifts. Both are fixed with simple measurements, if you know to make them.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the difference between the IMU's own axes (sensor frame) and the robot's axes (body frame), and why every reading must be rotated into the body frame.
  • Compute the sensor-to-body rotation from two calibration moments: standing still, then accelerating straight ahead.
  • Integrate a gyroscope's yaw rate into a heading and estimate its bias while the robot is known to be still.
  • Predict how a bias error turns into a heading drift that grows with time.
  • Diagnose a heading that turns while the robot is parked, from start-up calibration statistics.
Jargon box
Body frame
Simple words: directions fixed to the robot: forward, left, up. Analogy: "in front of my nose", "to my left hand". Precise: a right-handed coordinate frame attached to the robot; this course uses x forward, y left, z up, the convention of RB301 (F9-23). D2
Sensor frame
Simple words: the directions printed on the IMU chip or board. Analogy: the way your inner ear happens to sit in your head. Precise: the frame in which the sensor reports its measurements, defined by the axis markings in its datasheet; it is fixed to the robot but generally rotated (and offset) from the body frame. D3
Rotation matrix
Simple words: a table of nine numbers that turns a direction written in one frame into the same direction written in another. Analogy: a rule like "my friend's left is my forward". Precise: a 3 × 3 orthogonal matrix R with determinant +1; vbody = R vsensor. Its rows are the body axes written in sensor coordinates. D2
Mounting calibration (IMU alignment)
Simple words: finding out exactly how the IMU sits on the robot, by measuring. Analogy: standing still to feel which way is down, then walking forward to feel which way is ahead. Precise: estimating the sensor-to-body rotation from measurements with known motion: gravity at rest gives the body's up axis, a straight acceleration gives the forward axis. D1
Heading integration
Simple words: adding up small turns to know which way you face. Analogy: counting the turns of the swivel chair with your eyes closed. Precise: θ(t) = θ(0) + ∫ ωz dt, computed as a sum of rate × time step; any constant error in ωz adds an error that grows in proportion to time. D1
Zero-motion bias calibration
Simple words: measuring what the gyro says while the robot is truly still, and subtracting it. Analogy: noticing that you "feel" a slow turn even when the chair stands still, and ignoring that much from then on. Precise: averaging the gyroscope output over a window during which the robot is known not to rotate, and using the mean as the bias estimate; valid only if the robot really is still during the window. D1 D4

Terms from earlier chapters used here: IMU, accelerometer, specific force, gyroscope, bias, drift (all HW302, F1-65); pose (F9-09).

Note

Maths you need here (from MA201). A direction is a vector of three numbers. The dot product a·b = axbx + ayby + azbz measures how much two directions agree (1 for equal unit vectors, 0 for perpendicular ones). The cross product a × b gives a third direction perpendicular to both, following the right-hand rule: x × y = z. A rotation matrix whose rows are three perpendicular unit vectors turns a vector v into the three dot products of v with those rows. D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

An IMU feels two things. Its accelerometer feels which way is down and when the robot speeds up or brakes. Its gyroscope feels how fast the robot turns.

The IMU tells you these things using its own directions, the arrows printed on the chip. But the chip may be glued on sideways. So the robot must first learn how the chip sits. It stands still to feel where "down" is, then drives forward to feel where "ahead" is. From that it can turn every reading into the robot's own directions.

The gyroscope has a small fault: even when the robot stands still, it says "turning a tiny bit". Add that up for minutes and the robot thinks it has turned a lot. So at start-up the robot stands still for a moment, listens to how big the fault is, and takes it away from then on. That only works if nobody moves the robot during that moment.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Two frames, one robot

Like an inner ear fitted sideways, an IMU reports in its sensor frame, whose axes are marked in the datasheet, while the controller, the odometry and the maps work in the robot's body frame. D3 Boards are often mounted rotated to fit the wiring, or upside down under a plate. If the code ignores the rotation, a left turn appears as a pitch, or braking as sideways acceleration. A rotation matrix R fixes this: vbody = R vsensor, for accelerations and for rates alike. D2

Measuring the rotation instead of trusting the drawing

A level robot standing still feels only gravity; the accelerometer measures specific force, so it reads +g along the body's up axis (F1-65). D1 Normalising that reading gives body z in sensor coordinates. Accelerating straight ahead adds a component along body x; subtracting the still reading leaves the forward direction, and removing any part along z (a Gram–Schmidt step) makes it exactly perpendicular. Body y is then z × x. D2 Listing 1 does this with pretend readings and finds the board turned a quarter turn with a small tilt: body x = (0.008, −1.000, −0.001) and body y = (1.000, 0.008, −0.002) in sensor coordinates, which is the rule of Figure 1. R1 Applied to new readings, the same matrix turns "sensor y = +0.79" while braking into "body x = −0.80", and a pitch rate on the sensor's x axis into a rate about the body's y (left) axis, as it should. R1

Heading from the gyroscope

Rotating the gyro reading into the body frame gives ωz, the yaw rate. Summing ωz × Δt gives the heading change. D1 A constant bias b adds b × t to the heading: a pretend bias of 0.004 rad/s, small enough to look like zero on a single reading, adds 0.004 × 120 = 0.48 rad = 27.5° in two minutes. Listing 2's raw heading ends 27.33° off (the noise averages out almost, not quite). R2

The fix is zero-motion bias calibration: while the robot is known to be still, average the gyro and subtract the mean from then on. With the first 2 s (200 samples at 100 Hz), Listing 2 estimates 0.00376 rad/s against the true 0.00400 and ends 1.53° off after 120 s, about eighteen times better. R2 The remaining error comes from the noise in the 2 s average: a longer still window, or re-estimating the bias whenever the robot stops (wheel encoders say "no motion"), reduces it further. D4

Reading the IMU on a robot

The IMU chip is read over I2C or SPI (F1-47): set its range and output rate in configuration registers, then read raw values and scale them to SI units with the factors in its datasheet (F1-65). D3 On a robot, three extra rules apply: read at a fixed rate driven by a timer or the IMU's data-ready interrupt, so Δt is known; timestamp every sample, because fusion with odometry (RB302) needs both on one clock; and record the sensor's temperature if it reports one, because bias changes with temperature (Layer 3). D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Where to mount it. A gyroscope measures the same rotation rate anywhere on a rigid body, but an accelerometer away from the rotation centre also feels centripetal and tangential acceleration (lever-arm effect), and anywhere near a motor it feels vibration (the F1-65 forensic lab). Mount the IMU near the centre of rotation, on a stiff plate, away from motors, and record the offset if it is not at the centre. D1
  • Bias is not one number. Bias changes from power-on to power-on (turn-on bias), slowly over time (bias instability) and with temperature; the datasheet names and specifies these separately, and random noise integrates into a random walk of the angle. Gyro-only heading always drifts; RB302 corrects it with other sensors. D4 D3
  • Gravity as a reference, and its limit. At rest, the accelerometer gives roll and pitch from gravity, which corrects gyro drift on those two axes; heading (yaw) has no gravity reference, which is why yaw drifts and needs odometry, a magnetometer (disturbed by motors and steel indoors) or vision. D1
  • Integration method and rate. Summing rate × Δt is exact for a constant rate within each step; with fast turns and slow sampling, the order of rotations matters in 3D, and quaternion integration (RB302, DN201) is used. On a robot that turns only about z, the simple sum of this chapter is adequate. D1

How the hardware actually does it

A MEMS gyroscope contains a tiny mass driven to vibrate; when the chip rotates, the Coriolis effect pushes the vibrating mass sideways, and the chip's electronics measure that push, convert it with an ADC, filter it and store it in output registers at the configured rate (F1-65). D1 Most IMU chips can raise an interrupt pin when a new sample is ready and can buffer samples in a FIFO, so the microcontroller can read them in bursts without missing any; the register names and FIFO behaviour are specific to each chip. D3

Worked example

Task. An IMU is mounted upside down: its z axis points down, its x axis points forward. (a) Write the sensor-to-body rotation. (b) Standing still, what does the accelerometer read? (c) The robot turns left at 0.3 rad/s: what does the sensor's z gyro read? (d) A gyro bias of 0.002 rad/s is not removed: how far off is the heading after 10 minutes?

  1. Axes. Sensor x = body x (forward). Sensor z = −body z. For a right-handed sensor frame, sensor y = sensor z × sensor x = (−zb) × xb = −(zb × xb) = −yb: sensor y points right.
  2. Rotation. Rows are the body axes in sensor coordinates: body x = (1, 0, 0), body y = (0, −1, 0), body z = (0, 0, −1). This is a half turn about the forward axis.
  3. Standing still. Specific force is +g along body up, which is −sensor z: the accelerometer reads (0, 0, −9.81) m/s² (with g rounded).
  4. Turning left. ωbody = (0, 0, 0.3); in sensor coordinates (0, 0, −0.3): the sensor z gyro reads −0.3 rad/s. Without the rotation, the robot would think it turned right.
  5. Drift. 0.002 rad/s × 600 s = 1.2 rad = 68.8°.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • An inner ear cannot be fitted sideways; an IMU can, and nothing complains. The readings look perfectly normal; only a calibration motion or a test reveals that the axes are swapped or reversed.
    • 

  • Your sense of turning fades; the gyro's error grows. Sitting still, you stop feeling the chair's turn and do not "drift" further. An integrated gyro with a bias keeps adding error for as long as it runs, in proportion to time. D4
    • 

  • Your balance sense combines with your eyes automatically. The robot's IMU does not: correcting its drift with odometry or vision is a separate algorithm that someone must write (RB302).
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot turns left; the heading turns right. Cause: IMU mounted upside down (z reversed) or a sign error. How to find it: turn the robot by hand to the left and print body ωz: it must be positive. Fix: correct the rotation matrix.
    • 

  • Symptom: turning appears as pitch or roll. Cause: axes swapped (mounting rotation ignored). How to find it: the calibration of Listing 1. Fix: apply the measured rotation to every sample.
    • 

  • Symptom: the heading creeps steadily while the robot stands still. Cause: bias not removed, or removed with a wrong estimate. How to find it: integrate a minute of standstill and divide the change by the time. Fix: zero-motion calibration with a stillness check (forensic lab).
    • 

  • Symptom: heading error after fast turns but not slow ones. Cause: the gyro saturates at its full-scale range, or samples are lost. How to find it: look for flat tops at the maximum value; count samples per second. Fix: a larger range setting; read the FIFO.
    • 

  • Symptom: heading is right but "a bit late" against odometry. Cause: no timestamps; samples processed with a delay. Fix: timestamp at the data-ready interrupt.
    •

Forensic lab


Scenario: "The parked robot that spins". Tomás's robot estimates its gyro bias during the first 2 s after power-on, then waits in its dock. On Monday its heading crept by a few degrees in ten minutes. On Tuesday, the map view showed the parked robot turning clockwise by more than forty degrees every ten seconds, although it did not move. Method hints. A bias estimate is "mean of the window". If the window contains motion, the mean contains motion. The standard deviation tells you whether the window was quiet.

Summary


    

  • An IMU reports in its sensor frame; the robot works in its body frame; a rotation matrix converts every reading.
    • 

  • Measure the mounting: gravity at rest gives body z; a straight acceleration gives body x; y = z × x.
    • 

  • Heading = sum of yaw rate × Δt; a constant bias b adds b·t of error.
    • 

  • Zero-motion calibration: average the gyro while still and subtract; valid only if the robot really is still.
    • 

  • Check stillness with the window's standard deviation, and re-estimate the bias whenever the robot is known to be stopped.
    • 

  • Gyro heading always drifts; roll and pitch can be corrected by gravity, yaw needs other sensors (RB302).
    • 

  • Read at a fixed rate, timestamp every sample, and take every register and number from the chosen IMU's datasheet.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. parked_creep.cpp simulates a gyro with a constant pretend bias of 0.0040 rad/s and noise. Monday: the robot is still during the whole 2 s window. Tuesday: during the first 1.5 s of the window the robot is being turned by hand at 0.10 rad/s (someone straightening it in the dock), then left alone. Both then park for 600 s.


Root cause. Tuesday's window contains motion: its mean is 0.07888 rad/s (Monday 0.00376) and its standard deviation 0.04318 rad/s (Monday 0.00593), more than seven times larger: the window was not quiet. The mean is subtracted as "bias" from then on, so while parked the corrected rate is 0.004 − 0.0789 ≈ −0.075 rad/s; the log agrees: −2575.5° in 600 s = −4.29 °/s = −0.0749 rad/s, clockwise (negative). The gyro is fine.


Wrong answers. "Broken gyro": the same gyro gives a quiet window on Monday, and the parked rate equals exactly bias minus Tuesday's mean. "Temperature": would change the bias slowly, not make the window's standard deviation seven times larger.


Fix. Accept a bias estimate only if the window's standard deviation is below a threshold derived from known-still runs (and, on the robot, only if the wheel encoders show no motion); otherwise wait and retry, and report it. Test: replay Tuesday's samples through the new start-up; it must refuse the first window and accept a later quiet one.


Prevention. Monday's 8.1° in ten minutes is the residual error of a 2 s average of noisy samples (0.00376 vs 0.00400 rad/s): not a fault, but improvable with a longer window and re-estimation whenever the robot is stopped (lab step 3), and in RB302 by fusing with odometry.
