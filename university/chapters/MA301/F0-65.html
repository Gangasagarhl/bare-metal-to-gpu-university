Story hook

Analogy

Mei is filling the bath. At first the tap only trickles, then she opens it more, then a little less when the water gets too hot. The bath has no markings, but the tap has a little flow gauge that shows litres per minute. How much water is in the bath after ten minutes?

Her brother Kofi has an idea. Every minute he reads the gauge and writes the number down. One minute at 4 litres per minute adds about 4 litres; the next minute at 7 adds about 7. Adding the numbers gives the total. "But the flow changed during each minute," says Mei. "Then read it every ten seconds," says Kofi, "and add up smaller pieces."

Adding up many thin pieces of "rate × time" to get a total is integration. It is the reverse of the previous chapter: there, Tomás started from positions and found the speed; here, Kofi starts from a rate and finds the amount.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute totals from rates with Riemann sums and the trapezoid rule, and say how their error shrinks with the step.
  • Evaluate definite integrals exactly with antiderivatives and the fundamental theorem of calculus.
  • Apply integrals to physical problems: distance from speed, speed from acceleration, energy from power, charge from current.
  • Explain why integrating a measurement with a small constant error makes the result drift without bound.
  • Diagnose a dead-reckoning log whose position keeps growing while the robot stands still.
Jargon box
Accumulation
Simple words: the total amount that builds up when something flows in at a known rate. Analogy: the water collected in the bath. Precise: the quantity Q(T) = Q(0) + ∫₀T q(t) dt obtained from a rate q(t); its unit is the unit of the rate times the unit of time. D1
Riemann sum
Simple words: an estimate of a total made by adding up thin rectangles. Analogy: Kofi's list of "flow this minute × one minute", added up. Precise: Σk f(tk*)·Δtk over a partition of [a, b] into pieces of width Δtk, with tk* a point in each piece (left end, right end or midpoint). B2
Definite integral
Simple words: the exact total, the value the sums settle towards as the pieces get thinner. Analogy: the true amount of water, which Kofi's lists approach as he reads the gauge more often. Precise: ∫ab f(t) dt = the limit of the Riemann sums as the widest piece shrinks to zero, when that limit exists; geometrically the signed area between the graph and the t-axis. B2
Integrand
Simple words: the function being added up. Analogy: the flow gauge reading. Precise: f in ∫ f(t) dt. B2
Antiderivative
Simple words: a function whose derivative is the one you started with. Analogy: the bath's water-level formula, whose rate of change is the tap's flow. Precise: F is an antiderivative of f if F′ = f; any two differ by a constant, written ∫ f(t) dt = F(t) + C (the indefinite integral). B2
Fundamental theorem of calculus
Simple words: the rule that adding up a rate gives the change in the amount. Analogy: the water added equals the final level minus the starting level, however the tap was turned. Precise: if f is continuous on [a, b] and F′ = f, then ∫ab f(t) dt = F(b) − F(a); and d/dt ∫at f(s) ds = f(t). B2
Trapezoid rule
Simple words: adding up thin slanted strips instead of flat-topped rectangles. Analogy: using the average of the gauge readings at the start and end of each minute. Precise: ∫ ≈ Σk ½(f(tk) + f(tk+1))·Δt; for a function with a continuous second derivative its error shrinks like Δt². B2R1
Dead reckoning
Simple words: working out where you are only from how fast you went and for how long. Analogy: guessing the bath level only from the gauge, never looking at the water. Precise: estimating position by integrating measured velocity (or acceleration twice) from a known start, without an external position measurement; errors in the rate accumulate. B1D1
Average value (of a function)
Simple words: the steady rate that would give the same total. Analogy: the one tap setting that would fill the bath equally in the same time. Precise: (1/(b − a))·∫ab f(t) dt. B2

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows a cart's speed v(t) = 2(1 − e−t/2) m/s as it starts from rest. The distance it covers from 0 to 4 s is the area under that curve. Eight left rectangles undershoot it because the speed rises within each piece; the exact value is 4 + 4e−2 ≈ 4.541 m. Figure 2 shows how fast two methods approach the exact value as the number of pieces N grows, on logarithmic axes: the left-rectangle error falls by about 4 when N is multiplied by 4, the trapezoid error by about 16.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

If water flows in at 5 litres every minute for 3 minutes, you get 15 litres: rate times time. When the rate keeps changing, cut the time into short pieces. In each piece the rate hardly changes, so rate times the short time is nearly right. Add all the pieces and you have the total.

On a graph of the rate, each piece "rate × short time" is a thin rectangle: its height is the rate, its width is the time. So the total is the area under the curve. The thinner the strips, the closer their total gets to the true area. That true area is the integral.

Integration and the derivative undo each other. Start with the water level, take its rate of change, and you get the flow. Start with the flow, add it all up, and you get back how much the level rose.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Riemann sums and the definite integral

Cut [a, b] into N pieces of width h = (b − a)/N with points tk = a + k·h. Three standard sums are B2

  • left: LN = h·Σk=0N−1 f(tk);
  • right: RN = h·Σk=1N f(tk);
  • trapezoid: TN = (LN + RN)/2, and midpoint MN = h·Σ f(tk + h/2).

As N → ∞ all of them approach the same number for a continuous f, the definite integral ∫ab f(t) dt. The symbol is a stretched S for "sum", and dt is what is left of the width h in the limit. Where f is negative, the strips count negatively: the integral of velocity is the net displacement, not the distance walked back and forth. B2D1

Listing 1 computes all four sums for the cart's speed with N = 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024. With N = 4 the left sum gives 3.605 m and the right sum 5.334 m, bracketing the exact 4.541 m, because the speed rises; at N = 1024 they are 4.538 and 4.545 m. R1

Antiderivatives and the fundamental theorem

Summing strips is how a computer integrates. By hand there is a short cut. If F′ = f, then the fundamental theorem of calculus says ∫ab f(t) dt = F(b) − F(a). The reason is visible in the bath: the water added equals the change in the level, whatever the flow did in between. B2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

How fast the sums converge

For f with a continuous derivative, the left and right sums have error of order h: each strip misses a small triangle of area about ½·f′·h², and there are (b − a)/h strips. For f with a continuous second derivative, the trapezoid and midpoint rules cancel that first-order part and leave an error of order h², with the midpoint error about half the trapezoid error and of opposite sign. B2

Run R1 shows exactly this: from N = 64 to 256 to 1024 the left-sum error goes −5.43×10−2, −1.35×10−2, −3.38×10−3 (a factor 4 per factor 4 in N), and the trapezoid error goes −2.82×10−4, −1.76×10−5, −1.10×10−6 (a factor 16). At N = 4 the midpoint error is +0.0358, about half the trapezoid's −0.0718 with the opposite sign. Higher-order rules (Simpson's rule and the Runge–Kutta methods of F0-76) push the order further. R1R3

Integrals to infinity

Some integrals run over an unbounded interval, ∫₀∞ f(t) dt = limT→∞ ∫₀T f(t) dt. The total distance a released cart coasts under viscous friction is such an integral: ∫₀∞ v₀e−t/τ dt = v₀τ, finite although the cart never quite stops (F0-67). The Laplace transform of F0-69 is defined by an integral of this kind, and it exists only when the integrand decays fast enough. B2D1

Integrating errors: why dead reckoning drifts

Integration is linear: the integral of (true rate + error) is the true amount plus the integral of the error. A constant error b in a speed measurement therefore becomes a position error b·t that grows without limit, however small b is; HW302 meets the same effect as the drift of an integrated gyroscope. Random, zero-mean noise does not cancel either: its integral wanders like a random walk, whose spread grows like the square root of time (the statistics are in MA202). The cure is never "integrate more carefully"; it is to correct the integral with an independent measurement of the amount itself: a wall, a landmark, a level sensor in the bath. The Kalman filter of RB302 does this systematically. D1U2B1

The integral inside a controller

A PI or PID controller adds Kᵢ·∫ e(t) dt to its command. Because a constant error makes this term grow steadily, the controller keeps pushing until the error is exactly zero; that is how integral action removes the steady-state error that F0-70 finds in a proportional loop. The same property is dangerous when the actuator is at its limit: the integral keeps growing ("integrator windup") and the system overshoots badly when the limit is released. RB202 (F9-18, F9-21) implements anti-windup; F0-66 shows the discrete running sum that implements the integral. B1

How the hardware actually does it

A controller integrates with an accumulator: a variable that a periodic task or timer interrupt (HW204) updates with sum += rate × Δt, as in Listing 2. Three hardware facts shape the result. First, Δt must be the real time between samples, measured or guaranteed; a late interrupt that the code does not notice is an error in every strip. Second, the variable has finite precision: in floating point, adding a very small increment to a very large total loses digits of the increment, and in fixed-point or integer accumulators the total can overflow and wrap around (MA102 F0-24, MA302 F0-75 on summation error). Third, many sensors already integrate for you: an encoder (F1-66) counts steps, so its count is the integral of angular speed with no drift from bias, which is why wheels with encoders drift far less than integrated accelerometers. Nothing in this chapter was run on a microcontroller; the programs ran on the build container's CPU in double precision. U2R2

Worked example

Task. (a) How far does the cart with v(t) = 2(1 − e−t/2) m/s travel from 0 to 4 s, and what is its average speed? (b) Estimate the same distance with the trapezoid rule and N = 4, by hand. (c) The sample file of Listing 2 has power readings; compute the energy between t = 1.0 and 2.0 s by hand.

  1. (a) Exact. F(t) = 2t + 4e−t/2. F(4) − F(0) = (8 + 4e−2) − 4 = 4 + 4e−2 = 4 + 4 × 0.135335 = 4.5413 m. Average speed: 4.5413 / 4 = 1.1353 m/s.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Water in a bath can only go up while the tap runs. An integral of a rate that changes sign (velocity forwards and backwards, current charging and discharging) can go down; the "area" counts negatively below the axis.
    • 

  • The bath has a real level that Mei can look at whenever she likes. A robot doing dead reckoning has only the integral; nothing stops the error from growing unless another sensor measures the amount directly (Layer 3, forensic lab). R4
    • 

  • Kofi's readings come at fixed times he chooses. In a computer, the time between samples is set by timers and scheduling and may vary; the integral is only as good as the Δt used in each strip.
    •

Summary


    

  • An integral adds up "rate × small time"; it is the limit of Riemann sums and the signed area under the rate curve.
    • 

  • The fundamental theorem: ∫ab f dt = F(b) − F(a) for any antiderivative F; physical integrals need a starting value.
    • 

  • Distance, change of velocity, angle, energy and charge are integrals of speed, acceleration, angular speed, power and current.
    • 

  • Left and right sums converge like h; trapezoid and midpoint like h² (run R1).
    • 

  • Integrals to infinity exist when the integrand decays fast enough; F0-69 relies on this.
    • 

  • A constant error in a rate becomes an error growing like t in its integral: dead reckoning drifts and needs an absolute correction.
    • 

  • The I term of a controller is an integral, which removes steady errors but can wind up at actuator limits.
    •
