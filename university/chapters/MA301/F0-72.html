Story hook

Analogy

Tomás's washing machine stands on a wooden floor. On a slow wash it hums quietly. On the fastest spin it is quiet too. But for a few seconds while the drum speeds up, the whole floor shakes, the glasses in the cupboard rattle, and then it calms down again as the spin gets faster.

The drum is shaking the floor at every speed, with roughly the same unbalanced load. What changes is how the floor answers. Slow shaking, it just follows along a little. Very fast shaking, it is too heavy to keep up and hardly moves. At one particular speed, its own natural bounce, every push arrives at just the right moment and the movement builds up. Engineers describe this by shaking a system with sine waves of different frequencies and recording two things at each: how big the answer is, and how late it comes. That pair of curves, the frequency response, is the subject of this last chapter, and it gives a second way to see the stability limits of F0-71.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute the frequency response G(jω) of a transfer function and interpret its magnitude and phase.
  • Measure gain and phase from a simulated sine test and compare them with the formula.
  • Sketch and read Bode plots of first- and second-order systems using corner frequencies and slopes.
  • Find a loop's gain margin and phase margin from its frequency response.
  • Diagnose a resonance from a vibration sweep and estimate the natural frequency and damping ratio.
Jargon box
Frequency response
Simple words: how a system answers steady sine waves of different frequencies: how much bigger or smaller, and how much later. Analogy: how strongly the floor shakes at each spin speed, and how far its movement lags the drum. Precise: for a stable linear system G(s), a sine input sin(ωt) gives, after the transient has died out, the output |G(jω)|·sin(ωt + ∠G(jω)); the complex function G(jω) of ω is the frequency response. B1R1
Gain (magnitude) and phase
Simple words: the size ratio between output and input sine, and the delay between them measured in degrees of a cycle. Analogy: how far the floor moves per unit of drum push, and how far behind it is. Precise: |G(jω)| and ∠G(jω) = arg G(jω); a phase of −90° means the output peaks a quarter period after the input. B1
Decibel (dB) for gain
Simple words: a logarithmic way to write a gain, so that multiplying gains becomes adding numbers. Analogy: notches on a volume knob. Precise: gain in dB = 20·log10|G|; ×10 is +20 dB, ×2 is about +6 dB, ×1/√2 is about −3 dB. B1D1
Bode plot
Simple words: two graphs of the frequency response against frequency on a log scale: gain in dB above, phase in degrees below. Analogy: the washing machine's "shake chart" over its whole speed range. Precise: plots of 20 log10|G(jω)| and ∠G(jω) against log ω; for rational G they are close to straight-line asymptotes that change slope at corner frequencies. B1R2
Corner frequency
Simple words: the frequency where a simple lag stops passing signals unchanged and starts cutting them down. Analogy: the spin speed above which the floor can no longer keep up. Precise: for 1/(τs + 1), ωc = 1/τ, where |G| = 1/√2 (−3 dB) and the phase is −45°; above it the gain falls at 20 dB per decade. It is the cut-off frequency (−3 dB) of HW302. B1R1
Resonance
Simple words: a large response at one frequency, where the system's own bounce and the push keep in step. Analogy: the floor shaking hard at one spin speed. Precise: for an under-damped second-order system with ζ < 1/√2, |G(jω)| has a maximum near ωn; at ω = ωn exactly, |G| = 1/(2ζ) times the static gain and the phase is −90°. B1R2
Phase margin
Simple words: how much extra delay, in degrees, a loop could take before it becomes unstable. Analogy: how much later Mira (F0-70) could feel the shower water before her corrections started making things worse. Precise: 180° + ∠L(jωgc), where ωgc is the gain crossover frequency at which |L(jωgc)| = 1. B1R4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Push a child on a swing. If you push slowly, out of rhythm, the swing hardly goes anywhere. If you push very fast, it just jiggles. If you push once each time it comes back to you, it goes higher and higher. The swing has its own favourite rhythm.

Engineers find out a machine's favourite rhythms by wiggling its input at many speeds, slow to fast, and writing down how big the wiggle comes out and how late. The chart they make shows where the machine follows well, where it cannot keep up, and where it might shake itself apart. It also shows, for a control loop, whether the corrections will arrive in time.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Sine in, sine out

Feed a stable linear system with u(t) = sin(ωt). The response has two parts: a transient made of the system's own exponentials ept (which die out, since all poles are on the left, F0-71), and a steady part that is a sine of the same frequency ω. Its size and timing are given by the transfer function evaluated at s = jω:

y_steady(t) = |G(jω)| · sin(ωt + ∠G(jω))

No new frequency appears; a linear system can only scale and shift the sine. B1D1

For the pretend motor, G(s) = 98.04/(0.1961 s + 1), so G(jω) = 98.04/(1 + j·0.1961ω). Its magnitude is 98.04/√(1 + (0.1961ω)²) and its phase is −arctan(0.1961ω). At ω = 1/τ = 5.10 rad/s: magnitude 98.04/√2 = 69.32 rad/s per V (−3 dB from the static 39.8 dB) and phase −45°. Listing 1 drives the motor model with sine waves, waits for the transient to die out, and measures amplitude and phase by correlating the output with sin and cos over whole periods; the measured values agree with the formula to every printed digit at all six frequencies. R1R4

Bode plots and straight lines

On log–log axes the first-order lag 1/(τs + 1) is almost two straight lines:

  • for ω ≪ 1/τ: |G| ≈ 1 (0 dB), phase ≈ 0°;
  • for ω ≫ 1/τ: |G| ≈ 1/(τω), which falls by a factor of 10 for every factor of 10 in frequency: −20 dB per decade; phase → −90°;
  • at the corner ω = 1/τ the true curve is 3 dB below the corner of the lines, with phase −45°.

Listing 2 confirms it for τ = 0.1 s: −3.0 dB and −45° at 10 rad/s, −20 dB at 100 rad/s, −40 dB at 1000 rad/s. Gains in dB add when blocks are in series (because logs of products add), so the Bode plot of a series connection is the sum of the plots: this is why engineers like decibels. R2D1B1

The exponential moving average of F0-66 is a first-order low-pass filter (HW302 F1-72) and has exactly this shape: the speed filter with τf = 20 ms has its corner at 50 rad/s (7.96 Hz). R4U2

Second order: slopes and resonance

For G(s) = ωn²/(s² + 2ζωns + ωn²), the low-frequency gain is 1 (0 dB), and far above ωn the gain falls at 40 dB per decade with the phase approaching −180°. Near ωn the damping decides everything. At ω = ωn exactly, G(jωn) = 1/(2ζj): gain 1/(2ζ) and phase −90° for every ζ. For ζ = 0.1 that is a gain of 5 (14 dB); for ζ = 0.05 it is 10 (20 dB). The true maximum is at ωr = ωn√(1 − 2ζ²), with height 1/(2ζ√(1 − ζ²)); it exists only for ζ < 1/√2 ≈ 0.707. Listing 2 prints 5.025 at 9.90 rad/s for ζ = 0.1 and 1.747 at 9.06 rad/s for ζ = 0.3. For ζ = 0.7, just below 0.707, the "peak" is 1.000 at 1.41 rad/s: too small to see. R2B1

This ties back to F0-68: a lightly damped system that overshoots and rings after a step is the same system that resonates strongly under a sine input. Both measure ζ. D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Stability margins from the loop's frequency response

F0-71 found that the speed loop oscillates at 75.93 rad/s when Kp = 0.3475. The frequency response tells the same story without solving for poles. At the critical gain, a sine at 75.93 rad/s goes round the loop and comes back the same size (|L| = 1) and exactly half a cycle late (∠L = −180°); the minus sign at the summing junction turns that into exactly in step, so the oscillation sustains itself. So:

  • Phase crossover ωpc: where ∠L(jω) = −180°. For the three-lag loop it is 75.93 rad/s, the same for every Kp (gain does not change phase).
  • Gain margin = 1/|L(jωpc)|. At Kp = 0.05, |L(jωpc)| = 0.1439, so the gain margin is 6.95 (16.8 dB), the same as 0.3475/0.05 in F0-71.
  • Gain crossover ωgc: where |L(jω)| = 1. At Kp = 0.05 it is 21.8 rad/s, where the phase is −112.7°: a phase margin of 67.3°.

Figure 3 marks both margins. The phase margin answers "how much extra delay could the loop take?": an extra delay T adds −ωT radians of phase at ωgc, so here 67.3° = 1.175 rad buys T = 1.175/21.8 = 54 ms before the loop reaches the edge. That is a design margin on the linear model, not a promise about a real motor. R4D1B1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Gain and phase margins as defined here are reliable for loops like this one, where the phase crosses −180° once and the open loop is stable. For loops with several crossings or unstable open-loop poles the general tool is the Nyquist criterion, which is not covered in this course; that statement and the "rules of thumb" sometimes quoted for good margins are from the general literature (B1), not opened in this build. The margins computed for the pretend motor are confirmed by run R4 and agree with the root computation of F0-71.

Why the sine test is so useful

A step test (F0-68) excites all frequencies at once and mixes their answers. A sine test isolates one frequency at a time and is robust to noise: correlating with sin and cos over many whole periods, as Listing 1 does, averages away anything at other frequencies. That is how frequency responses are measured on real equipment, and it needs no model at all: the measured Bode plot is the model. A measured loop's phase includes every lag, known or not (sample delay, sensor filtering, mechanical flex), which is why measured margins are trusted over modelled ones. B1R1

Resonances inside control loops

A mechanical resonance (a flexible shaft, a belt, a loose mount) appears in the loop's Bode plot as a narrow peak with a rapid phase drop of 180°. If the peak lifts |L| above 1 at a frequency where the phase is near −180°, the loop goes unstable at that resonance even though its margins looked fine elsewhere. Engineers then lower the gain, add a notch filter at the resonance, or stiffen the mechanics. Measuring the frequency response is how such resonances are found. B1

How the hardware actually does it

On a bench, a frequency response is measured by a program that injects a sine into the controller's output (or adds it to the setpoint), waits for steady state, and correlates the measured response with sin and cos, frequency by frequency, as Listing 1 does in simulation. The test amplitude must be small enough to stay away from saturation and dry friction (both nonlinear, F0-67) and large enough to rise above sensor noise and encoder quantisation (F0-66). The sample rate limits the highest frequency that can be measured: nothing above half the sample rate (the Nyquist frequency) can be represented. The numbers in this chapter all come from simulations. B1U2

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. No real motor, shelf or fan was measured in this build. The motor model is the pretend motor of F1-69, the loop lags are the assumptions of F0-71, and the shelf's parameters are invented for the forensic lab. A frequency response measured on real hardware will differ, especially at high frequencies where unmodelled lags and resonances appear.

Worked example

Task. The speed filter of F0-71 is Gf(s) = 1/(0.02 s + 1). Encoder noise appears at about 300 rad/s, and the motor's speed changes that matter are below 10 rad/s. (a) How much does the filter reduce the noise? (b) How much phase lag does it add at the loop's gain crossover of 21.8 rad/s? (c) Express (a) in dB.

  1. (a) |Gf(j300)| = 1/√(1 + (0.02 × 300)²) = 1/√37 = 0.164: the noise is reduced to about one sixth.
  2. (b) ∠Gf(j21.8) = −arctan(0.02 × 21.8) = −arctan(0.436) = −23.6°. That 23.6° comes straight out of the phase margin.
  3. (c) 20 log10(0.164) = −15.7 dB. At 10 rad/s, the filter's gain is 1/√1.04 = 0.981 (−0.17 dB): the useful signal passes almost untouched.

This is the filter trade-off of F0-66 and F0-71 in numbers: stronger filtering removes more noise but costs phase margin. Number check, run R4 — real output of checks.cpp (motor, loop and shelf values used in this chapter):

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The washing machine's push grows with spin speed (an unbalanced mass pushes harder the faster it spins). The frequency response assumes a fixed input amplitude at every frequency; to compare real measurements you divide by the input size.
    • 

  • A floor has many resonances (different ways to bend); a second-order model captures one. Real structures need one second-order term per mode.
    • 

  • The washer passes through its resonance quickly while speeding up; the frequency response describes a steady state reached only if you stay at one frequency long enough. B1
