Story hook

Analogy

Tomás lets a toy cart roll down the long hallway at home. His sister Wanjiru claps once every second and Tomás sticks a note on the floor where the front wheel is at each clap. When they measure the notes, the gaps grow: 0.8 m, then 1.4 m, then 2 m. The cart is speeding up.

"How fast was it going at exactly one second?" asks Wanjiru. Tomás points at the gaps. The first second covered 0.8 m, the second 1.4 m, so the answer must be somewhere between. To get closer, they would need claps every half second, then every tenth of a second, and so on. Each time the gap divided by the time between claps gives a better answer, and the answers settle down towards one number.

That number, the speed at one instant, is a derivative. This chapter makes the "settle down towards" precise, gives you rules to get derivatives without any clapping, and checks every rule with a program.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute average rates of change from data and explain how the derivative arises as their limit.
  • Differentiate polynomials, exponentials, sines and their sums, products and compositions with the standard rules, and check each result numerically.
  • Interpret derivatives physically: velocity as the derivative of position, acceleration as the derivative of velocity, with units.
  • Use the linear approximation f(t + h) ≈ f(t) + f′(t)·h and predict the error of a difference quotient.
  • Diagnose a speed log whose values are wrong because of units and time stamps.
Jargon box
Rate of change
Simple words: how fast one quantity changes when another one changes. Analogy: how many metres the cart gains per clap. Precise: for a quantity x that depends on t, the change in x divided by the change in t; its unit is the unit of x divided by the unit of t (m/s for position over time). D1
Average rate of change (difference quotient)
Simple words: the change over a whole stretch of time, divided by the length of that stretch. Analogy: the gap between two sticky notes divided by the time between the claps. Precise: (x(t + h) − x(t)) / h for a step h ≠ 0; geometrically the slope of the secant line through the two points of the graph. D1B2
Limit
Simple words: the value an answer gets closer and closer to as you make a step smaller and smaller. Analogy: the number the clap-by-clap speeds settle towards when the claps come faster and faster. Precise: L is the limit of g(h) as h → 0 if g(h) can be made as close to L as you like by taking h close enough to 0 (h ≠ 0). B2
Derivative
Simple words: the rate of change at one instant. Analogy: the cart's speed at the exact moment of one clap. Precise: x′(t) = dx/dt = limh→0 (x(t + h) − x(t)) / h, when this limit exists; the slope of the tangent line to the graph at t. B2D1
Tangent line
Simple words: the straight line that just touches a curve at one point and points the way the curve is heading there. Analogy: the direction the cart would keep going if nothing changed its speed from that moment on. Precise: the line y = x(t₀) + x′(t₀)(t − t₀); the best straight-line approximation of the curve near t₀. B2
Velocity
Simple words: speed with a direction (a sign). Analogy: how fast the cart rolls, counted negative when it rolls back towards the start. Precise: v(t) = x′(t), the derivative of position with respect to time, in m/s for straight-line motion. B1D1
Acceleration
Simple words: how fast the velocity changes. Analogy: how much faster the cart gets from one clap to the next. Precise: a(t) = v′(t) = x″(t), the second derivative of position, in m/s². B1D1
Differentiation rules
Simple words: short-cuts that give a derivative without computing a limit each time. Analogy: a recipe card that turns a formula for position straight into a formula for speed. Precise: the power, sum, constant-multiple, product, quotient and chain rules, together with the derivatives of et, sin t and cos t; each follows from the limit definition. B2
Linear approximation
Simple words: pretending a curve is a straight line for a short moment. Analogy: guessing where the cart will be a moment later from where it is and how fast it goes now. Precise: x(t + h) ≈ x(t) + x′(t)·h, with an error that shrinks like h² when x has a second derivative (the first terms of Taylor's formula). B2

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 plots the cart's position x(t) = 0.3t² + 0.5t, the formula that fits Tomás's notes. The dashed lines are secants: each joins the point at t = 1 s with a later point, and its slope is an average speed. As the later point moves closer, the secants turn towards the tangent line, whose slope 1.1 m/s is the derivative at t = 1 s. Figure 2 shows the three curves an engineer reads together for a braking cart: position, its derivative velocity, and the derivative of velocity, acceleration.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

If you walk 6 m in 3 s, your average speed is 2 m per second. That is a rate of change: how much distance you gained, divided by how much time it took. It says nothing about whether you walked evenly or ran and stopped.

To know your speed at one moment, measure over a shorter and shorter time around that moment. Over a tenth of a second you cannot change speed much, so distance divided by time is nearly your speed right then. The number these answers get closer to is the derivative. Your speedometer, if you had one, would show it.

The same idea works for anything that changes: the water level in a bath (centimetres per minute), the temperature of a kettle (degrees per second), the speed of a motor (how fast it speeds up). A derivative is always "how much this changes per unit of that", right now.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From average to instantaneous

Let x(t) be the cart's position in metres at time t in seconds. Between t and t + h the cart moves x(t + h) − x(t), so its average velocity is the difference quotient (x(t + h) − x(t)) / h. On the graph this is the slope of the secant line through (t, x(t)) and (t + h, x(t + h)). D1

For Tomás's cart, x(t) = 0.3t² + 0.5t. Expand the quotient at t = 1:

x(1 + h) − x(1) = 0.3(1 + 2h + h²) + 0.5(1 + h) − 0.8 = 1.1h + 0.3h², so the quotient is 1.1 + 0.3h.

When h shrinks, 0.3h shrinks to zero and the quotient approaches 1.1. That limit is the derivative x′(1) = 1.1 m/s. Listing 1 computes the quotient for h = 1, 0.1, …, 10−12 s and prints the error, which is exactly 0.3h until h reaches about 10−7; below that a different error appears, which Layer 3 explains. D1R1

Formally, the derivative of x at t is B2

x′(t) = dx/dt = lim (h → 0) of (x(t + h) − x(t)) / h

when the limit exists. The notation dx/dt (Leibniz) reminds you of the units, metres per second; x′ (Lagrange) and ẋ (Newton's dot, common in mechanics and control books) mean the same. B2B1

Position, velocity, acceleration

For straight-line motion, velocity is the derivative of position, v = dx/dt, and acceleration is the derivative of velocity, a = dv/dt = d²x/dt². Units follow the quotient: m per s, then (m/s) per s = m/s². The sign matters: negative velocity means moving back towards the origin; negative acceleration while v > 0 means slowing down. B1D1

Read Figure 2 from the bottom up. Acceleration is constant at −0.5 m/s², so velocity falls along a straight line from 2 m/s, so position rises more and more slowly and stops rising where velocity reaches zero, at t = 4 s. A flat spot on any curve is a zero of the curve below it. Listing 3 prints the three columns. R3

Other rates in this course

The same quotient applies to any pair of quantities. A motor's angular speed ω (rad/s) is the derivative of its shaft angle θ (rad); its angular acceleration dω/dt is in rad/s². A battery's charge falls at a rate equal to the current. A kettle's temperature rises at a rate proportional to the heating power minus the heat lost. In each case writing the derivative with its units is the first step towards the differential equations of F0-67. B1D1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Linear approximation and the error of a difference

If x has a continuous second derivative, Taylor's formula gives B2

x(t + h) = x(t) + x′(t)·h + ½·x″(ξ)·h² for some ξ between t and t + h.

Two consequences matter in engineering. First, the linear approximation x(t + h) ≈ x(t) + x′(t)·h is what every simulator in this course does at each time step (F0-67), and its error per step is of order h². Second, dividing by h, the forward difference quotient differs from x′(t) by ½·x″(ξ)·h, an error proportional to h. For the cart, x″ = 0.6 everywhere, so the error is exactly 0.3h, which is what Listing 1 prints for h from 1 down to 10−6. D1R1

When the step is too small

Below h ≈ 10−8 the printed error stops shrinking and becomes erratic (about 10−7 to 10−5 at h = 10−9 to 10−12). The program subtracts two nearly equal positions, each stored with a relative rounding error near the precision of a double, and then divides by a tiny h, which magnifies those rounding errors. The total error is roughly (truncation) + (rounding) ≈ C₁h + C₂ε/h, where ε is the machine's relative precision, so there is a best h, near √ε for this forward difference. MA302 (F0-73, F0-74) measures ε and explains rounding; here the lesson is practical: never assume that a smaller step is always better. R1B3

Where derivatives do not exist

The limit can fail. At a corner, such as |t| at t = 0, the quotient tends to +1 from the right and −1 from the left, so there is no single slope. At a jump, such as a setpoint that steps from 0 to 1 at t = 0, the quotient grows like 1/h without bound. Both occur constantly in control: a step command has no derivative at the step, and a controller that differentiates its error naively produces a huge spike there, the "derivative kick" that F0-66 and RB202 deal with. Physical quantities with inertia, such as a cart's position or a motor's speed, cannot jump, which is why their derivatives exist. B2B1

The chain rule in practice

Most physical derivatives are chains. A wheel of radius r turning at angle θ(t) moves the cart x = r·θ(t), so v = r·θ′(t) = r·ω. A sensor that reports y = x² (for example, a quantity proportional to the square of a distance) changes at dy/dt = 2x·dx/dt. The motor speed curve of F0-68, ω(t) = ω∞(1 − e−t/τ), has derivative (ω∞/τ)·e−t/τ by the chain rule; at t = 0 this is ω∞/τ, the starting acceleration, which for the pretend motor of F1-69 is 588.24 / 0.19608 ≈ 3000 rad/s². How the hardware actually does it

No processor computes a limit. A robot's controller reads a sensor at moments set by a hardware timer (HW204), so it sees samples x[k] at times t[k], and it can only form difference quotients (x[k] − x[k−1]) / (t[k] − t[k−1]). Two hardware facts decide whether those quotients are any good. The first is the step: the timer's period sets h, and the analysis of Layer 3 says the error of the quotient grows with h. The second is the quality of the numbers: an encoder (F1-66) delivers whole counts, so the position is rounded to a step, and dividing that rounding by a small h produces large errors, the subject of F0-66. Time stamps matter as much as values: if the time between samples is not what the code assumes, every rate is wrong, as the forensic lab shows. This chapter's programs ran only on the build container's CPU in double precision; no sensor or microcontroller was used. U2R1R5

Worked example

Task. A cart on a carpet is released at 2 m/s and brakes evenly. Its position is x(t) = 2t − 0.25t² metres for 0 ≤ t ≤ 4 s. Find its velocity and acceleration, when it stops and how far it travelled. Then find the starting acceleration of the pretend motor's speed curve.

  1. Velocity. Power and sum rules: v(t) = x′(t) = 2 − 0.5t m/s. At t = 0, v = 2 m/s (the release speed, a useful check).
  2. Acceleration. a(t) = v′(t) = −0.5 m/s², constant. Negative while v > 0: the cart slows down.
  3. Stop. v(t) = 0 gives 2 − 0.5t = 0, t = 4 s.
  4. Distance. x(4) = 8 − 4 = 4 m. Listing 3 prints the whole table and the same stop.
  5. Motor. ω(t) = 588.24(1 − e−t/0.19608) rad/s. Chain rule: ω′(t) = (588.24/0.19608)·e−t/0.19608. At t = 0, ω′(0) ≈ 3000 rad/s². After one time constant the factor e−1 ≈ 0.368 leaves about 1104 rad/s².

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Sticky notes and claps can only ever give average speeds over a finite time. The derivative is defined by a limit that no measurement reaches; real systems only approximate it, and F0-66 shows that pushing h down with real sensors makes things worse, not better. R1
    • 

  • The story suggests that every motion has a speed at every instant. Mathematical functions can have corners and jumps where no derivative exists (Layer 3), and commands in software do jump. Only quantities with inertia behave like the cart.
    • 

  • Tomás's gaps are distances, always positive. A derivative can be negative (rolling back) or zero (stopped), and the sign carries meaning that "how far between notes" hides.

Summary


    

  • An average rate of change is a difference quotient: change in x divided by change in t, with units.
    • 

  • The derivative is the limit of the difference quotient as the step goes to zero: the slope of the tangent line.
    • 

  • The power, sum, product, quotient and chain rules, and the derivatives of eat, sin and cos, give derivatives without limits; check them numerically.
    • 

  • Velocity is the derivative of position, acceleration the derivative of velocity; a zero of the derivative marks a stop or a turning point.
    • 

  • The forward difference has error proportional to h; below a certain h, rounding error grows like 1/h and dominates.
    • 

  • Corners and jumps have no derivative, which matters for step commands in controllers.
    • 

  • In data, check units and time stamps before trusting any rate.
    •
