Story hook

Analogy

At Leila's birthday party the karaoke machine is set up in the living room. Her uncle turns the microphone volume up so the singing can be heard over the chatter. A little more, and a little more, and suddenly a loud howl fills the room. He turns it back down a notch, and the howl stops as quickly as it began.

Nothing broke. The microphone hears the loudspeaker, the amplifier makes it louder, the speaker plays it, the microphone hears it again. Below a certain volume each trip round the loop is a little quieter than the last, and sounds die away. Above it, each trip is louder, and one pitch, set by the room and the equipment, grows until the amplifier cannot go any louder. This chapter is about that line between "dies away" and "grows", for the motor loop of F0-70: where it lies, how to calculate it, and what happens when you cross it.

What you will be able to do

  • Decide whether a linear system is stable from the positions of its poles.
  • Compute closed-loop poles of a speed loop for a range of gains and describe how they move.
  • Apply the Routh–Hurwitz test to a cubic to find the critical gain and the frequency of oscillation at that gain.
  • Explain why lags (a driver, a filter, a delay) limit the gain a loop can use.
  • Diagnose a humming motor from its speed log as a loop pushed past its critical gain into saturation.
Jargon box
Stability (of a linear system)
Simple words: a system is stable if, after a disturbance, it settles down again instead of drifting or growing. Analogy: the karaoke loop at a low volume: a clap dies away. Precise: a linear time-invariant system is asymptotically stable if every solution of its unforced equation decays to zero; equivalently, every pole of its transfer function has a negative real part (lies in the open left half of the s-plane). B1
Left half-plane
Simple words: the part of the s-plane to the left of the vertical axis, where the real part of s is negative. Analogy: the "volume low enough" side of the knob. Precise: {s : Re(s) < 0}; a pole p there gives a term ept that decays. D1
Characteristic polynomial
Simple words: the polynomial whose roots are the closed-loop poles. Analogy: the recipe that decides whether the loop howls. Precise: for a loop with L(s) = N(s)/D(s), the denominator of 1 + L(s), that is D(s) + N(s); first met in F0-68. B1D1
Routh–Hurwitz criterion
Simple words: a test that tells you whether all roots of a polynomial are in the left half-plane, without finding them. Analogy: a rule printed on the karaoke box: "keep volume × room echo below this number". Precise: a polynomial with real coefficients has all roots in the open left half-plane exactly when every entry of the first column of its Routh array is positive; for a3s³ + a2s² + a1s + a0 with all ai > 0 this reduces to a2a1 > a3a0. B1R4
Critical gain
Simple words: the gain at which the loop is exactly on the edge between settling and growing. Analogy: the volume setting where the howl just starts. Precise: the smallest gain K for which a closed-loop pole reaches the imaginary axis; at that gain the loop oscillates steadily at the frequency of the crossing. D1R1
Gain margin
Simple words: how many times the gain could be raised before the loop becomes unstable. Analogy: how many more notches of volume are left before the howl. Precise: the ratio of the critical gain to the gain in use (often given in dB, 20 log10 of the ratio). F0-72 measures it from a frequency response. B1
Limit cycle
Simple words: a steady oscillation whose size is set by a limit in the system, not by how it started. Analogy: the howl that grows until the amplifier is flat out, then stays at that loudness. Precise: an isolated periodic solution of a nonlinear system; in a loop that is linearly unstable, saturation can stop the growth and produce one. B1R3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 plots the closed-loop poles of the motor loop in the s-plane for gains from 0 to 0.5. Two poles move towards each other, meet, and then travel up and to the right as a complex pair, crossing the imaginary axis at Kp = 0.3475. Figure 2 shows what that means in time: a step response that rings and settles, one that rings forever, and one that grows. Figure 3 is the real-world version: with a 12 V driver limit, the growing oscillation stops growing and becomes a steady hum.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Imagine passing a whisper round a circle of friends. If each friend whispers a bit more quietly than they heard, the message fades out. If each one speaks a bit louder, it soon becomes a shout. Machines with feedback are like that circle. Each trip round the loop either makes a wobble smaller or bigger.

Engineers have a map, called the s-plane, where they put a dot for each way the machine can wobble. Dots on the left side mean wobbles that fade; dots on the right side mean wobbles that grow. Turning up the gain moves the dots. The job is to keep all of them on the left, with some room to spare.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Stable means all poles on the left

F0-69 showed that each pole p of a transfer function contributes a term ept to the response. For p = σ + jω this is eσt times an oscillation at ω. If σ < 0 the term dies away; if σ > 0 it grows without bound; if σ = 0 it neither decays nor grows. So a linear system is stable exactly when all its poles have negative real parts. One pole on the wrong side is enough to ruin it, however well-behaved the others are. B1D1

For a closed loop T = L/(1 + L), the poles are the roots of 1 + L(s) = 0. If L = N/D, they are the roots of the characteristic polynomial D(s) + N(s). D1

Why the F0-70 loop could take any gain

In F0-70 the loop was a first-order motor with a P controller: characteristic polynomial τms + 1 + KpKdc, one pole at −(1 + KpKdc)/τm. Higher gain pushes it further left; it can never cross. Even with the driver's lag added (two poles), the characteristic polynomial τmτas² + (τm + τa)s + 1 + KpKdc has all coefficients positive for any Kp > 0, and for a quadratic that is enough for stability: more gain gives more overshoot, never instability. D1R4

Three lags: the loop now has a limit

A real speed loop has more lags. This chapter assumes a driver that responds with a time constant of τa = 10 ms and a speed measurement smoothed by a filter with τf = 20 ms (F0-66). Both values are assumptions for the exercise, not measurements. The loop gain is

L(s) = Kp · 1/(τa s + 1) · Kdc/(τm s + 1) · 1/(τf s + 1)

and the characteristic polynomial is the cubic (τms + 1)(τas + 1)(τfs + 1) + KpKdc = a3s³ + a2s² + a1s + a0 with a3 = 3.92×10−5, a2 = 6.08×10−3, a1 = 0.2261 and a0 = 1 + KpKdc. Listing 1 finds its roots for several gains:

The Routh–Hurwitz test for a cubic

Finding roots is not needed to decide stability. For a cubic with all coefficients positive, the Routh–Hurwitz criterion says: stable exactly when a2a1 > a3a0. Here that is 1 + KpKdc < a2a1/a3 = 35.07, so Kp < 34.07/98.04 = 0.3475. At equality the cubic has roots ±jω with ω² = a1/a3, so ω = 75.93 rad/s, a 12.08 Hz oscillation. Both numbers agree with the roots in the table. B1D1R1

Listing 2 checks it in the time domain: it simulates the loop (no voltage limit) for a 100 rad/s step and compares the oscillation size in two half-second windows. At Kp = 0.2 the ratio is 0.024 (dies out), at 0.3475 it is 0.993 (keeps going), at 0.5 it is 16.8 (grows). R2

Note

F0-70 found that a 2 % steady-state error with P control needs Kp ≈ 0.50. With the lags of this chapter that gain is unstable. This is why integral action, not just more gain, is used to remove steady-state error (RB202 F9-18). R4U2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The Routh array in general

For a polynomial of degree n, write the coefficients in two rows, alternating: an, an−2, … and an−1, an−3, …. Each further row is built from the two above it: entry = (left-below × right-above − left-above × right-below)/left-below, using the first column and the next column along. For the cubic the first column is a3, a2, (a2a1 − a3a0)/a2, a0. The number of sign changes in the first column equals the number of roots in the right half-plane. At Kp = 0.5 the third entry is −0.096: two sign changes, two unstable roots, matching the table. Zeros in the first column need special handling (described in B1 and B2). B1R4

Where the poles go: the root locus idea

As Kp rises from 0 to ∞, the closed-loop poles start at the open-loop poles (the roots of D) and move along paths called the root locus. With no zeros and three poles, the paths go to infinity along three straight lines (asymptotes) at angles of 60°, 180° and 300°, centred on the average of the poles, (−5.1 − 50 − 100)/3 = −51.7. Two of those lines point into the right half-plane, which is why any three-lag loop becomes unstable at some gain. The rules for sketching root loci are in B1; Figure 1 is drawn from computed roots, not from the rules. B1D1

Delays are the strongest lag

A pure delay T (a controller that acts one sample late, a long pipe, a slow network) has the transfer function e−sT. It does not reduce the size of a signal at all, but delays every frequency by T, which at frequency ω is a phase lag of ωT radians, growing without limit. Every loop with a delay therefore has a critical gain. A sampled controller running every Ts acts, on average, about half a period late, and the computation adds more; that is one reason for the sample-rate rules of RB202 F9-21 and MA302 F0-79. F0-72 measures the effect of phase lag directly. B1U2

Beyond linear: saturation and limit cycles

A linear model says an unstable loop grows forever. Real loops hit limits. With the driver clamped at ±12 V, the Kp = 0.5 loop grows until the command saturates, then settles into a steady oscillation of fixed size: a limit cycle. Its frequency is close to the frequency where the linear loop crosses the axis (12.00 Hz simulated versus 12.08 Hz from Routh–Hurwitz), because the oscillation is held at the frequency where going round the loop comes back in step. Approximating such limit cycles (the describing function method) is beyond this course. R3R4B1

Not verified — check before relying on this

The general Routh–Hurwitz statement (sign changes count right-half-plane roots), the root-locus asymptote rule and the remark about describing functions are given from the general theory with tag B1, which was not opened in this build. The cubic case, the critical gain and the crossing frequency are confirmed by computed roots and by simulation (runs R1, R2, R4). The driver lag of 10 ms and filter lag of 20 ms are assumptions, not measured values; a real loop's critical gain depends on its own lags.

How the hardware actually does it

Nothing in a microcontroller checks stability. A controller with too much gain will run exactly as programmed and make the motor oscillate, hum, or shake its mounts. Engineers protect against this in three ways: they design with a model and a margin (keep Kp well below the critical gain; at Kp = 0.05 this loop has a gain margin of 6.95, about 17 dB); they limit the output (the ±12 V clamp turns a runaway into a bounded hum, which is unpleasant but survivable); and they test new gains at low power with a way to stop the motor quickly. The extra lags a real loop contains (encoder update rate, PWM period, computation time, mechanical resonances) are only known after measuring the hardware. R4U2

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. No real motor was used in this build. The critical gain 0.3475 and the 12 Hz hum belong to the simulated pretend motor with assumed lags. On a real motor the critical gain must be found by experiment (carefully, in RB202 F9-20 and F9-22) or from a measured frequency response (F0-72).

Worked example

Task. Remove the speed filter (keep the driver lag τa = 10 ms) and add a different lag: the encoder speed estimate now behaves like a lag of τf = 5 ms. Find the new critical gain and crossing frequency, and the gain margin at Kp = 0.05.

  1. a3 = τmτaτf = 0.19608 × 0.01 × 0.005 = 9.804×10−6.
  2. a2 = τmτa + τmτf + τaτf = 1.9608×10−3 + 0.9804×10−3 + 0.05×10−3 = 2.9912×10−3.
  3. a1 = 0.19608 + 0.01 + 0.005 = 0.21108.
  4. Critical: 1 + KpKdc = a2a1/a3 = 2.9912×10−3 × 0.21108/9.804×10−6 = 64.40, so Kp = 63.40/98.04 = 0.647.
  5. Crossing: ω = √(a1/a3) = √(21530) = 146.7 rad/s (23.4 Hz).
  6. Gain margin at 0.05: 0.647/0.05 = 12.9 (22 dB).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A room has many echo paths and many possible howling pitches; the motor loop has one dominant crossing. Real mechanical systems are more like the room: shafts and mounts have resonances that can each become the unstable one (F0-72).
    • 

  • Turning the karaoke volume down stops the howl instantly. In a motor, energy is stored in the spinning mass and the oscillation takes a while to die away even after the gain is lowered.
    • 

  • The howl is harmless noise. A motor oscillating at its current limit heats its windings and driver and shakes its mechanics. U2

Summary


    

  • A linear system is stable when every pole has a negative real part; one right-half-plane pole is enough to make it unstable.
    • 

  • Closed-loop poles are the roots of 1 + L(s) = 0; they move as the gain changes.
    • 

  • One or two lags with P control can take any gain; three or more lags (or a delay) set a critical gain.
    • 

  • Routh–Hurwitz for a cubic: all coefficients positive and a2a1 > a3a0; at the edge, ω² = a1/a3.
    • 

  • For the pretend motor with 10 ms and 20 ms lags: Kp < 0.3475, crossing at 75.9 rad/s (12.1 Hz).
    • 

  • With saturation, an unstable loop becomes a limit cycle near the crossing frequency: a hum, not a crash.
