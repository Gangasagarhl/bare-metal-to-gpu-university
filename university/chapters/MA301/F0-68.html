Story hook

Analogy

Two things happen in Amara's kitchen at the same moment. She switches on an electric kettle, and her grandfather drops a bag of flour onto the old spring scale with a needle. The kettle's temperature climbs smoothly towards its final value: fast at first, then more and more slowly, never overshooting. The scale's needle swings past the right weight, comes back below it, swings past again a little less, and after a few wobbles settles.

The kettle is a first-order system: it stores one kind of thing (heat) and leaks it at a rate that depends on how much it has. The scale is a second-order system: it stores two kinds of thing, the spring's stretch and the moving platform's speed, and trades them back and forth, which is why it can overshoot. Almost every system in robotics and control, from a motor's speed to a drone's tilt, looks like one of these two, or a mix. This chapter teaches you to recognise them from a single experiment, the step response, and to read their numbers off the curve.

What you will be able to do

  • Write first- and second-order systems in standard form and name their parameters: gain K, time constant τ, natural frequency ωn, damping ratio ζ.
  • Predict a step response: final value, 63 % time, overshoot, peak time and settling time, from the parameters.
  • Model a DC motor as two coupled first-order equations, simulate its step response, and justify the first-order approximation (the course lab).
  • Identify ζ and ωn of an under-damped system from a logged step response (the course forensic lab).
  • Explain where the usual rules of thumb stop being valid.
Jargon box
Step response
Simple words: what a system does when its input is switched from zero to a fixed value and held. Analogy: the kettle switched on; the flour dropped on the scale. Precise: the output y(t), from rest, for the input u(t) = 0 for t < 0 and u(t) = U for t ≥ 0; often normalised to U = 1 (unit step). B1
First-order system
Simple words: a system that approaches its final value smoothly, without overshoot, closing the same fraction of the gap in each equal time. Analogy: the kettle warming up. Precise: τ·y′ + y = K·u; unit step response y = K(1 − e−t/τ). B1D1
Static gain (DC gain)
Simple words: how much output you finally get per unit of steady input. Analogy: how many degrees hotter per unit of heater setting, once everything has settled. Precise: K = lim y(t)/U for a step of size U in a stable system; for a motor's speed, rad/s per volt. B1
Second-order system
Simple words: a system with two stores of energy that can swing past its final value. Analogy: the scale's spring and moving platform. Precise: y″ + 2ζωn·y′ + ωn²·y = K·ωn²·u, for example a mass–spring–damper m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = F. B1
Natural frequency (ωn)
Simple words: how fast the system would swing if nothing slowed it down. Analogy: the needle's bouncing speed with no friction. Precise: ωn = √(k/m) for a mass–spring, in rad/s; the undamped oscillation frequency. B1
Damping ratio (ζ)
Simple words: a single number saying how strongly the swinging is slowed down. Analogy: how sticky the scale's mechanism is: a little, and it wobbles a lot; a lot, and it creeps to the value. Precise: ζ = c/(2√(k·m)) for a mass–spring–damper; under-damped 0 < ζ < 1 (overshoots), critically damped ζ = 1, over-damped ζ > 1. B1
Overshoot
Simple words: how far the output goes past its final value, as a percentage. Analogy: how far past the true weight the needle swings first. Precise: Mp = (ymax − y∞)/y∞; for the standard second-order system Mp = exp(−πζ/√(1 − ζ²)). B1R2
Peak time and damped frequency
Simple words: when the first, biggest overshoot happens, and how fast the wobbles really are. Analogy: when the needle is furthest past the mark. Precise: ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²) and tp = π/ωd, half a period of the damped oscillation. B1
Settling time
Simple words: how long until the output stays close to its final value for good. Analogy: how long before you can read the scale. Precise: the last time the response is outside a band (here ±2 %) around y∞; the estimate 4/(ζωn) holds for moderately under-damped systems only. B1R2
Logarithmic decrement
Simple words: how much each wobble shrinks compared with the one before. Analogy: the second swing of the needle past the mark compared with the first. Precise: δ = ln(a₁/a₂) for two successive overshoots a₁, a₂ one period apart; ζ = δ/√(4π² + δ²). B1D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A kettle has one thing that changes, its temperature. The hotter it is, the more heat leaks away, so it warms more slowly as it gets hot. It never overshoots: it creeps up to its final temperature. That is first order.

A spring scale has two things that change: how far the spring is stretched and how fast the platform is moving. When the platform reaches the right weight it is still moving, so it goes too far. The spring pulls it back, and it goes too far the other way. Each swing is smaller because friction eats a little energy. That is second order. Little friction: big, long wobbles. A lot of friction: no wobble, but a slow creep. Engineers often choose something in between.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

First order

F0-67's cart was τ·v′ + v = F/c. Any system of the form τ·y′ + y = K·u is first order. Its unit step response is y(t) = K(1 − e−t/τ), and two numbers describe it completely: K, read as the final value, and τ, read as the time at which the output reaches 63.2 % of it. The initial slope is K/τ: the tangent at t = 0 reaches the final value exactly at t = τ, a second way to read τ off a plot. B1D1

Second order

A mass m on a spring of stiffness k with damping c, pushed by F, obeys m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = F. Divide by m and define ωn = √(k/m) and ζ = c/(2√(km)): B1

x″ + 2ζω_n·x′ + ω_n²·x = ω_n²·(F/k)

The static gain is 1/k (metres per newton). The shape of the response depends only on ζ, and its time scale only on ωn: doubling ωn makes the same curve happen twice as fast. For 0 < ζ < 1 the response oscillates at the damped frequency ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²) inside an envelope e−ζωnt, which gives the three standard formulas B1

M_p = exp(−πζ / √(1 − ζ²)), t_p = π / ω_d, t_s(2 %) ≈ 4 / (ζω_n)

Listing 2 simulates ωn = 10 rad/s for six damping ratios and measures all three. Overshoot and peak time match the formulas to the printed digits (72.9 %, 52.7 %, 16.3 %, 4.6 % and 0.316, 0.321, 0.363, 0.440 s for ζ = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 0.7). The settling-time estimate is close for ζ ≤ 0.5 (3.838 against 4.000 s at ζ = 0.1) and wrong for ζ ≥ 1: at ζ = 2 the true 2 % time is 1.488 s, not 0.2 s. An over-damped system is slow, not fast; the rule of thumb comes from the envelope of the oscillation, which no longer exists there. R2D1

The DC motor: two first-order parts

The pretend brushed DC motor of F1-69 has winding resistance R = 2 Ω, inductance L = 1 mH, motor constant K = 0.01 (V·s/rad, equal to N·m/A), rotor inertia J = 10−5 kg·m² and viscous friction b = 10−6 N·m·s. Its electrical and mechanical laws are U2B1

L·di/dt = V − R·i − K·ω J·dω/dt = K·i − b·ω

Each is first order in its own variable, and they are coupled: the current makes torque, and the speed makes a back-EMF that opposes the current. Together they are second order. The electrical time scale is L/R = 0.5 ms; the mechanical one, after eliminating i with L neglected, is τm = J·R/(K² + R·b) = 0.1961 s, about 400 times slower. Over the mechanical time scale the current settles almost instantly, so the speed behaves like a first-order system with Kdc = K/(K² + R·b) = 98.04 rad/s per volt and τm. D1R1

Listing 1 checks this. For a 6 V step, the full model and the first-order formula agree within 0.5 rad/s at every printed time; both reach 63.2 % at t = τm and the final speed 588.24 rad/s (5617 rpm). The current starts near V/R = 3 A while the motor is still, and falls as back-EMF builds: 1.850 A at τm/2, 0.078 A at 5τm. In damping-ratio terms this motor's speed is strongly over-damped: ωn = √((K² + Rb)/(LJ)) ≈ 101 rad/s and ζ ≈ 9.9. R1R4

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Where the formulas come from

Try y = est in the free equation y″ + 2ζωny′ + ωn²y = 0. It works when s² + 2ζωns + ωn² = 0, the characteristic equation, with roots s = −ζωn ± ωn√(ζ² − 1). For ζ > 1 both roots are real and negative: two decaying exponentials, no oscillation. For ζ = 1 they coincide (critical damping). For ζ < 1 they are complex, −ζωn ± jωd, and e(−ζωn ± jωd)t combine into e−ζωnt times sines and cosines of ωdt. With the step input and zero initial conditions the response is B1B2

y(t) = 1 − e^(−ζω_n t)·(cos ω_d t + (ζ/√(1 − ζ²))·sin ω_d t)

Setting y′ = 0 gives the first peak at ωdt = π, and substituting gives Mp. The settling estimate takes the envelope e−ζωnt = 0.02, so t = ln 50/(ζωn) ≈ 3.9/(ζωn), rounded to 4. These roots are the poles of F0-71: their real part sets the decay, their imaginary part the oscillation. D1

Dominant poles and model reduction

The motor's characteristic equation is LJ·s² + (RJ + Lb)·s + (Rb + K²) = 0, with roots −5.11 s−1 and −1995 s−1 for the pretend values (time constants 0.196 s and 0.50 ms; run R4). The slow root dominates everything after the first few milliseconds; dropping the fast one is the first-order approximation. The rule: a pole can be neglected when it is much faster (here 400 times) than the dynamics you care about and than the controller's bandwidth. A current controller running at kilohertz rates cares about the fast pole; a speed controller at tens of hertz does not. D1R4B1

Identification from data

From a measured step response you can estimate the parameters: K from the final value, τ from the 63 % time (first order); ζ from the overshoot (ζ = −ln Mp/√(π² + ln² Mp)) and ωn from the peak time (second order). With noise or coarse sampling, two independent estimates are better than one: the logarithmic decrement between two successive peaks gives ζ again, and the time between them gives the period 2π/ωd. Real systems are rarely exactly second order (delays, friction, saturation, higher-order modes), so an identified model is a fit valid for the signal sizes and frequencies you tested, and its residual error should be stated. B1D1

How the hardware actually does it

In a real motor the "first-order" speed response is the result of physics on two time scales: the winding's inductance delays the current by fractions of a millisecond, the rotor's inertia delays the speed by a large fraction of a second, and the back-EMF, a voltage the spinning rotor generates, closes the loop between them (F1-69). The large starting current (V/R at stall) is why motor drivers have current limits and why HW302's F1-70 insists on them; in this pretend motor it is 3 A for a 6 V step, a number that comes from the model, not from any real product. A logged step response from a real motor would also show things this model omits: dry friction that needs a minimum voltage to start (F1-69's forensic lab), a driver that applies PWM rather than a smooth voltage (F1-70), encoder quantisation (F0-66) and gear backlash. Every motor parameter here is a pretend value; a real motor's R, L, K, J and b come from its datasheet and from measurements, and nothing was run on a real motor in this build. U2R1

Safety

This chapter's labs are simulations. If your teacher adds a real motor step test (RB202 F9-22 does, under supervision): clamp the motor, keep fingers, hair and loose clothing away from the shaft and anything attached to it, use a current-limited bench supply or a driver with a current limit, start at a low voltage, and have a switch within reach to cut power. Stall currents can heat windings quickly. Follow the motor's and driver's datasheet limits, never numbers from this chapter.

Worked example

Task A (first order). For the pretend motor, compute τm, Kdc, the final speed for 6 V, and the time to reach 95 %.

  1. K² + R·b = 10−4 + 2×10−6 = 1.02×10−4.
  2. τm = J·R/(K² + R·b) = 2×10−5/1.02×10−4 = 0.1961 s.
  3. Kdc = K/(K² + R·b) = 0.01/1.02×10−4 = 98.04 rad/s per V; final speed 6 × 98.04 = 588.2 rad/s = 588.2 × 60/(2π) = 5617 rpm.
  4. 95 % at 3τm = 0.588 s; Listing 1 prints 95.0 % at that time.

Task B (second order). A kitchen-scale model has ωn = 10 rad/s and ζ = 0.2. Predict overshoot, peak time and 2 % settling time.

  1. √(1 − ζ²) = √0.96 = 0.9798; ωd = 9.798 rad/s.
  2. Mp = exp(−π × 0.2/0.9798) = exp(−0.6413) = 0.527: 52.7 %.
  3. tp = π/9.798 = 0.321 s.
  4. ts ≈ 4/(0.2 × 10) = 2.0 s; the simulation measures 1.960 s (run R2).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A real kettle is not exactly first order: the water, the element and the metal body each store heat, and the thermometer has its own delay. The first-order model is an approximation, like the motor's, valid when one store is much slower than the others. B1
    • 

  • A spring scale's needle also has dry friction in its pivot, so it can stop slightly off the true weight; the linear second-order model would always settle exactly. Small, stuck errors near the final value are a sign of dry friction, not of a wrong ζ.
    • 

  • Kettles and scales have no controller. Most second-order behaviour in robots appears only when a feedback loop is closed around a first-order part (a motor under position control, F0-70): the "spring" is the controller's gain, not a piece of metal.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F0-67, one first-order ODE (the cart) solved exactly and by simulation. What carries over: standard forms, time constants, equilibrium plus decaying gap, simulation with small steps compared against exact solutions. D1 What changes: two coupled stores of energy can trade energy back and forth, so responses can overshoot and ring; the shape is set by ζ and the speed by ωn; real devices like the motor have several time scales, and choosing which to keep is a modelling decision. Expected observations


With L = 1 mH the log gives Kdc ≈ 98.0 rad/s per V and τ ≈ 0.196 s, matching the formulas to the log's resolution (run R1 shows 63.2 % at t = τm). With larger L the electrical time constant L/R grows (5 ms at 10 mH, 25 ms at 50 mH), the start of the curve becomes S-shaped instead of rising with its steepest slope at t = 0, and ζ falls (3.13 and 1.40, still over-damped). The 63 % time hardly moves: 0.1962 s and 0.1994 s against τm = 0.1961 s (check run R4). The first-order model stays good for the speed long after it has become wrong about the first few milliseconds, which is what "dominant pole" means. ζ = 0.3 gives 37.2 % overshoot at 0.329 s.

Your identified K and τ for L = 1 mH are within 1 % of the formulas; your function returns the same values for a synthetic first-order log you build from the formula; your ζ = 0.3 row matches the formulas to three digits.
