Story hook

Analogy

Mei from F0-65 now wants to know how fast the bath is filling. She tapes a ruler to the side and reads the level every two seconds. The water surface ripples, and the ruler only has centimetre marks, so her readings jump: 12, 12, 13, 12, 14, 14 cm. Divide one jump by two seconds and the "speed of filling" swings from zero to a whole centimetre per second and back.

Her brother Kofi suggests two tricks. Compare readings twenty seconds apart instead of two, so a single wobble matters less. Or keep a running guess that moves only a little towards each new reading, so it ignores most of the jitter. Both tricks give calmer numbers. Both are also a little late: if Mei suddenly opens the tap, the calm number notices a few seconds after she does.

This is exactly the problem of every motor controller that computes a speed from an encoder: differences magnify measurement steps and noise, filters calm them at the price of delay. This chapter makes both precise and measures them in code.

What you will be able to do

  • Write forward, backward and central differences in C++ and state and measure their error orders.
  • Explain why differentiating quantised or noisy samples amplifies errors by a factor of about 1/Δt, and choose a window that balances noise against lag.
  • Implement a first-order low-pass filter from its differential equation and compute its weight from the time constant and sample time.
  • Implement a running-sum integrator and explain how both pieces become the I and D terms of a discrete PID controller.
  • Diagnose a derivative term that produces spikes from a slowly turning encoder.
Jargon box
Sample time (Δt)
Simple words: the time between two readings. Analogy: Mei reads the ruler every two seconds. Precise: the interval between successive samples x[k] = x(kΔt) of a signal; its inverse is the sample rate. D1
Finite difference
Simple words: a derivative estimated from two or more nearby samples. Analogy: the change between two ruler readings divided by the time between them. Precise: forward (x[k+1] − x[k])/Δt, backward (x[k] − x[k−1])/Δt, central (x[k+1] − x[k−1])/(2Δt); errors of order Δt, Δt and Δt² for smooth signals. B2R1
Truncation error
Simple words: the error a formula makes even with perfect numbers, because it uses a finite step. Analogy: the difference between "speed over 2 s" and "speed right now" when the tap is being turned. Precise: the part of the error that comes from cutting off Taylor's series; for the forward difference ½·x″·Δt. B2
Quantisation (of a measurement)
Simple words: rounding a reading to the nearest step the sensor can show. Analogy: the ruler that only has centimetre marks. Precise: replacing a value x by q·round(x/q) for a step q, an error of at most q/2; for an encoder q is one count, 2π/(counts per revolution) radians. U2D1
Noise amplification (of differentiation)
Simple words: small wobbles in readings become big wobbles in the speed you compute from them. Analogy: a one-centimetre wobble over two seconds looks like a fast fill. Precise: a sample error of size ε produces an error up to 2ε/Δt in a two-point difference; the shorter Δt, the larger. D1R2
First-order low-pass filter (discrete)
Simple words: a running guess that moves part of the way towards each new reading. Analogy: Kofi's calm guess of the filling speed. Precise: y[k] = y[k−1] + α·(x[k] − y[k−1]) with α = Δt/(τ + Δt), the backward-Euler form of τ·y′ + y = x; HW302 calls the same update an exponential moving average. D1B1
Filter lag
Simple words: how late the calm value is compared with the real one. Analogy: the calm guess notices the tap opening a few seconds late. Precise: for slowly changing inputs, a first-order low-pass filter's output trails its input by about τ; a difference over a window of length W trails by about W/2. B1D1
Running sum (discrete integrator)
Simple words: a total to which you add "value × Δt" at every sample. Analogy: Kofi's list of flow readings added up. Precise: S[k] = S[k−1] + x[k]·Δt, the rectangle rule of F0-65 updated one sample at a time. D1
PID controller (discrete)
Simple words: a rule that computes a command from the error now, the sum of past errors, and how fast the error changes. Analogy: adjusting the tap from how far the bath is from full, how long it has been short, and how fast it is filling. Precise: u[k] = Kp·e[k] + Ki·S[k] + Kd·D[k], with S a running sum and D a (filtered) difference of the error or of the measurement (RB202 F9-21). B1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A computer cannot shrink the step to zero as the definition of a derivative asks. It has readings at fixed times. So it estimates a speed as "change between readings ÷ time between readings". Using the readings just before and just after the moment you care about is fairer than using two readings on one side.

Real readings wobble and come in steps. If you divide a small wobble by a tiny time, you get a huge, fake speed. Two cures work: compare readings further apart, or smooth the answer by moving it only part of the way towards each new value. Both cures make the speed a little late. Engineers choose how much calm to buy with how much delay.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Three differences and their errors

From Taylor's formula (F0-64), for a smooth x and step h: B2

  • forward: (x(t + h) − x(t))/h = x′(t) + ½x″(t)·h + …, error order h;
  • backward: (x(t) − x(t − h))/h = x′(t) − ½x″(t)·h + …, error order h, opposite sign;
  • central: (x(t + h) − x(t − h))/(2h) = x′(t) + (1/6)x‴(t)·h² + …, error order h², because the h terms cancel.

Listing 1 checks this for sin at t = 1: as h goes 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, the forward error goes −4.29×10−2, −4.22×10−3, −4.21×10−4 (÷10 each time) and the central error −9.00×10−4, −9.01×10−6, −9.01×10−8 (÷100). The predicted constants agree: ½·x″(1) = −½ sin 1 ≈ −0.421 and (1/6)·x‴(1) = −(1/6) cos 1 ≈ −0.0900. R1R5

There is a catch for controllers. The central difference at time k needs x[k + 1], a sample from the future. A real-time loop only has the backward difference, or a central difference evaluated one sample late, which is the same as accepting a delay of one sample. D1

Differences amplify measurement errors

Suppose each sample carries an error of at most ε. The backward difference is then off by up to 2ε/Δt from the difference of the true values. Halving Δt halves the truncation error but doubles this measurement error. For an encoder, ε is half a count; a change of one count between two samples looks like a speed of q/Δt, however slowly the shaft really turns. D1

Listing 2 simulates a shaft turning as θ(t) = sin(πt) rad, read by a pretend encoder with 4096 counts per revolution (q = 2π/4096 ≈ 0.001534 rad) at 1000 samples per second. One count per sample therefore reads as 1.534 rad/s. The true speed at t = 2.25 s is 2.22 rad/s, between one and two counts per sample, so the raw difference alternates between 1.534 and 3.068 rad/s. Over 1–4 s its RMS error is 0.572 rad/s, and its largest error 1.48 rad/s, about one count per sample. R2

Two cures: a longer window, or a filter

A longer window. (x[k] − x[k − W])/(W·Δt) divides the same count error by W·Δt instead of Δt. With W = 20 the count error becomes 0.077 rad/s; Listing 2 measures an RMS error of 0.076 rad/s, about 7.5 times smaller than raw. The price: the estimate is the average speed over the last 20 ms, which describes the moment 10 ms ago. R2D1

A low-pass filter. Take the first-order system of F0-68, τ·y′ + y = x, whose output follows its input smoothly with time constant τ. Replace y′ by the backward difference (y[k] − y[k−1])/Δt and solve for y[k]: D1

y[k] = y[k−1] + α·(x[k] − y[k−1]), α = Δt / (τ + Δt)

Each new sample moves the output a fraction α of the way towards it. With τ = 10 ms and Δt = 1 ms, α = 1/11 ≈ 0.0909. Feeding the raw difference through this filter gives an RMS error of 0.083 rad/s, close to the 20-sample window, with one multiply and two adds per sample and no stored history. R2R3

The running sum

The integral of F0-65, done one sample at a time, is S[k] = S[k−1] + e[k]·Δt. Listing 3 feeds an error that is 1 for half a second and then 0: the sum climbs to 0.500 and then stays there, remembering the past error; the low-pass output with τ = 0.1 s rises towards 1 (0.650 after 0.1 s, close to the 63 % of one time constant; the small difference comes from the discrete update) and then decays back towards 0. The sum never forgets; the filter does. R3

Putting them together: a discrete PID

A discrete PID controller computes, at each sample, u[k] = Kp·e[k] + Ki·S[k] + Kd·D[k], where e = setpoint − measurement, S is the running sum of e, and D is a filtered difference. Two refinements are standard and come from this chapter's analysis: differentiate the measurement instead of the error, so that a step in the setpoint (which has no derivative, F0-64) does not produce a spike; and filter D, so that encoder counts do not produce spikes either. RB202 (F9-19, F9-21) builds this controller and adds output limits and anti-windup. B1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Choosing the window: noise against lag

For a window of W samples, the estimate's error has two parts: a measurement part of size about q/(W·Δt), shrinking with W, and a lag part of size about |x″|·W·Δt/2 (the estimate describes the middle of the window), growing with W. Their sum is smallest when the two are comparable, W·Δt ≈ √(2q/|x″|). The same U-shape appeared in F0-64 with rounding instead of quantisation. There is no universally right window: a position loop that must react within milliseconds tolerates more noise than a display that only needs a steady number. D1R2

The filter in the frequency view

F0-72 shows that the first-order low-pass filter passes slow changes almost unchanged and weakens fast ones, with a corner at the angular frequency 1/τ (frequency 1/(2πτ), about 15.9 Hz for τ = 10 ms). Quantisation noise from an encoder sampled at 1 kHz has much of its power at higher frequencies than the motion, which is why the filter removes it well. The price is phase lag: for slowly varying signals the output trails by about τ, so a derivative filtered with τ = 10 ms describes the speed of about 10 ms ago. B1U2

Other discretisations

The backward-Euler update is one of several ways to turn τ·y′ + y = x into a recursion. Forward Euler gives α = Δt/τ, which overshoots and becomes unstable for Δt > 2τ; the exact solution for an input held constant over each sample gives α = 1 − e−Δt/τ; the Tustin (trapezoidal) method keeps frequency behaviour closest at a given sample rate. For Δt ≪ τ all agree to first order: with Δt = 1 ms and τ = 10 ms they give α = 0.1, 0.0952 and 0.0909 for forward Euler, the exact hold and backward Euler. MA302 F0-79 ("Discretising a controller") compares them properly. D1R5B1

Better speed from encoders

Counting edges in a fixed time window (as in Listing 2) works well at high speed and badly at low speed, where few counts arrive per window. The alternative is to measure the time between edges with a hardware timer and compute speed as q / (time per count), which is accurate at low speed and noisy at high speed; practical drivers switch between or combine the two. Which method a particular encoder interface supports is a datasheet question for the chosen part. U2

How the hardware actually does it

On a microcontroller, a hardware timer (HW204) raises an interrupt or wakes a task at each sample; the encoder interface (F1-66) keeps a running count of quadrature edges in a counter register; the control code reads the count, subtracts the previous count, and so computes a backward difference in counts per sample. Two hardware details bite. The counter has a fixed width and wraps around, so the subtraction must be done in the counter's own unsigned width (MA102 F0-24) to give the right difference across a wrap. And the real time between two reads is the timer period plus any jitter in when the code ran: if the code assumes Δt but the reads happened at Δt ± jitter, the speed has an error proportional to the jitter. Floating-point filters cost a few multiplies per sample on a processor with a floating-point unit; on one without, fixed-point versions of the same recursion are used. None of this was run on hardware in this build; the encoder in Listing 2 is simulated. U2R2

Worked example

Task. A motor shaft is read by an encoder with 4096 counts per revolution every 1 ms. (a) What speed does a change of one count per sample represent? (b) A display needs speed with error below 0.1 rad/s from quantisation alone: how long a window is needed? (c) Instead, design a low-pass filter for the raw difference with τ = 10 ms: compute α. (d) After 0.1 s with constant input 1, where is the filter output?

  1. (a) q = 2π/4096 = 0.0015340 rad. One count per 0.001 s is 1.5340 rad/s.
  2. (b) One-count error over W samples: 1.5340/W < 0.1 requires W > 15.34, so W = 16 samples, a 16 ms window, describing the speed of about 8 ms ago.
  3. (c) α = Δt/(τ + Δt) = 0.001/0.011 = 0.090909.
  4. (d) Starting from 0, after k samples y = 1 − (1 − α)k. With k = 100: 1 − (0.909091)100 = 1 − 7.3×10−5 ≈ 0.99993. After one time constant (k = 10) it is 1 − 0.90909110 = 0.6145, close to the continuous 1 − e−1 = 0.6321.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Mei's ruler wobbles randomly; an encoder's error is deterministic rounding, which repeats in a pattern (Listing 2's alternating 1 and 2 counts). Patterned errors can line up with the motion and fool filters that assume random noise. R2
    • 

  • Kofi can read the ruler again whenever he likes. A real-time controller cannot look into the future, so the central difference that Mei could compute afterwards is not available to it without accepting a delay.
    • 

  • In the bath, a late speed estimate only means a late tap adjustment. In a feedback loop, the lag of a filter is part of the loop and can make it oscillate (F0-71): filtering is not free.
    •

Summary


    

  • Forward and backward differences have error ∝ Δt; the central difference ∝ Δt², but it needs a future sample.
    • 

  • Differences magnify measurement errors by about 1/Δt; one encoder count per sample looks like a speed of q/Δt.
    • 

  • A longer window or a low-pass filter reduces the noise; both add a delay (about W/2 or τ).
    • 

  • The first-order low-pass filter is y += α(x − y) with α = Δt/(τ + Δt), derived from τy′ + y = x.
    • 

  • The running sum S += e·Δt is the discrete integral and the I term's memory.
    • 

  • A discrete PID combines them; differentiate the measurement and filter D.
    • 

  • Choose filter constants from requirements on noise and lag, measured, not guessed.

Forensic lab answer key


    

  1. The D term is either 0.000 V or 0.077 V. The count increases by one every 5 samples (ms 24, 29, 34, 39), so the spike appears every 5 ms, about 200 times per second.
    2. 

  2. Average: 4 spikes of 0.077 in 20 samples (ms 21–40) = 0.0153 V. Expected: Kd × 0.3 = 0.015 V. The average is right; the D term is a train of spikes with the right mean.
    3. 

  3. One count is 2π/4096 = 0.001534 rad; over 1 ms that is 1.534 rad/s; times Kd = 0.05 gives 0.0767 V. The shaft turns 0.3 rad/s = 195.6 counts/s, one count about every 5.1 ms.
    4. 

  4. Mechanical hypothesis (a loose gear or bearing): would produce clicking with the D term off too, and would not make the D term itself spiky; ruled out by "smooth when D is off". Quantisation hypothesis: a one-sample difference of whole counts gives exactly 0 or one count per sample, at the counting rate, about 200 Hz, an audible buzz when applied to the motor; fits all evidence.
    5. 

  5. (1) Low-pass filter the D term (for example τ = 10 ms): output near 0.015 V with a small ripple. (2) Compute speed over a 20 ms window: values alternate between 3 and 4 counts per window, D ≈ 0.0115–0.0153 V, much smaller steps. (3) Measure time between encoder edges for low speeds. Verify by re-running the log and checking that the spikes are gone and the mean is still 0.015 V.
    6. 



How the evidence was produced: dterm_log.cpp turns a true angle 0.3·t into whole counts of a pretend 4096-count encoder and computes Kd·(Δangle/Δt) with Δt = 1 ms and no filter (run R4). Wrong answers and why: "the encoder misses counts" — the counts rise steadily at the right average rate; "the gain is too high" — the mean D term is exactly the expected value, the problem is its spikiness.
