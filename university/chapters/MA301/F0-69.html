Story hook

Analogy

Ravi wants to make his grandmother's cake for a party of thirty. Her recipe is written in cups, spoons, "a knob of butter" and "a handful of nuts". Scaling that directly is a headache: how much is two and a half knobs? So he converts every ingredient into grams first, using a conversion table on the fridge. In grams, scaling is plain multiplication. When he is done he converts the totals back into cups and spoons for his grandmother, who prefers them.

Engineers do the same with differential equations. Solving them directly involves derivatives and integrals. The Laplace transform converts every function of time into a function of a new variable s, using a table, and in that world differentiating becomes multiplying by s. The differential equation turns into ordinary algebra. Solve the algebra, then convert back with the same table. This chapter teaches the conversion, the table, and the one trap where the shortcut lies to you.

What you will be able to do

  • Compute the Laplace transform of basic functions from its definition and check table entries numerically.
  • Use linearity and the derivative rule to turn a linear ODE with initial conditions into an algebraic equation in s.
  • Solve first- and second-order ODEs with step inputs by partial fractions and the table, and check the result by simulation.
  • Apply the final and initial value theorems, and state when the final value theorem is not allowed.
  • Diagnose a design note that predicted a final value for a system that does not settle.
Jargon box
Laplace transform
Simple words: a fixed recipe that turns a function of time into a function of a new variable s. Analogy: converting cups into grams with the fridge table. Precise: F(s) = ℒ{f}(s) = ∫₀∞ f(t)·e−st dt, defined for the values of s where the integral converges. B1B2
Complex frequency (s)
Simple words: the variable of the transformed world; it can be a real or a complex number. Analogy: the unit "grams" in Ravi's conversion. Precise: s = σ + jω with unit 1/s; e−st is a decaying or growing oscillation, so F(s) measures how much f contains of each such pattern. B1
Transform pair (table)
Simple words: a function of time and its transform, listed together. Analogy: one line of the conversion table: "1 cup of flour = 120 g". Precise: f(t) ↔ F(s), for example 1 ↔ 1/s, e−at ↔ 1/(s + a); the inverse transform of F is the f it came from (unique for the functions used in this course). B1R1
Derivative rule
Simple words: differentiating in time becomes multiplying by s, minus the starting value. Analogy: a step that is fiddly in cups but a single multiplication in grams. Precise: ℒ{f′} = sF(s) − f(0); ℒ{f″} = s²F(s) − s·f(0) − f′(0); and ℒ{∫₀t f} = F(s)/s. B1D1
Partial fractions
Simple words: splitting one complicated fraction into a sum of simple ones that are in the table. Analogy: splitting a big shopping total back into the items that made it. Precise: writing a proper rational function N(s)/D(s) with distinct roots pk of D as Σ Ak/(s − pk), with Ak = [(s − pk)·N/D] evaluated at s = pk (the cover-up rule). B2
Pole (of a transform)
Simple words: a value of s where the transform's formula blows up. Analogy: a line of the table that marks a strong ingredient: it decides the taste of the whole dish. Precise: a root of the denominator of a rational F(s); each pole p contributes a term ept to f(t). F0-71 builds stability on this. B1
Final value theorem
Simple words: a shortcut that gives where a signal ends up, without solving for all of it. Analogy: reading the final weight off the converted totals without converting every step back. Precise: limt→∞ f(t) = lims→0 s·F(s), valid only if f(t) has a limit, which is guaranteed when every pole of s·F(s) has a negative real part. B1R3
Initial value theorem
Simple words: a shortcut for where a signal starts just after t = 0. Analogy: the first ingredient on the converted list. Precise: f(0⁺) = lims→∞ s·F(s), when the limit exists. B1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the round trip: the hard road solves the ODE in time directly; the Laplace road transforms it, solves algebra, splits the answer into table entries, and transforms back. Figure 2 previews what the table means: each pole position in the s-plane corresponds to one kind of behaviour in time, from steady decay to growth to oscillation.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Some problems are hard in one language and easy in another. Multiplying "2 cups" by 15 is awkward when one cup of flour and one cup of sugar weigh different amounts. In grams, everything is just numbers you can add and multiply.

The Laplace transform is a translation for curves that change over time. You look up each curve in a table, like converting cups to grams. In the new language, the hard operation, "how fast is it changing", becomes simple multiplication. You solve the easy version, then translate the answer back with the same table. The translation always works the same way, so you can trust it, as long as you obey its rules.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The definition and the first entries

For a function f(t) defined for t ≥ 0, B1

F(s) = ∫₀^∞ f(t)·e^(−st) dt

This is an integral to infinity (F0-65); it exists when f grows more slowly than est decays. Two entries can be done by hand. For the unit step f(t) = 1: ∫₀∞ e−st dt = [−e−st/s]₀∞ = 1/s for s > 0. For f(t) = e−at: ∫₀∞ e−(s+a)t dt = 1/(s + a) for s > −a. D1

Linearity and the derivative rule

The transform is linear: ℒ{a·f + b·g} = a·F + b·G, because the integral is. The key rule comes from integrating by parts: ℒ{f′} = ∫₀∞ f′e−st dt = [f·e−st]₀∞ + s∫₀∞ f·e−st dt = −f(0) + sF(s). Apply it twice for the second derivative: ℒ{f″} = s²F − s·f(0) − f′(0). The initial conditions enter automatically; you cannot forget them. D1B1

Solving an ODE in four steps

(a) First order: y′ + 2y = 4, y(0) = 1 (a step input of size 4 switched on at t = 0).

  1. Transform: (sY − 1) + 2Y = 4/s.
  2. Solve the algebra: (s + 2)Y = 1 + 4/s = (s + 4)/s, so Y = (s + 4)/(s(s + 2)).
  3. Partial fractions: Y = A/s + B/(s + 2). Cover-up: A = (s + 4)/(s + 2) at s = 0, so A = 2; B = (s + 4)/s at s = −2, so B = −1.
  4. Back with the table: y(t) = 2 − e−2t.

(b) Second order: y″ + 3y′ + 2y = 2, y(0) = y′(0) = 0. Transform: (s² + 3s + 2)Y = 2/s, so Y = 2/(s(s + 1)(s + 2)). Cover-up: A = 2/((1)(2)) = 1 at s = 0; B = 2/((−1)(1)) = −2 at s = −1; C = 2/((−2)(−1)) = 1 at s = −2. So y(t) = 1 − 2e−t + e−2t. D1

Listing 2 simulates both equations with 10 µs Euler steps and compares with these formulas: the largest differences over 5 s are 3.7×10−6 and 2.0×10−6. Notice that the characteristic polynomial of (b), s² + 3s + 2 = (s + 1)(s + 2), appears as the denominator; its roots −1 and −2 became the exponents e−t and e−2t. That is the pattern of Figure 2. R2

Final and initial values without solving

Often you only need where the output ends up. The final value theorem gives it as lims→0 s·Y(s): for (a), s·(s + 4)/(s(s + 2)) → 4/2 = 2; for (b), 2/((s + 1)(s + 2)) → 1. Both match the formulas. The initial value theorem gives y(0⁺) = lims→∞ s·Y(s): for (a), (s + 4)/(s + 2) → 1, the initial condition. B1R2

Watch out

The final value theorem is only allowed when y(t) actually has a limit, which is guaranteed if every pole of s·Y(s) has a negative real part. If a pole is at zero (after multiplying by s) or in the right half-plane, or if there are poles on the imaginary axis, the formula still returns a number, and that number is meaningless. The forensic lab is a real example. B1R3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Why complex s, and where the sine row comes from

With Euler's formula ejωt = cos ωt + j·sin ωt and the exponential row with a = −jω, ℒ{ejωt} = 1/(s − jω) = (s + jω)/(s² + ω²). Its real part is the cosine row, its imaginary part the sine row. So oscillations are exponentials with imaginary exponents, and their poles sit on the imaginary axis; damped oscillations e−atsin ωt have poles at −a ± jω. In general a pole p = σ + jω contributes eσt(oscillating at ω): σ < 0 decays, σ > 0 grows. This one sentence is the heart of F0-71. B1D1

Existence and the region of convergence

The defining integral converges for Re(s) larger than the growth rate of f: for e2t, only for Re(s) > 2. Functions that grow faster than any exponential, such as et², have no Laplace transform. In engineering the formulas from the table are used everywhere in the complex plane by analytic continuation; the region of convergence matters for proofs and for the final value theorem, not for day-to-day algebra. B1B2

Three more rules you will meet

  • Time delay: a signal delayed by T, f(t − T) (zero before T), has transform e−sTF(s). Delays are not rational functions of s, which is why they are awkward in control (F0-71). B1
  • Repeated poles: a factor (s − p)² gives terms t·ept; the cover-up rule must be extended (differentiate, or match coefficients). Critical damping (ζ = 1 in F0-68) is this case. B2
  • Convolution: the transform of the response of a linear system at rest to any input u is G(s)·U(s), where G is the transform of the system's impulse response. That product is the transfer function of F0-70. B1

Initial slope from the transform

Applying the initial value theorem to y′ gives y′(0⁺) = lims→∞ s·(sY − y(0)). For the motor step of F0-68 in first-order form, Ω(s) = KdcV/(s(τs + 1)), this gives ω′(0⁺) = KdcV/τ, the 3000 rad/s² computed by differentiation in F0-64. Two independent routes to the same number are a good check of both. D1R4

How the hardware actually does it

No controller computes a Laplace transform while it runs. The transform is a design-time tool: engineers use it on paper or in analysis software to predict responses and choose gains, and then the controller runs as difference equations in time, as in F0-66. Sampled systems have their own transform, the z-transform, in which a delay of one sample is a factor z−1; turning an s-domain design into such a difference equation is "discretising a controller" (MA302 F0-79). A simulator, like Listing 2, works in the time domain too, and the agreement between its output and the inverse transform is the evidence that both are right. The listings ran on the build container's CPU; nothing here depends on particular hardware. B1R2

Not verified — check before relying on this

Worked example

Task. The pretend motor in first-order form is τ·ω′ + ω = Kdc·V with τ = 0.19608 s and Kdc = 98.04 rad/s per V, starting at rest. A 6 V step is applied. Find ω(t) by Laplace, its final value by the final value theorem, and its initial acceleration by the initial value theorem.

  1. Transform: τ(sΩ − 0) + Ω = Kdc·6/s.
  2. Solve: Ω(s) = 6Kdc/(s(τs + 1)) = (6Kdc/τ)/(s(s + 1/τ)).
  3. Partial fractions: with a = 1/τ = 5.100 s−1: (6Kdc/τ)·(1/a)·(1/s − 1/(s + a)) = 6Kdc·(1/s − 1/(s + a)).
  4. Table: ω(t) = 6Kdc(1 − e−t/τ) = 588.2(1 − e−t/0.19608) rad/s, the first-order curve of F0-68.
  5. Final value: s·Ω(s) = 6Kdc/(τs + 1) → 6Kdc = 588.2 rad/s as s → 0; allowed, because the only pole of s·Ω is at −5.1.
  6. Initial acceleration: s·(sΩ − 0) = 6Kdc·s/(τs + 1) → 6Kdc/τ = 3000 rad/s² as s → ∞.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Converting cups to grams changes units but keeps "one ingredient ↔ one number". The Laplace transform turns a whole curve over all time into a whole function of s: one value of F(s) depends on all of f(t), not on f at one moment.
    • 

  • Ravi's conversion works for any recipe. The Laplace transform's algebraic shortcuts work only for linear equations with constant coefficients; dry friction, saturation or a gain that changes over time break them (F0-67, Layer 3). B1
    • 

  • Converting back from grams can never give a wrong but plausible number. The final value theorem can (forensic lab): the shortcut has conditions that the kitchen version does not.

Summary


    

  • ℒ{f} = ∫₀∞ f(t)e−st dt; a short table covers the functions of this course.
    • 

  • The transform is linear, and ℒ{f′} = sF − f(0): differential equations become algebra with the initial conditions built in.
    • 

  • Solve for Y(s), split it by partial fractions, read y(t) from the table, and check by simulation.
    • 

  • Each pole p of Y(s) contributes ept; complex poles give oscillations, poles with positive real part give growth.
    • 

  • Final value: lim s·Y(s) as s → 0, only if all poles of s·Y(s) have negative real parts; initial value: lim s·Y(s) as s → ∞.
    • 

  • Delays become e−sT; linear time-invariant systems become products G(s)U(s).
