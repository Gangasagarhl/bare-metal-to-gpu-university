Story hook

Analogy

Lucía pushes the laundry trolley along the carpet with a steady, gentle push. At first it speeds up quickly. Then it speeds up more and more slowly, and soon it rolls at a speed that no longer changes, although she keeps pushing just as hard. When she lets go, it slows down and stops a short way further on.

Why does it stop speeding up? Because the carpet drags on the wheels, and the faster the trolley rolls, the harder the drag. At some speed the drag equals her push, and then nothing is left to speed it up. The rule "how fast the speed changes depends on the speed itself" is a differential equation. It tells you, at every moment, the rate of change; from that alone, and the speed you start with, the whole future of the trolley follows.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the differential equation of a cart from Newton's second law, with viscous and with dry (Coulomb) friction, and check its units.
  • Solve the linear first-order equation m·v′ = F − c·v exactly, with an initial condition, and read off the terminal speed and the time constant.
  • Simulate the cart in C++ with small time steps and compare the simulation with the analytic solution (the course lab).
  • Explain why dry friction needs special handling at zero speed, and how a naive model makes a cart creep.
  • Measure how the simulation error depends on the time step.
Jargon box
Differential equation
Simple words: a rule that says how fast something changes, in terms of what it is now. Analogy: "the trolley speeds up less the faster it already rolls". Precise: an equation relating an unknown function to its derivatives; an ordinary differential equation (ODE) has one independent variable, here time t. B2B1
Order (of a differential equation)
Simple words: the highest derivative that appears. Analogy: whether the rule talks about speed only, or also about how the speed changes. Precise: m·v′ = F − c·v is first order in v; written for position, m·x″ + c·x′ = F, it is second order in x. B2
Initial condition
Simple words: where things start. Analogy: the trolley standing still before Lucía pushes. Precise: the values of the unknown (and, for order n, its first n − 1 derivatives) at the starting time, for example v(0) = 0; with them, a well-behaved ODE has exactly one solution. B2
Viscous friction
Simple words: drag that grows in proportion to speed. Analogy: the carpet holding the wheels back harder the faster they roll. Precise: a force −c·v opposing the velocity, with coefficient c in N·s/m; a linear model, accurate for some systems over limited speed ranges. B1
Coulomb (dry) friction
Simple words: a drag of fixed size whenever something slides or rolls, whatever the speed. Analogy: the steady resistance of the trolley's stiff wheel. Precise: a force of magnitude Fc opposing the direction of motion, −Fc·sign(v) for v ≠ 0; at v = 0 the friction force is whatever is needed, up to a static limit, to keep the body at rest. B1
Terminal speed (equilibrium)
Simple words: the speed at which the push and the drag balance, so the speed stops changing. Analogy: the steady rolling speed of the trolley under a steady push. Precise: an equilibrium of v′ = f(v), a value with f(v*) = 0; for m·v′ = F − c·v it is v* = F/c. D1
Exponential approach
Simple words: closing the same fraction of the remaining gap in every equal stretch of time. Analogy: the trolley closes most of the gap to its steady speed quickly, then ever more slowly. Precise: the solution v(t) = v* + (v(0) − v*)·e−t/τ of the linear first-order ODE τ·v′ + v = v*; after τ, 63.2 % of the gap is closed, after 3τ 95.0 %, after 5τ 99.3 %. D1R4
Simulation step (Euler step)
Simple words: moving the state forward by a small time using the current rates. Analogy: "at this speed, in the next millisecond the trolley moves this far and speeds up this much". Precise: x ← x + Δt·f(x, u), the linear approximation of F0-64 applied repeatedly; its error over a fixed time span shrinks in proportion to Δt (explicit Euler method, studied in MA302 F0-76). B2R1
State (of a dynamic system)
Simple words: the few numbers that, together with the inputs, decide everything that happens next. Analogy: the trolley's position and speed right now. Precise: a set of variables whose present values and the future inputs determine the future solution; for the cart, (x, v). B1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A push makes the trolley speed up; the carpet's drag slows it down. The difference between them decides how quickly the speed changes. When the trolley is slow, the drag is small, so the push wins by a lot and the speed climbs fast. As it gets faster, the drag grows, the push wins by less, and the speed climbs more slowly. At one speed the two are equal: the speed stops changing.

That is a whole law in one sentence: "speed-up = (push − drag) ÷ mass". The computer can use it like a recipe. Start at speed zero. Work out the speed-up now. Add a tiny bit of it to the speed. Repeat a thousand times a second. You get the trolley's whole journey. Mathematics can also give a formula for the same journey, and the lab checks that both agree.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From Newton's law to an equation

Newton's second law for straight-line motion says mass times acceleration equals the sum of the forces: m·a = ΣF. With a = dv/dt (F0-64), a push F and viscous friction −c·v: B1

m·dv/dt = F − c·v, dx/dt = v

Check units: m·dv/dt is kg·m/s² = N; c·v is (N·s/m)·(m/s) = N. Both sides are forces. A unit check catches more modelling mistakes than any other single habit. Dividing by c gives the standard form D1

τ·dv/dt + v = F/c, with τ = m/c

τ has the unit of time: kg / (N·s/m) = kg·m/(N·s) = s. For the laundry-trolley numbers of this chapter, m = 2 kg, c = 0.8 N·s/m and F = 1.6 N, so τ = 2.5 s and F/c = 2 m/s. These are exercise values, not measurements of any real trolley. D1R1

Solving it

Equilibrium first. The speed stops changing when dv/dt = 0, that is when v = F/c. Call it v∞. Then the gap. Let g = v − v∞, the distance from equilibrium. Since v∞ is constant, g′ = v′, and the equation becomes τ·g′ = −g. A function whose derivative is −1/τ times itself is an exponential (F0-64, eat has derivative a·eat): g(t) = g(0)·e−t/τ. Back in v: D1B2

v(t) = v∞ + (v(0) − v∞)·e^(−t/τ)

From rest, v(0) = 0: v(t) = (F/c)(1 − e−t/τ). Integrate once more (F0-65) with x(0) = 0: x(t) = (F/c)·(t − τ(1 − e−t/τ)). Substitute both back into the equations to check: that is always allowed and always worth it. D1

Reading the solution: the remaining gap shrinks by the factor e−1 ≈ 0.368 every τ. After τ the cart has 63.2 % of its terminal speed, after 3τ 95.0 %, after 5τ 99.3 %. It never reaches v∞ exactly, but after a few τ the difference is smaller than anything you can measure. Run R1 prints v(2) = 1.1013 and v(10) = 1.9634 m/s for the trolley, and the simulation agrees to four decimals. R1R4

Solving it by simulation

The equation gives the acceleration from the current state: a = (F − c·v)/m. Over a short step Δt, the linear approximation of F0-64 gives x ← x + v·Δt and v ← v + a·Δt. Repeating this is the explicit Euler method. Listing 1 does it with Δt = 1 ms and compares every second with the formulas. It then repeats the whole run with Δt = 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001 s: the largest speed error is 1.50×10−2, 1.47×10−3 and 1.47×10−4 m/s, ten times smaller for each ten times smaller step. That is the signature of a first-order method; MA302 F0-76 introduces methods whose error falls much faster. R1B2

Dry friction

Real wheels and bearings also have a resistance that does not depend much on speed: Coulomb friction, of magnitude Fc while moving. After Lucía lets go (F = 0) with speed v₀ > 0, the equation while moving is m·v′ = −c·v − Fc. Its equilibrium would be v = −Fc/c, a negative speed, which the cart never reaches: friction cannot push it backwards. The same gap method gives B1D1

v(t) = (v₀ + F_c/c)·e^(−t/τ) − F_c/c while v > 0, stopping at t_stop = τ·ln(1 + c·v₀/F_c)

With v₀ = 1.5 m/s and Fc = 0.6 N, tstop = 2.5·ln 3 = 2.7465 s after 1.6901 m; Listing 2's simulation stops at the same time and place. With viscous friction alone, the cart would never stop, although it would travel only a finite distance, v₀·τ = 3.75 m (an integral to infinity, F0-65). R2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Existence, uniqueness and the trouble with sign(v)

For an equation v′ = f(v) with f smooth (more precisely, Lipschitz continuous), one initial value determines exactly one solution, and small changes in the initial value give small changes in the solution. Viscous friction is fine. The Coulomb term −Fc·sign(v) jumps at v = 0, and there the theorem says nothing. Physically, at v = 0 the friction force is not ±Fc but "whatever keeps the cart still", up to a static limit Fs. A simulator must therefore treat v = 0 as a separate mode (stuck until |applied force| > Fs), or use a regularised model that is smooth near zero. Plugging sign(v) into an Euler step does neither, and the forensic lab shows the result: a cart that creeps when it should stay put. B2B1R3

Linearity and superposition

m·v′ + c·v = F is linear: if v₁ solves it for force F₁ and v₂ for F₂, then v₁ + v₂ solves it for F₁ + F₂ (with the initial values added too). Consequences used in the rest of the course: the response to a push of 3.2 N is twice the response to 1.6 N; the response from a non-zero start is the free decay of the start plus the response to the force from rest. The Laplace transform of F0-69 and the transfer functions of F0-70 exist only for linear equations. Coulomb friction, saturation of a motor driver and the stick mode are not linear, and their effects must be checked by simulation. B1D1

State-space form

Collect the state as a vector s = (x, v). Then the cart is s′ = A·s + B·F with A = [[0, 1], [0, −c/m]] and B = (0, 1/m), using the matrix–vector product of MA201. This form, s′ = f(s, u), is what every simulator steps and what RB402 and DN301 use for state-space control: the motor of F0-68 adds a current to the state, a drone has twelve or more states. An n-th-order equation always becomes n first-order equations this way. B1D1

How big may the time step be?

Applying one Euler step to the free decay τ·v′ = −v gives v ← (1 − Δt/τ)·v. If Δt < τ the factor is between 0 and 1 and the simulated speed decays like the real one. If τ < Δt < 2τ the factor is negative: the simulated speed changes sign every step, which the real cart never does. If Δt > 2τ the factor's size exceeds 1 and the simulation blows up although the cart is perfectly calm. Every simulator has such a limit tied to the fastest time constant in the model; the motor of F0-68 has one of 0.5 ms, which is why its simulation uses microsecond steps. F0-71 and MA302 F0-78 return to this. D1B2

Not verified — check before relying on this

The friction coefficients in this chapter (c = 0.8 N·s/m, Fc = 0.6 N) are exercise values chosen to make the arithmetic clear; they are not measurements of any trolley, cart or robot. Real friction depends on wheels, bearings, floor and load, often has a static limit above the sliding value, and is not exactly viscous. Values for a real robot must be measured (lab extension) or taken from the datasheets of its wheels, gearbox and motor (RB201). The existence-and-uniqueness theorem and the friction models are tagged to B2 and B1, neither opened in this build.

How the hardware actually does it

Here "hardware" is the physical cart and the computer that simulates it. The cart does not compute anything: its velocity changes continuously according to the forces, at every instant. The simulation replaces the instants by steps of Δt and each step costs a few floating-point operations on the CPU, so a 1 ms step for 10 s of cart time is 10,000 steps, a tiny amount of work that completes much faster than the 10 s it represents. That difference matters later: a simulator that runs faster than real time is used for testing controllers (RB101 F9-06, RB202), while a robot's own controller runs in real time, with its Δt fixed by a hardware timer (HW204) and its forces delivered by a motor driver (F1-70). In a real robot the "push" F comes from motor torque through wheels of radius r, F = (gear ratio × motor torque × efficiency)/r, and the friction comes from the floor, bearings and gearbox; none of these was measured in this build. Worked example

Task. The trolley (m = 2 kg, c = 0.8 N·s/m) is pushed from rest with F = 1.6 N. (a) Terminal speed and time constant. (b) Speed after 2.5 s and after 7.5 s. (c) Position after 10 s. (d) How long until it is within 1 % of terminal speed? (e) It is released at 1.5 m/s on a floor that also has Fc = 0.6 N: when and where does it stop?

  1. (a) v∞ = F/c = 1.6/0.8 = 2 m/s; τ = m/c = 2/0.8 = 2.5 s.
  2. (b) v(2.5) = 2(1 − e−1) = 2 × 0.632 = 1.264 m/s; v(7.5) = 2(1 − e−3) = 1.900 m/s (95 %).
  3. (c) x(10) = 2(10 − 2.5(1 − e−4)) = 2(10 − 2.454) = 15.092 m; run R1 prints 15.0916 (exact) and 15.0915 (simulated).
  4. (d) e−t/τ = 0.01 gives t = τ·ln 100 = 2.5 × 4.605 = 11.5 s.
  5. (e) tstop = 2.5·ln(1 + 0.8 × 1.5/0.6) = 2.5·ln 3 = 2.747 s; xstop = (1.5 + 0.75)·2.5·(1 − 1/3) − 0.75 × 2.747 = 3.750 − 2.060 = 1.690 m (run R2).

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lucía's push is not really constant: she walks behind the trolley and adjusts. The model's F is a fixed number or a given function of time; anything that reacts to the cart's motion is feedback, the subject of F0-70.
    • 

  • Carpet drag is not exactly proportional to speed, and the trolley's wheels also have a stiff, constant resistance and a starting resistance higher than the rolling one. The linear model is a choice made for its solvability, not a fact about carpets. B1
    • 

  • The story says the trolley "stops speeding up" at its steady speed. Mathematically it gets closer forever and never arrives (Layer 2): the stop is a statement about measurement resolution, not about the equation.

Transition box

Where you came from: F0-64 to F0-66: derivatives, integrals and their numerical versions, applied to given functions of time. What carries over: the derivative as a rate with units; the integral with an initial value; the Euler step is the linear approximation; error orders measured by halving or dividing the step by ten. D1 What changes: the function is no longer given; only a rule for its rate of change is, and the solution must be found, exactly or by stepping; the step size can make a simulation wrong or unstable, and non-smooth models (dry friction) need special handling. R1R3 What comes next: F0-68 names the two patterns that most systems follow, first order (this cart) and second order (springs, motors with feedback), and reads their parameters from a step response.
