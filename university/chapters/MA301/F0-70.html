Story hook

Analogy

Mira steps into a shower that is too cold. She feels the water, compares it with what she wants, and turns the mixer tap a bit towards hot. The water takes a few seconds to come through the pipe, she feels again, turns again, and soon the temperature is right. Nobody taught her an equation, but she is running a control loop: sense, compare, act, repeat.

Last month a plumber fitted a new tap in the guest bathroom, back to front. Turning towards the red mark makes the water colder. Her guest, feeling cold water, turns "hotter", gets colder water, turns further, and ends up under ice-cold water with the tap hard against its stop. Same person, same habit, one sign flipped: the loop now pushes the wrong way. This chapter gives you the tools to draw such loops, to calculate what they will do, and to see from a single sign whether they will settle or run away.

What you will be able to do

  • Derive the transfer function of a linear system from its ODE, including the full DC motor model.
  • Draw a block diagram of a feedback loop with setpoint, error, controller, plant and sensor.
  • Reduce block diagrams with the series, parallel and feedback rules, by hand and in code.
  • Predict the steady-state error and speed of response of a proportional speed loop, and check the prediction by simulation.
  • Diagnose a loop with a sign error from its log, and explain why it runs away.
Jargon box
Transfer function
Simple words: a formula in s that says how a system turns its input into its output. Analogy: the rule "one quarter turn of the tap gives three degrees warmer, after a while". Precise: for a linear time-invariant system at rest, G(s) = Y(s)/U(s), the ratio of the Laplace transforms of output and input with all initial conditions zero; for an ODE it is a ratio of polynomials. B1
Block diagram
Simple words: a drawing of a system as boxes (each with a transfer function) joined by arrows that carry signals. Analogy: a sketch of the shower: tap, pipe, skin, brain, joined by arrows. Precise: a directed graph whose edges are signals and whose nodes are blocks (multiply by G(s)), summing junctions (add or subtract signals) and branch points (copy a signal). B1
Plant
Simple words: the thing being controlled. Analogy: the pipes and the water heater. Precise: the part of a control system whose output is to be regulated, including the actuator when it is not modelled separately; written G(s) or P(s). B1
Open-loop transfer function (loop gain)
Simple words: what you get going once round the loop with the loop cut open. Analogy: how much warmer the water gets per degree of "too cold" felt, if you turned the tap once and did not check again. Precise: L(s) = C(s)·G(s)·H(s), the product of all blocks round the loop; L(0) is the loop gain at DC. B1D1
Closed-loop transfer function
Simple words: how the whole loop, with feedback working, turns the setpoint into the output. Analogy: how the shower temperature follows what Mira wants. Precise: with negative feedback, T(s) = C(s)G(s)/(1 + C(s)G(s)H(s)); its denominator 1 + L(s) decides the loop's dynamics. B1R1
Steady-state error
Simple words: the difference between setpoint and output that is left once everything has settled. Analogy: the shower ending up a little cooler than wanted, because Mira only turns the tap in proportion to how cold she feels. Precise: e(∞) = limt→∞(r − y); for a step r and a stable loop, e(∞) = r/(1 + L(0)) when H = 1 (final value theorem, F0-69). D1R2
Positive feedback
Simple words: feedback that pushes the output further in the direction it is already going. Analogy: the back-to-front tap. Precise: a loop whose summing junction adds the fed-back signal (or, equivalently, whose loop gain has the wrong sign), giving T = CG/(1 − CGH); with enough gain it moves a pole into the right half-plane. B1R3
Saturation (actuator)
Simple words: the actuator cannot deliver more than its limit, however hard the controller asks. Analogy: the tap turned hard against its stop. Precise: a nonlinearity u = clamp(v, umin, umax) between controller and plant; while it is active, the loop is effectively open and the linear transfer-function analysis does not apply. B1R3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 is the picture this whole course is heading towards: the feedback loop for the motor's speed. Read it from the left. The setpoint r enters a summing junction, which subtracts the measured speed to form the error e; the controller turns the error into a voltage; the motor turns the voltage into a speed; a sensor measures the speed and sends it back. Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every machine that keeps something steady works like you in the shower. It measures what is happening, compares it with what it wants, and does something about the difference. Engineers draw this as boxes and arrows: a box for "the tap and pipes", a box for "your hand deciding how far to turn", and an arrow that goes back from the water to your skin. That arrow going back is feedback.

Each box gets a little formula that says what it does to the signal going through. Put the formulas together with three simple rules and you get one formula for the whole loop. From that formula you can tell, before building anything, how close to the target the loop will get, how fast it gets there, and whether it might go wild like the back-to-front tap.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From ODE to transfer function

Take a linear ODE with constant coefficients, start it at rest (all initial conditions zero) and transform it (F0-69). Every derivative becomes a factor s, so for a2y″ + a1y′ + a0y = b0u you get (a2s² + a1s + a0)Y = b0U, and

G(s) = Y(s)/U(s) = b0 / (a2 s² + a1 s + a0)

The denominator is the characteristic polynomial of F0-68; its roots are the poles. G(0) is the static gain: for a constant input, the output settles at G(0) times the input (if it settles). B1D1

The pretend motor. Transforming the two motor equations of F0-68 (with zero initial current and speed): (Ls + R)I = V − KΩ and (Js + b)Ω = KI. Eliminate I: (Js + b)(Ls + R)Ω = K(V − KΩ), so

G(s) = Ω(s)/V(s) = K / ((Ls + R)(Js + b) + K²) = 0.01 / (1e-8 s² + 2.0001e-5 s + 1.02e-4)

Its static gain is 0.01/1.02×10−4 = 98.04 rad/s per V, the Kdc of F0-68. Dropping L (the electrical part is 400 times faster) leaves the first-order model G(s) ≈ Kdc/(τms + 1) = 98.04/(0.1961 s + 1). D1R4U2

Three rules for block diagrams

  • Series: a signal through G1 and then G2 is multiplied by both: G1G2. (Speed through 1/s becomes angle: angle/V = G(s)/s.)
  • Parallel: two blocks fed by the same signal and summed give G1 + G2.
  • Negative feedback: with G forward and H in the return path subtracted at the junction: E = R − HY and Y = GE, so Y = G(R − HY), Y(1 + GH) = GR, and Y/R = G/(1 + GH).

Each rule is two lines of algebra, which is exactly why the s-domain is used: in the time domain, "series" would mean solving one ODE and feeding its solution into another. Listing 1 implements the three rules with polynomials and reproduces the hand results. B1D1R1

The proportional speed loop

Put a proportional controller C = Kp (volts per rad/s of error) in front of the first-order motor, with an ideal sensor H = 1. The loop gain is L = KpKdc/(τms + 1), and

T(s) = Kp·Kdc / (τm s + 1 + Kp·Kdc)

Divide top and bottom by 1 + KpKdc: the loop is again first order, with

  • static gain T(0) = KpKdc/(1 + KpKdc): always below 1, so a steady-state error of r/(1 + KpKdc) remains;
  • time constant τm/(1 + KpKdc): feedback makes the motor faster by the factor 1 + KpKdc.

For Kp = 0.05: KpKdc = 4.902, T(0) = 0.8306, so a 300 rad/s setpoint gives 249.2 rad/s with an error of 50.8 rad/s, and the time constant falls from 196 ms to 33.2 ms. Listing 2 simulates the full motor model (with L) under this controller and gets the same final speed and time constant for all three gains in the table. D1R2R4

Watch out

The prediction assumes the driver can deliver any voltage. At the first instant the error is the whole 300 rad/s, so the controller asks for Kp × 300 V: 3 V, 15 V and 60 V for the three gains. Listing 2 has no limit, so it delivers them. A real driver stops at its supply (12 V in F0-71's examples), the loop saturates, and the response is slower than the formula says until the error has shrunk. Always compare the largest command with the actuator's limit before trusting a linear prediction. R2U2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Not one transfer function but several

A real loop has more than one input: the setpoint r, a load disturbance d (a hand braking the shaft) and sensor noise n. With C, G and H = 1, the output is

Y = T·R + S·G·D − T·N, with S = 1/(1 + CG), T = CG/(1 + CG)

S is the sensitivity function and T the complementary sensitivity; S + T = 1. High loop gain makes S small (disturbances rejected, setpoint followed) but leaves T close to 1, so sensor noise passes straight to the output: feedback cannot fix a bad sensor. All of them share the denominator 1 + CG, which is why one set of poles decides the stability of the whole loop (F0-71). B1D1

Why P control leaves an error, and what removes it

The error that is left equals r·S(0) = r/(1 + KpKdc). Reducing it to 2 % needs KpKdc = 49, so Kp ≈ 0.50. F0-71 shows that once the driver and sensor lags are included, this gain makes the loop unstable. The other way is to put an integrator 1/s in the controller: then the loop gain is infinite at s = 0, S(0) = 0, and the steady-state error to a step is zero. That is the I in PID, taught with its practical problems in RB202 F9-18. B1R4U2

The sign of feedback

If the junction adds instead of subtracts, or the error is computed as y − r instead of r − y, the closed loop becomes T = CG/(1 − CG). For the speed loop the denominator becomes τms + 1 − KpKdc, with a pole at s = (KpKdc − 1)/τm. For KpKdc > 1 that pole is positive: the response contains e+st and runs away until something saturates. For Kp = 0.05 the pole is at +19.9 s−1, so a small deviation doubles every 35 ms. D1R4

Where block-diagram algebra stops

The rules require every block to be linear and time-invariant. Saturation, dead zones, dry friction (F0-67) and sampling with a changing period break them. The usual practice is to design with the linear model, check that commands stay within limits for the expected inputs, and confirm with a simulation that includes the nonlinearities, as Listing 2 does for the motor's inductance and as the forensic lab does for the 12 V clamp. Sampled controllers add a delay of up to one period, which is not a rational function of s (F0-69): its effect on stability is the subject of F0-71 and F0-72. B1

How the hardware actually does it

In a real speed loop the boxes of Figure 1 are physical parts. The controller is a few lines of code in a microcontroller's timer interrupt; the "voltage" is a PWM duty cycle given to an H-bridge driver (F1-70), which can only deliver between minus and plus its supply; the sensor is an encoder whose counts are turned into speed by a difference (F0-66), with noise and a delay of about one sample. Each of those adds a block or a nonlinearity to the diagram. A transfer function only exists for the linear parts; the rest is handled by limits in code (clamping, anti-windup, RB202 F9-21) and by simulation. The numbers in this chapter come from a simulated pretend motor, not from a measured one. U2B1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware. No real motor, driver or encoder was used in this build. The predictions match the simulation of the pretend motor (runs R2, R3); whether a real motor's speed loop behaves the same depends on its measured parameters, driver limits, encoder resolution and sample rate. RB202 F9-22 is the supervised lab where this is tried on real hardware.

Worked example

Task. For the P speed loop on the first-order pretend motor, (a) choose Kp so the closed loop's time constant is 10 ms; (b) find the steady-state speed for a 300 rad/s setpoint; (c) find the first voltage commanded; (d) find the transfer function from setpoint to motor angle.

  1. (a) τm/(1 + KpKdc) = 0.010 → 1 + KpKdc = 19.61 → Kp = 18.61/98.04 = 0.190 V per rad/s.
  2. (b) T(0) = 18.61/19.61 = 0.949, so 284.7 rad/s, an error of 15.3 rad/s (5.1 %).
  3. (c) 0.190 × 300 = 57 V. With a 12 V driver this loop saturates for most of its rise; the 10 ms is only reached for small setpoint changes (about 12/0.190 = 63 rad/s of error or less).
  4. (d) Series with 1/s: θ/R = T(s)/s = KpKdc/(s(τms + 1 + KpKdc)). The pole at 0 means the angle keeps growing (the motor keeps turning): no final value for the angle, correctly.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Mira's brain is clever: when the tap is back to front she may notice after a few turns and reverse her habit. A controller does exactly what its code says, forever, and runs into the end stop (forensic lab).
    • 

  • A shower has a long pipe delay, and people react by turning slowly. The motor loop in this chapter has no delay at all, which is why any gain is stable here. Add delays and the story changes (F0-71).
    • 

  • Temperature is limited by the hot water supply, a nonlinearity like the 12 V clamp; transfer functions describe neither. B1

Summary


    

  • A transfer function G(s) = Y/U is the Laplace transform of an ODE with zero initial conditions; its denominator holds the poles and G(0) is the static gain.
    • 

  • The pretend motor is K/((Ls + R)(Js + b) + K²) ≈ 98.04/(0.1961 s + 1).
    • 

  • Series multiplies, parallel adds, negative feedback gives G/(1 + GH).
    • 

  • A P speed loop is again first order: faster by 1 + KpKdc, with a steady-state error r/(1 + KpKdc).
    • 

  • Large first commands saturate real drivers; the linear prediction then fails until the error is small.
    • 

  • A sign error makes feedback positive: a pole moves to +(KpKdc − 1)/τm and the loop runs to its limit.
