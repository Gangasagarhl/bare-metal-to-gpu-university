Story hook

Analogy

Rosa is the expediter tonight. Her job is to ring the pass bell at a steady beat. At every ring, each cook hands the dish in front of them to the next station and starts on the one they have received.

The owner wants more dishes per hour, so he tells Rosa to ring faster. For a while it works. Then the soup station starts passing on pots that are still cold. The slowest job in the kitchen, chopping and simmering the soup, cannot finish between two rings any more. Nobody did anything wrong at the soup station. The bell came too soon.

A digital circuit has exactly this bell: the clock. This chapter measures it, shows how to work out how fast it may ring, and lets you watch, in a real simulation, what happens when it rings too fast.

What you will be able to do

  • Convert between clock period and clock frequency, with units.
  • Compute the minimum clock period of a path from clock-to-Q delay, logic delay and setup time, and say whether a given clock works.
  • Make slower rhythms from one clock with a counter, and explain why a clock enable is usually better than a new clock.
  • Diagnose a counter that fails because its clock is too fast for its logic (the forensic lab).
Jargon box
Clock (signal)
Simple words: a signal that goes 0, 1, 0, 1 at a steady beat and tells flip-flops when to store. Analogy: the pass bell, rung at a steady beat. Precise: a periodic digital signal whose edges (here the rising edges) trigger the flip-flops of a synchronous circuit. D1
Clock period
Simple words: the time from one rising edge to the next. Analogy: the time between two rings of the bell. Precise: the duration T of one cycle of the clock, measured in seconds (often ns). D1 W1
Clock frequency
Simple words: how many clock cycles happen in one second. Analogy: rings per second. Precise: f = 1 / T, measured in hertz (Hz, cycles per second); 1 MHz is 106 Hz and 1 GHz is 109 Hz. W1
Duty cycle
Simple words: the share of each period in which the clock is 1. Analogy: how long the bell's hammer stays up compared with the whole beat. Precise: high time divided by period, written as a percentage; the clocks in this chapter's simulations are 50 % (equal high and low halves). D1
Setup time
Simple words: how long before the edge a flip-flop's input must already be steady. Analogy: the dish must be on the pass a moment before the ring, not during it. Precise: the minimum time before the active clock edge during which D must be stable for the flip-flop to sample it reliably. D1
Hold time
Simple words: how long after the edge the input must stay steady. Analogy: nobody may snatch the dish off the pass in the instant of the ring. Precise: the minimum time after the active clock edge during which D must remain stable. D1
Clock-to-Q delay
Simple words: how long a flip-flop takes to show its new value after the edge. Analogy: the moment a cook needs to pick up the handed-over dish. Precise: the delay from the active clock edge to the flip-flop's output Q becoming valid. D1
Critical path
Simple words: the slowest route signals must travel before the result is ready. Analogy: the soup station: the longest job between two rings. Precise: the path between two clocked elements with the longest total delay; it sets the minimum clock period (see also F1-15 and HW202). D1
Slack
Simple words: how much time is left over in a clock period after the slowest work is done. Analogy: the seconds the soup cook can rest before the ring. Precise: clock period minus the time the path needs; negative slack means the path fails timing. D1 W1
Clock enable
Simple words: a signal that tells flip-flops "this edge counts" or "skip this edge". Analogy: a card held up at some rings that says "the dessert station works on this ring only". Precise: a control input that makes clocked elements update only on edges where it is 1, so slow parts of a design can share the main clock. D1
Clock skew
Simple words: the clock edge reaching different flip-flops at slightly different times. Analogy: cooks far from the bell hear the ring a little later. Precise: the difference in arrival time of the same clock edge at two clocked elements, caused by the clock distribution wiring. D1
Oscillator
Simple words: the part that makes the clock's steady beat in the first place. Analogy: Rosa's metronome. Precise: a circuit that produces a periodic signal; digital systems derive their clocks from one, often multiplied or divided on the chip. D1

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 names the parts of a clock signal: the period, the rising and falling edges, and the high and low halves.

Figure 2 is the key idea of the chapter: the timing budget. A value leaves one flip-flop at a rising edge, travels through logic, and must arrive at the next flip-flop before the next rising edge, with time to spare for that flip-flop's setup time.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A clock in a computer is not a watch with hands. It is a wire whose value goes up and down, 0, 1, 0, 1, very steadily. Every time it goes up, every flip-flop that listens to it stores its new value at the same instant.

Between two ticks, the gates of the circuit do their work: adding, comparing, choosing. The answer must be ready before the next tick, because that tick is when the answer is stored.

So how fast can the clock go? Only as fast as the slowest job allows. If one adder needs a little more time than the gap between ticks, the flip-flop after it stores an old or half-finished answer. Nothing smokes, nothing beeps. The circuit computes the wrong thing, without any warning.

The number of ticks per second is the clock frequency. The time between ticks is the clock period. When one goes up, the other goes down.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Period and frequency

Frequency and period are reciprocals: f = 1 / T W1. With powers of ten (MA101) this is quick: 1 MHz means 106 cycles per second, so T = 10−6 s = 1 µs = 1,000 ns; 100 MHz gives 10 ns; 1 GHz gives 1 ns. Listing 1 prints this table from the formula R1. Keep the units in every step: most mistakes in clock arithmetic are a lost factor of 1,000.

The timing budget

Look at one path in Figure 2. At a rising edge the launching flip-flop's output changes after its clock-to-Q delay. The new value travels through combinational logic, which takes the logic delay you met in F1-15. It must then be stable at the capturing flip-flop's input for the setup time before the next edge D1. So, for every path:

T ≥ tclock-to-Q + tlogic + tsetup D1

The path with the largest sum is the critical path; it sets the smallest period and therefore the highest frequency of the whole circuit D1. The difference between the period and what the path needs is the slack. Positive slack: the path works. Negative slack: the capturing flip-flop samples a value that is still changing.

There is a second rule, for the hold time: after an edge, a flip-flop's input must not change too soon. The fastest path from one flip-flop to the next must take longer than the hold time D1. Unlike the setup rule, the clock period does not appear in it, so slowing the clock does not fix a hold problem D1.

What "too fast" looks like

Listing 4 is the counter of F1-17 with a deliberately slow incrementer: its result appears 7 time units after its input changes. The flip-flops in a simulation are ideal (no clock-to-Q, no setup), so the budget is T ≥ 7. With a period of 10 the counter works R3. With a period of 6 it is too fast, and the forensic lab shows the result R4.

Slower rhythms: dividers and enables

Often part of a design should act less often: a display refreshed every few cycles, a timer that ticks every N cycles. Listing 2 shows two ways. The bits of a counter are themselves slower square waves: bit 0 has twice the period of the clock, bit 1 four times R2. Using such a bit as a clock for other flip-flops is possible, but it creates a second clock whose edges come a clock-to-Q delay after the main one, which brings back the problems of the ripple counter D1. The usual alternative is a clock enable: keep one clock everywhere, and generate a one-cycle pulse (here tick, every 5 cycles) that tells the slow part "this edge counts" D1. The register's load input from F1-17 is exactly such an enable.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Skew. The clock reaches flip-flops through wires and buffers, so the same edge arrives at slightly different times: clock skew. If the capturing flip-flop's clock is late, the setup budget gains time but the hold budget loses it, and the other way round D1. Chip designers build balanced distribution networks to keep skew small, and timing tools include it in their checks.

Static timing analysis. Real designs have very many paths. Tools add up the delays of every path from the cell library's data and report the worst slack for setup and hold, without simulating any input values D1. A simulation such as Listing 5 only shows the paths that particular inputs happen to exercise; this is why timing is checked by analysis, and function by simulation.

Several clocks. Larger systems have several clocks (a processor's core clock, a memory clock, a slow peripheral clock). A signal that crosses from one clock domain to another can change at any moment relative to the receiving clock, so it can violate setup or hold and cause metastability (F1-16). The classic remedy for a single control bit is a synchronizer: two flip-flops in a row on the receiving clock, which gives the first one a full cycle to settle D1. Multi-bit values need more care (handshakes or special queues).

Power. Every clock edge charges and discharges wire and transistor capacitance, so a circuit's switching power grows with its clock frequency D1. Turning off the clock to idle parts (clock gating) saves power, and must be done with special cells, not with an ordinary AND gate on the clock, which can create glitches D1.

Not verified — check before relying on this

How a real board makes its clock (a quartz crystal oscillator, an on-chip phase-locked loop that multiplies the frequency, spread-spectrum clocking) and every real number about it (frequencies, accuracy, jitter, lock time) depend on the named part. This build opened no data sheet and gives no values. How the hardware actually does it

In a chip, one clock signal must reach a very large number of flip-flops at almost the same instant. It is distributed through a tree (or a grid) of buffers designed to keep skew small, and it is one of the chip's largest consumers of power for that reason D1. The clock frequency printed on a processor's box is the result of the timing budget of its critical paths, at the voltage and temperature the maker specifies; HW202 shows how pipelining shortens the logic between flip-flops so that the clock can run faster D1 D2.

Worked example

Will this path run at 100 MHz?

Exercise A (made-up values, not a real part): clock-to-Q = 1.5 ns, logic = 6.0 ns, setup = 1.0 ns.

  1. Minimum period: 1.5 + 6.0 + 1.0 = 8.5 ns.
  2. Maximum frequency: 1 / 8.5 ns. Since 1 / (1 ns) = 1,000 MHz, f = 1,000 / 8.5 MHz ≈ 117.6 MHz.
  3. 100 MHz has T = 10 ns. Slack = 10 − 8.5 = +1.5 ns: the path works.
  4. At 125 MHz, T = 8 ns. Slack = 8 − 8.5 = −0.5 ns: it fails.
  5. Exercise B speeds the logic up to 4.0 ns: 1.5 + 4.0 + 1.0 = 6.5 ns, about 153.8 MHz.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Rosa can wait for a slow cook; a clock never waits. A clock does not know whether the logic has finished. Correctness depends entirely on the designer choosing a period longer than the critical path.
    • 

  • A cook with a cold pot knows it; a flip-flop does not. A flip-flop that samples a value still changing gives no error signal. It stores something, maybe the old value, maybe the new one, maybe (for a while) neither: a metastable level D1.
    • 

  • Everyone hears the bell at once; clock edges arrive at slightly different times. Skew is the gap the analogy hides D1.
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: F1-15 measured propagation delay through gates, and F1-17 built counters on a shared clock. What carries over: delays add along a path, exactly as in F1-15; the flip-flop's rule (store at the edge) is unchanged D1. What changes: delay is no longer only "how long until the output is right" but a budget that must fit inside one clock period; a too-fast clock fails silently, and hold problems cannot be fixed by slowing down. What comes next: F1-19 uses the clock and registers to build finite state machines, where "one edge = one step" becomes the whole design method.

Summary


    

  • A clock is a steady 0/1 signal; flip-flops act on its rising edges.
    • 

  • f = 1 / T: 100 MHz ↔ 10 ns, 1 GHz ↔ 1 ns. Keep units in every step.
    • 

  • Every path needs T ≥ clock-to-Q + logic delay + setup; the worst path is the critical path.
    • 

  • Slack = T minus what the path needs; negative slack means wrong values are stored, silently.
    • 

  • Hold violations come from paths that are too fast; slowing the clock does not fix them.
    • 

  • Make slow rhythms with a counter and a clock enable, not with extra clocks.
    • 

  • Skew, clock domains and power make clocks a design topic of their own; timing is checked by analysis, function by simulation.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: from edge 4 (t = 21) every edge is wrong; count reaches only 5 after 12 edges, half the expected rate: it changes every 12 units, every second edge R4. In the diagram, next changes at 10, 22, 34, 46, 58: always one unit after an edge (21, 33, 45, 57). Right before the edge at t = 21, next still showed 1 (it last changed at 10, and the new value 2 appears at 22), so the edge stored 1 again; at t = 27, next = 2, so count became 2. Hypotheses: (1) reset is wrong: ruled out, reset ends at t = 13 and the first count after it is correct (edge 3); (2) the adder computes the wrong value: ruled out, next always equals count + 1, only late; (3) the clock period is shorter than the incrementer's delay: confirmed. Root cause: the incrementer needs 7 units (Listing 4, line 11), the clock gives 6: slack 6 − 7 = −1 unit (Listing 1 prints "TOO FAST (slack -1.0 ns)") R1. Every edge that follows a change of count arrives before next has settled, so the register stores the old value and the counter stalls for one cycle. Fixes: go back to a period ≥ 7 (period 10 passes, R3); or make the logic faster; or keep the fast clock and split the work across two cycles (a pipeline). Verify each with the same self-checking testbench and with the budget calculation. Prevention: compute the slack of every path before choosing a clock (static timing analysis in real tools). Real hardware: a real flip-flop whose input is changing inside its setup window could store old, new, or a metastable level, so the failure could be irregular, not this neat every-second-edge pattern D1. How the evidence was made: the correct design and testbench, compiled with -DPERIOD=6 instead of 10. Wrong answers: "reset is held too long" (count is correct at edge 3); "the counter skips" (the opposite: it repeats values; compare with the counter that skips in F1-17).
