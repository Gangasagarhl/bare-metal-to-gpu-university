Story hook

Analogy

It is the busiest hour in the restaurant. Waiters call orders through the serving hatch, the window between the dining room and the kitchen. Chef Ama has a problem: an order is spoken once, and then the waiter is gone. If nobody writes it down, the kitchen forgets it.

So the kitchen keeps a ticket rail. Each order is clipped to the rail and stays there after the waiter leaves. But a new trouble appears. Waiters keep changing tickets while the cooks are reading them, and a cook starts a dish from a half-changed slip.

Ama's fix is a small bell. Tickets on the rail may be swapped only at the moment the bell rings. Between rings, the rail stands still and every cook reads the same thing. This chapter builds both ideas in hardware: the open hatch (a latch) and the ticket rail swapped at the bell (a flip-flop).

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why the circuits of HW102 cannot remember, and how feedback gives a circuit memory.
  • Trace an SR latch made of two NOR gates through set, reset, hold and the forbidden input, and check your trace against a simulation.
  • Tell a D latch from a D flip-flop by reading a timing diagram.
  • Build an edge-triggered flip-flop from two latches and verify it in a logic simulator.
  • Diagnose a description that accidentally creates a latch (the forensic lab).
Jargon box
Combinational circuit
Simple words: a circuit whose outputs depend only on its inputs right now. Analogy: a cook who answers "what goes with fish?" the same way every time, with no notebook. Precise: a circuit without memory: after the propagation delay, its outputs are a fixed function of its current inputs (the gates, adders and multiplexers of HW102). D2 §3.1
Sequential circuit
Simple words: a circuit whose outputs also depend on what happened before. Analogy: a kitchen with a ticket rail: what it cooks next depends on the tickets already clipped there. Precise: a circuit with memory: its outputs depend on its current inputs and on its stored state, which records information about past inputs. D6 “Sequential Circuits”
Feedback (in a circuit)
Simple words: an output wired back to an input of the same circuit. Analogy: two cooks who each keep repeating what the other one said a moment ago. Precise: a connection that forms a loop from a gate's output back to its own input, directly or through other gates; feedback is what lets a circuit hold a value. (The robotics and control courses use "feedback" for a different idea: measuring a result and correcting towards a target.) D6 “Bistable Circuit”
Latch
Simple words: a one-bit memory that can change whenever its control input says "open". Analogy: the serving hatch: while it is open, the newest plate is what you see; when it closes, the last plate stays in view. Precise: a level-sensitive storage element: while its enable (or clock) input is at the active level the output follows the data input ("transparent"); otherwise the output holds. D6 “D Latch”
SR latch
Simple words: a latch with a Set button and a Reset button. Analogy: a "table taken" sign with two levers: one flips it to taken, the other to free. Precise: a latch made of two cross-coupled gates (NOR gates in this chapter), with inputs S (set, makes Q = 1) and R (reset, makes Q = 0); S = R = 1 is not allowed. D6 “SR Latch”
D latch
Simple words: a latch with one data input D and one enable input. Analogy: the serving hatch with one plate slot. Precise: a latch whose output Q follows D while the enable input is 1 and holds its last value while the enable input is 0; it avoids the forbidden input of the SR latch. D6 “D Latch”
Flip-flop
Simple words: a one-bit memory that changes only at one instant: the clock's edge. Analogy: a ticket clip on the rail that may be swapped only when the bell rings. Precise: an edge-triggered storage element; a D flip-flop copies D to Q at the active edge of its clock (here the rising edge) and holds Q at all other times. D6 “D Flip-Flop” D2 §3.2.1
Clock edge
Simple words: the moment a clock signal changes, from 0 to 1 (rising) or from 1 to 0 (falling). Analogy: the instant the bell rings, not the time between rings. Precise: a transition of the clock signal; a rising-edge-triggered flip-flop samples its input at the 0-to-1 transition. Clocks themselves are the subject of F1-18. D6 “D Flip-Flop”
Timing diagram
Simple words: a picture of signals as lines that go up and down over time. Analogy: a strip showing, minute by minute, which tickets were on the rail. Precise: a plot of signal values (horizontal axis: time) used to show the order of events; in this course they are drawn from real simulation runs. D6 “D Latch vs. D Flip-Flop”
Testbench (see "Logic simulator, testbench", HW102)
Simple words: a pretend world written in code that pokes a circuit and watches what it does. Analogy: a practice service with fake orders before the restaurant opens. Precise: a simulation-only module that drives a design's inputs, observes its outputs and (ideally) checks them; it is not itself hardware. D7 “Testbenches”
Unknown value (x)
Simple words: the simulator's way of saying "this could be 0 or 1; I cannot tell". Analogy: a ticket clip before anyone has clipped a ticket. Precise: in Verilog simulation a 1-bit signal has four values: 0, 1, x (unknown) and z (not driven) D7 “Z: Floating Output”; in the runs of this course, storage starts as x until something sets it R1
Metastability
Simple words: a storage element caught exactly halfway between 0 and 1 for a while. Analogy: a coin that lands on its edge and wobbles before it falls. Precise: a state in which a bistable element's output lingers between the two valid levels for an unpredictable time, for example when a flip-flop's input changes too close to the sampling instant. D6 “Metastability”
Inferred latch
Simple words: a latch you did not ask for, created because your description forgot a case. Analogy: a cook who, when an order is missing, keeps serving the previous one. Precise: a latch that a synthesis tool creates from a hardware description in which some output is not assigned on every path through the logic, so the description implies "keep the old value". D7 “Latch” D5 proc_dlatch R3

The idea in pictures

Figure 1 shows the smallest memory there is: two NOR gates, each feeding its output into the other's input. The loop is the memory. The table beside it lists what each input pair does.

Figure 2 is the most important picture of the chapter. The same data signal d drives a D latch and a D flip-flop. The latch copies d for as long as the clock is high, so short wiggles of d pass straight through. The flip-flop looks at d only at the rising edges (dashed lines) and ignores everything in between.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every circuit you built in HW102 had a short memory: none at all. Change the inputs of an adder, and a moment later the old answer is gone. That is fine for adding, but a computer must also remember: which instruction comes next, what number is in a register, which letter you typed last.

The trick is to make a circuit talk to itself. Take two gates and feed each one's output into the other one's input. Now they hold each other in place, like two friends leaning back to back. Push one of them once, and they settle into the new position and stay there. That loop is a one-bit memory.

A latch is such a loop with a door in front of it. While the door is open, whatever you put in goes straight through, like plates through the open serving hatch. When the door closes, the last plate stays.

A flip-flop is stricter. It takes a new value only at one instant: the moment the bell rings, which in a computer is the clock edge. Everything that happens between rings is ignored. Computers use flip-flops almost everywhere because then every part of the machine changes at the same moments, in step.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Memory needs feedback

A combinational circuit computes its outputs from its present inputs only; its gates form no loops D2 §3.1. A sequential circuit has feedback: some output returns to an input. Its outputs then depend on the present inputs and on a stored state D6 “Sequential Circuits”. Every memory element in this course, from the latch to the DRAM cell of F1-20, is a way of holding state.

The SR latch

In Figure 1, gate g1 computes Q = NOT(R OR Qn) and gate g2 computes Qn = NOT(S OR Q) D6 “SR Latch Analysis”. Remember that a NOR gate outputs 1 only when both its inputs are 0 (F1-10).

  • Set (S = 1, R = 0): g2 has a 1 input, so Qn = 0. Then g1 sees R = 0 and Qn = 0, so Q = 1.
  • Hold (S = 0, R = 0): each gate's output is decided by the other gate's output. Q = 1 keeps Qn = 0, and Qn = 0 keeps Q = 1. The latch remembers.
  • Reset (S = 0, R = 1): the mirror image: Q = 0, Qn = 1.
  • Forbidden (S = 1, R = 1): both gates have a 1 input, so Q = Qn = 0. The outputs are no longer opposites. Worse, if S and R return to 0 at the same instant, both gates see two 0 inputs and both switch to 1, then both see a 1 and switch to 0, and so on.

Listing 2's simulation shows all four cases, with a delay of one time unit per gate. You can watch the set ripple through the gates (Qn falls at t = 6, Q rises at t = 7) and the forbidden release make the latch oscillate from t = 30 to the end of the run R1. A bistable loop also has a balanced in-between state, between its two stable states, where it can linger for an unpredictable time before falling to one side. That is metastability D6 “Metastability”. The simulator, with its perfectly equal delays, oscillates forever instead: a model, not the real behaviour. (Whether a particular real latch released from S = R = 1 goes metastable, oscillates for a while or falls at once to one side depends on the circuit; see the box in Layer 3.)

The D latch

The forbidden input is a trap, so designers add two gates in front of the SR latch: S becomes "D AND enable", and R becomes "NOT D AND enable" D6 “D Latch”. Now S and R can never both be 1. While enable = 1, Q follows D: the latch is transparent. While enable = 0, both S and R are 0 and the latch holds. That is the D latch. Listing 3 describes it in Verilog by its behaviour rather than its gates.

The D flip-flop

A transparent latch has a weakness. Suppose its output feeds logic that feeds back into its own input, as in the counter of F1-17. While the latch is open, a new value can race around the loop several times. The flip-flop fixes this: it captures D only at the rising clock edge and holds Q for the whole rest of the period D6 “D Flip-Flop” D2 §3.2.1.

One classic construction uses two D latches in a row (Listing 5). The first, the master, is open while the clock is 0. The second, the slave, is open while the clock is 1. The two are never open at the same time. When the clock rises, the master closes, holding the value D had right before the edge, and the slave opens and passes that value to Q. Until the next rising edge nothing new can get through D6 “D Flip-Flop Internal Circuit”. The run of Listing 6 checks that this two-latch flip-flop and the one-line behavioural flip-flop of Listing 4 agree at every edge R2.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

An edge-triggered flip-flop is only edge-triggered if its input is stable around the edge. Its data sheet gives a setup time (D stable before the edge), a hold time (D stable after it) and a clock-to-Q delay (how long Q takes to change) D6 “Timing”. F1-18 uses these three to compute how fast a clock may run. If D changes inside the setup-and-hold window, the flip-flop can become metastable, exactly like the SR latch released from S = R = 1 D6 “Metastability”.

Why do almost all designs use flip-flops and not latches? Timing analysis of a flip-flop design is simple: each path goes from one edge to the next. With latches, a signal may pass through several open latches in one phase, so the analysis must follow it across phases. The course textbook's authors avoid latches altogether ("We don't use latches in this text") and warn that a description can create one by accident D7 “Latch”.

The simulator matters too. Verilog is simulated as a sequence of events. Listing 4 uses the non-blocking assignment q <= d: all flip-flops that share an edge read their inputs first and update afterwards, as real flip-flops do D7 “Blocking vs. Nonblocking Assignment”. F1-21 shows what goes wrong with the other kind of assignment. The simulator used here, Icarus Verilog, also starts every stored value as x (unknown), as the runs show. The x at the start of Figure 2 is not a bug: it is the honest answer before any clock edge or reset has stored something R1 R2.

Not verified — check before relying on this

Real setup times, hold times, clock-to-Q delays and the probability that a flip-flop stays metastable longer than a given time are properties of a specific part or chip process. No data sheet of a named part was used for this chapter, so it gives no numbers. Also not confirmed by an opened source: that a real NOR latch released from S = R = 1 goes metastable; the chapter reasons this from the description of bistable circuits and metastability in D6, which does not discuss this case. Before using any value, read it from the data sheet of the named part (or the timing library of the chip process) and record it in the dossier (AH-21).

How the hardware actually does it

In silicon, the NOR gates of Figure 1 are made of transistors used as switches, as in F1-09 and F1-10 D8 “Transistors as switches”, “CMOS gates”. The textbook's slides call a pair of cross-coupled inverters (the same loop with no S and R inputs) the fundamental building block of other state elements; F1-20 meets the same loop again inside an SRAM cell D6 “Bistable Circuit”. A processor contains many flip-flops: its registers (F1-17) and its program counter, which is a register plus counting logic D2 §3.2.4; HW202 adds more between the stages of a pipeline. All of them listen to the same clock, which is why the clock network gets its own chapter (F1-18).

Worked example

Tracing the SR latch by hand

Each NOR gate takes 1 time unit. At t = 5, S becomes 1 while Q and Qn are still unknown.

  1. g2 sees S = 1. A NOR with any 1 input gives 0, whatever the other input is. One unit later, at t = 6, Qn = 0.
  2. g1 now sees R = 0 and Qn = 0, so its output becomes 1 one unit later: t = 7, Q = 1.
  3. At t = 10, S returns to 0. g2 sees S = 0 and Q = 1: output stays 0. g1 sees 0 and 0: output stays 1. Nothing changes. The latch holds 1.
  4. At t = 25, S = R = 1: both outputs fall to 0 (t = 26).
  5. At t = 30, both inputs return to 0. Each gate sees 0, 0 and switches to 1 at t = 31. Then each sees a 1 and switches to 0 at t = 32. The pattern repeats every 2 time units.

Compare with the recorded output of Listing 2 below: rows t = 6, 7, 26, 31, 32 are exactly these steps R1.

Predicting a latch and a flip-flop

Use Figure 2. The clock has period 20 and rises at 10, 30, 50 and so on. Between t = 10 and t = 20 the clock is high. d falls at t = 15 and rises at t = 18.

  • Latch: open while clk = 1, so q_latch falls at 15 and rises at 18. It shows the wiggle.
  • Flip-flop: at the edge t = 10, d = 1 (it rose at 5), so q_ff = 1 until the next edge. At t = 30, d = 0 (it fell at 27), so q_ff = 0. The wiggle at 15–18 never reaches q_ff.

The run prints "t=10 rising edge: d was 1" and "t=30 rising edge: d was 0", matching this prediction R2.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The bell is not a person deciding. Chef Ama can choose when to ring. A clock edge comes from a clock signal at regular times (F1-18), and every flip-flop connected to it reacts, whether or not anything useful changed.
    • 

  • A ticket can be read halfway through being clipped; a flip-flop's output can be neither 0 nor 1. A half-clipped ticket still has readable words on it. A metastable flip-flop has an output between the two valid levels for an undetermined time, so it is neither a clean 0 nor a clean 1 D6 “Metastability”.
    • 

  • The hatch has no "forbidden" position. Nothing bad happens if two waiters push plates at once. The SR latch does have one: S = R = 1 breaks the rule that Q and Qn are opposites, as the run shows R1.
    • 

  • A rail holds many tickets; a flip-flop holds one bit. To hold a number you need one flip-flop per bit, side by side: a register (F1-17).
    •

Transition box

Where you came from: HW102 built combinational circuits, ending with propagation delay in F1-15: outputs follow inputs after a delay, and forget them. What carries over: gates, truth tables and delays; a latch is built from the same NOR gates, and its behaviour is traced with the same delay reasoning D6 “SR Latch”. What changes: outputs now depend on the past; the order of events matters; storage can start unknown; some input combinations are forbidden; and a description can create memory by accident. What comes next: F1-17 puts flip-flops side by side to make registers, counters and shift registers, all moving together on one clock.

Summary


    

  • Combinational circuits have no memory; sequential circuits store state, and storing needs feedback.
    • 

  • Two cross-coupled NOR gates form an SR latch: set, reset, hold; S = R = 1 is forbidden.
    • 

  • A D latch is transparent while enabled and holds while not; it removes the forbidden input.
    • 

  • A D flip-flop copies D only at the clock edge; two latches open on opposite clock levels make one.
    • 

  • Real flip-flops need D stable around the edge (setup and hold) or they can become metastable.
    • 

  • Simulators start storage as x; a timing diagram from a real run is the best evidence of behaviour.
    • 

  • An output left unassigned in some case of a combinational block creates an inferred latch.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation: exactly 4 of 32 patterns fail, all with sel = 3 and c = 1; y is 1 instead of 0 R3. Hypotheses: (1) the simulator is broken; (2) the description stores a value. In counting order each failing row follows a sel = 2 row with the same c, where y = c = 1 was correct; at sel = 3 nothing assigns y, so y keeps 1. The fixed description, run with the same simulator, passes all 32 patterns R4, which rules out (1). Root cause: Listing 7, lines 12–17: no assignment to y when sel = 3. In an always @(*) block that means "keep the old value", which is the behaviour of a latch. The synthesis tool confirms it: "Latch inferred for signal `\mux_bug.\y'" R3. Fix: assign a default first (y = 1'b0; at the top of the block, as in mux_fixed.v) or add a final else y = 1'b0;; after the fix the tool reports 0 inferred latches R4. Prevention: a coding rule "every combinational block assigns every output first", a testbench that tries every input pattern in more than one order, and a check that the synthesis log contains no "Latch inferred" lines. How the evidence was made: the correct selector with its default assignment deleted, simulated and synthesized in this build. Wrong answers: "the simulator is broken" (the fixed version passes in the same simulator); "a flip-flop is involved" (there is no clock; the stored value is level-sensitive, a latch).
