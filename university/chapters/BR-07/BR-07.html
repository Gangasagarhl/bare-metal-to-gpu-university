Story hook

For years Ms Achieng ran the old school building. Every morning the caretaker had already unlocked the doors, switched on the heating and turned on every light. When she wanted to know what a room was for, she knocked, and the teacher inside told her.

Next term she takes over the new annex. The caretaker there gives her one day in the building exactly as the last principal left it, working. She spends that day walking, listening and copying the building plan. That evening she writes a moving-in plan before she moves a single desk.

The plan saves her three times. A classroom where nobody answers is not empty: its lights are switched off at the main panel, and nothing in the room works until someone switches them on. The plan's room numbers are the architect's numbers, and the corridor signs use different ones, so she writes a conversion table. And when a locksmith offers to change the front-door lock, she first checks that the spare key to the side door still works. Without it, one bad lock would have shut everybody out.

A PC is the old building. An SoC board is the annex. This chapter is the day in the working building, and the plan you write that evening.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what carries over from PC driver work to an SoC board (drivers written against specifications, the universal method, devicetree reading) and what changes, most dangerous first.
  • Boot a known-good image on a board, capture its serial log, read what the boot ROM handed over, and dump the devicetree the system was given.
  • Produce a dependency inventory from that tree: every device's CPU address after translation and the clocks, resets, pins, supplies, power domains and DMA coherence it needs, in a providers-first order.
  • Write a bring-up plan with a tested recovery path before writing any driver code.
  • Diagnose the three bridge traps from real evidence: a device that reads zeros because its clock is off, a bus address used as a CPU address, and on-board storage overwritten without a way back.
Jargon box
SoC board
Simple words: a small computer built around one chip that holds the processor cores and most devices. Analogy: the new annex: everything under one roof, but nothing switched on for you. Precise: a board whose main chip is a system on a chip: CPU cores, an interconnect, memory controllers, on-chip ROM and SRAM and many peripheral blocks at fixed physical addresses, plus board parts (regulators, storage, connectors) around it. C1 Defined in full in F4-31.
Enumeration versus description
Simple words: finding devices by asking them, versus reading a list somebody wrote. Analogy: knocking on doors versus reading the building plan. Precise: PCI enumeration reads a configuration space every device implements; on most SoC boards there is no such space for the on-chip blocks, and the OS learns them from a devicetree (or, on some Arm systems, ACPI tables). C1 D1
Boot ROM and boot chain
Simple words: the first program, built into the chip, and the line of programs that follow it until your kernel runs. Analogy: the caretaker who opens the gate, then hands the keys to the next person. Precise: a mask ROM inside the SoC runs at reset, chooses a boot source, loads the next stage in a vendor-specific format, and the next stages (a first-stage loader, resident firmware, a general loader) bring up DRAM and hand over to the OS. C1 Defined in F4-31 and followed stage by stage in F4-32.
Boot mode pins (boot source selection)
Simple words: pins or fuses that tell the boot ROM where to look for the next program. Analogy: a note on the gate: "today, the keys are in the side office". Precise: inputs sampled at reset (on some SoCs, fuses) whose value selects the boot medium and mode the ROM uses; the SiFive FU540 calls them MSEL. QEMU's model of that board exposes the choice as a machine option. D4 R2
Clock gate and reset line
Simple words: a switch that stops a block's clock, and a signal that holds the block in its starting state. Analogy: the room's lights at the main panel, and a "do not enter until the bell" sign. Precise: a clock controller enables or gates each block's clock inputs; a reset controller asserts or releases each block's reset; a devicetree node names its clocks with clocks (cells per #clock-cells of the provider) and its resets with resets. D2 Clock gating in hardware: F1-75.
Pin multiplexing
Simple words: choosing which block uses each pin of the chip. Analogy: one doorway shared by two classes; a sign says which class uses it this term. Precise: a pin controller connects each pad to one of several functions; a node's pinctrl-0 points at the pin group it needs. D2 Taught in F4-36.
Regulator (supply in a devicetree)
Simple words: the part that provides one voltage to one group of devices, which software may have to switch on. Analogy: the room's own heating circuit. Precise: a voltage regulator described as a devicetree node; a consumer names it with a property ending in -supply (for example vmmc-supply), and an OS regulator driver enables it and sets its voltage before the consumer is used. D2
Power domain
Simple words: a part of the chip whose power can be switched on and off as a whole. Analogy: a wing of the building on its own fuse. Precise: a region of the SoC with a switchable supply; a power controller node with #power-domain-cells provides it and a consumer names it with power-domains; while the domain is off, the blocks inside it do not respond. D2
Non-coherent DMA and dma-coherent
Simple words: a device that reads and writes memory without seeing what is still sitting in the CPU's cache. Analogy: a delivery truck that takes the boxes from the storeroom, not the ones still on the teacher's desk. Precise: a DMA master whose accesses are not snooped by the CPU caches; software must clean dirty lines before the device reads and invalidate lines before the CPU reads what the device wrote. The devicetree property dma-coherent marks a device whose DMA is coherent. D1 D8 Caches and snooping: F1-36; DMA: F1-44.
Known-good image
Simple words: software that is known to boot on this exact board, used as a reference before you run your own. Analogy: the one day in the working building. Precise: a boot stack (vendor or mainline loader and OS, or firmware alone) whose serial log, devicetree and kernel log you capture as the baseline every later difference is measured against (curriculum 11.5, step 3). C1
Dependency inventory
Simple words: a list, made from the devicetree, of what each device needs before it can work. Analogy: the list of switches, keys and signs for every room, made before moving in. Precise: this course's name for the output of Listing 1: for each node with a compatible, its status, its translated CPU address and its providers (clocks, resets, pins, supplies, power domains, interrupt parent, GPIOs, DMA channels), plus a stage order in which every provider precedes its consumers. It is an input to the bring-up plan (F4-17, F4-31).

Figure 1 puts the two worlds side by side. On the left, a PC: firmware in a flash chip runs first, sets up memory, clocks and power, and the kernel then asks the hardware what is there. Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Analogy

A PC is a building where the caretaker switches everything on before you arrive, and every room tells you what it is when you knock. A small board, like the one inside a robot or a drone, is a building where nobody answers and many lights are off. So before you move in, you spend one day watching the building work the way somebody else left it, you copy its plan, and you write down, room by room, which switch, key and sign each room needs. You also make sure you have a spare key, because on this kind of building one wrong lock can shut everybody out.

Mapping: the caretaker is the firmware; knocking is enumeration; the plan is the devicetree; the switches, keys and signs are clocks, resets and pins; the day in the working building is the known-good image; the spare key is the recovery path.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What carries over

Drivers written against specifications. A driver for a block is written from that block's register description, not from the board it sits on. The PL011 UART driver of F4-24 worked on QEMU virt and, with a different address and clock, on the Raspberry Pi model of F4-27. SoC vendors license the same blocks for many chips, and the compatible string names the block, so a driver written once against its specification serves every board that carries it. C1 D1

The universal method. DR401 taught a sequence for any device: identify it (F4-14), find its documentation and its tier (F4-15), observe it lawfully (F4-16), design the driver (F4-17) and test it against a plan (F4-18). On a board the same steps apply. What changes is where the evidence comes from: "observe" now starts with a boot of software that already works on that board, and "identify" starts with the devicetree that software was given.

Devicetree reading from DR402. You can already read a DTB: F4-23's fdt.h and its nine-row platform checklist, F4-27's aliases and ranges, the cell counts that belong to the parent. In this chapter's lab that tool ran, unchanged, on the devicetree of a board model and answered its checklist (R4). The parser and the habits carry over. R3 R4

What changes

No enumeration. The blocks inside an SoC sit at fixed addresses and do not answer a question about themselves. The devicetree is the only description, and somebody else wrote it. C1

Boot ROMs and vendor boot chains. The first code is a ROM inside the chip. It picks a boot source from pins or fuses and loads the next stage in a format the vendor chose. Your kernel arrives late, after several programs you did not write, and it gets what they left behind: a devicetree address in a register and some hardware already set up. C1 R2

Explicit clocks, resets, pins and regulators. On a PC, firmware has switched everything on before the OS starts. On an SoC, the OS may have to enable a clock, release a reset, power a domain, switch on a regulator and select the pin function before a device answers. Miss one, and the device may not fail loudly: in the model of this chapter it reads zeros. C1 R10

Non-coherent DMA. Devices on a PC see what the CPU caches hold. Many SoC devices do not, so the driver must clean and invalidate cache lines around every transfer, or use uncached buffers. C1 R16

Documentation quality varies by vendor. A PC is described by public standards. An SoC is described by its vendor's technical reference manual, which can be complete and public, available after registration, partial, or absent. Which board you choose decides how much you can know. C1

Watch out

Of these, the most dangerous is the one with no undo: writing to a board's own storage. A wrong driver can be fixed by booting again. A boot flash overwritten without a recovery path can leave nothing to boot (trap 3, run in this chapter). That is why the bring-up plan starts with the console and the recovery path, before any code. C1 R14

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

The board for this chapter's runs is QEMU's sifive_u machine, a model of SiFive's HiFive Unleashed board, the stand-in for a real board that F4-29 already used. It is a model: it shows the shape of a board's boot and description, not the behaviour of the silicon. Each section below names what the model showed and what only the real board's documents can answer. D5

Description, and what the description leaves out

The board's devicetree (R3, 4699 bytes as QEMU dumped it) lists 24 nodes with a compatible string (R6). QEMU's own memory map of the same machine (R5) is an independent view: it lists what the model decodes. Lay the two side by side and four regions appear in the map with no node in the tree: riscv.sifive.u.mrom at 0x1000, riscv.sifive.u.l2lim at 0x08000000, riscv.sifive.u.dmc at 0x100b0000 and riscv.sifive.u.flash0 at 0x20000000. The ROM, a scratchpad SRAM, the DRAM controller and the boot flash are things early boot stages use; this tree does not offer them to the OS. R3 R5

So "not in the tree" means "not described to you", not "not there". Whoever wrote the tree decided what the OS may touch. That is the first reason never to scan for devices (F4-31's forensic lab shows the crash on QEMU virt), and the reason the plan records the tree's origin and checksum: a different loader version may hand you a different tree. D1

The PC's checklist tool still works. F4-23's fdt_tool checklist answered every row it knows on this tree (R4): two CPU nodes, memory at 0x80000000, the PLIC, a timebase of 1000000 Hz, the console from /chosen. It also shows what it was never built to ask: nothing about clocks, boot sources or recovery. Row 1 hides a board fact worth a line in the plan: the two harts differ. cpu@0 reports rv64imac with no mmu-type; cpu@1 reports an FPU and riscv,sv48 (R3). A kernel that starts every hart in the tree as a supervisor-mode CPU repeats the mistake F4-29 recorded on this machine. R3 R4

The boot ROM and the chain behind it

R2 stops the board model before its first instruction and reads the ROM. The words at 0x1000 and the first instructions executed tell the whole hand-over in six instructions: auipc t0,0 takes the ROM's own address; csrrs a0,mhartid,zero puts the hart number in a0; ld a1,32(t0) loads the word at 0x1024, which is 0x8fe00000; ld t0,24(t0) loads the word at 0x101c, which is 0x80000000; jr t0 jumps there. The next block executed is at 0x80000000, the firmware's base. R2

The firmware is OpenSBI (R1). Its banner confirms each value from its own side: "Firmware Base : 0x80000000", "Platform HART Count : 2", "Platform Console Device : sifive_uart", and the hand-over it would make to the next stage: "Domain0 Next Arg1 : 0x000000008fe00000" in "S-mode". That number is the devicetree address the ROM put in a1; OpenSBI passes it on. Here there is no next stage, so the run ends at the time limit: exit code 124 is the expected result, the banner is the pass. R1 D6

On the real chip, the ROM's choice of source comes from the boot mode pins. QEMU's model describes its own version of that choice in its help text (end of R2): "Set on to tell QEMU's ROM to jump to flash. Otherwise QEMU will jump to DRAM or L2LIM depending on the msel value". The pattern, a ROM that reads pins and loads the next stage from one of several media in a vendor format, is the pattern of curriculum 11.1 and 11.2, and it is what makes a recovery path possible: if the pins can select a second source, a broken first source is not fatal. R2 C1

Clocks, resets, pins, regulators and power domains

Each of these is a provider–consumer relation written into the tree. A provider node declares how many cells a reference to it carries (#clock-cells, #reset-cells, #power-domain-cells); a consumer lists phandles followed by that many cells (clocks, resets, power-domains); pins and supplies are referenced by phandle alone (pinctrl-0, vmmc-supply). D1 D2

On the board model, 9 nodes need a clock and every one of them points at one provider, clock-controller@10000000 with #clock-cells = 1: the UARTs, SPI controllers, PWMs and GPIO name clock 3, the Ethernet MAC names clock 2 twice (R6). That provider is the SoC's PRCI block (compatible sifive,fu540-c000-prci), itself fed by two fixed clocks, hfclk (0x1fca055 = 33333333 Hz) and rtcclk (0xf4240 = 1000000 Hz) (R3). No node of this tree has a reset, a pin group, a supply or a power domain (R6). R3 R6

That zero is a property of QEMU's tree, not a promise about the chip, and it is exactly the trap a plan must not fall into. The teaching board of Listing 2 shows what a fuller tree looks like: 11 nodes; 5 need a clock, 3 a reset, 1 a pin group, 1 a supply and 2 a power domain (R7). Its UART needs clock 5 and reset 5 from two different controllers and its pins from a third; its MMC controller needs two clocks, a reset, the supply node vcc-3v3 and power domain 1. The stage order (Figure 2) is the order the providers' drivers must be ready in. R7

Curriculum 11.5 adds a practical rule: trust the clocks firmware left running at first, and add clock and reset drivers only when a device needs one the firmware left off, recording each in the design log. The inventory tells you what each device depends on; the known-good boot tells you what firmware already switched on; the difference is the work. C1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Which of the PRCI's clocks index 3 and index 2 are, whether firmware leaves them running, and what the FU540 does when software touches a block whose clock is gated are not known from this build; they are in the FU540-C000 manual's clock chapter (D4) if anywhere. In general, the order in which a block needs clock enable and reset release, and what a register access does while either is missing (reads as zero, hangs the bus, or aborts), differs between SoCs and is documented, if at all, in each TRM. The model of trap 1 picks "reads as zero" because it produces the most confusing symptom.

Non-coherent DMA

A write-back cache keeps a written line in the cache and writes it to memory later (F1-35). If a DMA master reads memory without the interconnect snooping the CPU caches (F1-36), it reads the old contents; if it writes memory while the CPU holds a cached copy, the CPU keeps reading the copy. The fix is cache maintenance in the right places: clean (write back) the buffer's lines before the device reads them; invalidate (discard) them before the CPU reads what the device wrote. D8

Listing 6 is a small model of exactly that, run in this build (R16): with no maintenance a non-coherent device sends "....." instead of "HELLO" and the CPU receives "....." instead of "WORLD"; with a clean before the transmit and an invalidate before the receive both are correct; a device marked coherent needs neither. R16



The devicetree records coherence per device. QEMU's Arm virt tree marks 34 nodes dma-coherent, every virtio-mmio transport, the PCIe host and fw-cfg (R8): on that machine, the devices are modelled as coherent. The board model's tree marks none (R6). Read that absence with care, because the default it implies is a convention of the architecture and the OS, not of the tree alone (box below). R6 R8 D1

The author recalls that on Arm the OS treats a device without dma-coherent as non-coherent, while for RISC-V the Devicetree Specification and Linux added a dma-noncoherent property because RISC-V systems were assumed coherent by default; and that the maintenance instructions are DC CVAC / DC IVAC / DC CIVAC on AArch64 (D3) and the Zicbom cbo.clean / cbo.inval / cbo.flush on RISC-V (D9). How the hardware actually does it


A clock gate is a switch on a branch of the chip's clock tree: when it is open, the flip-flops of that block stop changing state (F1-75). A reset line holds those flip-flops at their reset values. The block's registers are flip-flops too, and the bus interface that answers a load or store is part of the same block; what a gated or held block does with an access depends on how the chip designer connected that interface, which is why the outcome is a TRM question. D8 D4


Pin multiplexing is a multiplexer per pad (HW102): a register value selects which block's signal drives the pin. A regulator is a separate part on the board or a block in a power-management chip that turns one supply into the voltage a group of circuits needs; a power domain is a region of the chip behind a power switch. Both are physical: with the supply off, the circuit has no power, and no register write inside it can work until the supply is on. D2


Address translation is physical as well. The interconnect decodes addresses separately for each master. On the Raspberry Pi family, the VideoCore and the Arm cores reach the same peripherals at different addresses, and the devicetree writes the peripheral bus's addresses (0x7e......), so the /soc node's ranges converts them for the CPU. On QEMU's raspi3b model, the CPU address 0x7e201000 decodes to nothing, and a store there comes back as a synchronous external abort (R11). D7 R11


DMA coherence is a property of the interconnect: a coherent port makes the device's reads and writes look up the CPU caches (snooping, F1-36); a non-coherent port goes straight to the memory controller. The same chip may have both kinds of port. D8


Safety
Real-board work: use a 3.3 V USB-to-serial adapter on the debug UART unless the board's documentation states another level, never 5 V; connect only ground, TX and RX, with the board's power off; power the board from its own supply. Check pin positions and voltage in the board's documentation before wiring (the curriculum's 11.5 rule; the pin facts are per board and are not given here). Never run address-scanning code on a board. Do not write to on-board storage (SPI flash, eMMC, boot partitions) until the recovery path of your plan has been tested on that board. See the university safety rules. C1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A dark room is visible; a gated block is not. Ms Achieng can see that a room's lights are off. A gated block can return plausible values (zeros) and accept writes that vanish, so nothing shows that anything is off (R10).
    • 

  • The plan is not written once. A building plan belongs to the building. A devicetree may be edited by every loader on the way, and different firmware versions can hand the same board different trees; the plan must record which tree it read (checksum in R3's log).
    • 

  • Room numbers are not "old" and "new". The architect's numbering in the story sounds historical. Bus addresses are a second, simultaneous view used by other masters: DMA engines on such a bus use bus addresses, so a driver may need to translate in both directions.
    • 

  • A lost key can be copied; a bricked board may need tools. In the story a locksmith can always make a new key. A board whose boot flash is overwritten may need a boot-mode jumper, a USB recovery tool or an external flash programmer, and some locked boards have none of these.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


Trap 1: a device that "reads zeros" because its clock is off


Symptom. The driver's probe reads an ID register and gets 0; configuration registers read back 0 after being written. Cause. The block's clock is gated or its reset is held (or its domain or supply is off), and this chip answers such accesses with zeros. How to find it. Look up the node's clocks, resets, power-domains and *-supply in the inventory (Listing 1), read the providers' state registers, and compare with the known-good system (on Linux, its clock and regulator debug views, if enabled; to be checked for your kernel). Fix. Enable the providers in stage order before the first access to the block, and write the configuration after that. The model (Listing 7) shows all three states:




Note the second line: with the clock on but the reset still held, the ID is still zero. A first "fix" that enables only the clock looks as if it failed, and the debugging goes the wrong way. Note also the lost divisor: configuration written before the block was alive does not survive. R10 F4-35's forensic lab is a full case of this trap from a register dump.



Trap 2: wrong bus-address translation


Symptom. On QEMU raspi3b: a synchronous external abort whose fault address is the devicetree's reg value (R11). On a board: possibly silence, possibly something worse (unverified box above). Cause. The driver used reg directly; the node's parent bus has a non-empty ranges. How to find it. Compare the fault address with the node's reg, and run Listing 1: its "CPU address" column shows the translated value (R9: serial@7e201000 → 0x3f201000, watchdog@7e100000 → 0x3f100000). Fix. Route every address through one translation function; never print or use a raw reg in a driver. R9 R11




A second form of the same trap goes the other way: a DMA engine on a translated bus needs the bus address of a memory buffer, not its CPU address. The devicetree's dma-ranges describes that direction; the bring-up plan's section 7 asks for it. D1

With a recovery path. The same update, but the plan's section 2 was followed first: the flash contents were saved and their checksum compared with the original before anything was written. Writing the backup back restores the board (R15):




Symptom on a real board: no output at all after an update, often with no way to load anything new. Cause: the only boot source was overwritten and no second source was prepared. Summary


    

  • From PC to SoC board, three things carry over: drivers written against a block's specification, the universal method, and DR402's devicetree reading (its tool ran unchanged on the board model's tree).
    • 

  • Five things change: no enumeration; a boot ROM and a vendor boot chain run first; clocks, resets, pins, regulators and power domains become explicit; DMA may be non-coherent; documentation depends on the vendor.
    • 

  • The first day on a board is not for code: console, recovery path, known-good boot, devicetree dump, then the plan.
    • 

  • A dependency inventory from the tree gives every device's translated address, its providers and a providers-first order; it cannot say what firmware left running or what the silicon does when something is off.
    • 

  • Trap 1: a gated or held block can read as zeros and drop writes, in this chapter's model; real chips vary.
    • 

  • Trap 2: reg under a bus with ranges is a bus address; on QEMU raspi3b using it aborted with the bus address as the fault address.
    • 

  • Trap 3: an update without a recovery path turned a working board model into a silent one; a verified backup restored it.
    •
