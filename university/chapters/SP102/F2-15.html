Story hook

Analogy

Ama is new in the kitchen and has to find the jar of cumin. She puts her finger on the first jar of the spice shelf and moves it along, one jar at a time, reading each label, until she finds cumin or her finger reaches the painted line after the last jar. The head chef does not care whether the shelf is a straight board, a turning rack or a row of hooks: "Start at the first one, move to the next one, stop at the line." The same instructions work everywhere.

The kitchen also keeps a book of standard procedures: "find", "count", "sort", "add up". Nobody writes their own sorting method for each shelf; they point the procedure at the first jar and the line after the last, and it does the work.

In C++ the finger is an iterator, the pair "first jar, painted line" is a range, and the book of procedures is the header <algorithm>.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain iterators and half-open ranges [begin, end), and why end() must never be dereferenced.
  • Use std::sort, std::find, std::count, std::count_if, std::max_element and std::accumulate instead of hand-written loops.
  • Pass a function to an algorithm to change what it compares or tests.
  • Use the C++20 std::ranges versions with projections.
  • Explain why std::remove does not shrink a container, and use erase–remove or std::erase.
  • Show what a std::vector iterator is made of in memory.
Jargon box
Iterator
Simple words: A thing that points at one element of a container and can move to the next. Analogy: A finger on a jar of the spice shelf. Precise: An object that supports at least *it (the element) and ++it (the next element), and can be compared with another iterator; stronger categories add --, jumps such as it + n, and contiguous storage. D1, D2
Half-open range
Simple words: "From this element up to, but not including, that one". Analogy: From the first jar to the painted line after the last jar. Precise: A pair of iterators [first, last); first is included, last is not, and first == last means an empty range. end() is one past the last element and must not be dereferenced. D2, R1
Algorithm
Simple words: A ready-made procedure from the standard library that works on any range. Analogy: A page in the kitchen's procedure book. Precise: A function template in <algorithm> or <numeric> that operates on a range given by iterators (or, in std::ranges, by a whole container), with documented complexity. D1, D2
Predicate
Simple words: A function that answers yes or no about an element. Analogy: The question "is this order quick?" asked of each ticket. Precise: A callable returning a value convertible to bool, passed to algorithms such as std::count_if; a comparison function for std::sort must define a strict weak ordering. D2
Projection
Simple words: Telling a ranges algorithm which part of each element to look at. Analogy: "Sort the tickets, but read only the table number." Precise: An extra argument of the std::ranges algorithms, such as a pointer to member &Order::table, applied to each element before comparing (R3). D2, R3
Erase–remove idiom
Simple words: Removing elements in two steps: move the keepers forward, then cut off the tail. Analogy: Sliding the good jars to the left, then taking the leftover jars off the end of the shelf. Precise: v.erase(std::remove(v.begin(), v.end(), x), v.end()); since C++20, std::erase(v, x) does both (R6). D2, R6

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

To look through a row of things, you point at the first one, look, then point at the next one, and stop when you reach the end. A computer program does the same thing. The "pointing finger" is called an iterator. The end is marked by a spot just after the last thing, so you know when to stop without falling off the shelf.

Some jobs are needed again and again: find something, count something, put things in order, add things up. C++ comes with these jobs already written and well tested. You hand them the start and the end, and they do the job. That is quicker than writing your own loop, and it has fewer mistakes, because many people have used these jobs for years.

One job surprises everyone once: "remove". It slides the good things to the front, but it does not throw away the end of the shelf. You have to do that yourself.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Iterators and ranges

Every standard container offers begin() and end(). In Listing 1, one function template print_all walks a std::vector<int> and a std::list<int> with the same three operations: compare with end(), read *it, step with ++it. std::distance(begin, end) gave 4, the number of elements; *(end - 1) is the last element, 8. *end() would read past the last element and is undefined behaviour (F2-12). R1, D2

Iterators come in categories. A vector's iterator can jump (it + 2) and its elements are stored next to each other: R1 printed vector iterator is contiguous: 1. A list's iterator can only step forwards and backwards: list iterator is random access: 0. The algorithms state which category they need. std::sort needs random access, so it does not accept a std::list; a list has its own sort() member. R1, D2

The everyday algorithms

Listing 2 sorts five cooking times, then asks the standard library for the total, the largest and the number of fives. It printed sorted: 5 5 8 12 20 and total 50, longest 20, fives 2. std::find returns an iterator to the first match, or end() when there is none: "20 found at position 4; 99 found: 0". Always compare the result of find with end() before dereferencing it. R2, D1

Algorithms take functions as arguments. std::sort(orders.begin(), orders.end(), takes_longer) sorts by "takes longer goes first", giving curry(25) rice(15) soup(8) salad(6), and std::count_if(..., is_quick) counts orders of at most 10 minutes: 2. The comparison must behave like "less than": it must return false for equal elements. A comparison using >= breaks that rule, and std::sort may then misbehave. F2-17 replaces these small named functions with lambdas written in place. R2, D2, D3

Ranges (C++20)

Writing v.begin(), v.end() every time is repetitive and allows mixing up two containers. The std::ranges algorithms take the whole container. They also take a projection: in Listing 3, std::ranges::sort(orders, std::ranges::less{}, &Order::table) sorts by table number (2:salad 4:soup 7:rice 9:curry), and std::ranges::find(orders, "rice", &Order::dish) finds the order whose dish is rice: table 7. R3, D2

"remove" does not remove

An algorithm sees only iterators, not the container, so it cannot change the container's size. std::remove(first, last, 0) moves every element that is not 0 to the front, keeps their order, and returns the new logical end. The elements after it are leftovers. In the forensic run the vector still had size 6: 4 9 2 7 2 7. The fix is to erase from the returned iterator to end() (erase–remove), or, since C++20, std::erase(v, 0). It returned 2 removed and left 4 9 2 7 with size 4. R5, R6, D2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Guaranteed complexity

The standard specifies the work each algorithm may do. std::find, std::count and std::accumulate look at each element at most once (linear). std::sort does O(N log N) comparisons. A hand-written sort is rarely better and often wrong. The Core Guidelines advise preferring standard algorithms to hand-written loops, both for correctness and because the name says what the loop does. D2, D3

Iterator invalidation

An iterator refers to a place in the container's storage. F2-12 showed that a vector moves its elements to a new block when it grows (R5 there). Every iterator into the old block then dangles, just like a reference. After erase, iterators at or after the erased point are invalid too. Rules of thumb: do not keep iterators across operations that add or remove elements; take begin() again afterwards, or keep an index. D2

Types inside algorithms

std::accumulate(first, last, 0) adds in the type of its third argument: 0 is an int, so adding double values would truncate each partial sum to int. Write 0.0 for doubles. std::count returns the iterator's difference type (a signed integer, long on this platform), which is why the lab's slow_orders returns long and not int. D2, R7

Note

In this build, ignoring the result of std::remove (Listing 5) produced no warning, even with -Wall -Wextra -Werror (R5 built and ran). The compiler did not protect us here. F2-16 shows the attribute [[nodiscard]] that makes such warnings possible for your own functions. R5

How the hardware actually does it

For a std::vector, an iterator is in practice a pointer with a class around it. Listing 4 measured it: R4



A vector iterator is 8 bytes, the size of a pointer on this machine, and the address of the element it refers to is exactly data() + 2. Moving it with ++ adds sizeof(int) bytes, as pointer arithmetic does (F2-19 explains this). The four ints span 16 bytes with no gaps. A list iterator is also 8 bytes, but it points at a separately allocated node; two neighbouring elements were not 8 bytes apart, because each node lives wherever the allocator put it. Moving a list iterator means loading the next node's address from memory. R4


This difference matters for speed: the processor's caches fetch memory in blocks, so walking a vector reads neighbouring bytes that are already fetched, while walking a list jumps around memory. SP302 measures this; here, remember "prefer std::vector unless you have a measured reason not to". D1



Not verified — check before relying on this
That libstdc++'s std::vector<int>::iterator is a class holding a single pointer, and that a list node stores links to its neighbours next to the value, is inferred from the sizes and addresses in R4, not read in the library's source or documentation. The standard requires only the iterator category, not the representation. Read the library headers (SP202) before relying on the layout. R4, D2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A finger cannot point at a jar that is no longer there, but an iterator can: after a vector grows or an element is erased, an old iterator may refer to freed memory (Layer 3), and nothing warns you. D2
    • 

  • Ama sees the painted line; an iterator does not. end() is only a value to compare with. Stepping past it is undefined behaviour, not a bump into a wall. D2
    • 

  • In the kitchen, "remove the cancelled tickets" means they are gone. std::remove only rearranges (R5); the analogy hides exactly the trap of the forensic lab. R5

Transition box

Where you came from: F2-14 wrote your own templates; F2-12 showed references that dangle when storage moves. What carries over: the standard algorithms are function templates exactly like larger, instantiated per iterator type; iterators dangle under the same rules as references. What changes: instead of indexing into one container type, you describe a range with two iterators and let a named algorithm do the loop; correctness now depends on respecting [begin, end) and on reading what an algorithm promises (for example, remove does not change the size). R5, D2 What comes next: F2-16 asks what a function should do when it cannot do its job, such as find with no match: return a marker like end(), return an empty std::optional, or throw an exception.

Summary


    

  • An iterator points at an element and moves with ++; a range is [first, last), and end() is never dereferenced.
    • 

  • Iterator categories decide which algorithms apply: std::sort needs random access.
    • 

  • Prefer std::find, count, count_if, max_element, accumulate, sort to hand-written loops; pass functions to change their behaviour.
    • 

  • std::ranges takes whole containers and projections.
    • 

  • std::remove does not change the size: use erase–remove or std::erase.
    • 

  • A vector iterator is an address in one contiguous block; a list iterator follows links between nodes (R4).
    •
