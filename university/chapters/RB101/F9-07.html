Story hook

Ravi is finally allowed to ride his bicycle to the park on his own. Before he goes, his grandmother asks him the same questions as always.

"Brakes?" Ravi squeezes them: they work. "Helmet?" On his head. "Where will you ride?" On the path, not the road. "How fast?" Slowly near the playground, where small children run around.

Ravi rolls his eyes a little. He knows all this! But on the way, a ball bounces onto the path, and a little boy runs after it. Ravi squeezes the brakes, stops in time, and smiles at the boy.

That evening he tells his grandmother. She nods. "Checking the brakes is boring," she says, "until the day you need them."

Robots are the same. Before a real robot moves, someone checks the stop, the space, the speed and the battery. Every time. This chapter is about those boring, important checks.

Safety

The robot safety rules of this university (F9). (1) Simulator first, always. A real robot runs a program only after that program's simulator lab has passed. (2) Adult supervision for every real-robot activity of learners below level L3, which includes everyone in this course. (3) A clear zone around the robot: no people, pets or loose objects inside while it can move. (4) An emergency stop within reach of the supervising adult, tested before every session; it must be a physical switch that does not depend on the robot's program. (5) Speed limits configured in the program before the first run, starting at the lowest useful speed. (6) Battery rules: only battery-powered kits designed for children of the learner's age; no LiPo batteries and no mains electricity in this course; follow the kit maker's instructions for charging, storing and disposing of batteries. Moving parts can pinch fingers, and motors can start unexpectedly: keep fingers away from wheels whenever the robot is switched on. S1

Jargon box
Emergency stop
Simple words: a big, easy button or switch that makes the robot stop at once. Analogy: the brakes on your bicycle. Precise: a stop control that removes the power to the robot's motors (or otherwise forces them to stop) immediately and stays active until a person deliberately resets it; in this university it must be a physical switch that does not rely on the robot's program. S1
Latched
Simple words: once switched on, it stays on until someone switches it off on purpose. Analogy: a door that locks when it shuts, and needs a key to open. Precise: a state that, once set by an event, remains set after the event ends, until an explicit reset. R1
Speed limit
Simple words: the fastest the robot is allowed to go, whatever its program asks for. Analogy: riding slowly near the playground. Precise: an upper bound applied to every motor command before it is sent, so that no part of the program can command a faster speed. R1 S1
Clear zone
Simple words: an empty space around the robot that people stay out of while it can move. Analogy: the path being clear before you ride fast. Precise: a marked area around a robot that is kept free of people, animals and objects whenever the robot is powered and able to move. S1
Supervision
Simple words: a grown-up who watches, helps and can stop everything. Analogy: Amara's uncle walking next to her bicycle. Precise: the continuous presence of a responsible adult who has read the kit's instructions and controls the emergency stop during a real-robot activity. S1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A real robot is a machine with motors. Motors can pinch fingers. A robot can drive off a table, or into a person's foot. Robots do not know they are hurting anyone. So people must keep things safe.

The first rule is simulator first. You already did this in F9-05 and F9-06. If a program bumps or zig-zags in the simulator, it will surely misbehave on a real robot too. Fix it where mistakes are free. (Our simulators are this course's own small programs. Engineers use big 3D robotics simulators such as Gazebo, which you meet in later courses. D14)

The second rule: a grown-up is always there when a real robot moves. Like Amara's uncle walking next to her bicycle. The grown-up has read the kit's instructions and can stop everything.

The third rule: a clear zone. Before the robot moves, clear a space on the floor. Mark it with tape if you can. Nobody stands inside it while the robot is switched on. No toys, no pets, no feet.

The fourth rule: an emergency stop within reach. It is like your bicycle's brakes. It must stop the robot at once, and stay stopped until someone resets it on purpose. You test it before every session, as Ravi squeezed his brakes.

The fifth rule: a speed limit. Your program might ask for "speed 9" by mistake. The speed limit says: never more than 5, whatever happens. Start slow. You can always go faster later.

The sixth rule is about batteries. Use only kits made for children your age, with the batteries the kit maker says. No big rechargeable batteries of the kind used in drones (LiPo), and never anything plugged into the wall socket. Charge and store batteries exactly as the kit's instructions say.

These rules can feel boring. But remember Ravi's grandmother: checking the brakes is boring until the day you need them.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

This university's rules for every robot lab are: simulator first; real-robot labs only after the simulator lab passes; an emergency stop within reach; a clear zone; speed limits configured; adult supervision for learners below L3; and the battery rules of the safety section (for this course: kits designed for children, no LiPo, no mains). S1

Two of these rules can also be built into the program, and Listing 1 shows how. The speed limit clamps every command: std::min(wanted, speedLimit) picks the smaller of the two, so a mistaken "9" becomes "5". The stop is latched: once set, nothing in the loop clears it, and it overrides every other decision. R1

The order inside the loop matters. Listing 1 checks the stop at the start of every round, before computing the speed, so the robot stops in the same round the stop is pressed. A program that checks the stop only now and then keeps driving in between, as the forensic lab shows. R2

A software stop is not enough on its own. If the program itself has a bug, or the computer freezes, a software check may never run. That is why this university requires a physical kill switch for real robots: a switch that cuts motor power without asking the program. S1 The software stop in Listing 1 is an extra layer, not a replacement.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Engineers who build safety-related machines work with written functional-safety standards. IEC 61508, for electrical, electronic and programmable systems, is one of them. They also make a careful study of what could go wrong, called a hazard analysis. They add safety mechanisms such as watchdogs, redundancy and safe states, so that one failure is not enough to hurt someone. The F11 faculty of this university teaches this in depth (SS402); what the standards themselves say is taught there from their official documents, not here. S1 §5.16 S1 §4.9

How the hardware actually does it

On a real kit, the safest stop is the simplest: a physical switch that cuts the motors' power. This university requires one, because it works whatever the program does. Worked example

Ines tests Listing 1's speed limit and stop by hand. The plan wants speeds 2, 4, 9, 9, 9, 9 … The limit is 5. The stop is pressed at step 6.

  1. Steps 1–2: wanted 2 and 4, both below 5, so allowed 2 and 4. Position: 2, then 2 + 4 = 6.
  2. Steps 3–5: wanted 9, but the limit allows only 5. Position: 6 + 5 = 11, 16, 21.
  3. Step 6: the stop is pressed. Speed 0. Position stays 21.
  4. Steps 7–10: the stop is latched, so speed stays 0. Position stays 21.

Without the speed limit, steps 3–5 would have moved 9 + 9 + 9 = 27 instead of 5 + 5 + 5 = 15: 12 units further, at nearly double speed. The program's log matches Ines's table exactly. R1

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Ravi sees the ball; a robot may not. Ravi braked because he saw the boy. A simple robot has no sensor for people at all. That is why a human, not the robot, holds the emergency stop. S1
    • 

  • Bicycle brakes need a hand; a robot's software stop needs working software. If the program has a bug or freezes, a stop that lives in the program may never run. A real robot needs a physical switch that cuts motor power, which does not depend on the program. S1 R2
    • 

  • A bicycle slows when you stop pedalling; a robot does not get tired. A robot keeps doing exactly what its program says, at full power, until something stops it. S1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot keeps moving for a while after the stop is pressed. Cause: the program checks the stop only now and then. How to find it: in the log, find rounds where the button is down but the robot still moves. Fix: check the stop at the start of every round, and keep a physical switch as well. See the forensic lab. R2
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot starts moving again by itself after a stop. Cause: the stop is not latched: it was set back to "not stopped" when the button was released. How to find it: the log shows stop? yes followed later by no without a reset. Fix: only a deliberate reset may clear the stop.
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot drives faster than the limit. Cause: one part of the program sends commands without going through the limit. How to find it: compare the "allowed" column with the limit. Fix: apply the limit in one place, right before every command goes to the motors.
    • 

  • Symptom: during a real session, the emergency stop does nothing. Cause: it was not tested before the session. How to find it: rule 4: test it first. Fix: stop the session, switch off with the main switch, and let the adult check the kit against its instructions before anyone continues.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. The button is "down" from step 6, but "stopped?" only becomes "yes" at step 8. In steps 6 and 7 the robot still moved at speed 5.


Extra travel. Position 21 at step 5; it ends at 31. Extra: 31 − 21 = 10 units (2 steps × 5). A correct stop would have kept it at 21, as R1 shows.


Root cause. The button works (the "button" column changes at step 6). The program only looks at it on steps 4 and 8 (every 4th step: step % 4 == 0). The first check after step 6 is step 8.


How the fault was made. The Lab Engineer copied Listing 1 and made the stop check run only when the step number is a multiple of 4, keeping the real button in a separate variable so the log can show both.


Fix and prevention. Check the stop at the start of every round, before computing the speed (Listing 1, lines 18–20). On a real robot, also have a physical power switch that does not depend on the program at all. Prevention: every stop test logs both the button and the program's belief, and fails if they ever differ for even one step.


Wrong answers and why. "The button is broken": the log shows it went down at step 6. "The speed limit failed": the speed never exceeded 5.
