Story hook

Lucía has a new challenge: ride her bicycle along a chalk line in the playground. Her cousin Arjun watches.

On her first try, Lucía drifts a little to the right. She panics and yanks the handlebars hard to the left. Now she shoots over the line to the left side! She yanks hard to the right. Over the line again. Left, right, left, right: she zig-zags across the line, and almost falls off.

"Gently!" Arjun calls. "When you are a little off, steer a little. When you are far off, steer more."

On her second try, Lucía drifts right again. This time she steers back just a bit. She comes closer to the line, steers a bit less, closer still, a tiny bit less… and rolls smoothly along the chalk.

Notice what she did: she sensed how far off she was, compared it with where she wanted to be, and corrected by an amount that fit. Then again, and again. That loop is the heart of this chapter. Today you build it.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain a control loop as sense, compare, correct, again and again, using the bicycle picture.
  • Calculate by hand a few steps of a robot steering back to a line with the rule "steer = − gain × error".
  • Write (change) a C++ program that steers a simulated robot back to a line.
  • Read a text plot of a robot's distance from the line and recognise over-correction.
  • Fix a zig-zagging robot by choosing a gentler gain, and show the fix with a real run.
Jargon box
Control loop
Simple words: a loop that keeps checking how far you are from what you want, and corrects a bit each time. Analogy: riding a bicycle and correcting your balance. Precise: a feedback loop that repeatedly measures a system's output, compares it with a target, and computes a command that reduces the difference. D1 §1.3
Feedback
Simple words: using what you just measured to decide your next action. Analogy: seeing that you drifted, and steering because of it. Precise: the use of a measured output of a system to influence its input; a loop with feedback is called closed loop, one without is open loop (taught in F9-16). D1 §1.1
Target (setpoint)
Simple words: where you want to be. Analogy: right on the chalk line. Precise: the desired value of the controlled quantity; here, a distance from the line of zero. Engineers call it the reference or the setpoint. D1 §11.1
Error (control)
Simple words: how far you are from where you want to be. Analogy: how far Lucía's wheel is from the chalk. Precise: the difference between the measured value and the target; in this chapter, the measured distance from the line (target zero), positive to the right and negative to the left. Control books usually subtract the other way round (error = target − measured) and then use a plus sign in the rule; both describe the same correction. Not the same as a program "error" or bug (F0-08). D1 §1.6 D1 §11.1
Gain
Simple words: a number that says how strongly to correct. Analogy: how hard Lucía turns the handlebars for each bit she is off. Precise: the constant that multiplies the error to produce the correction; with this chapter's error, the proportional rule is correction = − gain × error. D1 §11.1
Proportional correction
Simple words: a little off, steer a little; far off, steer a lot. Analogy: Arjun's advice, "gently". Precise: a control law in which the command is proportional to the error; it is the P of PID, taught fully in F9-17. D1 §1.6 D1 §11.1
Over-correction
Simple words: correcting so hard that you shoot past the target to the other side. Analogy: Lucía yanking the handlebars and zig-zagging. Precise: a response that crosses the target (overshoot) because the correction is too large for the system; with this chapter's rule it happens when the gain is larger than 1. R3
Line follower
Simple words: a robot that drives along a line on the floor. Analogy: Lucía riding along the chalk. Precise: in this course, a mobile robot that uses one or more floor sensors (reflective light sensors, F9-02) to measure its position relative to a line and steers to stay on it. D11 Applications

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Think about riding a bicycle. You never sit perfectly still on it. You wobble a little, notice, and correct. Wobble, notice, correct. You do it so often that it feels like one smooth thing. Engineers call this a control loop.

A line-following robot does the same. Its sensor senses how far it is from the line. Its program compares that with where it wants to be: right on the line. The difference is called the error: "I am 8 units too far to the right." Then the robot corrects: it steers back toward the line.

But how hard should it steer? That is the whole secret of this chapter. The robot multiplies the error by a number called the gain. A small gain means "steer gently". A big gain means "steer hard".

The rule is: steer = − gain × error. The minus sign means "steer the opposite way to the error". If you are off to the right, steer left. If you are off to the left, steer right.

Try it with gain 0.5. You are 8 to the right. Steer = − 0.5 × 8 = − 4. So you move 4 to the left, and now you are 4 to the right. Next time: steer − 2, now you are 2 off. Then 1. Then a half. You glide smoothly onto the line, like Lucía's second try.

Now try gain 1.8. You are 8 to the right. Steer = − 1.8 × 8 = − 14.4. You move 14.4 to the left. But you were only 8 off! Now you are 6.4 to the left of the line. You over-corrected. Next round you steer hard right, and shoot over again. That is the zig-zag of Lucía's first try.

So the lesson is surprising: steering harder does not get you there faster. Too strong, and you zig-zag. Gentle is better here. Finding a good gain is called tuning, and you will do a lot of it.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A control loop measures the output of a system, compares it with a target (engineers say setpoint), and computes a correction from the difference. Because the measurement is fed back into the decision, this is called feedback, or closed-loop control. D1 §1.1 D1 §1.3

Our rule makes the correction proportional to the error: correction = − gain × error. This is proportional control, the P of the PID controller you will study in RB202. D1 §1.6 D1 §11.1 (Control books write the error as target − measured, e = r − y, and the rule as correction = gain × e. With our error, measured − target, the same correction needs the minus sign.)

Our simulator is the course's own teaching tool, and it is deliberately simple. Later courses test robots in a full robot simulator such as Gazebo. D14 Each step, the robot moves sideways by exactly the steering amount. So the new distance is: new distance = distance − gain × distance = (1 − gain) × distance. You can check this against the printed numbers: with gain 0.5 the distance halves each step (8, 4, 2, 1 …); with gain 1.8 it is multiplied by −0.8 each step (8, −6.4, 5.12 …). R1 R2

That one number, (1 − gain), explains everything in our simulator. Listing 2 tries seven gains and shows it: R3

  • Gain between 0 and 1: (1 − gain) is between 0 and 1, so the distance shrinks without crossing the line (0 crossings for 0.2, 0.5 and 1.0). A smaller gain is slower: gain 0.2 is still 0.55 away after 12 steps.
  • Gain between 1 and 2: (1 − gain) is negative, so the robot crosses the line every step, but the swings shrink (12 crossings for 1.5 and 1.8).
  • Gain 2: the robot jumps from +8 to −8 and back forever (still 8.00 away after 12 steps).
  • Gain above 2: the swings grow. With gain 2.2 the robot is 71.33 away after 12 steps: it has driven off the course. Too much correction is worse than none.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious: why gain 1.0 is not "perfect" in real life. In our simple simulator, gain 1.0 lands exactly on the line in one step. Real robots are not that tidy. Their sensors report a little late, the computer needs time to calculate, and their motors take time to respond. D1 §2.3 D1 §11.5 Listing 3 makes one small change: the robot steers using the reading from one step ago. Now gain 1.0 swings to ±8 forever (7 crossings in 20 steps), gain 0.5 crosses the line 5 times before settling, and only the gentler gain 0.25 approaches without crossing. R4 Control books explain that even a small delay can make a feedback loop oscillate, and that a larger proportional gain makes a loop more oscillatory. That is one reason engineers tune gains carefully and test in simulation first. D1 §10.1 D1 §11.1 Also, a real line-following robot cannot slide sideways: it turns, and then drives at an angle, so its distance changes over several steps. D2 §13.1 RB202 models that properly.

How the hardware actually does it


A real line follower cannot measure "8 units right" directly with one light sensor; one sensor only says "line" or "floor" (F9-02). To turn, the program sets the two wheel speeds differently (F9-03). D8 §17.1.20 Real designs can use a row of light sensors side by side. From which sensors see the line, the program works out where the line is under the robot. One sensor maker's software computes this as a weighted average, and its documentation suggests the result for this kind of control loop. D15 readLine() The control loop is the same idea: measure the error, multiply by a gain, correct. A kit's sensor layout and the numbers it reports come from its own documentation, recorded in the dossier.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lucía corrects all the time; the program corrects in steps. A computer loop runs at separate moments and sees only what its sensor measured at that moment. Between steps it is blind. D1 §11.5
    • 

  • Lucía's eyes see the line ahead; our sensor sees only "how far off now". A rider looks ahead and turns early for a bend. Our robot reacts only to the error it has already got. R1
    • 

  • Lucía learns; the gain stays fixed. After one zig-zag, Lucía steered more gently by herself. The robot will zig-zag forever with gain 1.8 until a person changes line 9. R2
    • 

  • Real robots have delays and limits. Our simulator moves the robot sideways instantly by exactly the steering amount. Real loops have delays, and a real command has limits (a motor can only go so fast). Listing 3 shows that even a one-step delay changes which gains work. R4 D1 §2.3 D1 §10.1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot zig-zags across the line. Cause: gain too strong (between 1 and 2 here). How to find it: in the text plot, the * jumps from one side of | to the other every step; the distances change sign. Fix: lower the gain. R2
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot flies off the course, further each step. Cause: gain above 2 in this simulator. How to find it: the distances grow; the * disappears from the plot. Fix: a much smaller gain. R3
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot drifts further away smoothly, never coming back. Cause: the minus sign was forgotten: steer = gain * error steers away from the line. How to find it: the distance grows without changing sign. Fix: put the minus sign back: steer opposite to the error.
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot takes very long to reach the line. Cause: gain too small (0.2 is still 0.55 away after 12 steps). How to find it: the distance shrinks only a little each step. Fix: raise the gain a little, and watch for crossings. R3
    •

Summary


    

  • A control loop senses, compares with a target, corrects, and repeats.
    • 

  • The error is how far off you are; here it is the distance from the line.
    • 

  • Proportional correction: steer = − gain × error. The minus sign steers back toward the line.
    • 

  • In our simulator, the new distance is (1 − gain) times the old one.
    • 

  • Gentle gains (below 1) approach smoothly; gains between 1 and 2 zig-zag; above 2 the robot flies off.
    • 

  • Over-correction shows in a plot as the robot crossing the line at every step.
    • 

  • Delays in real robots make strong gains even riskier: tune in a simulator first.

Measure. −6.40 ÷ 8.00 = −0.8; 5.12 ÷ −6.40 = −0.8; −4.10 ÷ 5.12 ≈ −0.8. The size 0.8 means each swing is a bit smaller; the minus sign means the robot ends up on the other side each time.


Root cause. The gain 1.8 is too strong. With steer = −1.8 × error, the robot moves 1.8 times as far as it is off, so it overshoots by 0.8 times the error: new distance = (1 − 1.8) × old = −0.8 × old. A noisy sensor is ruled out: the swings are perfectly regular (exactly −0.8 each time), and this simulator has no noise.
