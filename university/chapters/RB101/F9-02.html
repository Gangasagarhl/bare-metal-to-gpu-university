Story hook

Ravi rides his bicycle along a painted line in the school yard. His teacher has made a game: stay on the white line all the way to the gate.

Ravi keeps his eyes on the line. When the line looks a little to the left, he steers a little left. When the sun goes behind a cloud, everything looks darker. For a moment the grey yard and the line look almost the same. Ravi slows down and looks harder.

His friend Noor rides with her eyes closed for one second, to try it. She wobbles at once. "I can't feel the line!" she laughs.

Without senses, you cannot follow a line. A robot is the same. Today you give your robot its senses, and you find out why a robot's eyes are never perfect.

What you will be able to do

  • Name three kinds of robot sensors and what each one notices.
  • Explain what a sensor reading is, and why two readings of the same thing can differ a little.
  • Choose a threshold that separates "line" from "floor" using measured readings.
  • Run a simulated light sensor and read its log.
  • Diagnose a robot that "sees a line everywhere" from its log.
Jargon box
Reading
Simple words: the number a sensor gives when the robot asks it "what do you notice now?" Analogy: how bright the line looks to Ravi at one moment. Precise: one measurement value produced by a sensor at one moment, in the sensor's own units. D3 §2 D10 §2.1.1.4
Light sensor
Simple words: a sensor that tells how bright something is. Analogy: your eyes telling light from dark. Precise: a sensor whose output changes with the amount of light reaching it; a reflective sensor pairs its light detector with a small light source (in the example datasheet an infrared one, which people cannot see) and reports how much light the surface below reflects. D11 Description
Distance sensor
Simple words: a sensor that tells how far away the nearest thing in front is. Analogy: judging with your eyes how far the wall is. Precise: a range sensor; some send out a pulse of sound or light and time the echo that comes back from an object. D12 §1.1 D13 §3.6.2
Bump sensor
Simple words: a sensor that only says "I touched something" or "I did not". Analogy: the skin on your hand feeling a wall. Precise: in this course, a contact switch that closes (or opens) when pressed; its reading has only two values. (This is the course's own definition of the word, so it has no source tag.)
Sensor noise
Simple words: small random wobbles in a sensor's readings. Analogy: the line looking slightly different every time a cloud moves. Precise: the part of a measurement that differs from the true value in ways that change from reading to reading; even very precise instruments have some. D10 §2.1.1.4 D3 §1
Threshold
Simple words: a dividing number: below it means one thing, above it means another. Analogy: "if the yard looks this dark or darker, I am on the line". Precise: a fixed value a program compares a reading with to turn it into a yes/no decision.
Calibrate
Simple words: to measure known things first, so you can set your numbers well. Analogy: looking at the white line and the grey yard before the race starts. Precise: to determine how a sensor's readings relate to known conditions (here: readings on floor and on line) and set the program's constants from those measurements. D10 §2.3.1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

You have many senses. Your eyes see light. Your ears hear sound. Your skin feels touch. A robot has sensors instead. Each sensor notices only one kind of thing.

A light sensor notices how bright something is. Point it down at the floor. A white floor bounces back lots of light. Dark tape bounces back little. So the sensor can tell "floor" from "line".

A distance sensor notices how far away a wall is. A bump sensor is the simplest of all. It is a tiny button on the front. When the robot touches a wall, the button is pressed.

When the program asks a sensor, the sensor answers with a number. That number is called a reading. A bright floor might give a big reading. Dark tape might give a small reading.

Here is the surprise. Ask the same sensor about the same floor twice, and the numbers can be a little different. The light changes. The robot shakes a tiny bit. These little wobbles are called noise. Real sensors always have some.

So how does the robot decide "line or floor"? It uses a dividing number called a threshold. If the reading is below the threshold, it says "line". Otherwise it says "floor".

A good threshold sits in the middle, far from both kinds of reading. Then a small wobble cannot fool the robot. Ravi does the same when the cloud comes: he looks for "clearly darker", not "a tiny bit darker".

Layer 2 — the student explanation

A sensor turns something in the world into a value the computer can read. A light sensor's output changes with the amount of light that reaches it. A reflective sensor has a small light source and a light detector side by side. Pointed down, it measures the light reflected by the floor: light surfaces reflect more than dark ones. D11 Description, Applications

Some distance sensors send out a pulse of sound or light and time the echo that comes back from an object. D12 §1.1 D13 §3.6.2 In this course, a bump sensor is a switch: its reading has only two values, pressed or not pressed.

Every real measurement differs a little from the true value, and repeated measurements of the same thing come out slightly different. Measurement scientists call these differences random errors; robotics researchers call them noise and count them among the reasons a robot is never quite sure about its world. D10 §2.1.1.4 D3 §1 This course models a reading as "true value plus a random error". Our simulator does exactly that: line 15 of Listing 1 adds a fixed list of small wobbles to the true brightness. The list is fixed so that every run prints the same numbers, which makes the lab repeatable. R1 This simulated sensor is the course's own teaching tool; full robot simulators such as Gazebo (later courses) play the same role for whole robots. D14

To turn a reading into a decision, the program compares it with a threshold. You set a good threshold by calibrating: measure on the floor, measure on the line, and pick a value between the two groups, with room on both sides for noise. Measuring known conditions first is what calibration means. D10 §2.3.1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Engineers describe noise with numbers: how big the wobbles are on average, and how they spread. That needs probability, which the university teaches in MA202. Later courses then combine several noisy sensors into one better estimate (the Kalman filter, in RB302). Some errors are not random at all: a sensor might always read a bit too high. That kind of error is called a bias, and calibration is how you find it and reduce it. D10 §2.1.1.3 D10 §2.3.1

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a light sensor is a part whose electrical behaviour changes with light, for example a phototransistor. D11 Description The robot's small computer measures an electrical signal that depends on it, and an analog-to-digital converter turns that signal into a number. D1 §1.3 How big the numbers are, and which light colours the part responds to, depends on the exact part, and only its datasheet can tell you. This chapter does not quote numbers for any real sensor (AH-21); our simulator's numbers are made up for the simulator and labelled "simulator units".

Worked example

Lucía calibrates her robot's light sensor. She reads the floor seven times and the line three times. Here are her numbers (the same ones our simulator printed, R1):

  • Floor readings: 83, 80, 80, 77, 84, 75, 82.
  • Line readings: 20, 20, 21.

Step 1. Find the darkest floor reading: the smallest floor number is 75.

Step 2. Find the brightest line reading: the biggest line number is 21.

Step 3. Any threshold above 21 and up to 75 sorts all these readings correctly. That is a wide gap.

Step 4. Choose the middle, so noise has the most room on both sides: (21 + 75) ÷ 2 = 96 ÷ 2 = 48. Our listing uses 50, which is also safely in the gap: 50 − 21 = 29 units above the brightest line reading, and 75 − 50 = 25 units below the darkest floor reading.

Step 5. Check a bad choice. A threshold of 76 would call the floor reading 75 a "line". A single wobble would fool the robot. That is why "in the middle" beats "just past the edge".

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Your eyes see a whole picture; a simple light sensor sees one spot. Ravi sees the whole line ahead and where it bends. Our light sensor only reports one number for the spot right under it. It cannot tell where the line goes next. R1
    • 

  • Your brain hides noise from you; a robot gets it raw. You do not notice your eyes' tiny wobbles. A robot's program receives every wobble in the number and must deal with it, for example with a well-placed threshold. D10 §2.1.1.4
    • 

  • Sensor errors are specific and measurable. "Eyes get tired" is vague. A real sensor's errors can be measured and written down, and a careful engineer does that during calibration. D10 §2.1.1.3 D10 §2.3.1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot says "LINE" on every square. Cause: the threshold is above all the readings. How to find it: compare the threshold with the floor readings in the log. Fix: calibrate again and pick a value between the two groups. The forensic lab shows a real run. R2
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot never sees the line. Cause: the threshold is below all line readings. How to find it: look at the line squares in the log; their readings are above the threshold. Fix: raise the threshold into the gap.
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot sometimes flickers between LINE and floor on the same square. Cause: the threshold is right at the edge of one group, so noise pushes readings across it. How to find it: look for readings close to the threshold. Fix: move the threshold to the middle of the gap.
    • 

  • Symptom: the comparison is the wrong way round (dark squares say floor). Cause: > written instead of <. How to find it: the decisions in the log are exactly backwards. Fix: remember: dark means small readings.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Root cause. Line 12 sets threshold = 90. Every reading in the log (the largest is 84) is below 90, so every square is called LINE.


Evidence. The readings are the same as in the healthy run (83, 80, 80, 77, 20, 20, 21, 84, 75, 82), so the sensor works. Only the decisions changed. The comparison of the two listings shows one changed number on line 12.


Fix. Recalibrate and choose the middle of the gap, for example 48 or 50. Verify: run again; exactly squares 4, 5 and 6 say LINE.


How the fault was made. The Lab Engineer copied Listing 1 and changed only the threshold on line 12 from 50 to 90 (and the comment on line 1).


Wrong answers and why. "The sensor is broken": its readings match the healthy run. "The noise is too big": the noise is identical to the healthy run. Prevention: after every change, run a test with known squares and check that line and floor are both recognised.
