Story hook

Amara is learning to ride a bicycle in the park. Her uncle walks next to her. At first she wobbles a lot.

Then something changes. Amara starts to notice things. She sees the path bend. She feels the bicycle lean a little to the left.

Each time, she decides what to do. "Lean back a little. Turn the handlebars a bit." Then her arms and legs do it.

Notice, decide, do. Notice, decide, do. She does this again and again, many times each minute, without thinking about it.

"You are riding!" her uncle shouts. Amara laughs. She did not know it yet, but she had just shown him how a robot works.

Jargon box
Robot
Simple words: a machine that notices the world, decides what to do, and then moves or changes something, again and again. Analogy: a body with senses, a brain and muscles. Precise: a machine that measures its surroundings with sensors, computes a decision with a program, and acts on the world with actuators, in a repeating loop. D2
Sense–think–act
Simple words: the three jobs a robot does over and over: notice, decide, do. Analogy: Amara noticing the bend, deciding to steer, and steering. Precise: a way to describe a robot's work as a repeating cycle of measuring (sense), computing a decision (think) and commanding its actuators (act). D2
Sensor
Simple words: a part that lets a robot notice something, like light or distance. Analogy: your eyes, ears and skin. Precise: a device that measures a physical quantity and turns it into a signal or number the robot's computer can read. D2
Actuator
Simple words: a part that makes the robot move or change something. Analogy: your muscles. Precise: a device that turns a command from the computer into motion or another physical effect; an electric motor is one example. D2
Simulator
Simple words: a program that pretends to be the robot and its world, so you can test safely. Analogy: practising bicycle balance on a game screen before the real park. Precise: software that computes how a model of a robot and its surroundings would behave, step by step, so programs can be tested without real hardware. In this course, the simulators are tiny C++ programs written by this university. R1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Your body has three kinds of parts that work together. Your senses notice things. Your brain decides. Your muscles move. A robot has the same three kinds of parts.

A robot's senses are called sensors. A sensor might notice light, a bump, or how far away a wall is. A robot's brain is a computer running a program. A robot's muscles are called actuators. The robots in this course use electric motors as their muscles.

Here is the important part. A robot does not sense once and then stop. It senses, thinks and acts, and then it does it all again. It keeps going round the circle in Figure 1, like Amara on her bicycle.

Why does it need to go round again? Because the world changes. When the robot moves, the wall gets closer. The line on the floor bends. If the robot never looked again, it would not notice.

So what is not a robot? Think of a toaster with a timer. It heats the bread for a fixed time. It does not look at the bread. It does not notice if the bread is already brown. It acts, but it does not sense and decide. So most people would not call it a robot.

Now imagine a cleaning robot that rolls across a floor. When it bumps a chair, a bump sensor notices. Its program decides to turn. Its motors turn it. Then it does it all again. That fits sense, think, act.

Some machines are in between. Imagine a door that opens when you walk up to it. It senses you and acts. But it only does one tiny thing. People argue about whether that is a robot. That is fine! In this course, we will ask a clear question: does it sense, think and act, again and again?

You learned in F0-10 that robots are computers with muscles. In this chapter you add the senses and the circle. In the next chapters you will meet each part closely.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Robotics books describe a robot as a machine with three groups of parts. Sensors measure the world. A computer runs a program that turns measurements into decisions. Actuators turn decisions into motion. D2

The program runs these three jobs in a loop. Each time round the loop, the program reads the sensors, decides, and sends commands to the actuators. The action changes the robot's position, so the next sensor reading is different. D2 D1

This "act, then measure again" idea is called feedback. You will meet it properly in F9-05. The listing in this chapter already shows it in a tiny way: the robot measures the wall every step, so it stops at the right place. R1

In this course the robot and its world live inside a simulator. A simulator is a program that pretends to be the robot and the world. Our simulators are tiny C++ programs that this university wrote and ran. They print what happens, step by step, as text. R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Engineers do not always split a robot into exactly three neat boxes. Big robots run many loops at once. A fast inner loop might keep a motor at the right speed. A slower outer loop might plan a path across a room. Each loop still senses, decides and acts. You will see loops inside loops in later courses, starting with RB202. D1 D2

How the hardware actually does it

In a real small robot, a sensor is an electronic part that changes a voltage when the thing it measures changes. A small computer called a microcontroller reads that voltage as a number. The program compares the number with a rule and decides. Then the computer switches power to a motor through a motor driver, because the computer's own pins cannot power a motor directly. D4 D2 You will see the parts of real kits in later courses (RB201). The exact parts of any kit come from its own datasheets, never from memory.

Worked example

Let us be the robot from the listing, by hand. A corridor has squares numbered 0, 1, 2 and so on. A wall stands at square 10. The robot starts at square 0. Its rule is: "If the wall is 2 squares away or closer, stop. Otherwise roll forward one square."

  1. Step 1. Sense: the wall is 10 − 0 = 10 squares away. Think: is 10 two or less? No. Act: move to square 1.
  2. Step 2. Sense: 10 − 1 = 9 squares. Think: no. Act: move to square 2.
  3. Steps 3 to 8 work the same way. After step 8 the robot stands on square 8.
  4. Step 9. Sense: 10 − 8 = 2 squares. Think: is 2 two or less? Yes. Act: stop.
  5. Step 10. Sense: still 2. Think: yes. Act: stay stopped.

So the robot stops at square 8, two squares before the wall. The program run below agrees, step by step. R1

Try one change in your head. What if the rule said "3 squares or closer"? Then step 8 would see 10 − 7 = 3 and stop. The robot would stop on square 7.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A robot has no common sense. Amara knows a puddle is not a wall. Our robot only knows the one number its rule looks at. If a box appears in the corridor, Listing 1 does not notice it, because its "sensor" only measures the wall. R1
    • 

  • A robot senses only what its sensors measure. Your eyes see colour, shape and movement at once. A real sensor measures one kind of thing, in its own way, with small errors. You will see these errors in F9-02. D3
    • 

  • A robot's loop runs in steps. Your body feels balance all the time. A computer program reads its sensors at separate moments, one loop round after another. Between two readings it does not know what changed. D1
    • 

  • A robot does not learn by itself in this course. Amara gets better with practice. Our robots do exactly what the program says, every time. If the rule is wrong, the robot repeats the same mistake until a person fixes the program.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot drives into the wall (the log says "BUMP"). Cause: the robot sensed once, before the loop, and never looked again. How to find it: look at the distance in the log. If it never changes while the robot moves, the sensing is not inside the loop. Fix: move the sensing line inside the loop. The forensic lab below shows this from a real run. R2
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot never moves at all. Cause: the stop rule is too wide, for example "stop if the wall is 20 squares away or closer" in a corridor of 10. How to find it: the first log line already says "stop". Fix: make the number in the rule smaller than the starting distance.
    • 

  • Symptom: you call a machine a robot because it moves. Cause: looking only at the "act" part. How to find it: ask "what does it sense, and does that change what it does?" Fix: check all three jobs and the loop.
    • 

  • Symptom: the program does not build after you edit it. Cause: a missing ; or brace. How to find it: read the first error message; it names a line. Fix: compare your line with the listing. F0-36 shows how to read such messages calmly.
    •

Summary


    

  • A robot senses, thinks and acts, again and again.
    • 

  • Sensors are its senses, the program is its brain, and actuators (mostly motors) are its muscles.
    • 

  • The loop matters: after acting, the robot must sense again, because the world has changed.
    • 

  • A machine that only acts on a timer, without sensing, is not a robot in this course's sense.
    • 

  • In this course you test robots in tiny text simulators first.
    • 

  • Sensing once, outside the loop, makes a robot blind to its own movement.

Forensic lab answer key


Root cause. Line 9 of sensed_once.cpp measures the distance before the loop starts. Inside the loop nothing ever updates distance, so the robot always believes the wall is 10 squares away.


Evidence. Every log line says "wall is 10 squares away", while the square goes 1, 2, … 9. Those two cannot both be true. At step 10 the log shows "BUMP!".


Fix. Move line 9 inside the loop, as line 10 of Listing 1. Verify by running again: the distance should go down by one each step, and the robot should stop on square 8 as in R1.


How the fault was made. The Lab Engineer moved the sensing line of Listing 1 above the loop, made the loop run 12 times, and added a "BUMP" message for a move into the wall.


Wrong answers and why. "The rule <= 2 is wrong": no, the same rule works in Listing 1. "The loop runs too many times": more steps only make the bump visible; with correct sensing the robot keeps saying "stop". Prevention: in every log, check that what the robot senses changes when the robot moves.
