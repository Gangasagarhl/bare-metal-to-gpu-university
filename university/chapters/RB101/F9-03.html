Story hook

Kofi is pushing his little sister Ines around the yard in a cart. The cart has two big wheels at the back. Kofi has an idea: "Ines, you push the left wheel with your hand, and I'll push the right one."

When they push equally hard, the cart rolls straight. When Ines pushes harder than Kofi, the cart swings to the right. When Kofi pushes harder, it swings left. When nobody pushes, it stops.

"We're steering without a steering wheel!" Ines shouts.

Then Kofi counts out loud each time the right wheel goes all the way round. "One, two, three…" After ten turns they have rolled past the tree. "Next time," says Kofi, "I'll know how far ten turns is."

Kofi and Ines have discovered how the robots in this course move and measure. Their hands were the motors. Kofi's counting was the encoder.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a motor does for a robot, using the "muscles" picture.
  • Predict which way a two-wheeled robot goes when its wheels turn at different speeds.
  • Calculate how far a wheel rolled from its turn count.
  • Run a simulation of two wheel motors with turn counters.
  • Diagnose a robot that drives in circles from its encoder log.
Jargon box
Motor
Simple words: a part that turns electricity into turning. Analogy: a muscle that pulls when your brain tells it to. Precise: an electric motor converts electrical energy into rotation of its shaft; a robot's program controls it indirectly, through a motor driver that switches the power. D4
Motor command
Simple words: the instruction the program gives a motor, such as "turn this fast". Analogy: your brain telling a leg how hard to push the pedal. Precise: the value a control program sends to a motor driver; what the motor then really does also depends on the load, the battery and friction. D2
Encoder
Simple words: a sensor that counts how much a wheel has turned. Analogy: counting the turns of a wheel. Precise: a sensor on a motor or wheel shaft that produces counts as the shaft rotates; distance travelled is estimated from the counts and the wheel size. D2
Differential drive
Simple words: steering by turning two side wheels at different speeds. Analogy: Kofi and Ines pushing the two cart wheels. Precise: a wheeled robot with two independently driven wheels on one axle; equal wheel speeds drive straight, different speeds turn it toward the slower wheel's side. D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Your muscles move your body when your brain tells them to. A robot's muscles are its motors. A motor turns electricity into turning. Fix a wheel to the motor, and the wheel turns too.

Many small robots have two wheels, one on each side, each with its own motor. They have no steering wheel at all. They steer like Kofi and Ines's cart.

Both wheels turn at the same speed? The robot goes straight. The left wheel turns faster? The robot curves to the right. The right wheel turns faster? It curves to the left. Look at Figure 1 and try it with your hands on a toy car.

Here is a puzzle. The program tells both motors "go at speed 4". Does the robot surely go straight? Not always! One motor might be a bit weaker. One wheel might rub on something. The program told the muscles what to do, but it does not know what they really did.

That is why robots often count their wheel turns. A sensor called an encoder counts how far each wheel has turned. It is like Kofi counting "one, two, three" out loud.

Counting turns also tells you how far you went. If one turn of the wheel rolls 10 units along the floor, then 20 turns roll 20 × 10 = 200 units. You will do this sum in the worked example.

So the motor is a muscle, and the encoder is a sense that feels the muscle's work. Sense, think, act again.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

An electric motor converts electrical energy into rotation of its shaft. A small robot's computer cannot power a motor from its own pins, so it sends a motor command to a motor driver, which switches the battery's power to the motor. D4

A robot with two independently driven wheels on one axle is called a differential drive. Equal wheel speeds drive it straight; different speeds turn it toward the side of the slower wheel. D2 Our simulator does not model the turning itself. It compares how far each wheel rolled and names the direction, using exactly this rule (lines 22–30 of Listing 1). R1

A motor command is a wish, not a fact. What the motor really does also depends on load, battery and friction. An encoder measures what really happened: it produces counts as the shaft turns. From the counts and the wheel size, the robot estimates how far each wheel rolled. D2

In our simulator, one "count" is one full turn, and one turn rolls 10 simulator units. Real encoders give many counts per turn, and the real distance per turn comes from measuring the real wheel. D2 R1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. From the two wheels' distances and the gap between the wheels, an engineer can calculate how much the robot turned and where it is now. This is called odometry, and it needs a little geometry. You will do it in RB201 (F9-09). Engineers also use the encoder counts to control motor speed in a loop: "too slow? push harder". That is the PID loop of RB202. D2 D1

How the hardware actually does it

Inside a simple electric motor, current through coils of wire makes magnetic forces that turn the shaft. More power makes it try to turn harder or faster. A gearbox can sit between motor and wheel, trading speed for pulling force. D4 How fast or strong a real motor is, and how many counts per turn a real encoder gives, come only from that part's datasheet (AH-21). Our simulator's numbers are made up for practice.

Worked example

Arjun's simulated robot runs for 5 steps. Each step, the left wheel turns 4 times and the right wheel 2 times. One wheel turn rolls 10 units.

  1. Left count: 4 turns each step, 5 steps: 4 × 5 = 20 turns.
  2. Right count: 2 × 5 = 10 turns.
  3. Left distance: 20 turns × 10 units = 200 units.
  4. Right distance: 10 × 10 = 100 units.
  5. Direction: the left wheel rolled further, so the robot curved toward the slower, right side: it curves to the right.

The second line of the program's output says exactly this: "counts 20 and 10, wheels rolled 200 and 100 units -> curves to the right". R1

Now backwards: the encoder log says the right wheel counted 35 turns. How far did it roll? 35 × 10 = 350 units.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Wheels slip. An encoder counts turns of the wheel, not distance on the floor. On a slippery floor the wheel can spin without moving the robot, and the count still goes up. Kofi's counting would be fooled the same way on ice. D2
    • 

  • Your muscles have feeling built in; a motor does not. You feel how hard your legs push. A simple motor has no idea what it did. Only an added sensor, like an encoder, tells the program. D2
    • 

  • A motor never gets tired, but its battery runs down. Muscles tire and recover. A motor does the same work all day, but as its battery empties the same command may give less turning. That is one reason commands and real turns can differ. D4
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the robot curves although both motors get the same command. Cause: the motors do not really turn equally (weak motor, rubbing wheel). How to find it: compare the two encoder counts in the log. Fix: fix the mechanical problem, or later (RB202) let a control loop correct each motor's speed. See the forensic lab. R2
    • 

  • Symptom: you predict "curves left" but the robot curves right. Cause: mixing up which wheel is faster with which way it turns. How to find it: look at Figure 1 again: the robot turns toward the slower wheel. Fix: draw the robot from above and the two arrows before predicting.
    • 

  • Symptom: the distance is 10 times too big or too small. Cause: counts used as distance without multiplying by units per turn (or multiplied twice). How to find it: check the units in your sum. Fix: distance = turns × units per turn.
    • 

  • Symptom: the robot goes backwards. Cause: a negative command, or (on a real kit) a motor wired the other way round. How to find it: in the simulator, look at the command values; on a kit, an adult checks the wiring. Fix: change the sign of the command.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. Both motors were commanded 4/4 every step, but after 8 steps the left encoder counted 24 and the right 32.


Real turns per step. Left 24 ÷ 8 = 3; right 32 ÷ 8 = 4. The left wheel turns one less per step than commanded.


Direction. The left wheel is slower, so the robot turns toward the left: it circles to the left.


Root cause. The left motor (or its wheel) delivers less turning than commanded: in this simulation a weaker left motor. On a real robot, hypotheses to check would be: something rubbing the left wheel, a weaker or damaged left motor, a loose connection. A slipping wheel would not explain it, because slipping wheels still turn and are still counted.


How the fault was made. The Lab Engineer's program commands 4 turns for both wheels but adds only 3 per step to the left counter (a simulated weak motor), and prints the log.


Fix and prevention. Fix the mechanical cause. In software, compare the encoder counts every step and give the slower motor a bit more command until the counts match: a feedback loop, taught properly in RB202. Prevention: always log both commands and encoder counts.
