Story hook

Before Mei rides her bicycle to the new library for the first time, she and her aunt sit at the kitchen table with a paper map. Mei traces the route with her finger. "Along this street, turn right at the bakery, then left at the park…"

Her finger hits a dead end. "Oops! There's no road there." She laughs, goes back, and tries another turn. Nobody fell off a bicycle. Nobody got lost. The mistake cost two seconds.

On the real ride the next day, Mei knows the way. At one corner she almost turns the wrong way, but she remembers the dead end from the map, and goes the right way.

Practising on a map is cheap and safe. Robot engineers have a map like that too: a simulator. Today you program a robot through a whole course, in a simulator, and learn to fix its mistakes before any real wheel turns.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a robot simulator is and why robots are tested in one first.
  • Program a simulated robot through a course using the commands F, L and R.
  • Predict the robot's position and direction after a list of commands.
  • Read the simulator's printed map and result line to find where a program went wrong.
  • Diagnose a robot that bumps into a wall because of a left/right mix-up.
Jargon box
Grid world
Simple words: a pretend world made of squares, like a board game. Analogy: Mei's paper map with streets. Precise: a simulated environment divided into cells; each cell is free or blocked, and the robot moves from cell to cell. D3 R1
Command
Simple words: one instruction for the robot, like "go forward one square". Analogy: "turn right at the bakery". Precise: in this chapter's simulator, one character: F moves forward one cell, L turns 90 degrees left, R turns 90 degrees right, on the spot. R1
Heading
Simple words: the direction the robot is facing. Analogy: which way your bicycle's front wheel points. Precise: the robot's orientation; in the grid world one of east, south, west or north. "Left" and "right" are measured from the robot's own heading, not from the map. D2
Test run
Simple words: trying the program to see if it does what you wanted. Analogy: tracing the route on the map with your finger. Precise: an execution of a program with known inputs whose output is compared with the expected result. R1
Simulator
Defined in F9-01: a program that pretends to be the robot and its world. R1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A simulator is a program that pretends to be a robot and its world. Our simulator's world is a grid world: a board of squares. Some squares are floor. Some are walls. One is the start, S. One is the goal, G.

You program the robot with three commands. F means "forward one square". L means "turn left on the spot". R means "turn right on the spot". You write them in a row, like FFRFF, and the simulator follows them one by one.

The tricky part is left and right. They are the robot's left and right, not yours. Imagine sitting on the robot. If the robot faces down the map (south), its right hand points to the left of the map (west)! Mei had the same problem with her paper map when she turned it upside down.

The robot also senses. Before each step forward, it looks at the square in front. If it is a wall, the robot stops and reports a BUMP. In a real robot that would be a crash, so the simulator stops there and tells you exactly where it happened.

After the run, the simulator prints the map with your robot's path marked by stars (*). It also prints where the robot ended, which way it faced, and what happened: reached the goal, bumped, or ran out of commands.

Why bother with a simulator? Because mistakes are free in it. A real robot that bumps can break, or bump into a person. In the simulator you change a letter and try again. That is why every robot lab in this university starts in a simulator, and moves to a real robot only after the simulator lab passes (F9-07).

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Our simulator keeps the robot's state in three numbers: its row, its column and its heading (0 = east, 1 = south, 2 = west, 3 = north). A right turn adds 1 to the heading, and a left turn adds 3; the remainder after dividing by 4 keeps the heading between 0 and 3. R1

Turning right four times brings you back to where you started facing, and so does turning left four times. A left turn is the same as three right turns, which is why line 34 adds 3. R1

A forward command first senses the cell ahead, using two small tables of moves (one for rows, one for columns) indexed by the heading. If the cell is a wall, the run ends with a BUMP report; if it is the goal, the run ends with GOAL. R1

Notice what kind of program you write here: a fixed list of commands that never looks at the sensors to change its plan. Engineers call such a plan open loop: it works only if the world is exactly as you expected. D1 The robot of F9-04 and the line follower of F9-05 instead decided every round from what they sensed. Real robots combine both: a plan, and a control loop that corrects as they go. D2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Note

For the curious. Professional robot simulators model much more than squares: masses, friction, motors, sensors with noise, cameras, even light and shadows. They let engineers test the same program that will later run on the real robot. But every simulator is a model, and models leave things out. A program that works in simulation can still fail on hardware, which is why engineers test in simulation first and then carefully on the real machine. Later courses (RB303, RB401) use a full robot simulator, named and recorded in their dossiers. D2 S1

How the hardware actually does it

A real robot has no "squares". To move "forward one square" it would run both wheel motors until the encoders have counted the right number of turns (F9-03); to turn on the spot it would run one wheel forward and one backward. Because wheels slip and motors differ, a real robot drifts from its plan, and it needs sensors and control loops to correct (F9-05). D2

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The simulator's world is perfect; the real world is not. On a paper map, a street is exactly where it is drawn. In our grid, "forward" always moves exactly one square. A real robot's wheels slip and its motors differ, so it ends up slightly off, and the errors add up. D2
    • 

  • Mei could adapt on the ride; our command list cannot. Mei changed her mind at a corner. A fixed list of commands does not look at the sensors to change its plan; if one wall moves, the program bumps. D1 R2
    • 

  • A simulator only contains what someone put in it. Our simulator has no people, no pets, no slopes and no battery. Passing in it shows that your logic is right, not that a real robot will be safe. That is why real-robot labs still need the safety rules of F9-07. S1
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: "BUMP" right after a turn. Cause: left and right mixed up, thinking of the map's left instead of the robot's left. How to find it: read the heading in the result line ("while facing north") and compare with the heading you wanted. Fix: sit on the robot in your mind (or turn the paper) at each turn. See the forensic lab. R2
    • 

  • Symptom: "BUMP" after a straight run. Cause: one F too many. How to find it: count the free squares in Figure 1 before the wall. Fix: remove one F. The first program in grid_sim.in is an example. R1
    • 

  • Symptom: "program ended before the goal". Cause: the program is too short. How to find it: look at where the stars end on the map. Fix: add the missing commands from there.
    • 

  • Symptom: "unknown command". Cause: a small letter (f), a space or another character in the line. How to find it: the message names the character and the step. Fix: use only capital F, L and R, with no spaces.
    •

Troubleshooting.


    

  • A result with "program: " and nothing after it → an empty line in grid_sim.in → remove empty lines.
    • 

  • Strange results or a sanitizer error message after editing the map → a row is shorter than the others or the outer wall has a gap, so the robot looked outside the map → make all rows the same length with # all around.
    • 

  • "unknown command" → a space or small letter → see Common mistakes.

Forensic lab


Scenario. Sami copied the working route from a friend, but typed one letter from memory. "The robot crashed into a wall in the middle of the maze!" Sami says. You get the simulator's printout and nothing else.


Evidence pack. The printout of a real run of Sami's program (mixup.cpp, which runs Sami's command list on the same course) in this build: R2




Questions.


    

  1. Observation: at which step did the robot bump? Where was it, and which way was it facing?
    2. 

  2. Compare Sami's program with the working one (in the worked example) letter by letter. Where is the first difference?
    3. 

  3. Hypotheses: was it one F too many, or a wrong turn? Which line of the printout decides?
    4. 

  4. Root cause: which way should the robot have faced at that moment, and which turn gives that?
    5. 

  5. Fix and prevention: correct Sami's program, and suggest a habit that prevents left/right mix-ups.
    6. 



Method hints. The printout tells you the step number of the bump. Count that many letters into Sami's program. Then sit on the robot: which way is it facing just before the last turn?



Mini-project


Maze for a friend. Design a course of at least 7 by 9 squares with at least four turns needed. Solve it in the simulator. Then give the map (not the solution) to a friend, and watch them solve it. Write down every bump they get and why.


Rubric. 2 points: a valid map that the simulator runs, with your own working program. 1 point: at least four turns needed. 1 point: a written list of your friend's bumps and their causes. 1 point: one improvement to your map or instructions after watching.

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. The run ended with "BUMP at step 10: wall ahead at row 2, column 1 while facing north". The robot stood at row 3, column 1.


First difference. Sami: FFRFFRFFRFF…; working route: FFRFFRFFLFF…. The 9th letter differs.


Root cause. At (3, 1) the robot faces west. The route continues down the map (south). Left of west is south, so the turn must be L. Sami's R turned it to north, toward the wall at (2, 1), and the next F (step 10) bumped. The printout's "while facing north" proves a wrong turn rather than an extra F: an extra F would have bumped while still facing west.


How the fault was made. The Lab Engineer changed the 9th command of the working program from L to R and ran it through the same simulator (mixup.cpp).


Fix. FFRFFRFFLFFLFFFFLFFFFRFFRFFFF; verify that the run says "GOAL reached at step 29". Prevention: at every turn, write the heading before and after next to the program, or turn the paper map so the robot's heading points up.
