Story hook

Line 2 of the shared notebook says "pizza x=2". Four of the five friends have it in their copies; the friend writing the orders counted four copies, called it final, and the pizzeria was told. A few days later the notebooks say line 2 is "pizza x=3". Nobody broke a rule they knew about: every vote was cast correctly, every copy checked the line before. Yet an order that was final has been replaced.

This is the most famous trap in Raft, drawn in the paper as its Figure 8, and it is this course's forensic case: "Committed entry lost". The cure is one sentence in the paper, and in this chapter you will see exactly when it matters. Then we change the group itself — friends join and leave — and see why switching from the old group to the new one in one step can produce two order-writers at once.

What you will be able to do

  • State Raft's five safety properties and say which mechanism guarantees each.
  • Explain how the election restriction guarantees Leader Completeness.
  • Explain why a leader may commit only entries of its own term by counting replicas, using a concrete trace.
  • Diagnose the "committed entry lost" incident and name the violated rule.
  • Show by counting quorums why a direct configuration switch is unsafe and joint consensus is safe.
  • Trace a membership change from Cold through Cold,new to Cnew, including removing the leader.
Jargon box
Leader Completeness
Simple words: a new leader always already has every final entry. Analogy: whoever becomes order-writer has every order that was ever phoned to the pizzeria. Precise: if a log entry is committed in a given term, that entry will be present in the logs of the leaders for all higher-numbered terms. D1
State Machine Safety
Simple words: no two servers ever execute different commands at the same position. Analogy: no two friends ever phone different pizzas for line 2. Precise: if a server has applied a log entry at a given index to its state machine, no other server will ever apply a different log entry for the same index. D1
Leader Append-Only
Simple words: a leader never erases or rewrites its own lines. Analogy: the order-writer only adds lines. Precise: a leader never overwrites or deletes entries in its log; it only appends new entries. D1
Configuration
Simple words: the list of servers whose votes count. Analogy: who is in the group chat. Precise: the set of servers participating in the consensus algorithm; Raft stores configurations as special log entries, and a server uses the latest configuration in its log whether or not it is committed. D1
Joint consensus
Simple words: during a change, decisions need a majority of the old group and a majority of the new group. Analogy: while friends are joining the chat, an order needs most of the old members and most of the new member list. Precise: a transitional configuration Cold,new in which log entries are replicated to all servers of both configurations and agreement (for elections and commitment) requires separate majorities from Cold and Cnew. D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Two rules keep final orders final. First, you only let a friend become order-writer if their notebook is at least as up to date as yours — so a friend missing a final order cannot collect enough yeses. Second, the order-writer only calls a line final when it is a line from their own round and most friends have it. Old lines become final automatically once a newer line after them is final. Why? Because "most friends have this old line" can be true for a while and then stop being protected: a friend with a newer-round line that is different can still win votes. A line from your own round, held by most friends, cannot be beaten like that.

When the group itself changes, nobody switches lists at the same moment. So for a while, decisions need most of the old group and most of the new group. Only after that is settled does the new group decide alone.

The election restriction

A committed entry is on a majority. A candidate needs votes from a majority. The two majorities share a voter, which holds the committed entry. The election restriction makes that voter refuse unless the candidate's log is at least as up-to-date as its own (later last term; or same last term and at least as long). D1 The paper's proof then shows that a candidate whose log passes this comparison must contain every entry committed in earlier terms — provided "committed" is defined by the commit rule below. D1

Committing entries from previous terms

Read the forensic trace (it is also the evidence of the forensic lab, so you may prefer to do the lab first). The servers run with one fault switched on, and Figure 1 shows the key moments.

  • Term 1: n1 replicates entry 2 (t1 x=2) only to n2, then crashes (a, 310–320 ms).
  • Term 2: n5, elected with votes from n3 and n4 (whose last entry is also entry 1 of term 1), appends its own entry 2 (t2 x=3) while cut off, then crashes (b, 470–620 ms).
  • Term 3: n1 returns and wins; it brings n3 and n4 up to date, so entry 2 (t1 x=2) is on four of five servers. n1 "commits up to entry 2" at 880 ms (c). Then n1 crashes at 1,000 ms.
  • Term 4: n5 returns. Its last entry has term 2, later than everyone else's term 1, so the election restriction lets n2, n3 and n4 vote for it (d, 1,420 ms). The checker reports at once: "Leader Completeness: new leader n5 of term 4 lacks committed entry 2". n5 then overwrites entry 2 everywhere (e) and commits t2 x=3; "State Machine Safety: entry 2 applied as 't1 put x=2' earlier, and as 't2 put x=3' by n5". R1


Every election obeyed the restriction; the error was in step (c). Counting replicas proves that an entry is safe only for an entry of the leader's current term: once such an entry is on a majority, any candidate lacking it has a last term older than the current term (or the same term and a shorter log) and loses against the majority that holds it. An entry from an older term gives no such guarantee: a server with a different entry from a later old term (n5's t2 x=3) still wins against it. Raft's rule therefore is: a leader never commits entries from previous terms by counting replicas; only entries from the leader's current term are committed that way, and once one is, all earlier entries are committed indirectly by the Log Matching Property. D1 The lab implements the rule in advanceCommitIndex (raft_replication.h, lines 139–141).


In the fixed run (answer key), n1 still replicates entry 2 to four servers in term 3, but does not commit it: commit index 0 when it crashes. n5 then overwrites an uncommitted entry, which is allowed, and all states end as x=3 y=4 with no violation. No client was ever told that x=2 succeeded. R1



The no-op entry


Under the correct rule, a new leader cannot commit entries of earlier terms until it commits something of its own term. If no client sends a command, those entries stay uncommitted indefinitely. The fix is for each new leader to append a blank no-op entry at the start of its term. D1 The lab does this by default (Options::noopOnElection); the forensic story switches it off, as in the paper's figure, so that the window is visible. The no-op also closes the window for the faulty rule: in F5-21's fuzzer, the commitOldTermByCount fault was found in 2 of 150 targeted runs without the no-op and in none of 150 with it.



Changing the configuration


Servers cannot all switch from Cold to Cnew at the same instant. During a direct switch, some servers decide with Cold majorities while others already use Cnew majorities, and those can be disjoint. D1 Listing 3 counts it for growing {1,2,3} to {1,2,3,4,5}:




Three pairs of majorities share no server: for example, {1,2} can elect a leader under Cold while {3,4,5} elects another under Cnew, in the same term. Joint consensus requires a majority of both configurations during the transition; every such joint quorum intersects every Cold quorum, every Cnew quorum and every other joint quorum: zero disjoint pairs. R1


The procedure: the leader appends a Cold,new entry; every server uses a configuration as soon as it is in its log. Once Cold,new is committed (under joint rules), the leader appends Cnew; once that is committed, the old configuration is irrelevant and servers not in Cnew can be shut down. D1




In the run: n4 and n5 start empty and reject their first AppendEntries, then receive entries 1–4 in one batch. Cold,new commits at 315 ms (n1, n2, n3 are a majority of both {1,2,3} and {1,2,3,4,5}); Cnew commits at 325 ms. The second change removes the leader n1: Cnew {2,3,4,5} commits at 525 ms "without me", n1 steps down, and n3 wins term 2 at 712 ms with votes from the new configuration only. R1



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


The Leader Completeness argument


The paper proves Leader Completeness by contradiction. D1 In outline: suppose leaderT commits entry e in term T (by counting, so e is of term T, or it follows such an entry), and U is the first later term whose leader lacks e. Some voter v both accepted e from leaderT and voted for leaderU; it must have accepted e before voting (otherwise it would have rejected leaderT's AppendEntries with its higher term), and it still had e when voting (leaders between T and U all contained e, and followers only delete entries that conflict with a leader's). LeaderU's log was at least as up-to-date as v's. If the last terms are equal, leaderU's log is at least as long and, by Log Matching, contains v's log, including e. If leaderU's last term is greater, the leader that created that last entry contained e, so by Log Matching leaderU contains e. Contradiction. D1


Where does the old-term rule enter? In "leaderT commits e, so a majority holds e and every later leader up to U has it". If e is of an older term than T, a server holding a different entry of a term between e's term and T (n5's term-2 entry) can be more up-to-date than the voters, and the induction breaks — exactly the forensic trace.



Three practical issues of membership changes


    

  • New servers start empty. If they become voting members at once, commits may stall while they catch up. The paper adds them first as non-voting members that receive entries but are not counted. D1 The lab does not; in joint.out catching up took one batch.
    • 

  • The leader may not be in Cnew. It manages the cluster until Cnew is committed, without counting itself in majorities, then steps down. D1 Lab: membershipStep lines 73–77; quorum counts only members.
    • 

  • Removed servers can disrupt. A removed server stops receiving heartbeats, times out and sends RequestVote with a higher term. The paper's remedy is that servers ignore RequestVote while they believe a current leader exists (within the minimum election timeout of hearing from it). D1 The lab takes a simpler route: a server that is not in its own latest configuration never starts an election (tick checks isMember), which works only because the removed server's log contains Cnew.
    • 



Not verified — check before relying on this
Single-server membership changes (adding or removing one server at a time without joint consensus) are described in Ongaro's PhD dissertation, with a later published correction to that approach; neither is in the F5 source registry, and this chapter does not teach them. The statement that the Raft paper's version adds new servers as non-voting members first must be confirmed when the paper is opened (dossier gate G1).



One configuration rule the lab enforces


The leader refuses a new configuration change while another is in progress (handleClientRequest: joint configuration in effect, or an uncommitted configuration entry in its log). Two overlapping changes would reintroduce disjoint majorities. A server uses the latest configuration in its log, committed or not (refreshConfig), and after truncation a follower reverts to the previous one automatically, because refreshConfig runs on every log change.



How the hardware actually does it


Nothing in this chapter depends on hardware speed — that is the point of a safety argument. What the hardware decides is whether the persistent state really is persistent: the forensic case of this chapter needs only crashes and restarts, and every server in it restarted with exactly what it had written ("restarted from disk ... 2 log entries"). On real machines, the restart is only that clean if the log write and its flush completed before each reply, and if the device honours its flush (F5-15's unverified box). Membership changes do have a hardware side: adding a server means copying the whole state over the network and onto its disk, which is why the catch-up phase exists and why it is bounded by disk and network bandwidth that you must measure.



Worked example


Task. Five servers, current leader L = n1 in term 5. Log terms: n1 [1, 2, 4, 4], n2 [1, 2, 4], n3 [1, 2, 3], n4 [1, 2], n5 [1, 2, 3]. n1 was elected in term 5 and has appended nothing yet. (1) Which entries may n1 mark committed now? (2) Which server could win term 6 if n1 crashed now? (3) What should n1 do?


    

  1. matchIndex reports (assume n1 knows them exactly): entry 2 is on all five; entry 3 of term 4 is on n1, n2 only; entry 3 of term 3 on n3, n5 — different entries at index 3.
    2. 

  2. Commit by counting is allowed only for term-5 entries: n1 has none, so by the rule it may commit nothing new. (Entry 2 may already be committed from an earlier term's leader; n1 cannot conclude it by counting.)
    3. 

  3. Term 6 candidates: n2 [.., 4] at index 3: n3, n4, n5 (last terms 3, 2, 3) would vote for it: n2 wins. n3 [.., 3]: n4 and n5 vote yes (n5 equal) → 3 of 5, n3 could win too — and would then overwrite index 3 of term 4 on n1 and n2. That is allowed: the term-4 entry was never committed (it is on two servers only).
    4. 

  4. So n1 should append a no-op of term 5 at index 5 and replicate it. Once it is on a majority, entries 1–5 (including n1's term-4 entries at 3 and 4) are committed together, and n3 can no longer win: a majority has a last term of 5.
    5.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • "Most friends have the line" sounds final. In Raft it is final only for a line of the current leader's round. Everyday intuition says four copies out of five is safe; Figure 1 shows it is not.
    • 

  • Joining a group chat is instant. Configuration changes are log entries that take effect on each server at a different moment — that is why joint consensus is needed.
    • 

  • A friend who leaves stops talking. A removed server keeps running unless shut down, and can disrupt the group.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a committed entry is overwritten after several leader changes. Cause: committing old-term entries by counting. How to find it: enable a Leader Completeness check at every election (the cluster does this). Fix: count only current-term entries; append a no-op on election.
    • 

  • Symptom: committed entries stay uncommitted after a leader change until a client writes. Cause: correct rule without the no-op. Fix: no-op on election.
    • 

  • Symptom: two leaders during a membership change. Cause: direct switch, or two overlapping changes. Fix: joint consensus; refuse a change while one is pending.
    • 

  • Symptom: the cluster stops committing after adding servers. Cause: new empty servers counted as voters before they caught up. Fix: non-voting catch-up phase.
    •

Summary


    

  • Five properties: Election Safety, Leader Append-Only, Log Matching, Leader Completeness, State Machine Safety.
    • 

  • The election restriction makes voters refuse candidates whose logs are behind theirs.
    • 

  • A leader commits by counting replicas only entries of its own term; older entries are committed behind them. Breaking this lost a committed entry in the forensic trace.
    • 

  • A no-op entry at the start of each term lets a new leader commit earlier entries promptly.
    • 

  • A direct configuration switch allows disjoint majorities (3 pairs for 3 → 5 servers); joint consensus allows none.
    • 

  • Joint consensus: Cold,new (majority of both), then Cnew; servers use the latest configuration in their log; a leader not in Cnew steps down after it commits.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. "880 ms n1 term 3 LEADER commits up to entry 2 (t1 put x=2)": the leader of term 3 committed an entry of term 1, then on four servers (n1–n4).


Elections. Term 1: all voters empty. Term 2: n2 refused n5 ("its log is behind mine": n2 had entry 2 of term 1, n5 only entry 1); n3, n4 equal → correct. Term 3: n1 (2, t1) ≥ everyone → correct. Term 4: n5's last term 2 > voters' last term 1 → correct. No voter broke the restriction.


Hypotheses. (a) ruled out: every restart line shows the correct term and vote ("term 1, votedFor 1"; "term 2, votedFor 5"), and the term-3 refusals of n5 ("already voted in this term") show votes remembered. (b) ruled out: the followers rejected and repaired correctly at 865 ms and 1,425 ms. (c) confirmed.


Root cause. The rule: Raft never commits log entries from previous terms by counting replicas; only log entries from the leader's current term are committed by counting replicas, and earlier entries are then committed indirectly by the Log Matching Property. D1 The deployed servers ran with commitOldTermByCount, which disables the check in raft_replication.h lines 139–141: if (termAt(n) != st_.currentTerm && !faults_.commitOldTermByCount) continue;.


Timing of the reports. The cluster checks Leader Completeness when a leader is elected (1,420 ms) and State Machine Safety when an entry is applied; n5 applied its entry 2 only when it committed it at 1,440 ms.


Fix and prevention. Restore the rule and keep the no-op on election. The fixed run (below) has the same elections and the same overwrite, but entry 2 was never committed, no state machine applied x=2, and the checker finds nothing. Prevention: the fault is hard to find by random testing (F5-21: 0 of 100 random seeds) — test with a nemesis that targets leaders, with the no-op switched off in a test build.
