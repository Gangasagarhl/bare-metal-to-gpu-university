Story hook

After the two-pizza disaster, Tomás proposes a stricter rule: "Nobody orders until we have all heard from everybody." It works for three weekends. On the fourth, Mei's phone dies again, and Amara and Tomás wait, and wait. On Saturday evening Kofi has no pizza.

"Then let's wait for everybody except one," says Amara. "Two of three is enough." It works until the weekend when Amara and Mei disagree and Tomás's letter reaches each of them at a different moment. Amara counts Tomás and herself; Mei counts Tomás and herself; they order different pizzas.

Priya, who studies mathematics, listens to the whole story and says: "Stop looking for the perfect rule. Three researchers proved in 1985 that it does not exist — not if letters can be arbitrarily late and one of you may stop answering. Every rule either sometimes disagrees or sometimes waits forever." She is right, and this chapter explains her argument without the formulas, then lets a program check it on two tiny rules by trying every possible order of letters.

What you will be able to do

  • State the FLP impossibility result precisely: model, properties, and exactly what is impossible.
  • Explain valence (0-valent, 1-valent, bivalent configurations) and the two steps of the proof in words.
  • Use an exhaustive explorer to find bivalent initial configurations and the runs in which a protocol breaks agreement or termination.
  • Explain what FLP does not forbid, and how Paxos and Raft live with it: safety under any timing, termination only when timing behaves.
  • Evaluate a claim such as "our protocol always agrees and always finishes, even if a server fails" and say which assumption it must be making.
Jargon box
Asynchronous system
Simple words: a system where nobody can promise how long a message or a step takes. Analogy: the post can hold a letter for a day or a month, and nobody can tell you which. Precise: a model with no bound on message delay, no bound on relative process speeds and no synchronized clocks; every message is eventually delivered to a non-crashed receiver. D3
Configuration
Simple words: a snapshot of the whole system. Analogy: every friend's notes plus every letter still in the post. Precise: the internal state of every process together with the set of messages sent but not yet delivered (the message buffer). D3
Valence
Simple words: which final answers are still possible from a snapshot. Analogy: "from here, could we still end up with tomato? with mushroom?" Precise: a configuration is 0-valent if every decision reachable from it is 0, 1-valent if every reachable decision is 1, and bivalent if both are reachable. D3
FLP impossibility
Simple words: the proof that no fixed rule can always agree and always finish if one computer may stop and messages can be arbitrarily late. Analogy: Priya's theorem about the pizza rules. Precise: in the asynchronous model, no deterministic protocol solves consensus (agreement, validity and termination in every fair run) if even one process may crash. D3 D4
Fair run (admissible run)
Simple words: a run in which nothing is delayed forever, except by a crash. Analogy: every letter is delivered eventually, however late. Precise: an infinite run in which at most one process crashes and every message sent to a live process is eventually delivered. D3
Partial synchrony
Simple words: the network is usually well behaved, but you do not know when the bad periods end. Analogy: the post is reliable except during storms, and nobody announces the storms. Precise: a timing model in which bounds on delays and speeds exist or hold eventually but are not known in advance; consensus protocols guarantee termination only in such periods. D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Imagine you are waiting for a friend's letter. It has not come. Is your friend's phone broken, or is the post slow? You cannot know. If you decide now, you might decide without your friend's vote, and your friend might decide differently. If you wait, you might wait forever. Three researchers proved that every possible rule runs into one of these two problems in some unlucky week. Real systems live with it: they never decide wrongly, and they accept that in a very bad week they may decide late.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The statement, carefully

The result of Fischer, Lynch and Paterson (FLP) says: in an asynchronous system in which at most one process may crash, there is no deterministic protocol that guarantees agreement, validity (they use a weaker "non-triviality": both decisions must be possible for some inputs) and termination in every fair run. D3 Read every word:

  • Asynchronous: no bound on delays. If you had a known bound, "silent for longer than the bound" would mean "crashed", and the problem would be solvable. D3
  • At most one crash: even the mildest failure, one process stopping silently, is enough.
  • Deterministic: each process's next step is a function of its state and the message received. Randomized protocols are not covered. D4
  • Guarantees termination in every fair run: the result is about the worst run, not the typical one. It does not say consensus usually fails; it says no protocol can promise it never stalls.

The model is generous to the protocol: messages are never lost (only delayed), never corrupted, and every message is eventually delivered. D3 Adding losses only makes things harder.

Valence: how undecided is the system?

A configuration is a snapshot of all process states plus the messages still in the post. From a configuration, many futures are possible, depending on which message is delivered next. Call a configuration 0-valent if every future that decides decides 0, 1-valent if every such future decides 1, and bivalent if both outcomes are still reachable. D3 A correct protocol must eventually reach a univalent configuration in every run (otherwise it never decides), and once univalent it stays so.

The proof in two steps

Step 1: some initial configuration is bivalent. Line up the initial configurations so that neighbours differ in one process's input (Figure 1). The all-0 configuration must decide 0 and the all-1 configuration must decide 1 (non-triviality and validity). If every initial configuration were univalent, somewhere along the chain two neighbours would have different valences, and they differ only in one process p's input. Now consider the runs in which p crashes before sending anything. The other processes see exactly the same messages from both configurations, so their run is the same, and they must decide the same value in both. That contradicts one being 0-valent and the other 1-valent. Hence, for a protocol that tolerates one crash, some initial configuration is bivalent. D3

Step 2: from a bivalent configuration, the scheduler can always reach another bivalent one. Take a bivalent configuration and any pending message e. The proof shows that among the configurations reachable by delivering e last (after any other steps), at least one is bivalent; otherwise one can find two configurations, one step apart, whose futures differ only by the steps of a single process, and crashing that process makes them indistinguishable — the same trick as in step 1. D3 A malicious scheduler therefore delivers every message eventually (the run is fair) but always at a moment that keeps the configuration bivalent (Figure 2). The run never decides.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


What FLP does not say


    

  • It does not say consensus is impossible in practice. It says termination cannot be guaranteed in every run of the asynchronous model. Runs that defeat a good protocol need the network to behave like a hostile scheduler, for as long as it wants.
    • 

  • It does not limit safety. A protocol may keep agreement and validity in every run, however bad the timing; Paxos and Raft do exactly that. D1 D2
    • 

  • It does not cover randomized protocols, or models with timing assumptions or failure detectors. D4
    • 




The three ways out


    

  1. Timing assumptions (partial synchrony). Assume that delays are bounded at least eventually, with bounds you do not need to know. Use timeouts to suspect crashes; a wrong suspicion must never break safety, only slow progress. The Raft paper states its timing requirement as an inequality: the broadcast time must be much smaller than the election timeout, which must be much smaller than the mean time between failures; when it holds, Raft elects a stable leader and makes progress. D1 "Paxos Made Simple" makes the same move: progress needs a distinguished proposer, and the paper notes that electing one reliably requires randomness or real time, for example timeouts, because of FLP. D2
    2. 

  2. Randomization. Let processes flip coins. The adversarial schedule must then defeat chance as well, and protocols exist that terminate with probability 1. Raft uses randomness in a milder way: random election timeouts make repeated split votes unlikely (F5-18 measures this), though correctness never depends on luck. D1
    3. 

  3. Failure detectors. Package the timing assumptions into an oracle that eventually suspects only crashed processes, and prove the protocol correct relative to the oracle's properties. D4 The heartbeat detector of F5-11 is an implementation of such an oracle that is right only when the network behaves.
    4.

Engineering consequences


FLP turns into four habits you will see in every remaining chapter:


    

  • Separate safety from liveness in the design and in the tests. F5-21's checker tests safety properties on every run, whatever the faults; liveness is measured only in the quiet period at the end of each run.
    • 

  • Timeouts are hints. A timeout may fire for a live process. The protocol must stay safe when it does (two candidates in F5-18, a deposed leader in F5-19).
    • 

  • Expect unlucky schedules in tests. A deterministic simulator with many seeds explores schedules a real network shows once a year.
    • 

  • Tune timing to make bad runs rare. Raft's randomized timeouts, Paxos's single distinguished proposer and random backoff (F5-17) are all ways to make the network behave like the friendly scheduler most of the time.
    • 




How the hardware actually does it


Asynchrony is not a mathematician's invention; it is how real machines behave in their bad moments. A process can be paused without warning for a long time: the operating system schedules other work, a virtual machine is paused or migrated by its host, a garbage collector or a page fault on a swapped-out page stops the program, a disk write blocks. Messages wait in socket buffers, in network-card queues and in switch queues, and a TCP retransmission after a loss adds delay. During such a pause, the paused process and its peers see only silence, exactly the indistinguishability that drives the proof. D4 No hardware gives a hard upper bound on these delays across a general-purpose stack; real-time systems obtain bounds only by controlling every layer (F9 and F10 courses show what that costs).


Watch out
The lab explores an abstract model, not a network. Its "delivery orders" include orders a real network would almost never produce; that is the point of an impossibility proof. Do not read its tables as probabilities.



Worked example


Task. A colleague proposes protocol W2 for a three-replica configuration store, because "it never blocks, even with a dead replica". Find a concrete run in which it breaks agreement, and say which FLP step predicted it.


    

  1. From the table, W2 with nobody silent shows "0/1!" for inputs 011: p1 = 0, p2 = 1, p3 = 1.
    2. 

  2. p1 holds its own 0. Whatever it receives first, its pair contains a 0, so it decides 0.
    3. 

  3. p2 holds 1. If p3's message (1) arrives first, p2 decides 1. If p1's message (0) arrives first, p2 decides 0.
    4. 

  4. Choose the order: p3 → p2 first. Then p2 decides 1 while p1 decides 0: agreement is broken. No message was lost and no process crashed; only the order mattered.
    5. 

  5. FLP step 1 predicted that a protocol which decides even with one silent process (W2 does) must have a bivalent initial configuration; 011 is one. A correct protocol would have to stay undecided from there until enough information arrives, and step 2 says a scheduler can keep it undecided forever. Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Real friends have clocks and calendars. A friend silent for a month is, in practice, gone. The asynchronous model deliberately forbids that inference; the analogy makes the model sound unreasonable when it is a worst-case abstraction.
    • 

  • "Unlucky week" suggests probability. FLP is about the existence of one bad run chosen by an adversary, not about how often bad runs happen. The lab's table counts reachable outcomes, not likelihoods.
    • 

  • Friends can talk again later. A process in the proof may exchange any number of messages; the impossibility does not come from too few rounds. The tiny protocols of the lab send once only to keep the search small.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a design review says "FLP means we can't build this". Cause: reading FLP as "consensus is impossible". Fix: restate it: no termination guarantee in the fully asynchronous model; design for safety always and liveness under stated timing assumptions (Raft's timing inequality).
    • 

  • Symptom: a protocol is "proven" to terminate by a test that ran 10,000 times. Cause: confusing typical runs with all runs. How to find it: look for the adversarial schedule — what if this specific message is delayed until just after the timeout? Fix: state the timing assumption under which termination holds.
    • 

  • Symptom: a timeout tuned "safe" by making it long, and the system breaks safety anyway after a long pause. Cause: correctness that depends on the timeout being right (for example a lease used without a fencing token). Fix: make safety independent of timing; use timeouts only to trigger actions whose safety is protected by quorums and terms.
    • 

  • Symptom: the explorer runs forever after you extend it. Cause: the state space explodes with more processes, more rounds or more values. Fix: memoize configurations already explored, as F5-21's linearizability checker does, and keep the model small.
    •

Summary


    

  • FLP: in an asynchronous system with one possible crash, no deterministic protocol guarantees agreement, validity and termination in every fair run.
    • 

  • Configurations are 0-valent, 1-valent or bivalent; a protocol that tolerates a crash has a bivalent initial configuration (chain argument), and a scheduler can keep it bivalent forever.
    • 

  • The lab's exhaustive search shows both escapes of tiny rules: W3 never disagrees but blocks on a silent process; W2 never blocks and can disagree.
    • 

  • FLP limits termination, not safety: Paxos and Raft keep safety always and terminate when timing behaves.
    • 

  • Ways out: timing assumptions (timeouts, partial synchrony), randomization, failure detectors.
    •
