Story hook

The friends give up on clever voting rules and borrow an idea from Hana, who runs a busy café. In her café every order form has a number printed on it, and the numbers only go up. "Here is how we choose Kofi's pizza," she says. "Whoever wants to suggest a pizza first takes a new form with a number bigger than any they have used and asks the others: will you ignore all forms with smaller numbers? And if you have already said yes to some pizza on an older form, tell me which. If most of you answer, the suggester must propose the pizza from the highest older form anyone reports — not their own favourite — unless nobody reported one. Then the suggester asks most of you to say yes to that form. Once most of you have said yes to the same form, that pizza is chosen, forever."

It sounds bureaucratic. It is also the protocol that Leslie Lamport described in "Paxos Made Simple", and it never lets Kofi get two pizzas — whatever letters are late, lost or duplicated, and whoever falls silent. In this chapter you will run it, break it with one wrong comparison, and watch two eager suggesters keep each other from ever finishing.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe single-decree Paxos: roles, the two phases, and what each acceptor must remember on stable storage.
  • Explain why at most one value can be chosen, using the overlap of majorities and the "adopt the highest accepted value" rule.
  • Read a Paxos trace and decide which value is chosen, when, and whether any proposer knows it.
  • Diagnose a safety violation caused by an acceptor that compares with the wrong number.
  • Explain why two proposers can prevent progress, and how a distinguished proposer and randomized timeouts restore it.
  • Relate Multi-Paxos to the replicated log, as preparation for Raft.
Jargon box
Proposer, acceptor, learner
Simple words: the three jobs in Paxos: suggesting, voting, and finding out the result. Analogy: the friend with a new order form; the friends who sign forms; whoever needs to know the final pizza. Precise: proposers issue numbered proposals; acceptors respond to prepare and accept requests and are the processes whose majorities choose a value; learners find out which value has been chosen. One process may play several roles. D2
Ballot (proposal number)
Simple words: a number attached to every attempt, bigger for later attempts. Analogy: the number printed on an order form. Precise: a number from a totally ordered set, unique to one proposer's attempt (for example, a round number combined with the proposer's id); a proposal is a pair (number, value). D2
Promise
Simple words: "I will ignore anything older than your number". Analogy: "I'll ignore forms with smaller numbers than yours". Precise: an acceptor's response to prepare(n) when n exceeds every number it has responded to: it promises never to accept a proposal numbered below n and reports the highest-numbered proposal it has accepted, if any. D2
Chosen value
Simple words: the value that can never change any more. Analogy: Kofi's pizza, once most friends signed the same form. Precise: a value is chosen when a single proposal with that value has been accepted by a majority of acceptors. D2
Distinguished proposer (leader)
Simple words: one friend who is the only one making suggestions. Analogy: the friend who writes the order. Precise: a single proposer selected to issue proposals, so that competing proposers do not keep invalidating each other's ballots; needed for progress, not for safety. D2
Multi-Paxos
Simple words: Paxos repeated for every slot of a log, with one leader doing the first phase once. Analogy: one friend writes the whole week's orders. Precise: the state machine implementation in which a sequence of Paxos instances decides successive log entries and a stable leader executes phase 1 once for all instances it has not yet decided. D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Every attempt to choose gets a number, and later attempts get bigger numbers. Before suggesting anything, you ask most friends to promise to ignore older attempts and to tell you about anything they already agreed to. If somebody already agreed to a pizza, you must suggest that same pizza. Because any two "most of the friends" groups always share at least one friend, a later attempt always hears about a pizza that most friends already agreed to. So once a pizza is chosen, every later attempt chooses the same one.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The protocol

Single-decree Paxos chooses one value. Each acceptor stores two things on stable storage: the highest ballot it has promised and the highest-numbered proposal (ballot and value) it has accepted. D2

  1. Phase 1a — prepare. A proposer picks a ballot n larger than any it has used and sends prepare(n) to the acceptors.
  2. Phase 1b — promise. An acceptor that receives prepare(n) with n greater than every prepare it has answered promises not to accept any proposal numbered less than n, and replies with the highest-numbered proposal it has accepted, if any. Otherwise it may ignore the request.
  3. Phase 2a — accept. If the proposer receives promises from a majority, it sends accept(n, v), where v is the value of the highest-numbered proposal reported in those promises, or its own value if none was reported.
  4. Phase 2b — accepted. An acceptor that receives accept(n, v) accepts it unless it has already promised a ballot greater than n.

A value is chosen when a majority of acceptors have accepted the same proposal. Learners find out by collecting "accepted" notifications from a majority (or from a distinguished learner). D2 In Listing 1, onPrepare, onPromise, onAccept and learn are these four steps, and the proposer counts its own "accepted" replies to find out (onAccepted).



Story 1 is Figure 1: one proposer, two round trips, chosen at 15 ms, known at 20 ms. Story 2 is Figure 2: P1's messages to a3 are slow; A is chosen at 15 ms by a1 and a2. At 30 ms P2 starts ballot 12 for value B. a1 and a2 report that they accepted (11, A); a3 has accepted nothing. P2 must adopt A, and ballot 12 chooses A again. P1's late prepare(11) and accept(11, A) reach a3 after it promised 12, and a3 ignores both. R1 Two ballots chose a value, and it is the same value: the safety property is "at most one value", not "at most one ballot".



Why at most one value is chosen


Lamport derives the protocol from one invariant: if a proposal with value v is chosen, every higher-numbered proposal that is issued has value v. D2 Here is the argument in words. Suppose (m, v) is chosen: a majority A accepted it. A proposer issues ballot n > m only after promises from a majority B. A and B share an acceptor x (F5-15). Either x accepted (m, v) before promising n — then its promise reports a proposal numbered at least m — or it promised n first, but then it could not have accepted m < n, a contradiction. So the proposer of n sees a reported proposal numbered at least m. By induction on ballot numbers between m and n, every such reported proposal has value v; the proposer adopts the highest one, so it proposes v. D2


Two details carry the whole proof: ballots must be unique (two proposers never use the same number, which is why the lab uses round × 10 + proposer id), and acceptors must keep their promises across crashes (stable storage). D2



Progress, and the duel


Safety never depends on timing. Progress does: "Paxos Made Simple" describes two proposers that keep issuing higher ballots, each one's prepare invalidating the other's accept, so that nothing is ever chosen. D2 Listing 3 measures a version of this. Each proposer starts again with a higher ballot if it has not learned a chosen value within a timeout after its prepare.




Read the trace first. P2's prepare(12) arrives between P1's promises and P1's accepts, so a1, a2 and a3 ignore accept(11, A). P1 times out at 14 ms and sends prepare(21) before P2's accepts arrive at all acceptors, and so on: ballots 11, 12, 21, 22, 31, 32, 41… R1 Notice line "value B is CHOSEN (ballot 12)" at 20 ms: a1 and a2 accepted (12, B) before P1's prepare(21) reached them. B is chosen, yet no proposer knows it, and every later ballot adopts B ("must adopt value B"): safety holds while progress, in the sense of a proposer learning the result, does not happen.


The statistics show what cures it in this simulator. With a 14 ms timeout, no proposer ever learned the value within 1,000 ms in any of 100 seeds, with 14,400 ballots started. The row "ONE proposer, timeout 14 ms" is the honest control: it fails too, because 14 ms is shorter than one complete round of two phases here (four message delays of 4–6 ms each), so a lone proposer also abandons every ballot before hearing back. Adding a random part of 0–30 ms to the timeout let a proposer learn the value in all 100 seeds, the slowest after 80 ms, with 296 ballots in total; a fixed 40 ms timeout also worked in all 100 seeds (slowest 30 ms). R1 Two lessons: a timeout shorter than the round trip destroys progress on its own; and randomization breaks the symmetry between competitors. The paper's real fix is the distinguished proposer: let one proposer, elected with timeouts or randomness, be the only one issuing ballots. D2



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


What must be on disk, and when


The acceptor's promised ballot and accepted proposal must be on stable storage before it sends the promise or the accepted reply. D2 If an acceptor promised 12, crashed, forgot, and then accepted (11, A), it would break exactly the step of the proof that says "it could not have accepted m < n". The lab's acceptor keeps its state in memory and the stories crash nobody, so this is not tested in Listing 1; F5-18's forensic lab shows the same class of bug in Raft. A proposer must also not reuse a ballot after a restart, so it either stores its highest ballot or derives a fresh one from something durable. D2



From one value to a log: Multi-Paxos


To implement a replicated state machine, run one Paxos instance per log slot. The expensive part, phase 1, does not depend on the value, so a stable leader can run phase 1 once for all slots it has not yet filled ("promise me ballot n for every slot from 17 onwards"), and then each new command costs only phase 2, one round trip to a majority. D2 When the leader changes, the new leader runs phase 1 for the open slots, learns any values that may have been chosen, re-proposes them, and fills gaps with no-op commands. D2 Everything Raft does has a counterpart here: Raft's term plays the role of the leader's ballot, its election plays the role of phase 1 for all future slots, AppendEntries plays phase 2, and the new leader's no-op entry is the same trick. Raft's authors present their algorithm as an alternative to Paxos designed for understandability, and specify the parts that Multi-Paxos descriptions leave open (how to elect, how to repair logs, how to change membership). D1



Common variations


    

  • Negative acknowledgements. Instead of ignoring an old prepare or accept, the acceptor may tell the proposer the higher ballot it promised, so the proposer abandons sooner. D2 The lab's acceptors stay silent and proposers rely on timeouts, which makes the duel easier to see.
    • 

  • Distinguished learner. Acceptors send "accepted" to one learner, which tells the others: fewer messages, one more hop and a single point of delay. D2
    • 

  • Roles on the same machines. In practice each server is proposer, acceptor and learner; the roles are logical. D2

How the hardware actually does it


A Paxos round costs messages and durable writes, and on real machines both cost time. Phase 2 for one command needs the leader's accept message to reach a majority, each acceptor in that majority to write its accepted proposal to stable storage, and the reply to come back: one network round trip plus one durable write on the slowest member of the fastest majority. That is why implementations batch many commands per accept and why the speed of the storage device's flush path matters as much as the network. The CPU work of the protocol itself is a few comparisons per message, as Listing 1 shows; time goes into waiting. Measure these costs on your own machines (the F5-07 methods apply); do not use numbers from a book.



Worked example


Task. Three acceptors. State before: a1 accepted (21, X); a2 accepted (13, Y); a3 promised 25 and accepted nothing. Proposer P4 runs prepare(31) and receives promises from a2 and a3 only. What must it propose? Then: could X have been chosen?


    

  1. Promises received: a2 reports (13, Y); a3 reports nothing. Highest reported: (13, Y). P4 must send accept(31, Y).
    2. 

  2. Could X have been chosen in ballot 21? It would need a majority to have accepted (21, X): a1 and one of a2, a3. a2's latest accepted proposal is 13, and an acceptor's accepted ballot only grows, so a2 never accepted 21; a3 accepted nothing. So X was not chosen — and P4 proposing Y does not violate safety.
    3. 

  3. Could Y have been chosen in ballot 13? Only with a1 or a3 also accepting (13, Y). a3 accepted nothing; a1 might have accepted (13, Y) before moving on to 21. So Y may have been chosen — which is exactly why P4 must propose Y.
    4. 

  4. Check the invariant: whichever of the two pasts happened, ballot 31 carries the only value that might have been chosen. Now suppose P4 had heard from a1 and a2 instead. The highest reported proposal would be (21, X), so P4 would propose X. Is that safe, given that Y might have been chosen in ballot 13? Yes: if Y had been chosen in ballot 13, the invariant says every higher-numbered proposal issued afterwards, including (21, X), would carry Y, so X ≠ Y tells us Y was not chosen. Both answers are safe for the promises actually received; Paxos needs no global knowledge.
    5.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Café order numbers come from one counter. Paxos has no central counter: uniqueness comes from combining a round number with a proposer id, and two proposers can both believe they hold the newest number.
    • 

  • Friends would notice "we already chose". In story 2 and in the duel, a value is chosen before any proposer knows it; nobody in the analogy would keep filling in forms after the pizza is ordered, but proposers do, and safety must hold anyway.
    • 

  • A promise between friends is about trust; an acceptor's promise is about storage. If the acceptor forgets (crash without stable storage), the promise is gone; good intentions do not survive a power cut.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: two different values chosen. Cause: an acceptor accepts a ballot below its promise (forensic lab), or forgets promises after a restart, or two proposers use the same ballot number. How to find it: for each acceptor, list (time, promised, accepted) and check that accepted ballots never go below the promise. Fix: compare accept requests with the promised ballot; persist before replying; make ballots unique.
    • 

  • Symptom: the proposer proposes its own value although a value was already chosen. Cause: it adopted the value of the first or the most common reported proposal rather than the highest-numbered one. Fix: track the maximum ballot among reports (highestSeen).
    • 

  • Symptom: nothing is ever learned, ballots climb. Cause: dueling proposers, or a timeout shorter than a full round. How to find it: plot ballot numbers over time; compare the timeout with measured round-trip times. Fix: one distinguished proposer; randomized, longer timeouts.
    • 

  • Symptom: a proposer counts promises from the previous ballot. Cause: replies are matched by sender only. Fix: every reply carries the ballot it answers; ignore mismatches (onPromise line 237).
    •

Summary


    

  • Paxos: prepare/promise, then accept/accepted, with unique increasing ballots.
    • 

  • Acceptors persist their promised ballot and accepted proposal; a promise means "never accept a lower ballot".
    • 

  • A proposer must propose the value of the highest-numbered proposal reported by a majority; intersecting majorities make every later ballot carry a chosen value.
    • 

  • Several ballots may choose a value; it is always the same value.
    • 

  • Progress needs timing luck or design: in the lab, a 14 ms timeout never let a proposer learn the value; randomization or a longer timeout did in all 100 seeds.
    • 

  • Multi-Paxos runs phase 1 once per leader; Raft's terms, elections and AppendEntries are the same structure made concrete.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. A was chosen at 50 ms in ballot 11 (a1 at 15 ms, a2 at 50 ms); B was chosen at 90 ms in ballot 12 (a3 at 35 ms, a2 at 90 ms).


The wrong decision. At 50 ms a2 "accepts (11, A)", although at 25 ms it had "promises ballot 12". Accepting 11 after promising 12 breaks the promise.


Hypotheses. (a) ruled out: ballots 11 and 12 are distinct. (c) ruled out: P2's promises at 25 ms came from a2 and a3, which had accepted nothing, so P2 was free to propose B. (b) confirmed by the a2 lines. Note that a3 correctly ignored accept(11, A) at 50 ms — only because a3 had already accepted ballot 12.


Root cause. The rule: an acceptor accepts a proposal numbered n only if it has not responded to a prepare request with a number greater than n. The code compares the accept's ballot with the last accepted ballot instead of the last promised one: paxos.h line 219, const int limit = bug_ ? a.acceptedBallot : a.promised;. a2 had promised 12 but accepted nothing, so 11 passed the wrong check.


Fix and prevention. Compare with promised; two_values_fixed.out shows a2 ignoring accept(11, A) and only B chosen. Test acceptors as a unit with a table of (promised, accepted, request) → expected action, including "promised but not yet accepted"; run the deterministic story in CI.




How the evidence was made. two_values.h scripts delays so that P1's accept to a2 arrives after a2's promise to P2 and before P2's accept; two_values.cpp runs it with the comparison bug, two_values_fixed.cpp without.
