Story hook

The friends now order pizza every Friday, and they want one friend to write the order each week so nobody has to vote on every topping. Amara has been writing it. This Friday the phone line from Amara's town is dead. Kofi waits, hears nothing, and after a while says: "I'm calling a new round. Round 2 — will you let me write the orders?" Two friends answer yes; with his own vote that is three of five. Kofi writes the order.

But a nasty question waits. What if Mei had also called round 2 and somebody said yes to both of them? Or what if somebody said yes to Mei, forgot, and then said yes to Kofi? Two friends would both believe they write the order. In this chapter you will build Raft's election, measure why it uses random timers, and examine a real-looking incident in which a cluster had two leaders in the same term because one server forgot its vote.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe Raft's three server states and the transitions between them.
  • Explain terms as a logical clock and the rule "any message with a higher term makes you a follower".
  • Trace an election: timeout, RequestVote, votes, a winner, heartbeats.
  • Explain why each server votes at most once per term and why that vote must be on stable storage before the reply.
  • Measure split votes with equal and randomized election timeouts in a simulator.
  • Diagnose a two-leaders incident from server logs.
Jargon box
Term
Simple words: a numbered period of time with at most one leader. Analogy: "round 2 of being in charge". Precise: Raft divides time into terms numbered with consecutive integers; each term begins with an election, and at most one candidate wins in a given term; servers exchange their current term in every message and use it as a logical clock to detect stale information. D1
Follower, candidate, leader
Simple words: listening, asking for votes, in charge. Analogy: the friends who wait for the order; a friend calling a new round; the friend who writes the order. Precise: the three server states of Raft; followers respond to requests, a candidate is a server trying to become leader, and the leader handles all client requests and replicates the log. D1
Election timeout
Simple words: how long a follower waits without news before calling an election. Analogy: how long Kofi waits before calling a new round. Precise: a period after which a follower that has received no valid AppendEntries or granted no vote becomes a candidate; Raft chooses it at random from a fixed interval to make split votes rare. D1
RequestVote
Simple words: the message "please vote for me". Analogy: "will you let me write the orders this round?" Precise: an RPC sent by candidates carrying the candidate's term, id, last log index and last log term; a server grants at most one vote per term, first come first served, subject to the election restriction. D1
Split vote
Simple words: an election that nobody wins because the votes were divided. Analogy: Kofi and Mei both call round 2 at the same moment and each gets two yeses. Precise: a term in which several candidates divide the votes so that none reaches a majority; the term ends without a leader and a new election starts after another timeout. D1
Election restriction
Simple words: you only vote for someone whose notebook is at least as up to date as yours. Analogy: you only let a friend write orders if their copy of the shared notebook is not behind yours. Precise: a voter denies its vote if its own log is more up-to-date than the candidate's: the log whose last entry has the later term is more up-to-date; with equal last terms, the longer log is. D1 Its purpose is explained in F5-20.

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

One friend writes the order. Everybody else just listens. If you have not heard from the order-writer for a while, you start a new, numbered round and ask the others to let you write the orders. Each friend says yes to only one person per round. Whoever gets yes from most of the friends writes the orders for that round. Because each friend says yes only once per round, and "most of the friends" twice would need more friends than there are, two people can never both win the same round. If two people ask at the same moment and nobody wins, everybody waits a little — a different random little — and tries again.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Terms

Raft's time is divided into terms 1, 2, 3, … Each server stores its currentTerm. Every message carries the sender's term. Two rules use it: D1

  • If a server receives a message with a higher term, it updates its term and becomes a follower (Listing 1, receive and becomeFollower).
  • If a server receives a request with a lower term, it rejects it (and its reply carries its own, higher term, which demotes the stale sender).

Terms play the role of Paxos ballots (F5-17): an old leader that wakes up after a partition learns at its first contact that the world has moved on.

The election

  1. A follower whose election timer expires increments its term, becomes a candidate, votes for itself, saves term and vote to stable storage, resets its timer and sends RequestVote to all other servers. D1
  2. A server receiving RequestVote grants its vote if the candidate's term is not older, it has not voted for someone else in this term, and the candidate's log is at least as up-to-date as its own. It saves the vote before replying. D1
  3. The candidate becomes leader when votes from a majority (including its own) arrive; it then sends AppendEntries to all servers as heartbeats, and keeps sending them so that no follower times out. D1
  4. If the candidate receives AppendEntries from a leader whose term is at least its own, it accepts that leader and becomes a follower. If its timer expires again without a winner, it starts a new election in a new term. D1


Read the run. n2's timer expires first (166 ms); four votes arrive between 169 and 172 ms; n2 announces victory with n1, n2 and n4 because those were the first three it counted — n3's and n5's votes had been sent at 169 ms but were still in flight, delayed by the random 2–10 ms network. At 400 ms the script crashes the leader. Nothing happens until n5's timer expires at 566 ms: 166 ms after the crash, a value drawn from 150–300 ms after n5's last heartbeat. n5 wins term 2 and appends its own no-op entry. R1 The crashed n2 still believes it is leader of term 1 (state DOWN, term 1); if it came back, the first message carrying term 2 would make it a follower.



Why at most one leader per term


Election Safety: at most one leader can be elected in a given term. D1 The argument has two parts: each server votes for at most one candidate in a term, and a winner needs votes from a majority; two majorities share a server, which would have had to vote twice. The first part only holds if the server remembers its vote, including across a crash. That is why currentTerm and votedFor are persistent state, written to stable storage before the server answers. D1 The forensic lab shows what happens when that write is late.



Random timeouts against split votes




Listing 4 starts four servers (an even number, so a 2-against-2 tie is possible) and records the term of the first leader in 200 seeds. With equal 150 ms timeouts, all four servers become candidates in the same millisecond, each votes for itself and refuses the others, and the pattern repeats forever: no leader within 3,000 ms in any of 200 seeds. With timeouts drawn from 150–155 ms, the spread is too small compared with message delays of 2–10 ms; leaders appear, but often only after many terms (as late as term 18), and 3 seeds had no leader in 3,000 ms. With 150–300 ms, the first leader came in term 1 in 190 of 200 seeds and in term 2 in the other 10. R1 The Raft paper reaches the same design conclusion: a range that is wide compared with message delays lets one server time out and win before others wake. D1



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


The timing requirement


Raft's safety does not depend on timing, but its availability does. The paper states a requirement of the form broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF: the time to send messages to all servers and receive replies must be much smaller than the election timeout, which must be much smaller than the mean time between failures of a single server. D1 If the timeout is too short, followers start needless elections while the leader is alive (each one disrupts service); if it is too long, a crashed leader leaves the cluster without a leader for a long time. The lab's 150–300 ms default with 2–10 ms messages and 50 ms heartbeats satisfies the left inequality by a wide margin; your real network must be measured, not assumed.



What the code adds to the rules


    

  • Persist before sending. startElection calls persist() before the first RequestVote leaves; handleRequestVote calls it before the reply (Listing 2, lines 13 and 56). In the simulator, persist() copies the state into disk_, and restart() copies disk_ back: only what reached the disk survives.
    • 

  • Reset the timer when granting a vote. A follower that grants a vote resets its timer (line 54), so it does not immediately compete with the candidate it just supported. D1
    • 

  • Stale replies are ignored. handleVoteReply counts a vote only if the server is still a candidate in the same term (line 71). A vote from an earlier term must not count toward a later one.
    • 

  • A fresh timer after stepping down. becomeFollower resets the timer when a leader or candidate steps down (raft.h line 266). Without it, a deposed leader's expired deadline would fire immediately and start a disruptive election — a bug found while building this lab.
    • 




Disruptive servers


A server cut off from the others keeps timing out, incrementing its term each time. When it reconnects, its high term forces the current leader to step down, although the isolated server's log is behind and it cannot win. The fixed run of the forensic lab shows a milder form of this: n2, cut off from n1, raises its term to 2 and then 3; n1 learns term 2 from n3's reply and steps down; there is a short period without a leader until n1 wins term 4. R1


Not verified — check before relying on this
Remedies for disruptive servers — a "pre-vote" phase in which a candidate first checks that it could win before incrementing its term, and ignoring RequestVote while a follower believes a leader is alive — are described in Ongaro's PhD dissertation and later work, which are not in the F5 source registry. Whether the Raft paper itself describes the second remedy (in its membership-change discussion) must be confirmed when the paper is opened (dossier gate G1). The lab implements neither.



How the hardware actually does it


Two physical things sit under an election. The first is the timer: a server's election timeout is measured by its local clock, and Raft only needs clocks to measure intervals at roughly the same rate, not to agree on the time of day. A pause of the whole process — garbage collection, a virtual machine being descheduled, swapping — looks to the other servers exactly like a crash, and when the paused leader resumes it may still believe it leads; terms make that safe, but its first few actions are rejected. The second is the disk: the vote is only durable after the storage device confirms the write (the flush path of F5-15's "Not verified" box), and that confirmation can be the slowest step in an election. A server that replies before the confirmation has the bug of this chapter's forensic lab. Measure your own flush latency and pause times; this book gives no hardware numbers.

Task. Five servers. Their (last log term, last log index) are: n1 (3, 7), n2 (3, 9), n3 (4, 5), n4 (2, 12), n5 (3, 9). All are in term 5 and nobody has voted in term 6. n2 times out and starts term 6. Who may vote for n2, and can it win? Then n3 times out instead: can n3 win?

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Friends know each other's voices. Raft servers trust message contents; a server that lies (a Byzantine fault) is outside Raft's model.
    • 

  • Friends remember yesterday. A server remembers only what reached its disk. The analogy hides the very bug of the forensic lab.
    • 

  • "Most of the friends" sounds informal. In Raft it is an exact count over the current configuration (F5-20 changes the configuration).
    • 

  • A leader is not a boss. The leader has no authority over safety: it cannot overwrite a committed entry, and a leader that is cut off keeps believing it leads until a message proves otherwise.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: two leaders in one term. Cause: the vote is not persisted before replying, or votedFor is not reset when the term changes, or votes from an old term are counted. How to find it: for each server, list (term, votedFor) after each event; check that a server never grants twice in one term. Fix: persist before sending; compare reply terms.
    • 

  • Symptom: elections never end. Cause: equal or too-narrow timeout ranges; timers not reset when granting a vote. Fix: randomize over a range wide compared with message delays.
    • 

  • Symptom: a healthy leader keeps being replaced. Cause: election timeout close to the heartbeat interval or round-trip time; or followers not resetting the timer on AppendEntries. How to find it: plot heartbeat arrival gaps against the timeout.
    • 

  • Symptom: a new leader is elected immediately after a leader steps down. Cause: the deposed leader's old election deadline is in the past. Fix: reset the timer when stepping down (raft.h line 266).
    •

Summary


    

  • Raft servers are followers, candidates or leaders; terms are numbered rounds, carried in every message.
    • 

  • A higher term in any message makes a server a follower; lower-term requests are rejected.
    • 

  • A candidate needs a majority of votes; each server votes once per term, first come first served, for candidates whose log is at least as up-to-date.
    • 

  • currentTerm and votedFor must be on stable storage before replying; forgetting a vote allowed two leaders in term 1 in the forensic lab.
    • 

  • Randomized timeouts make split votes rare: equal timeouts never elected a leader in 200 seeds; 150–300 ms elected in term 1 in 190.
    • 

  • Safety never depends on timing; availability needs broadcast time ≪ election timeout ≪ time between failures.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. n1 became leader of term 1 at 160 ms (votes n1, n3); n2 became leader of term 1 at 162 ms (votes n2, n3). Both committed different entries 2 and replied "done" to their clients at 270 ms. The checker reports Election Safety, State Machine Safety and Log Matching violations.


The action. "155 ms n3 votes for n1"; "156 ms n3 restarted from disk: term 0, votedFor 0"; "157 ms n3 votes for n2". n3 voted twice in term 1.


Hypotheses. (a) ruled out: after the restart n3 believed it had not voted (votedFor 0), so the vote rule was applied to the wrong state. (b) confirmed: the restart line shows term 0, although n3 was in term 1 one millisecond earlier. (c) ruled out: the blocked link explains why n1 and n2 did not hear each other until 400 ms ("ERROR: AppendEntries ... two leaders!" once unblocked), but the two leaders exist independently of the checker.


Root cause. The rule that persistent state — currentTerm, votedFor and the log — is updated on stable storage before responding to RPCs. D1 The deployed servers ran with lazyPersist: persist() only marks the state dirty, and tick() writes it at the next multiple of 100 ms (raft.h lines 228–231 and 286–289). n3's vote at 155 ms had not been written when it crashed at 156 ms.


Fix and prevention. Persist (and flush) before every reply that depends on the state. The fixed run shows n3 restarting with "term 1, votedFor 1" and refusing n2. A test: crash and restart every server at random times right after it sends a message, many seeds, Election Safety checked — the mini-project's tester detects this fault.




How the evidence was made. two_leaders.h fixes the timeouts (n1 150 ms, n2 152 ms, n3 400 ms), blocks the n1–n2 link from 1 ms to 400 ms, crashes and restarts n3 at 156 ms and sends two client puts at 250 ms; two_leaders.cpp runs it with lazyPersist, two_leaders_fixed.cpp without.
