Story hook

Amara, Tomás and Mei live in three different towns. Their friend Kofi has a birthday on Saturday, and they want one pizza delivered to his door as a surprise. Exactly one of them has to phone the pizzeria, and the pizzeria must receive exactly one order. If two of them order, Kofi gets two different pizzas and a confused delivery driver. If nobody orders, Kofi gets nothing.

They can only write to each other. Some letters arrive the next day, some arrive after a week, and one in ten never arrives. On Thursday Mei's phone falls into the sea, and from then on she neither reads nor writes. Each friend has a favourite pizza: Amara wants mushroom, Tomás wants mushroom, Mei wants tomato. "Let's vote," says Tomás. "Everyone writes their choice to the others; on Friday evening everyone counts the letters they have and goes with the majority."

On Friday evening Amara and Tomás count two letters for mushroom. Mei has received nothing, counts only her own vote, and orders tomato. Kofi gets two pizzas. In this chapter you will see why every simple voting rule has a story like this one, write down exactly what "agreeing" must mean, and run 3,000 simulated weekends to watch three simple rules break in three different ways.

What you will be able to do

  • State the consensus problem precisely: the system model (asynchronous messages, crash-stop processes) and the properties agreement, validity, integrity and termination.
  • Classify a property as safety ("nothing bad ever happens") or liveness ("something good eventually happens") and explain why a protocol can keep one and lose the other.
  • Explain why majority quorums are the building block of every protocol in this course, and compute how many crashes a cluster of n servers can tolerate.
  • Connect consensus to the replicated state machine and the replicated log that Raft and Paxos build.
  • Run the course's deterministic simulator, read its property checker and diagnose which property a broken protocol violated, and why.
Jargon box
Consensus
Simple words: several computers settle on one single answer and never change it. Analogy: the friends agreeing on one pizza for Kofi. Precise: a set of processes, each with an input value, must each decide on an output value such that agreement, validity, integrity and termination (below) hold, in a stated failure model. D3 D4
Agreement
Simple words: nobody decides differently from anybody else. Analogy: Kofi gets one pizza, not two. Precise: no two processes decide different values (the "uniform" form also covers processes that decide and then crash). D4
Validity
Simple words: the answer was somebody's real suggestion. Analogy: nobody orders a pineapple pizza that no friend asked for. Precise: a decided value was the input (proposal) of some process. D4 D2
Integrity
Simple words: each one decides once and does not change its mind. Analogy: no second phone call to the pizzeria. Precise: every process decides at most once. D4
Termination
Simple words: everyone who is still working eventually has an answer. Analogy: by Saturday, every friend whose phone works knows the pizza. Precise: every process that does not crash eventually decides some value. D4 D3
Safety property
Simple words: a promise that nothing bad ever happens. Analogy: "Kofi never gets two pizzas". Precise: a property whose violation, if it happens, happens at a definite point of a run and can be shown with a finite prefix of that run (agreement, validity, integrity). D4
Liveness property
Simple words: a promise that something good eventually happens. Analogy: "Kofi eventually gets a pizza". Precise: a property that no finite prefix of a run can violate, because the good thing might still happen later (termination). D4
Quorum (majority)
Simple words: more than half of the group. Analogy: two of the three friends. Precise: a set of more than n/2 of the n processes; any two such sets have at least one process in common, which is the fact the protocols of this course rely on. D1 D2
Replicated state machine
Simple words: several copies of the same program that stay identical because they perform the same commands in the same order. Analogy: everyone's copy of the shared notebook. Precise: deterministic state machines on several servers that apply the same sequence of commands from a replicated log, so they compute the same states and outputs; consensus keeps the logs identical. Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Several computers want to settle on one answer. They can only send each other messages, and messages can be late or lost. One computer may stop working at any moment, and the others cannot see whether it stopped or is only slow. Consensus is the promise that, despite all this, everybody who decides decides the same answer, the answer was somebody's real suggestion, and everybody who keeps working gets an answer in the end.

That sounds easy until you try. "Wait for everybody" gets stuck when somebody stops. "Wait until Friday, then count" lets two friends count different letters. "Let one friend decide" gets stuck when that friend stops. The rest of this course is about the rules that really work.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The system model: what may go wrong

Before a protocol can be right or wrong, you must say what the world may do to it. The model of this course, used by the Raft paper and by "Paxos Made Simple", is: D1 D2

  • Processes (servers) run the protocol faithfully. They may crash: stop and say nothing more. A crashed process may later restart with whatever it saved on stable storage (disk); its memory is gone. This is the crash-recovery variant of the crash-stop model of F5-11.
  • Messages may be delayed for any length of time, lost, duplicated and reordered, but not corrupted undetectably and not forged. This is a non-Byzantine model: nobody lies. D2
  • Time: there is no common clock, and there is no bound you can rely on for message delay or for how long a process takes to do a step. A system with no such bounds is called asynchronous. D3

The lab's simulator implements exactly this model, in simulated milliseconds: NetOptions sets a random delay range, a loss percentage and a duplication percentage; delays are drawn independently for each message, so two messages between the same pair can overtake each other. R1 One deliberate simplification: in the pizza runs, a crashed friend never restarts.

The four properties

Each process p starts with an input vp and may at some point decide an output. A protocol solves consensus if every run satisfies: D4 D3

  1. Agreement: no two processes decide differently.
  2. Validity: a decided value is the input of some process. (This rules out the useless protocol "always decide tomato", which trivially agrees.)
  3. Integrity: a process decides at most once.
  4. Termination: every process that does not crash eventually decides.

"Paxos Made Simple" states the same safety requirements in its own words for a value that is "chosen": only a proposed value may be chosen, only a single value is chosen, and a process never learns that a value has been chosen unless it actually has been. D2 F5-17 uses that vocabulary.

Safety and liveness

Agreement, validity and integrity are safety properties: if one is broken, you can point at the moment it broke (the second, different decision) and show the finite run that led there. Termination is a liveness property: no finite run can break it, because the missing decision might still come. D4 This difference shapes every real protocol: Raft and Paxos never give up safety under any timing, and give up liveness (they may stall) when the network or the timing is bad enough. D1 D2 F5-16 shows that some such trade is unavoidable.

Three simple rules, measured

Listing 2 runs three rules 1,000 times each, every run with a new seed: delays of 1–80 ms, 10 % of messages lost, and in one run out of three one friend crashes at a random time in the first 60 ms. R1

  • Rule A — everybody sends their vote to everybody; at 50 ms everybody decides the majority of the votes they hold (a tie goes to the first pizza in the list).
  • Rule B — everybody sends their vote to everybody; a friend decides when all three votes are in, by the same majority rule.
  • Rule C — friend 1, the organiser, decides its own vote and tells the others; they decide what they are told.


The table is the whole lesson in numbers. Rule A broke agreement in 418 of 1,000 runs and never broke termination: it always decides, sometimes differently. Rules B and C never broke agreement, but B left a working friend without a decision in 433 runs and C in 170 runs: they wait for a message that was lost or for a friend who crashed. R1 None broke validity, because each rule only ever decides a value somebody voted for. Each rule kept the safety it was built for and paid with the other property. In this simulator, B and C also suffer from something real protocols fix cheaply: nobody resends a lost letter. Even with retransmission, though, B waits forever for a crashed friend and C for a crashed organiser.



Majorities: the trick that works


All protocols in this course replace "everybody" by "a majority". If any decision needs the support of more than half of the n processes, then two decisions always share at least one supporter, because two sets each larger than n/2 cannot fit side by side in a group of n: their sizes add up to more than n. That shared process is the one that can say "careful, something was already agreed". D2 D1 And because a majority, not everybody, is needed, the protocol keeps going when a minority has crashed: with n = 2f + 1 processes, f crashes still leave f + 1, a majority. D1


The majority alone does not make a protocol: rule A also counts votes, but each friend counts a different set. What makes Paxos and Raft work is that a value is decided only after a majority has stored it in a way that every later attempt must see. That is what F5-17 to F5-20 build.



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


From one decision to a replicated log


A single decision is rarely the goal. A database, a lock service or a configuration store needs a sequence of decisions: what is command 1, what is command 2, and so on. If every server applies the same commands in the same order to the same deterministic program, every server computes the same state (Figure 2). That is the replicated state machine approach; consensus is used once per log slot. D1 D2 Kleppmann shows that agreeing on such a sequence, total order broadcast, is equivalent to repeated consensus. D4 Two consequences matter for the rest of the course:


    

  • Determinism is part of correctness. If the state machine reads the clock, a random number or a hash-map iteration order that differs between servers, identical logs no longer mean identical states. The lab's state machine is a std::map of keys to integers, applied in log order.
    • 

  • The log is the source of truth, not the state. A restarted server rebuilds its state by applying the log again (F5-18 onwards shows this in every restarted from disk line). Log compaction with snapshots is the paper's answer to logs that grow forever; this course does not implement it. D1
    • 




Where consensus hides in real systems


Several problems that do not sound like "agreeing on a value" are consensus in disguise: electing exactly one leader (agree on who it is), granting a lock or a lease to exactly one client (agree on the holder), deciding whether a distributed transaction commits (agree on commit or abort), and changing the membership of a cluster (agree on the new member list). D4 The DS301 forensic lab "Two leaders" (F5-10) is the symptom of solving one of these without consensus. Many systems therefore delegate these decisions to a small consensus service rather than implementing consensus themselves; MP2 asks you to build the service.



How many servers, and why odd numbers


With majority quorums, n servers tolerate f = ⌊(n − 1)/2⌋ crashes: 3 tolerate 1, 4 tolerate 1, 5 tolerate 2. Going from 3 to 4 servers adds a machine and a message per round but no tolerance, which is why clusters are usually odd-sized. This is arithmetic, worked out in the worked example below. Larger clusters tolerate more crashes but each decision needs more replies, so a decision waits for a slower member of the majority. The trade is a design decision, not a constant; measure it for your network.


Note
Byzantine faults are out of scope. If processes may lie (bugs that send wrong data, compromised machines), the problem changes: Byzantine fault-tolerant protocols need more processes per tolerated fault and signatures or extra message rounds. Raft and Paxos as taught here assume nobody lies. D1 D2 F11 courses return to adversaries.

How the hardware actually does it


"Crash", "lost message" and "stable storage" are abstractions of physical things. A crash is a power loss, a kernel panic, an out-of-memory kill or an operator restarting the wrong machine: every byte in DRAM and in CPU caches is gone; only what reached a persistent device survives. A protocol that says "store this before replying" therefore needs the operating system to have pushed the data through its page cache to the device and the device to have made it durable (the fsync family of calls on POSIX systems). Each of those steps costs time, which is why implementers are tempted to skip them; F5-18's forensic lab shows what happens when they do. A lost message is a frame dropped by a full queue in a switch or a network card, a cable pulled, or a process that died after the kernel accepted the bytes. A "late" message is one that waited in such queues or behind a stalled process. None of these can be told apart from a crash by the receiver: silence looks the same. D4


Not verified — check before relying on this
Whether a particular storage device honours a cache flush (some devices with volatile write caches have been reported to acknowledge writes that are not yet durable) is product-specific. Check the device's datasheet and the documentation of fsync and of the file system you use (the Linux kernel documentation and the POSIX specification) before trusting a durability claim; this build did not open them.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Friends can phone each other and wait. Real friends would notice that Mei went silent and call her mother. A process cannot distinguish a crashed peer from a slow one or a lost message: silence is all it sees (F5-11). That is the whole difficulty, and the analogy hides it.
    • 

  • Friends vote once; servers agree again and again. The pizza is one decision; a replicated log is millions of decisions, and the hard cases come from overlapping attempts with leftovers from earlier attempts (F5-19, F5-20).
    • 

  • Letters are not duplicated. Networks also duplicate and reorder messages; the simulator does both when asked (duplicatePercent, random delays). R1
    • 

  • Voting is not the hard part. The registered analogy says "agreeing by vote", but rule A shows that voting alone does not give agreement when voters see different ballots. Real protocols must work when friends crash and messages are lost, which voting rules alone do not handle.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: two parts of a system act on different decisions ("two pizzas", two leaders, a lock held twice). Cause: each part decided from the messages it happened to receive before a deadline (rule A). How to find it: log every decision with the inputs it was based on; compare the sets of inputs. Fix: decide only on values a majority has stored (F5-17 onwards).
    • 

  • Symptom: the system hangs after one machine dies. Cause: a rule that waits for everybody, or for one special process (rules B, C). How to find it: list every wait in the protocol and ask "what if this sender is dead?". Fix: wait for a majority; elect a new coordinator when the old one is silent (F5-18).
    • 

  • Symptom: a run fails once and never again. Cause: real timing, real threads, real network: the failing interleaving is gone. Fix: test protocol logic in a deterministic simulator first; record the seed of every run (this course's method, F5-21).
    • 

  • Symptom: "validity" violated: a value nobody proposed. Cause: a default or sentinel value (0, empty string) decided when no message arrived. How to find it: the checker of Listing 2 compares every decision with all inputs. Fix: never decide a default; wait or fail visibly.
    • 

  • Symptom: replicas with identical logs report different states. Cause: the state machine is not deterministic (clock, randomness, iteration order). Fix: make every input of the state machine part of the log entry.
    •

Summary


    

  • Consensus: every process that decides decides the same value (agreement), the value was proposed (validity), once (integrity), and every live process decides (termination).
    • 

  • Agreement, validity and integrity are safety properties; termination is liveness.
    • 

  • Simple rules trade one for the other: deciding at a deadline broke agreement in 418 of 1,000 runs; waiting for all or for an organiser broke termination in 433 and 170 runs.
    • 

  • Majority quorums intersect; n = 2f + 1 servers tolerate f crashes; even sizes add cost, not tolerance.
    • 

  • A replicated log is consensus repeated per slot; deterministic state machines applying it stay identical.
    • 

  • A deterministic simulator makes every failure replayable from its seed.
    •

Observation. Agreement is violated: friends 1 and 2 decided mushroom, friend 3 decided tomato. The "heard from" lists show friend 3 held only its own vote, while friends 1 and 2 held two mushroom votes.


Hypotheses. (a) is ruled out: all three use the same majority function, and for friend 3's input {tomato} the answer tomato is correct. (c) is ruled out: the model has no corruption and the values held are exactly the values sent. (b) is confirmed by the lists.


Root cause. Rule A decides at a deadline from whatever arrived. Friend 1's letter to friend 3 was lost or late, and friend 2's letter to friend 3 had not arrived by 50 ms (friend 3's list contains neither). Different inputs to a deterministic function give different outputs.


Later deadline. Not a fix: with unbounded delays (the model) and 10 % loss, some run will still deliver different sets by any deadline you choose; the lab's question 6 shows the count shrinking, not vanishing.


Fix and cost. Wait for all votes (rule B) or for the organiser (rule C): agreement holds, termination is lost. A real fix (F5-17) decides only on a value a majority has accepted and makes every later attempt discover it.
