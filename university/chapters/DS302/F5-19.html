Story hook

The friends keep a shared notebook of every order, one numbered line per order, and each friend has a copy. Kofi, who writes the orders, sends each new line to everybody by letter. One week the phone lines to Mei's town are down; her copy stops at line 1 while the others reach line 4. Then Kofi's own town is cut off just after he wrote line 5 — "pizza d" — which nobody else ever received. Amara takes over and writes her own line 5.

When everybody is reachable again, three copies of the notebook disagree. Mei is missing lines; Kofi has a line 5 nobody else has. Whose copy is right, and how do the copies become identical without anybody erasing an order that was already delivered? That is Raft's log replication, and the answer is a single comparison that the follower makes before writing anything: "do I have the line just before these, with the same round number?"

What you will be able to do

  • Describe a Raft log entry and the fields of AppendEntries.
  • Explain the consistency check and how it gives the Log Matching Property.
  • Trace how a leader repairs a lagging follower and a follower with conflicting entries, using nextIndex and matchIndex.
  • Decide when an entry is committed and when it may be applied to the state machine.
  • Explain how clients find the leader and why requests need numbers to avoid duplicates.
  • Diagnose log divergence caused by a follower that skips the consistency check.
Jargon box
Log entry
Simple words: one numbered line of the shared history. Analogy: one numbered line of the shared notebook, with the round in which it was written. Precise: a record holding a state machine command and the term in which the leader received it; its position in the log is its index. D1
AppendEntries
Simple words: the leader's letter "please add these lines after line k". Analogy: Kofi's letter with new notebook lines. Precise: the RPC by which the leader replicates entries and sends heartbeats; arguments: term, leader id, prevLogIndex, prevLogTerm, entries (empty for a heartbeat), leaderCommit. D1
Consistency check
Simple words: "I only add your lines if my line k matches yours". Analogy: before copying new lines, compare the last shared line's round number. Precise: a follower rejects AppendEntries if its log does not contain an entry at prevLogIndex whose term equals prevLogTerm. D1
Log Matching Property
Simple words: if two copies agree on a line and its round, they agree on everything before it. Analogy: two notebooks with the same line 4 from round 1 have the same lines 1–4. Precise: if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, the logs are identical in all entries up through that index. D1
Committed entry; commitIndex
Simple words: a line that is safe to act on, because it can never be erased. Analogy: an order that may be phoned to the pizzeria. Precise: an entry is committed once the leader that created it has replicated it on a majority of servers; commitIndex is the highest index known to be committed. Committed entries are durable and will eventually be executed by all available state machines. D1 The exact rule for entries of earlier terms is in F5-20.
nextIndex, matchIndex
Simple words: the leader's two numbers per follower: what to send next, and how much is known to match. Analogy: Kofi's note "Mei: send from line 2; known equal up to line 1". Precise: for each follower, the index of the next entry to send (initialized to the leader's last index + 1) and the highest index known to be replicated on it (initialized to 0). D1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The leader sends new notebook lines and says "these go after line 4, which I wrote in round 1". You only copy them if your line 4 is also from round 1. If it is not, you say "no", and the leader tries again starting one line earlier, until you find a line you both have. Then you copy everything from there, throwing away any of your lines that disagree with the leader's. Because everybody checks the line before, two notebooks that agree on a line agree on everything before it. An order is phoned to the pizzeria only after most friends have copied it.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Replicated state machines

Raft's job is to keep a replicated log: each server runs the same deterministic state machine (here, a key-value map) and executes the same commands in the same order, so they all reach the same state. D1 Consensus decides which command goes in each slot; the leader proposes, the followers copy. Kleppmann describes the same structure as total order broadcast. D4

The normal case

  1. A client sends a command to the leader. The leader appends it as a new entry with its current term and sends AppendEntries to every follower (appendAsLeader). D1
  2. Each follower runs the consistency check, appends the entry, saves its log and replies.
  3. When the leader knows the entry is on a majority (its own copy counts), the entry is committed; the leader advances commitIndex, applies the entry to its state machine and answers the client. D1
  4. Later AppendEntries (including heartbeats) carry leaderCommit; followers apply entries up to it. D1

In the run below the round trip is visible: the client's put a=1 arrives at 200 ms, the leader appends at 205 ms, n2 stores at 210 ms, the leader commits at 215 ms (n1 and n2 are a majority of three — n3 is cut off) and the client hears "done" at 220 ms. R1




Repairing logs


The leader keeps nextIndex for each follower, optimistically starting just past its own last entry. If a follower rejects, the leader lowers nextIndex and retries; eventually the two logs agree at prevLogIndex, the follower accepts, and it deletes any conflicting entries and appends the leader's. D1 The leader never deletes or overwrites entries in its own log: the flow is always leader → followers. D1


In the run: n2 became leader at 770 ms and sent n3 "entry 5 after entry 4 of term 1"; n3 had only entry 1 and rejected. The lab's leader then jumps nextIndex straight to the follower's end (the reply carries the follower's last index), so the second attempt sent entries 2–5 and n3 stored them at 785 ms. At 1010 ms the old leader n1 reconnected, heard term 2, stepped down, found that its entry 5 (t1 put d=4) conflicted with the leader's (t2 no-op), deleted it and stored entries 5–6. R1 The put d=4 vanished — and that is correct: it was never on a majority, never committed, and its client was never told "done". A client that times out must retry (with the same request number, see below).



Why the logs match


The Log Matching Property has two halves. D1 First, a leader creates at most one entry with a given index in a given term, and entries never change position, so two entries with the same index and term hold the same command. Second, the consistency check: a follower accepts entries after index k only if its entry k has the leader's term. By induction from the empty log, whenever AppendEntries succeeds, the follower's log equals the leader's up to the new entries. The cluster's checker tests this property every 50 ms (checkLogMatching); the forensic lab shows what happens when the check is skipped.



Clients


Clients send requests to the leader; a follower rejects and, if it knows one, names the leader (handleClientRequest, lines 164–169). If a leader crashes after committing a command but before answering, the client retries and the command could run twice; Raft's answer is for clients to give each command a unique serial number and for the state machine to remember the latest number per client and skip duplicates. D1 The lab's applyCommitted does exactly that (lastRequest_). Reads are a separate problem, handled in F5-21.



Layer 3 — the engineer's depth


What "committed" means for followers


A follower sets its commitIndex to the smaller of leaderCommit and the index of its last new entry (Listing 1, line 103). D1 The "smaller of" matters: a follower that has just received entries 2–5 but whose log beyond 5 is stale must not apply its own stale entry 6 just because the leader's commit index is 6. In the run's final state, n1 reached commit 6 only after receiving entry 6 from n2.



Persist before replying


A follower must write the new entries to stable storage before it replies "ok"; the leader counts that reply toward a majority, and a commit based on a copy that a crash then erases is not a commit. D1 In handleAppendEntries, persist() (line 101) runs before send(r) (line 109). The leader also persists its own new entry before sending it (line 13) — though it has been argued that the leader can write to its disk in parallel with sending, because its own copy is only one of the majority (see the box below).


Not verified — check before relying on this
The statement that a leader may write its own log entry to disk in parallel with replicating it is recalled from Ongaro's dissertation, which is not in the F5 source registry; whether the Raft paper says it must be confirmed when the paper is opened (dossier gate G1). The lab does not test it: persist() is instantaneous in the simulator.



Faster back-off


Lowering nextIndex by one per rejection costs one round trip per missing entry. The Raft paper mentions an optimization in which the follower's rejection includes the term of the conflicting entry and the first index it stores for that term, so the leader skips a whole term per round trip, and argues that it is often unnecessary because failures are rare. D1 The lab uses a simpler hint: the follower's last index (handleAppendReply, line 126), which fixes a short follower in one step but still steps back one entry at a time through conflicting entries.



Batching and flow


The lab sends at most maxBatch = 8 entries per AppendEntries and immediately sends more when a reply shows the follower is behind (line 121). Real implementations also pipeline (send the next batch before the previous reply), bound the bytes in flight, and send a snapshot instead of entries when a follower is so far behind that the leader has discarded the needed prefix (log compaction). D1 Snapshots are not implemented in the lab.



How the hardware actually does it


The latency of a committed write is set by three physical steps on the critical path: the leader's message reaching the followers, the followers' disk writes becoming durable, and the replies returning. With several followers, the leader waits only for the fastest majority, so one slow disk or one slow link does not slow every write — until it is the disk or link that the majority needs. Disk writes dominate when durability is real: the follower must not reply before its device confirms the flush. Batching amortizes that flush across many entries, which is why throughput and latency trade against each other in every Raft implementation. None of this is measured in the simulator, which charges zero time for disk writes; measure it on your own machines with the methods of F5-07.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Friends would never tear out a page of an order that was delivered. Raft never deletes a committed entry either — but it does delete uncommitted ones without asking anybody, which friends would find rude.
    • 

  • Notebooks are paper. A server's log is only as durable as its last flush; the analogy hides the persist-before-reply rule.
    • 

  • Friends can read each other's handwriting and spot nonsense. A follower does not check whether entries make sense; it only checks index and term.
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a follower's state lacks a key everyone else has. Cause: entries appended at the wrong index (consistency check skipped or off by one). How to find it: print every server's log as (index, term, command) and diff them (printLogs). Fix: check prevLogIndex and prevLogTerm exactly.
    • 

  • Symptom: committed entries disappear after a delayed message arrives. Cause: the follower truncates its log at prevLogIndex + 1 on every AppendEntries, instead of only on a conflict. Fix: truncate only when an existing entry's term differs (lines 83–89).
    • 

  • Symptom: a client's command runs twice. Cause: client retries without request numbers. Fix: per-client serial numbers and duplicate detection in the state machine.
    • 

  • Symptom: the leader sends the whole log forever. Cause: nextIndex not raised from matchIndex on success. Fix: nextIndex = matchIndex + 1 (line 119).
    •

Summary


    

  • A Raft log entry holds a command and the term it was created in; only the leader creates entries.
    • 

  • AppendEntries carries prevLogIndex and prevLogTerm; a follower accepts only if it has that entry, deletes conflicting entries, and appends the rest.
    • 

  • That check gives the Log Matching Property: same index and term means identical prefixes.
    • 

  • The leader repairs followers by lowering nextIndex until the logs agree; it never changes its own log.
    • 

  • An entry is committed when the leader that created it has it on a majority; followers apply up to min(leaderCommit, last new entry).
    • 

  • Entries and votes are persisted before replying; client commands carry serial numbers so retries run once.
    •
