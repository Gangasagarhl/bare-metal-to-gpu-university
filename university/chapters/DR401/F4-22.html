Story hook

The school's receptionist has three ways to handle a quiet afternoon. She can stand at the desk watching the door, lights on, ready in an instant. She can sit down in the back room and come out when the doorbell rings. Or she can switch off the hall lights and go upstairs to the staff room, which saves the most, but if a visitor arrives two minutes later, the visitor waits while she walks back down and the lights warm up again. A good receptionist guesses how long the quiet will last and picks accordingly.

In the summer the building also has a thermometer on the wall. Above one mark, the principal closes some classrooms to keep the heat down; above a second, higher mark, she sends everyone home in an orderly way before something breaks.

This chapter is about the receptionist and the principal in your kernel: idle states and the governor that chooses between them, frequency, and the thermal limits that must never be crossed — measured as far as an emulator allows, modelled where it does not.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a halt-based idle loop driven by a timer interrupt, and show from outside that it saves host CPU time.
  • Design an idle governor that chooses a state from predicted idle length, exit latency and target residency.
  • Read what a machine says about its own power features (CPUID leaf 6, the OS's idle, frequency and thermal folders) and cross-check two sources.
  • Explain passive and critical trip points, hysteresis and an orderly shutdown.
  • Name the documents and interfaces C15 builds on: ACPI _CST, _LPI, _PSS, _PCT, _PPC, _CPC, thermal zones; Intel HWP, RAPL, APERF/MPERF; AMD CPPC.
  • Find a unit bug that silently turns a governor off.
Jargon box
Idle state
Simple words: a way for the processor to rest when there is nothing to do; deeper rests save more but take longer to wake from. Analogy: standing at the desk, sitting in the back room, going up to the staff room. Precise: a C-state (C0 running, C1 halt, deeper states with caches or voltages reduced); the platform lists the states it supports through ACPI _CST or _LPI or through a processor-specific driver. C1 D2
Exit latency and target residency
Simple words: how long it takes to wake up from a rest, and how long the rest must last to be worth it. Analogy: the walk back down from the staff room, and how long the afternoon must stay quiet to make the walk pay. Precise: per-state figures an idle governor uses: a state is chosen only if the predicted idle time is at least its target residency and its exit latency is within what the system tolerates. D3
Idle governor
Simple words: the small program that decides how deep to rest each time. Analogy: the receptionist's guess about the quiet. Precise: C15's "idle governor choosing between halt and deeper states from expected idle length"; Linux has menu, ladder and others. C1 R6
Performance state
Simple words: a speed setting for the processor: faster uses more power. Analogy: how many classrooms are open. Precise: a P-state, chosen through ACPI _PSS/_PCT/_PPC, through CPPC (_CPC), or by hardware within limits the OS sets (Intel HWP, AMD CPPC); the delivered frequency is measured with the APERF/MPERF ratio. C1
Trip point
Simple words: a temperature at which the system must act. Analogy: the marks on the thermometer. Precise: ACPI thermal zones define passive trip points (reduce performance), active ones (turn on a fan) and a critical one (shut down); C15 requires that "A simulated critical trip point (test build) triggers an orderly shutdown with file systems synced." C1 D2
RAPL
Simple words: counters inside the processor that add up how much energy it has used. Analogy: the school's electricity meter. Precise: Intel's Running Average Power Limit interface (energy status counters and power limits in MSRs, in the SDM's power and thermal chapter); C15's acceptance uses package energy from RAPL at idle. D1 C1

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A computer is often doing nothing, waiting for the next thing. It can wait by running around in circles asking "is it time yet?" — that wastes energy and makes heat. Or it can take a nap and ask the clock to wake it. Short naps are easy to wake from; deep sleeps save more but take longer to wake from, so you only take a deep sleep if you expect to wait a long time. And if the computer gets too hot, it slows down; if it gets far too hot, it saves its work and turns itself off before anything breaks.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

C15's goal: "Your kernel saves energy and respects thermal limits: idle states chosen by a simple governor, frequency control through HWP, CPPC or ACPI P-states, and thermal-zone monitoring with a critical shutdown." C1 It has three parts that share one idea: the processor's power is a resource with a policy, and the policy needs numbers from the platform.

Idle. The simplest saving is the hlt instruction: the CPU stops until the next interrupt. A kernel whose idle loop halts instead of spinning uses almost no CPU while idle. In QEMU with TCG the guest CPU is a host thread, so you can see this from outside: the lab's idle kernel used 11 % of a host CPU per second of wall time when halting and 90 % when polling in the saved runs (an earlier run, log not kept: 12 % and 86 %). R2 R3 That is C15's first acceptance line in its own terms: "an idle kernel keeps host CPU usage near zero (halt-based idle works) and wakes for timers on time". C1

Deeper idle. Real processors have states deeper than halt. Each costs time to enter and leave; a governor picks the deepest state whose target residency fits the predicted idle time and whose exit latency is acceptable. The lab models this with an invented table. L2

Frequency and heat. Running slower uses less power; the platform offers performance states (ACPI _PSS, CPPC, or hardware-managed HWP). A thermal zone reports temperature; at a passive trip point the OS slows down, at the critical one it shuts down in order. The lab models the policy; it cannot read a real temperature here (this build machine has no thermal zones). R6

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Halting correctly

The idle loop must not miss the interrupt it waits for. The lab's loop reads the tick counter, then runs sti; hlt; cli while the counter has not changed. On x86, sti enables interrupts only after the next instruction, so an interrupt that is pending cannot slip in between sti and hlt and leave the CPU halted with nothing to wake it. D1 The timer is the 8254 PIT at 100 Hz through the 8259A PIC, the simplest pair on q35; a real kernel uses the local APIC timer or TSC deadline (B8). L1 One honest caveat about the lab's own loop: it is entered with interrupts enabled, so its first check of the counter is not protected; a tick arriving between that check and sti; hlt would make it sleep through one extra tick. The run shows 100 halts for 100 ticks, so it did not happen here, but a production loop disables interrupts (cli) before the check. Fixing it is lab step 3's first task.

Waking on time, and ARAT

The lab also records the TSC interval between timer interrupts. In the saved runs, halting, the 100 intervals ranged from 14297034 to 27737254 ticks; polling, from 17872112 to 24266374. R2 R3 An earlier run of the same lab, whose log was overwritten when the lab was re-run, gave the opposite picture (halting 20899904 to 21174646, polling 12930834 to 28869920). So in this TCG build single intervals scatter by up to about a third of a tick either way, and which variant scatters more changes from run to run: it is the emulator's scheduling on a shared host, not the idle loop. The average holds (the calibration over 10 ticks agrees with the host clock rate in every run), so "wakes for timers on time" has to be judged by the B8 tests and, for single intervals, under KVM or on hardware, never from one TCG run.

On real hardware the question is the reverse: does the timer keep running in deep idle? CPUID leaf 6 has a bit, ARAT ("APIC timer always running"), that says the local APIC timer is not stopped by deep C-states; without it, a kernel that uses deep states needs another wake-up source. D1 The build machine's CPU sets ARAT (and /proc/cpuinfo lists arat, agreeing on all 8 bits checked); the QEMU guest CPU model clears every bit of leaf 6's EAX. R5 R2 So the guest correctly tells the kernel "no power features here", and the deeper-state part of C15 needs the spare PC.

Not verified — check before relying on this

The CPUID leaf 6 bit meanings (power_readout.cpp lines 52–60) are from memory of the Intel SDM. The cross-check against Linux's /proc/cpuinfo flag names agrees on all 8 bits on this machine, but only one bit is set, so a swapped pair of unset bits would not be caught. A governor, modelled

The governor model (governor.cpp) has four states with invented exit latencies, target residencies and powers, a predictor (average of the last three idle periods) and a latency limit of 300 µs. On 60 periods — a busy phase, a quiet phase, a mix — it chose C1 21 times and C6 39 times; in the model's units it used 10669.9 µJ against 131495.0 for always-polling and 13240.0 for always-deepest, and added 7842 µs of wake-up delay against 12000 for always-deepest. R6 The pattern is the lesson, not the numbers: a predictor that lags (period 20: actual 5000 µs, predicted 25 µs; period 40: actual 100, predicted 5000) costs energy one way and latency the other, which is why real governors such as Linux's menu governor combine several predictors and correct for patterns. D3

Not verified — check before relying on this

Invented teaching values: the state table in governor.cpp (lines 12–17), the wake-energy rule (line 18), and every constant in thermal.cpp (temperatures, thermal resistances, heat capacity, frequency steps, the power formula) describe no real processor. A real table comes from _CST, _LPI or the processor driver; real trip points from the thermal zone's objects. Compare the rows of the outputs, never their numbers with a CPU.

Frequency and measuring it

C15 accepts three ways to control frequency: HWP, CPPC or ACPI P-states. C1 With _PSS the OS picks from a table of states and writes a control value; with CPPC (_CPC) it requests an abstract performance level within limits; with HWP the hardware picks within a range the OS sets. In every case the request is not the result: C15 asks that "requested frequency changes are visible in the measured effective frequency", measured with the APERF/MPERF ratio, two counters whose ratio gives delivered against nominal frequency over an interval. C1 D1 This build machine does not expose APERF/MPERF or HWP to its guest OS (both bits clear) and has no cpufreq folder, so none of it could be exercised. R5

Trip points and hysteresis

The thermal model shows two policies at work. At the passive trip (80 °C in the model) the frequency steps down one level every 5 s while the temperature stays above it, and steps up only below 80 − 5 °C: without that hysteresis it would step down and up on every check. With normal cooling it settles into a cycle around the trip and never reaches critical; with the fan failed (thermal resistance four times higher) it reaches the lowest frequency step, keeps warming slowly, and at 95.0 °C after 329 s starts an orderly shutdown. R7 In a kernel "orderly" means what C15 says: file systems synced before power-off, which needs the shutdown path of your file systems (section 7) and the ACPI S5 sequence (C10). C1

How the hardware actually does it

The curriculum's catalog row for processor power management lists the interfaces: ACPI performance states (_PSS, _PCT, _PPC), CPPC (_CPC), idle states (_CST, _LPI), sleep states (\_S3, \_S4, \_S5, _PTS, _WAK); Intel HWP and RAPL power limits (Intel SDM, power and thermal management chapter); AMD CPPC; Arm PSCI CPU_SUSPEND and SYSTEM_SUSPEND. Its reference drivers are Linux cpufreq (acpi-cpufreq, intel_pstate, amd-pstate) and cpuidle (intel_idle, acpi_idle, cpuidle-psci), FreeBSD cpufreq(4), est(4), hwpstate_intel(4), and Windows intelppm.sys, amdppm.sys and processr.sys. Its note: "cpufreq chooses frequencies, cpuidle chooses idle depth", and suspend to RAM and Modern Standby "need every driver to save and restore state: plan for it late." C1

On the build machine (itself a VM), Linux shows the shape of this from the OS side: a cpuidle framework with no idle driver bound ("none"), the menu governor selected from "ladder menu haltpoll", no per-CPU idle-state folder, no cpufreq folder, no thermal zones and no cooling devices. R5 A guest is told almost nothing about power, which is correct: the host owns it. Everything beyond halt is therefore spare-PC work.

Safety

Power and thermal code can damage hardware or data when wrong: never raise power limits, disable thermal protection or write unknown MSRs on a machine you depend on; keep the firmware's thermal settings; test the critical-trip path with a simulated temperature (as C15's test build does), never by overheating a real machine. Long load tests "stress power and cooling" (curriculum safety box): run them only on the designated spare. C1

Not verified — check before relying on this

Untested on hardware: no spare PC, no RAPL or APERF/MPERF access, no real thermal zone. C15's spare-PC acceptance line (RAPL energy at idle, effective frequency) was not run. Descriptions of HWP, CPPC, _PSS and RAPL above are from the curriculum and from memory; no specification was opened.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • The receptionist has an appointment book; the CPU has only its timers. Timer expiries are known in advance; device interrupts are not. Governors predict, and the prediction is often wrong (R6's periods 20 and 40). R6
    • 

  • The principal runs the building alone. In a VM the guest runs nothing below it: it can only halt, and the host decides the rest (R5). On a laptop the firmware (the F4 caretaker) and the embedded controller also act on temperature, independently of your kernel. R5
    • 

  • Closing classrooms is instant; slowing the CPU is not. Frequency requests take effect after a delay and may be overridden by hardware limits; that is why the effective frequency must be measured. C1

Answers to Check yourself


    

  1. "In QEMU: an idle kernel keeps host CPU usage near zero (halt-based idle works) and wakes for timers on time (B8 tests still pass)." The lab measures host CPU against wall time for a halting and a polling build (11 % and 90 % in the saved runs) and records timer intervals; it does not run B8's tests.
    2. 

  2. sti enables interrupts only after the next instruction, so no interrupt can be taken between it and hlt; a pending interrupt then wakes the halt. With sti, check, hlt, the interrupt can arrive after the check and before the halt: its handler runs, then the CPU halts with the work it had announced still undone, until some later interrupt.
    3. 

  3. Exit latency: how long leaving the state takes. Target residency: the minimum stay for the state to save energy overall, entry and exit included. Residency decides whether a state pays off for the predicted idle; latency decides whether the system can tolerate the wake-up delay. A state can pass one and fail the other.
    4. 

  4. That the local APIC timer keeps running in every idle state, so it can wake the CPU from deep idle. The host CPU sets it (and Linux agrees); the QEMU guest's leaf 6 EAX is 0, ARAT clear.
    5. 

  5. The temperature hovers around 90: step down at 90.0, cool to 88.9, step up, warm past 90 again, so the log shows a step on almost every check.
