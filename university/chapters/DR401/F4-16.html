Story hook

A visitor arrives who speaks a language nobody at the school knows, and no book about it exists. But the visitor came with a companion who interprets for them. The office is allowed to sit in the room. So a teacher sits in the corner with a notebook and writes down, for every exchange, what was said to the visitor and what the visitor answered. She does not copy the companion's private notes, she does not open the visitor's bag, and she asks the head teacher first whether listening in is allowed. After a week she has a phrasebook in her own words: "when you say this, the visitor answers that".

The next week a new teacher uses her phrasebook alone. On Thursday the visitor stops answering. Every question after the first one gets the same reply: "still waiting for an answer to your previous question". The new teacher had copied every phrase except the small one the companion said at the end of each exchange, the one that sounded like a polite "thank you". It was not politeness. It meant "I have heard your answer; you may stop repeating it".

This chapter is about that teacher in the corner: observing a working driver at the hardware boundary, lawfully, and turning what you see into a specification you wrote yourself. It is slow and it is honest about its limits, and it is the only way forward for tier-4 and tier-5 devices.

What you will be able to do

  • State the principle of interoperability observation: observe behaviour at the hardware boundary, write a specification in your own words, implement from your specification.
  • Choose an observation technique (MMIO tracing, hypervisor tracing, USB capture, bus capture, firmware descriptions, differential dumps) and name its limits.
  • Apply the legal and licence rules the curriculum gives, and record the check, without treating them as legal advice.
  • Capture every register access a driver makes to one device with QEMU's memory-access trace, and filter it to that device.
  • Annotate a trace: sequence, per-register behaviour, cross effects between registers; and say what the trace cannot tell you.
  • Write an observed specification that separates "seen" from "unknown", with open questions.
  • Diff two traces to find what one driver forgets to do.
Jargon box
Interoperability observation
Simple words: learning how to talk to a device by watching a program that already can, without copying that program. Analogy: the teacher in the corner with her own notebook. Precise: stage (c) of the method: observe a working driver at the hardware boundary (register reads and writes, bus transactions, USB requests), write a specification in your own words, then implement from it; the curriculum calls it lawful interoperability reverse engineering. C1
Clean-room implementation
Simple words: one person watches and writes the rules, another person who never saw the original writes the new driver from those rules. Analogy: the phrasebook passed to a teacher who was never in the room. Precise: a stricter variant that separates observer and implementer; historically used to keep implementations independent of the observed code. C1
MMIO trace
Simple words: a list of every read and write a driver makes to a device's memory-mapped registers, in order, with values. Analogy: the notebook's lines: "said this, heard that". Precise: produced on Linux by mmiotrace for a driver on real hardware, or by an emulator or hypervisor that traps each access (QEMU's memory_region_ops_read/write trace events in this lab). C1 R2
Differential register dump
Simple words: read all the registers you safely can, let the working driver do one thing, read them again, and compare. Analogy: photographing the classroom before and after one lesson. Precise: read-only dumps around a single action; shows which registers that action changed, not how; reads with side effects (read-to-clear status) make it unsafe on some devices. C1
Observed specification
Simple words: the document you write from your notes, saying only what you saw. Analogy: the phrasebook. Precise: a register-by-register and sequence description in your own words, with every statement traceable to evidence and every gap marked "unknown"; the input to the design note (F4-17). R4
Cross effect
Simple words: writing one register changes what another register reads. Analogy: saying one phrase makes the visitor change their answer to a different question. Precise: in a trace, a write at offset A followed by a read at offset B that differs from B's previous read, with no write to B in between; the usual sign of command, status and acknowledge registers. R3

The idea in pictures

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Sometimes nobody wrote down how a part works, but there is a program that already knows how to use it. You are allowed to watch what that program does to the part: every time it sends the part a number, and every time the part answers. You write it all down in your own notebook, in your own words. You do not copy the program, and you do not break any locks. Then you read your notebook and write your own program from it. Before you start, you ask a grown-up whether watching is allowed where you live.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

Law and licences, as the curriculum states them

The curriculum's note is explicitly "not legal advice". It says that interoperability reverse engineering is explicitly permitted in some jurisdictions under conditions, naming Article 6 of the EU Directive 2009/24/EC on the legal protection of computer programs (decompilation for interoperability) and the interoperability exemption in section 1201(f) of the US Digital Millennium Copyright Act; other countries differ, and licence agreements may add restrictions whose enforceability varies. Its practical rules: observe behaviour rather than copying code; never use leaked or NDA documents; never circumvent copy protection or security mechanisms; check the redistribution licence of any firmware blob before loading or shipping it; publish only your own specification text and code; record what you observed, how, and from which product; when in doubt, ask someone qualified in your jurisdiction. C1 The runbook adds that observation starts only after the learner has confirmed it is lawful where they live, and that the confirmation is recorded. C1

What a trace can and cannot tell you

A trace is a list of accesses: offset, direction, value, width. From it you can learn sequence ("ID, then CHECK, then COMPUTE"), per-register behaviour ("a read after writing V returns ~V"), and cross effects ("writing 0x60 changes what 0x24 reads"). R3 You cannot learn:

  • Intent. The trace shows that the driver wrote 0x42 to 0x60 and then 0x42 to 0x64. Whether 0x64 means "acknowledge" or "reset the interrupt logic" is a name you give it; the evidence only shows the effect on 0x24.
  • Paths not taken. The driver never wrote 0x80–0x98; the trace says nothing about what they do. Error paths, power management, and features the driver did not use in your run are invisible. Run more scenarios to see more.
  • Timing guarantees. In the saved run, the first COMPUTE needed a poll of 34 reads of 0x20 and the second 29; in other runs of the same lab the first finished before the first status read, and one run needed over 3000 reads. R3 That says the device takes some time, not how long it may take. The specification must say "poll with a timeout", not "34 polls".
  • Anything below the boundary. Interrupts that the driver did not use, DMA the device might do on its own: the lab traces memory accesses by the CPU only. The observed specification lists "does IRQ_RAISE raise a real interrupt?" as an open question for this reason. R4

Three views of one trace

The annotator prints three views because each hides something the others show. In the per-register view, 0x24 "changed between two reads with no write in between" twice: on its own, that looks like a device-driven status bit. The cross-effect view explains it: the changes follow writes to 0x60 and 0x64. R3 Reading one view alone would have produced a wrong specification line.

Reads are not always harmless

The differential dump reads 64 registers, twice, including offsets the driver never touched. On this device that is harmless: the two dumps differ only where the driver wrote (0x04 and 0x08). R1 On real hardware, reading a read-to-clear status register loses an event, and reading some FIFOs consumes data. The curriculum's rule: prefer dumps that the datasheet or an open driver says are safe. C1 For a tier-5 device you have neither, so start with the registers the working driver itself reads, and widen only with care.

How the hardware actually does it

On real hardware, an MMIO access is a load or store instruction to an address the CPU's memory map routes to a PCI Express BAR; the root complex turns it into a memory read or memory write transaction to the device. No CPU-side tool sees the transaction itself, so tracers make the access trap. Linux's mmiotrace, as the curriculum describes it, runs the traced driver with its MMIO mappings arranged so that each access faults and is logged; QEMU's VFIO path can be configured not to map device BARs directly, so that every access traps to QEMU and is logged. C1 Either way the cost is a trap per access, which is why tracing is slow and why timing-sensitive devices may behave differently while traced.

In this lab nothing needs to be arranged: QEMU emulates the device, so every guest access to the BAR already goes through QEMU's memory-region dispatch, and the trace events fire there. QEMU prints the absolute address, the value (64 bits wide), the size and the region's name for every access to every region; the full trace of the saved boot had 38781 lines, of which 202 were to edu-mmio (the number changes from run to run with the poll counts). R1 R2

Not verified — check before relying on this

How mmiotrace and VFIO trapping work internally is from memory and the curriculum's one-line descriptions; neither was run in this build (no real device, no IOMMU). QEMU's trace event names and output format are as this build's QEMU 8.2.2 printed them; other versions may differ (the filter in Listing 2 would then need a new pattern).

Worked example

Task. From the trace alone, explain offset 0x08.

  1. Sequence view. "#68 write 0x08 = 0x00000005", "#69-#102 poll 0x20 x34: 0x00000001 ... 0x00000000", "#103 read 0x08 = 0x00000078"; later "#104 write 0x08 = 0x0000000a", "#105-#133 poll 0x20 x29", "#134 read 0x08 = 0x00375f00". The access numbers are the saved run's; yours will differ with the poll counts. R3
  2. Values. 0x78 = 120 = 5!; 0x375f00 = 3628800 = 10!.
  3. Cross effect. "#104 write 0x08 = 0x0000000a -> #105 0x20 now reads 0x00000001 (was 0x00000000)": writing 0x08 starts something that 0x20 bit 0 reports as busy. R3
  4. Hypothesis. 0x08 is a compute register: write N, wait while 0x20 bit 0 is 1, read N!. Two data points; factorial is a guess that fits both.
  5. What would test it. More values (0, 1, 13, 14, where 32-bit overflow would show) and a read of 0x08 while busy. F4-17's driver computes n = 0..14 and confirms the modulo-232 results; F4-18's model records "reads N while busy" as observed. R5

Check. The specification line says "after writing N, 0x20 bit 0 became 1; when it returned to 0, 0x08 read N! (5 -> 120, 10 -> 3628800)": what was seen, with the evidence. R4

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A visitor answers in words; a device answers in bits. The teacher can ask "what did you mean?". You cannot: the meaning of a bit is only what you can make it do, and bits the driver never touched stay unknown (0x80–0x98 in this lab). R4
    • 

  • Listening can change the conversation. In a classroom, a quiet observer changes nothing. A tracer traps every access, which slows the driver down and can change timing-dependent behaviour; this lab's poll counts differ between runs. C1 R5
    • 

  • Even looking can break things. Reading a classroom's whiteboard never erases it; reading a read-to-clear register does. C1

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. The run prints "drv: at the end, 0x24 reads 0x00000042" and its differences list has a third line, "offset 0x24: 0x00000000 -> 0x00000042"; self-test result 3, exit code 35. The diff reports one difference: "delete at reference step 13: reference only: W 0x64 = 0x00000042; reference only: read(s) of 0x24".


Root cause. The learner's driver never writes 0x64 after raising the interrupt status through 0x60 (in the lab source, the write is compiled out by F416_FORGET_ACK). The specification's row for 0x24 says "after a write of V to 0x64 it read 0": the acknowledge. In the trace it looks like a redundant echo of the previous write (same value), which is why it is easy to drop as noise. Without it the status stays set; any later code that checks "is an interrupt pending?" sees one forever.


Why tests missed it. COMPUTE results do not depend on 0x24; only a test that checks the interrupt status at the end, or a trace diff against the reference, sees it.


Fix and verification. Write the same value to 0x64 after handling, then read 0x24 and require 0. Verification: the reference build's run ends "0x24 reads 0x00000000", exit code 33, and the diff of the two traces is empty. Test to add: "after raise and acknowledge, interrupt status is 0" (F4-18's T8 checks the related "status that does not clear" case).


Wrong answers and why. "The device is broken": the reference driver's run on the same device ends with 0. "A timing problem": the diff shows a missing write, not a different number of polls (polls are folded). "0x64 must be written with a different value": the reference writes the same value it raised.
