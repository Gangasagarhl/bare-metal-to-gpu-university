Story hook

The school's office keeps a big folder: one page per language a visitor might speak. Each page lists the official phrasebook and who publishes it, which phrasebooks are free and which must be bought from an association, which other schools have interpreters for that language and where their notes can be read, and a short honest line at the bottom such as "this one is hard: the visitors bring sealed letters only their own school can read". When a visitor arrives, reception does not start from nothing; it opens the folder at the right page.

One autumn a new assistant filed visitors by continent instead of by language. "Europe" went to the French page. The German visitor got a French phrasebook and a French interpreter, and the day's report said "visitor received, interpreter assigned", which was true and useless.

This chapter is the tour of that folder: the curriculum's hardware catalog, section 6. You will walk through its groups, learn to read its columns, put every device of three machines into a row with a program, and find out what happens when the program files devices by continent.

What you will be able to do

  • Navigate the catalog's seven groups and name typical rows in each.
  • Read a row: interfaces and standards with their issuing bodies, open documentation (and what "public" means), reference drivers in Linux, the BSDs and Windows, and the honest-scope notes.
  • Classify every device of a machine into a catalog row by its full class code, ACPI ID or most specific compatible string, and say "unclassified" when nothing matches.
  • Follow 6.2's procedure from a device list to a real-hardware roadmap for a machine.
  • Use the emulator's own device list as a second catalog for practice, and know its limits.
  • Diagnose a classifier that matches on too little of the class code.
Jargon box
Hardware catalog
Simple words: the curriculum's big table of every kind of device and where to learn about it. Analogy: the office folder with one page per language. Precise: section 6.1 of the Systems Curriculum: one row per device class with interfaces and standards (issuing body), open documentation, reference drivers (Linux · BSD · Windows) and notes on honest scope; the entry point to stage (b). C1
Catalog row
Simple words: one line of the catalog, for one class of device. Analogy: one page of the folder. Precise: a class such as "NVMe" or "I2C, SMBus, I3C and sensors", with its documents and drivers; the device record's "class and catalog row" field names it. C1
Issuing body
Simple words: the organisation that writes and publishes a standard. Analogy: the association that publishes a language's official phrasebook. Precise: for example PCI-SIG, USB-IF, NVM Express, the UEFI Forum, JEDEC, MIPI Alliance, IEEE, OASIS; several restrict current editions to members or sell them, which the catalog's "Open documentation" column says. C1
Reference driver
Simple words: a working driver in another OS, read to learn and to compare. Analogy: another school's interpreter whose notes you may read. Precise: the catalog's fourth column, naming drivers in Linux, the BSDs and Windows (Windows binaries are closed and studied through the Windows Driver Kit documentation and samples). C1
Real-hardware roadmap
Simple words: the list, for one machine, of every device with its row, tier and driver status. Analogy: the visitor list for the week with an interpreter planned for each. Precise: the result of 6.2: "Add a line to your design log for each device: class row, tier, driver status. That list is your real-hardware roadmap for the machine." C1

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

There is a big book that lists every kind of part a computer can have. For each kind it says: here is the rulebook, here is who wrote it, here is whether you can read it for free, and here are other people's programs for this part that you can learn from. When you meet a part, you look up its kind in the book. You must look it up by its full name, not by a rough group, or you will read the wrong rulebook. And if a part is not in the book, you write "not in the book" and find out by hand.

Each row has the same columns. "Open documentation" needs care: "Public" means downloadable without an NDA, sometimes after a free registration; several standards bodies (the catalog names PCI-SIG, VESA, MIPI Alliance, the HDMI organisations, ISO, some JEDEC and USB-IF documents) restrict current editions to members or sell them; IEEE makes many IEEE 802 standards free through its "GET Program" some time after publication; where a class depends on proprietary or signed firmware, the notes say so plainly. C1

Section 6.2 turns the catalog into a procedure: run M1; for each device find its row; if the row's class has an open standard and you already have the class driver, test it and record quirks; if not, follow the "Open documentation" column into stage (b), and if it says "members only" or "NDA", go straight to the open drivers in the next column; add a line to your design log for each device (class row, tier, driver status). That list is the machine's real-hardware roadmap. C1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

What key finds the row

A classifier needs a key per bus, and each key has a trap:

  • PCI: the full class code, then vendor rules. The three bytes together name a programming interface: 01 06 01 is AHCI, 01 08 02 is NVMe. The base class alone (01, "mass storage") covers both and many more. Some rows need the vendor too: class 02 00 00 is "Ethernet", and the row "Ethernet: Intel controllers" applies only when the vendor is 8086; the lab's classifier checks that (line 69). Virtio is recognised by vendor 1AF4 before any class rule, and its storage devices go to "virtio storage". R2
  • ACPI: the hardware ID, then the compatible IDs. PNP0501 (16550 serial port), PNP0303 (PS/2 keyboard), PNP0A08 (PCI Express root bridge) are the curriculum's own examples. C1 Vendor-specific IDs (AMZNC10C, FCVMGID on the build VM) have no row: the classifier says "unclassified". R2
  • Devicetree: the first, most specific compatible string. "arm,pl011 arm,primecell" goes to the UART row by its first string; "arm,primecell" alone would say only "an Arm PrimeCell peripheral" (UART, RTC and GPIO all carry it in this DTB). R1

Twelve devices with no row

Of the 50 devices of the three machines, 12 matched no key. R2 They fall into three kinds, each with its own next step:

  • Bookkeeping entries, not devices. LNXSYSTM and LNXSYBUS (twice) are Linux-made objects for the ACPI namespace (F4-14); linux,dummy-virt is the root node's compatible; qemu,platform with simple-bus is a container for dynamically added devices. Record them, do not write drivers for them.
  • Platform-specific devices. qemu,fw-cfg-mmio (QEMU's firmware configuration interface), AMZNC10C and FCVMGID (the build VM's vendor IDs), ACPI0013. Their rows would be "platform-specific": stage (b) from step 2 for each.
  • Real classes the classifier's table lacks. cfi-flash (a parallel NOR flash) and gpio-keys (buttons wired to GPIO lines) are real devices; the catalog has neighbouring rows (SPI NOR flash; GPIO), but the lab's key table does not map them. Extending the table is the lab's step 3. (The edu device, 1234:11e8, is the platform-specific kind: QEMU's teaching device, class 00 ff 00, belongs to no row.)

The emulator as a second catalog

QEMU's own list of device types is a catalog of a different kind: what the emulator can pretend to be. This build's QEMU lists 426 device types in 11 categories (145 of them CPU models). R3 It is valuable for practice, because every type is a device you can drive without owning it, and the curriculum's milestones lean on that (e1000, NVMe, xHCI, HD Audio, virtio). C1 But its categories are not the catalog's: QEMU files i8042, the ISA and PCI serial ports and usb-kbd under "Input devices", and an emulated device is the emulator authors' description of the hardware, which the curriculum calls "useful, but second-hand". R3 C1 One more thing the list shows by what it lacks: there is no HID-over-I2C device in this QEMU's input category, which is why F4-21 has to build its own model of a touchpad.

How the hardware actually does it

A catalog row exists because many devices share one programming model. On PCI the class code is how a device says which model it follows; the lab's q35 machine shows an AHCI function with an Intel ID (8086:2922) and NVMe and xHCI controllers with vendor ID 1B36, which QEMU uses for its own devices; by class they are "AHCI", "NVMe" and "xHCI", and the class driver does not care who made them. R2 On SoCs the same sharing happens one level down: the catalog notes that the same Synopsys or Cadence Ethernet MAC appears in dozens of SoCs, so one driver plus small glue per SoC covers them; there the shared model is found through the compatible string's family part, not a class code. C1

Some rows describe hardware that is not a separate device at all: "CPUs and SMP", "Memory controllers and DRAM", "IOMMU". They appear in a device list as CPU nodes, firmware tables (MADT, SRAT, DMAR) or not at all; on the Arm machine, the CPU and its performance-monitoring unit (arm,armv8-pmuv3) come from devicetree nodes. R1

Worked example

Task. Make the roadmap line for the q35 machine's 00:1f.3.

  1. Record (F4-14). PCI 8086:2930, class 0c 05 00, Intel ICH9 SMBus controller (pci.ids). R1
  2. Row. The classifier: "I2C, SMBus, I3C and sensors [read: NXP UM10204; SMBus Specification; milestone H2, C14]". R2
  3. Row's notes. The catalog: the Intel PCH SMBus controller is described in the PCH datasheets; "The SPD EEPROMs of memory modules sit on SMBus: read-only, never write them. I2C has no enumeration: the address comes from ACPI, devicetree or the schematic." C1
  4. Tier (F4-15). 2: vendor documents. R4
  5. Driver status. Not bound (F4-15's boot trace says "NO DRIVER").
  6. Roadmap line. "00:1f.3 | I2C, SMBus, I3C and sensors | tier 2 (Intel chipset datasheet) | no driver | read-only: SPD on this bus".

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A visitor speaks one language; a device can follow several models. A USB device can have several interfaces with different classes, a PCI function can be both "AHCI" and a vendor's RAID mode, and a devicetree node lists several compatible strings. The record may need more than one row. C1
    • 

  • Folders do not have bookkeeping pages. Device lists do: LNXSYSTM, simple-bus containers and similar entries are real lines in the list that are not devices to drive. R2
    • 

  • The phrasebook may be for sale. Some issuing bodies sell or restrict their standards; the catalog's "Open documentation" column says when to go straight to open drivers instead. C1

Forensic lab answer key


Observation. In the forensic output, the q35 machine touches 2 rows instead of 8: "AHCI (SATA) x2" and "virtio 1.x x1". The NVMe controller (class 01 08 02) moved to "AHCI (SATA)". Unclassified now: the host bridge and ISA bridge (06 00 00, 06 01 00), the e1000 (02 00 00), the xHCI controller (0c 03 30), the HD Audio controller (04 03 00) and the SMBus controller (0c 05 00), plus the build VM's host bridge: 19 unclassified in all, against 12.


Root cause. Under F419_BASE_ONLY, the key is the first two hex digits of the class (line 51). The table's only two-digit key is "01" → "AHCI (SATA)", so every mass-storage function is filed as AHCI, and every other class finds no key at all. Virtio devices are unaffected because the vendor rule (line 55) runs before the class lookup.


Which is worse. The wrong row: "unclassified" makes someone look; "AHCI" sends the storage team to the wrong specification and the wrong class driver, and an AHCI driver bound to an NVMe controller fails in confusing ways.


Fix and verification. Key on all six digits (the normal build); the normal output has 12 unclassified and NVMe under "NVMe". Test: a fixed input with one device per known class and the expected row, run on every change (the mini-project's regression test).


Wrong answers and why. "The NVMe controller reports the wrong class": F4-14's report and QEMU's list both say 01 08 02. "QEMU's AHCI and NVMe are the same device": different IDs, BARs and class bytes. "Add more two-digit keys": the base class cannot separate AHCI from NVMe whatever keys you add.
