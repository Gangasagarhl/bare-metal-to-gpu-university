Story hook

In a busy restaurant, Aiko carries a tray across the room. The head waiter stands at the bar and steers her with hand signals: left, slow down, stop. Aiko calls back "two left on the tray" so he knows how she is doing. They agreed one rule at the start of the shift: if she loses sight of his hands, she does not keep walking blind; she goes back to the meeting point by the bar.

A drone has the same arrangement. A pilot's RC transmitter sends stick positions to a receiver on the drone, many times a second. A pair of telemetry radios carries the drone's status back to a ground station. And when the stick signals stop arriving, the flight controller must notice quickly and carry out a rule agreed in advance, the failsafe. This chapter is about those links: how a frame of stick positions is built and checked, how the flight controller decides that a link is lost, how far a radio can reach, and how to test all of it on the bench before anything flies.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the path from RC transmitter sticks to the flight controller's channel values, and the role of a telemetry link to a ground station.
  • Decode a serial RC frame of packed channel values and check it with a CRC (on the university's teaching protocol U-RC).
  • Analyse how a flight controller detects RC loss for three receiver behaviours, and why one of them hides the loss.
  • Calculate a free-space link budget and explain why real range is shorter.
  • Diagnose a direction-dependent link problem from a range-check log, and assemble the course project's bench checks.
Safety

Every test in this chapter is done on the ground with propellers removed, including range checks and failsafe tests: a failsafe test is exactly the moment when the vehicle does something you did not command. S1 Follow the LiPo rules of F10-11. Transmit only on frequencies and at powers that your national radio rules allow for your equipment and licence (unverified box below). Learners below L3 work with a supervising adult. No outdoor flight belongs to this course; it comes in DN401 after sign-off, only where the national aviation authority's current rules allow it.

Jargon box
RC transmitter and receiver
Simple words: the pilot's hand-held controller and the small radio on the drone that hears it. Analogy: the head waiter's hand signals and Aiko's eyes (a proposed F10 mapping, see NOTES). Precise: a radio pair that sends the positions of the transmitter's sticks and switches as channel values in repeated frames; the receiver passes them to the flight controller as PWM per channel, a pulse train, or a serial protocol, as its maker documents. D6 D1 D2
RC channel
Simple words: one number carried by the radio link, such as the throttle stick's position. Analogy: one of the head waiter's hand signs. Precise: one value in each frame, scaled by the flight stack to a stick or switch position and mapped (channel map) to a function such as roll, pitch, throttle, yaw, flight mode or arming. D1 D2
Telemetry radio (telemetry link)
Simple words: a two-way radio that sends the drone's status to a ground station and carries commands back. Analogy: Aiko calling back "two left on the tray". Precise: a pair of radio modems that carry a serial byte stream between a flight controller port and a ground station, usually carrying MAVLink messages; its throughput and range are much lower than a wired link. D3 D1 D2
Failsafe (RC loss)
Simple words: what the drone does on its own when the pilot's signal stops. Analogy: "if you get lost, go back to the meeting point" (registered F10 analogy). Precise: a detection rule (no valid frame for a timeout, or a failsafe indication from the receiver) plus a configured action (for example hold, return or land); both must be configured and tested, because defaults may not suit your field. D1 D2 C1
Link budget
Simple words: adding up how much signal is sent, gained and lost, to see whether enough arrives. Analogy: how loudly the head waiter must call so that Aiko still hears him at the far end of the room. Precise: received power (dBm) = transmit power (dBm) + antenna gains (dBi) − cable losses (dB) − path loss (dB); the link works while received power stays above the receiver's sensitivity with a fade margin to spare. D5
Free-space path loss (FSPL)
Simple words: how much weaker a radio signal gets with distance in empty space. Analogy: a voice getting fainter across an empty hall. Precise: FSPL = 20·log10(4π·d·f/c) dB between isotropic antennas; it grows by 6.02 dB per doubling of distance and with frequency; obstacles, the ground and the vehicle body add loss on top. D5
RSSI (received signal strength)
Simple words: the receiver's measure of how strong the incoming signal is. Analogy: how clearly Aiko can see the hand signals. Precise: received signal strength indicator, reported by many receivers and radios in dBm or a maker-specific scale, often alongside a link-quality figure (share of frames received); the scale and meaning come from the maker. D6
Range check
Simple words: a ground test that the link still works at a distance before flying. Analogy: checking that Aiko can see the signals from every corner of the room before the shift. Precise: a test, often with the transmitter in a reduced-power mode documented by its maker, at a set distance with the vehicle turned through all headings, logging frame loss and RSSI. D6 D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The pilot holds a controller with sticks. It sends the stick positions to the drone by radio, about as often as a film shows pictures, many times every second.

The drone checks each message. If a message is damaged, it throws it away. If messages stop coming for a short time, the drone decides that it has lost the pilot and follows a plan agreed before the flight, such as landing.

A second radio sends the drone's news back to a computer on the ground: battery, position, warnings. Radio signals get weaker with distance and are blocked by things in the way, so the links are tested on the ground before every flight, with the propellers taken off.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From sticks to channel values

The head waiter's signals are a small fixed vocabulary repeated all the time. An RC transmitter samples its sticks and switches and sends them as channel values in a frame, over and over; the receiver passes the values on to the flight controller. D6 Older receivers output one servo-style pulse per channel (the pulse widths of F10-10); most current setups use one serial line carrying all channels in each frame, over a UART (F1-47). Which receiver protocols each flight stack accepts, and on which port, is listed in its documentation. D1 D2 This chapter uses an invented protocol, U-RC, so that every byte is known: a start byte, eight 11-bit channels packed into 11 bytes, a flags byte with a failsafe bit and a CRC-8 byte, 14 bytes in all. Listing 1 packs sticks at centre, low, high and in between into A5 00 04 20 00 00 F8 7F 00 00 08 C0 00 F7 and decodes them back to 1500, 1500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 1000, 1250 and 1750 µs; flipping one bit makes the CRC reject the frame. R1 A CRC catches every single-bit error and most longer bursts, which is why receivers and flight controllers drop a bad frame instead of acting on it (the same idea as the checksum of F10-10). D8

Noticing that the link is lost

Aiko needs a rule for "I can no longer see his hands": how long without a signal before she turns back. The flight controller uses the same rule: no valid frame for longer than a timeout means RC loss. D1 D2 But it can only judge what the receiver tells it, and receivers differ in what they do when the transmitter goes quiet. Listing 2 runs the same 600 ms silence three times with a 20 ms frame period and a 300 ms timeout: if the receiver stops sending frames, failsafe starts at 1,281 ms, 301 ms after the last good frame; if the receiver keeps sending frames with its failsafe flag set, failsafe starts at 1,000 ms; if the receiver keeps sending the last stick values without any flag, the flight controller never notices the silence at all. R2 In case C the drone would keep flying the last command, perhaps a climb, for as long as the transmitter is gone. That is why the receiver's own failsafe setting matters as much as the flight controller's, and why both are tested on the bench: switch the transmitter off with propellers removed and watch the flight controller report RC loss. D6 D2

Telemetry to a ground station

Aiko's "two left on the tray" is telemetry. A telemetry link carries a serial byte stream between a flight controller port and a ground station, which shows the vehicle's state and can send commands, parameters and missions. PX4 and ArduPilot use MAVLink for this: a set of agreed messages, the registered analogy's "walkie-talkie code words between drone and ground". D3 D1 D2 The messages themselves come in DN303 (F10-30). For this course the important points are physical: the telemetry radio's air data rate must carry the stream the flight controller sends, the serial settings at both ends must match, and losing telemetry is a separate event from losing RC, with its own detection and its own configured action. D1 D2 Listing 1's frames at 100 per second are 1,400 bytes/s; at 10 line bits per byte on a UART that already needs 14,000 bit/s before any telemetry. R1

How far a radio reaches

A voice gets fainter across a hall, and fainter still behind a pillar. The link budget adds transmit power and antenna gains, subtracts cable losses and the free-space path loss, and compares the result with the receiver's sensitivity plus a fade margin. D5 Listing 3 does this with exercise values (20 dBm, two 2 dBi antennas, 1 dB of cables, −100 dBm sensitivity, 10 dB margin): free space allows 24.6 km at 433 MHz, 11.6 km at 915 MHz and 4.4 km at 2,400 MHz; every doubling of distance costs 6.02 dB. R3 These are best cases: the ground, trees, buildings, the pilot's body, the vehicle's own frame, battery and carbon parts, antenna orientation and interference all take away margin, which is why range checks turn the vehicle through every heading. D5 D6 Lower frequencies reach further for the same budget, but which frequencies and powers you may use is not an engineering choice: national radio rules decide it.

Real receiver protocols, their wiring and the ports that accept them come from the receiver maker's documentation (D6) and the flight stack's documentation (D1, D2). The 20 ms frame period, the 300 ms timeout, the radio powers, gains, sensitivity and the three frequencies in Listings 2 and 3 are exercise values. Which frequency bands and transmit powers are legal for you depends on your country's radio regulations and your equipment's approval (D7); this build did not check them. How PX4 and ArduPilot detect RC and telemetry loss, the setting names and the available failsafe actions must be read in their documentation (D1, D2) for the recorded versions; they are covered in depth in F10-35.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Latency budget. Stick-to-motor delay is the frame period plus the receiver's processing plus the serial transfer plus the flight controller's polling of the RC input and its control cycle (F10-07). A faster frame rate shortens the first term but needs more bandwidth; the timeout must be several frame periods so that one lost frame does not trigger failsafe. D1 D6
  • Debouncing the decision. A timeout of N frame periods tolerates N − 1 consecutive losses; Listing 2's 300 ms timeout tolerates 14 lost frames. Too short a timeout makes false failsafes in brief fades; too long delays the response to a real loss. R2
  • Link quality vs RSSI. RSSI says how strong the signal is; the share of frames received says whether the link is working. Mean RSSI computed over received frames only is biased upwards, because the weakest frames are the ones lost (the forensic lab shows this). D6
  • Antennas and diversity. A dipole radiates poorly along its own axis; two antennas at right angles, or two receivers, reduce deep nulls. Antenna placement away from carbon and metal and away from the ESC and power wiring belongs in the wiring plan (F9-15). D5
  • Interference on board. Switching ESCs, video transmitters and GNSS receivers share the vehicle: a radio can desensitise a nearby receiver even on another band. Test with everything powered (propellers removed). D5 D2
  • Security. Whether a link is encrypted or authenticated depends on the system; MAVLink defines message signing, and the threat model of a drone's radio link is the subject of F11-16. D3

How the hardware actually does it

An RC receiver contains a radio chip that demodulates the transmitter's packets, often hopping between frequencies to avoid interference, and a small microcontroller that checks each packet, tracks whether the link is alive and writes frames to its output at a fixed rate. D6 On the flight controller the serial frames arrive through a UART, often with DMA, and a driver finds the start byte, checks the CRC and hands channel values and flags to the flight stack (F1-44, F1-47); the flight stack scales and maps channels, and its RC-loss logic runs in a task (F10-07). D1 D2 A telemetry radio is a pair of radio modems that buffer serial bytes, send them in packets and deliver them at the other end; its air data rate, serial rate and settings are configured by its own tools. D6 Some serial receiver protocols are inverted or half-duplex, which matters for which UART pins can take them; the board's documentation lists what each port supports. D1 D4

Not verified — check before relying on this

No transmitter, receiver, telemetry radio or flight controller is chosen for the course and none was tested in this build. Frequency hopping, packet rates, inversion and half-duplex wiring, receiver failsafe modes (stop output, send a flag, hold or preset values), the reduced-power range-check mode and RSSI scales are described in general terms and must be confirmed for the chosen equipment from D6 and for the flight stack from D1 and D2. Whether the board's RC and telemetry ports follow the Pixhawk connector standard is confirmed from D4 and the board's documentation.

Worked example

Task. Check two numbers from Listings 1 and 3 by hand.

  1. Bit packing. Channel 1 is 1024 = 210: only bit 10 is set. Channel 1's bits are stream bits 0–10, so bit 10 lands in byte 1 + 10/8 = byte 2 (counting the start byte as byte 0), bit position 10 mod 8 = 2: byte 2 = 0x04. Channel 2 (also 1024) has its bit 10 at stream bit 21: byte 1 + 2 = byte 3, position 5: 0x20. Listing 1 prints A5 00 04 20 …. R1
  2. Scaling. 1000 + 1024 × 1000/2047 = 1500.24, printed as 1500; 512 gives 1250.1; 1536 gives 1750.4. R1
  3. Path loss at 2.4 GHz, 1 km. 4π·d·f/c = 4π × 1000 × 2.4×109 / 299,792,458 = 100,601; 20·log10(100,601) = 100.05 dB. Received: 20 + 4 − 1 − 100.05 = −77.05 dBm. Listing 3 prints 100.1 and −77.1. R3
  4. Range with margin. Allowed loss = 20 + 4 − 1 − (−100) − 10 = 113 dB. Every 20 dB is a factor 10 in distance, so the range is 1 km × 10(113 − 100.05)/20 = 1 km × 100.6475 = 4.44 km. Listing 3 prints 4.4 km. R3

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Aiko knows at once when she cannot see the signals; a flight controller may never know. A receiver that repeats the last values hides the loss completely (Listing 2, case C). R2
    • 

  • The meeting point is the same for every waiter; the right failsafe action depends on the field. Return, land or hold can each be the wrong choice somewhere: a return path over trees, a landing on water. The action must be chosen and tested for each site (F10-35). C1
    • 

  • The head waiter's signals do not fade with distance in a room; radio does, and unevenly. The vehicle's own body can block the link in one direction (forensic lab). R5
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: switching the transmitter off does not trigger RC loss on the bench. Cause: the receiver's own failsafe is set to hold or preset values without a flag. How to find it: watch the channel values in the ground station while switching off. Fix: set the receiver to stop output or to signal failsafe, as its documentation allows; repeat the test.
    • 

  • Symptom: no channels at all. Cause: wrong port, protocol, inversion or baud setting; receiver not bound. How to find it: the receiver's own indicator, the flight stack's RC input status. Fix: wire per the board's documentation; bind per the receiver's.
    • 

  • Symptom: a stick moves the wrong function. Cause: channel map or transmitter mixing. How to find it: move one stick at a time in the RC calibration screen. Fix: correct the map; recalibrate.
    • 

  • Symptom: telemetry connects at first then drops. Cause: stream larger than the radio's air rate, or serial settings mismatched. How to find it: radio's own statistics; lower stream rates. Fix: match settings; reduce the stream.
    • 

  • Symptom: range check passes facing the transmitter but fails sideways or away. Cause: antenna placement, orientation or shading by the frame or battery. How to find it: log loss against heading (forensic lab). Fix: move or reorient antennas; add diversity; recheck.

Summary


    

  • An RC transmitter sends channel values in repeated frames; the receiver passes them to the flight controller over pulses or a serial protocol.
    • 

  • A CRC rejects damaged frames; a frame that fails is dropped, never half-used.
    • 

  • RC loss is detected by a timeout or a receiver flag; a receiver that holds the last values can hide the loss completely.
    • 

  • Telemetry radios carry MAVLink between the flight controller and a ground station; losing telemetry is a separate event from losing RC.
    • 

  • Free-space path loss grows 6.02 dB per doubling of distance; real range is shorter than free space, so range checks turn the vehicle through every heading.
    • 

  • Frequencies and powers are set by national radio rules; all link tests are done on the ground with propellers removed.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. link_log.cpp simulates 6,000 frame slots (120 s, 20 ms) while the heading turns at 6° per second (two full turns). The transmitter is at heading 0. RSSI is −78 dBm ± 3 dB, minus a shading term of up to 22 dB with a cosine shape for headings within 40° of 190° (the vehicle's body between the receiver antenna and the transmitter when facing away). A frame is lost when RSSI is below −96 dBm ± 2 dB.


Observation. 489 of 6,000 frames lost (5,511 received), all in headings 150–240°: 81 in 150–180, 405 in 180–210, 3 in 210–240; none elsewhere. Longest gap 940 ms starting at t = 30,220 ms (heading 181°); 23 gaps over 100 ms and 9 over 300 ms (3 in the first turn, at headings 181–193°, and 6 in the second, at 182–197°; a second 940 ms gap starts at t = 91,460 ms). R5


Timeout. Yes: each of the 9 gaps over 300 ms would trigger a 300 ms RC-loss timeout, all at headings 181–197°, facing away from the transmitter, in both turns.


Hypotheses. Interference at certain times would not repeat at the same heading in both turns; a loose connector would give losses at random headings; shading repeats at the same headings in both turns and grows smoothly towards one direction. The evidence fits shading.


RSSI bias. Only 98 of 503 frames in sector 180–210 arrived, and those were the strongest; the true mean signal there was lower than −95.0 dBm. Lost-frame share is the honest measure.


Root cause and fix. The receiver's antenna is shaded by the vehicle (frame, battery, wiring) when the vehicle faces away from the transmitter. Fix: move the antenna to a clear position or add a second antenna at a different position or orientation (diversity), then repeat the range check and show no loss at any heading. Until then the vehicle is not fit to fly, even though the transmitter's own display looked fine: the transmitter cannot see what the vehicle's receiver loses.
