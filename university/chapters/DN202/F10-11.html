Story hook

Priya works a long shift carrying trays. She has a water bottle on her belt, and every hour she checks how much is left. She has learned two tricks. Right after a sprint up the stairs she feels more tired than she really is, so she waits a moment before judging. And she does not trust her feeling alone: she counts the glasses of water she has drunk, because that number does not change with how out of breath she is.

A drone's battery is its water bottle, and the flight controller must know how much is left. A small board called a power module measures the battery's voltage and current. Voltage drops during a climb and recovers afterwards, like Priya's breath; counting the charge used, like counting glasses, is steadier but only as good as the current measurement. And the bottle itself is a lithium-polymer battery that must be handled with real care, because it can burn.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how a power module measures battery voltage and current and how the flight controller turns ADC counts into volts, amperes and consumed charge.
  • Calibrate a power module's voltage and current scales from reference measurements.
  • Explain voltage sag and estimate a pack's internal resistance from a load step; compare raw-voltage, sag-corrected and charge-based battery warnings.
  • Apply the LiPo safety rules to charging, storage, transport, damage and disposal, using the battery maker's and charger maker's documents.
  • Diagnose a mis-calibrated current sensor from a flight log and a charger report.
Safety — LiPo batteries (read before anything else in this chapter)

The guide's safety rule, quoted: "LiPo batteries (lithium polymer) can catch fire if punctured, over-charged, over-discharged, short-circuited or charged unattended." S1 Therefore, in every lab of this university: use a charger made for LiPo and set it to the pack's chemistry and cell count; charge in a fire-resistant bag or container on a non-flammable surface; never charge unattended; store packs at the storage voltage the battery maker documents; never use or charge a pack that is swollen, dented, punctured or has damaged wires; never short the terminals (cover them, and do not let tools or loose wires touch them); disconnect the pack whenever you are not actively testing; and dispose of damaged packs according to local rules. S1 D1 D2 This chapter quotes no voltage, current or temperature limit for any real battery: those come only from the maker's documents (D1, D2). Learners below L3 handle LiPo packs only with a supervising adult; if a pack becomes hot, swells or smokes, move away and follow your site's emergency procedure, do not try to handle it.

Jargon box
Power module (power monitor)
Simple words: a small board in the battery cable that feeds the flight controller and measures the battery's voltage and current. Analogy: the marks on Priya's water bottle plus her count of glasses (a proposed F10 mapping, see NOTES). Precise: a board between the battery and the vehicle's power distribution that supplies the flight controller from a regulator and reports battery voltage and current, either as analog voltages for the controller's ADC or as digital values over a bus (I2C, SMBus or CAN). D4 D5 D6
Voltage divider (sense divider)
Simple words: two resistors that scale a big voltage down to a small one the chip can measure. Analogy: a measuring cup that holds one eleventh of the bottle, so it fits on the scale. Precise: a series pair R1, R2 with Vout = Vin·R2/(R1 + R2); the flight controller multiplies the measured pin voltage by the inverse ratio (the "divider" setting). D7 D4
Current sense (shunt or Hall sensor)
Simple words: a part that turns the battery current into a small voltage. Analogy: a flow meter in the bottle's straw. Precise: a low-value shunt resistor with an amplifier, or a Hall-effect sensor, whose output voltage is offset + gain × current; the flight controller needs the gain (or its inverse, amperes per volt) and the offset. D7 D6
Consumed charge (mAh counter)
Simple words: how much charge has been taken from the battery since take-off. Analogy: counting the glasses of water drunk. Precise: the integral of measured current over time, in milliampere-hours (1 mAh = 3.6 coulombs); it is exact only if the current measurement is calibrated. D3 D4
Voltage sag
Simple words: the battery voltage dropping while a large current is drawn, and coming back afterwards. Analogy: feeling more tired right after a sprint than you really are. Precise: the drop I·R across the pack's internal resistance R (plus wiring), so terminal voltage = open-circuit voltage − I·R; it hides the state of charge during high current. D3 (HW302's term "Internal resistance and voltage sag", F1-71.)
Sag compensation
Simple words: adding the expected drop back to the measured voltage, to judge the battery fairly during a climb. Analogy: Priya allowing for the sprint before she judges how tired she is. Precise: estimating the open-circuit voltage as V + I·R with an estimate of R, so that battery warnings do not trigger on short high-current events. D3 D4
Balance charging
Simple words: charging so that every cell in the pack ends up at the same level. Analogy: filling every glass on the tray to the same line. Precise: charging a series pack through its main leads while the charger monitors each cell through the balance connector and equalises cells, so that no cell is over-charged; required by LiPo packs as their makers document. D1 D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A drone flies on a rechargeable battery. A little board called a power module sits in the battery cable. It tells the flight controller the battery's voltage and how much current is flowing.

When the drone climbs fast, it pulls a lot of current and the voltage dips for a moment, like being out of breath after a sprint. So the drone also counts how much charge it has used, which is steadier, but only if the current was measured correctly.

The batteries are LiPo batteries. They hold a lot of energy and can catch fire if they are damaged, short-circuited or charged the wrong way. So they are always charged with the right charger, in a fireproof bag, with someone watching.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

From battery to ADC counts

Priya reads the marks on her bottle; the flight controller reads two pins. An analog power module scales the battery voltage down with a voltage divider and turns the current into a small voltage with a current sensor; the flight controller's ADC converts both pin voltages to counts (F1-64), and two settings convert them back: the divider ratio for voltage, and the amperes per volt (plus an offset) for current. D4 D5 D7 In Listing 1's pretend module (divider 11, 0.050 V per A, 0.010 V offset, 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference), a 15.20 V, 12.5 A battery gives 1,715 and 788 counts; one count is 0.0089 V of battery voltage and 0.0161 A of current. R1 Digital power modules do the conversion themselves and send values over a bus; the flight controller then needs no scales, but the module must still be calibrated by its maker or by you. D4 D6

Calibrating the scales

A count of glasses is only as good as the glass size. If the controller's settings do not match the module, every number is wrong by a factor. Listing 1's settings "as found" have the right divider but a current scale of 14 A per volt copied from another module, and no offset: the voltage reads 15.203 V (right) and the current 8.890 A instead of 12.5 A. R1 A two-point calibration fixes both gain and offset: measure the counts at two known currents (a clamp meter or an electronic load) and solve for the line. Listing 1 does this with 137 counts at 2.0 A and 1,253 counts at 20.0 A, gets 20.015 A per volt and an offset of 0.0105 V, and then reads 12.500 A. R1 Voltage is calibrated the same way against a multimeter at the battery's connector. Flight stacks document both calibrations and the setting names. D4 D5

Consumed charge

Counting glasses is steadier than judging by breath. The consumed charge is the sum of current × time; at 10 samples per second each sample adds I × 0.1 / 3.6 mAh. D3 In Listing 1, 10 minutes at 12.5 A is 2,083 mAh; with the uncalibrated current scale, the counter shows 1,482 mAh, 29 % too little, for the whole flight. R1 A charge counter does not know the pack's real capacity or how full it was at take-off, so flight stacks combine it with voltage and with the capacity you configure. D4 D5

Voltage sag and better warnings

Priya waits after a sprint before she judges. The terminal voltage of a pack is its open-circuit voltage minus I·R, where R is the internal resistance of the cells plus wiring (F1-71). D3 In Listing 2's pretend pack (4 cells, 3.0 Ah, 0.040 Ω), a climb at 45 A instead of 12 A drops the raw voltage by 1.32 V (0.33 V per cell). A warning on raw voltage at 3.65 V per cell triggers in the first climb, at 100 s with only 333 mAh used; the same warning on sag-corrected voltage V + I·R triggers at 564 s with 2,100 mAh used; and a warning at 80 % of the configured capacity triggers at 638 s with 2,402 mAh. R2 A warning that fires in every climb is soon ignored; one that never fires is dangerous. Sag compensation and charge counting both need calibration (R, the current scale, the capacity), which is why the course project measures them on the bench. Listing 2 uses the model's true R; in the lab you estimate it from a load step, as the worked example does. D4 D5

Handling LiPo packs

Priya's bottle is harmless; a LiPo pack is not. A lithium-polymer pack stores a large amount of energy in thin, flexible cells; mechanical damage, over-charge, over-discharge, short circuits and heat can make a cell fail and burn. D3 S1 The rules in the safety box at the top of this chapter come from the guide; each pack's numbers come from its maker. In practice: charge only with a balance charging LiPo charger set to the correct cell count, in a fire-resistant container, attended; check every pack before use for swelling, damage and cell imbalance (the charger shows per-cell voltages); store at the maker's storage voltage; never leave a pack connected to the vehicle in storage, because the electronics slowly discharge it; and treat transport of lithium batteries as regulated, checking the current rules of your carrier and authority. D1 D2

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Accuracy of an analog chain. The voltage reading's error is the sum of the divider resistors' tolerance, the ADC reference's error and the ADC's own errors; calibration against a multimeter removes the fixed part, not drift with temperature. The current reading adds the sensor's offset drift, which dominates at low current. D7 D6
  • Estimating R. From two operating points, R = (V1 − V2)/(I2 − I1), measured within a second or two so that the state of charge has not changed. R depends on temperature, state of charge and age; a pack whose R has grown sags more and is a candidate for retirement. D3
  • Where to put the sense point. A voltage measured at the power module includes the drop in the battery's leads and connector; a voltage measured at the flight controller after a long, thin wire includes more. Measure where you mean to measure, and include wiring in R. D7
  • Noise. ESC switching makes the current waveform noisy; the flight stack filters the readings. A filter with a long time constant smooths the warning but delays it; the battery failsafe must still trigger in time (F10-35). D4 D5
  • Smart batteries. Some packs contain a fuel-gauge chip that counts charge, knows the pack's capacity and health and reports them over SMBus or CAN; the flight stack then reads them instead of estimating (F3-42 studied SMBus battery reporting on laptops). D4 D5
  • The flight controller's own supply. The regulator in the power module feeds the flight controller; a brown-out during a current spike resets the controller in the air (F1-71), so the regulator's input range and the supply's redundancy (some boards accept two power modules) belong in the power design. D6 D4

How the hardware actually does it

A shunt-based current sensor puts a resistor of a few milliohms in the main current path and amplifies the small voltage across it with a current-sense amplifier; a Hall-effect sensor measures the magnetic field of the conductor instead, with no resistor in the path but more offset drift. D7 The divider is two resistors chosen so that the highest battery voltage stays below the ADC's reference. On the flight controller the ADC samples both pins, often with DMA, and the flight stack applies the scales and filters (F1-64). D4 A digital power monitor chip contains the same amplifier and an ADC of its own and returns voltage, current and sometimes accumulated charge in registers read over I2C. D6 Inside each LiPo cell, lithium ions move between electrodes through an electrolyte in a soft pouch; the pouch is why a damaged or gassing cell swells, and the chemistry is why over-charge and over-discharge damage it. D3

Worked example

Task. From Listing 2's output, estimate the pack's internal resistance, and check the sag-corrected voltage at 105 s.

  1. Two operating points close in time. At 105 s: 45.0 A, 14.07 V. At 106 s: 12.0 A, 15.38 V. R2
  2. Resistance. R = (15.38 − 14.07)/(45.0 − 12.0) = 1.31/33.0 = 0.0397 Ω, close to the model's 0.040 Ω (the 1 s between rows used 12.5 mAh, which lowers the open-circuit voltage a little and causes the small difference).
  3. Corrected voltage at 105 s. (14.07 + 45.0 × 0.0397)/4 = (14.07 + 1.79)/4 = 3.965 V per cell; Listing 2 prints 3.968 with the exact R. R2
  4. Current scale check. If the current reading were 30 % low (as with the settings "as found" in Listing 1), the same data would give R = 1.31/(0.7 × 33.0) = 0.0567 Ω: a wrong current scale also corrupts sag compensation.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Priya's bottle cannot hurt her; a LiPo pack can. A damaged, shorted or wrongly charged pack can catch fire, which is why this chapter starts with a safety box and not with the analogy. S1
    • 

  • Counting glasses is exact; counting charge is not. The count is only as good as the current sensor's calibration: Listing 1's uncalibrated counter showed 1,482 mAh for 2,083 mAh used. R1
    • 

  • Priya's breath recovers the same way every time; a pack's sag grows with age and cold. Internal resistance depends on temperature and wear, so a sag correction calibrated on a new pack on a warm day underestimates the drop of an old pack on a cold one. D3
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the battery warning fires at every climb early in the flight. Cause: warning on raw voltage with sag. How to find it: plot voltage and current together; the dips line up with current peaks. Fix: sag compensation with a measured R, or a charge-based warning (after calibrating current).
    • 

  • Symptom: the charger puts back much more than the log says was used. Cause: current scale or offset wrong. How to find it: compare logged mAh with the charger's report over several flights (forensic lab). Fix: two-point current calibration; check again.
    • 

  • Symptom: voltage reads a few percent off a multimeter. Cause: divider setting not calibrated, or the multimeter measured elsewhere. How to find it: measure at the module's input with the vehicle idle. Fix: correct the divider setting from that reading.
    • 

  • Symptom: the flight controller resets when motors start. Cause: brown-out of its supply (F1-71). How to find it: the controller's reset reason and a supply-voltage log. Fix: a supply path that stays in range under load; separate or redundant supply.
    • 

  • Symptom: a pack's cells differ noticeably at the end of a flight. Cause: a weak or ageing cell. How to find it: the charger's per-cell display. Fix: retire the pack per the maker's guidance; never fly a pack you are unsure of.
    •

Summary


    

  • A power module supplies the flight controller and measures battery voltage (divider) and current (shunt or Hall sensor); the controller needs the right scales and offset.
    • 

  • Two-point (or least-squares) calibration against a multimeter and a clamp meter or load fixes gain and offset.
    • 

  • Consumed charge integrates current; a 30 % current-scale error makes the counter 30 % low for the whole flight.
    • 

  • Voltage sags by I·R under load; raw-voltage warnings fire in climbs; sag-corrected and charge-based warnings are steadier if calibrated.
    • 

  • LiPo packs can catch fire: LiPo charger, balance charging, fire-resistant container, never unattended, storage voltage and limits from the maker.
    • 

  • A log-versus-charger comparison and a resting-voltage check reveal current-sensor errors.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. flight_power_log.cpp simulates 481 s of flight at 13 A, alternating with 15 A every 40 s, on the pretend pack of Listing 2 (4 cells, 3.0 Ah, 0.040 Ω, the same open-circuit table). The log's current is the true current × 14/20 = 0.70 (a current scale of 14 A per volt on a module that needs 20 A per volt); the voltage is correct. The resting voltage is the open-circuit voltage after the flight; the charger reports the true charge used × 1.02 (an exercise charging overhead).


Observation. Logged 1,307 mAh; charger 1,908 mAh; ratio 0.685. The logged current is 9.10 or 10.50 A, values 0.70 times 13 and 15 A. Resting voltage 14.75 V, 3.688 V per cell. R3


State of charge. In the pretend table, 3.688 V lies between 3.60 V (20 %) and 3.75 V (50 %): 20 % + (0.088/0.150) × 30 % = 37.6 %, so about 62 % of 3.0 Ah (1.87 Ah) was used. This agrees with the charger (1,908 mAh including the 2 % overhead) and not with the log (1,307 mAh, 44 %).


Root cause. The current scale (amperes per volt) is set too low by a factor of 0.70 (14 instead of 20): the setting copied from another module in Listing 1. A pure offset error would add a constant to the current and would not give a constant ratio at both 13 A and 15 A; a pack with less capacity would not change the logged current; a charger error would not change the resting voltage.


Fix and verification. Calibrate the current scale (and offset) as in Listing 1 and the lab: the setting becomes about 20 A per volt. Verify with a test whose logged consumed charge agrees with the charger's report within the chosen tolerance. Risk: a 12-minute flight at about 13–15 A would use about 2.8 Ah of the 3.0 Ah pretend pack while the log showed about 1.96 Ah: the charge-based warning would have come far too late, and the pack would have been over-discharged or the vehicle would have lost power in the air.


Prevention. Current calibration on every new power module and after any configuration change; an automatic log-versus-charger comparison after every flight (the mini-project's checker).
