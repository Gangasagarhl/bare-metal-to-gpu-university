Story hook

Mateo carries a tray of glasses across a ship's dining room while the engines hum. Through his shoes and arms the deck's shaking reaches the tray, and the drinks ripple. An old waiter shows him a trick: a folded cloth under the glasses. The fast shaking of the deck is soaked up by the cloth, while the slow, deliberate tilts of Mateo's arms still reach the glasses.

The cloth must be right. Too thin, and it does nothing. Too soft, and the glasses sway on it, sloshing more than before. And if the shaking is so strong that a glass jumps and lands hard, no cloth helps. A drone's flight controller has the same problem with motors and propellers shaking its frame, and the same solution: a soft mount, chosen with care, and a sensor that is never shaken beyond its range.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain where vibration on a multirotor comes from and how it reaches the IMU through the frame and the mount.
  • Compute the transmissibility of a soft mount and choose its natural frequency relative to the vibration frequencies.
  • Explain why accelerometer clipping shifts the measured mean, and why that is worse than noise.
  • Analyse an accelerometer log for vibration level, clipping and dominant frequencies, and cross-check the analysis with an independent tool.
  • Diagnose a mounting fault from two flight logs and specify the fix and its verification.
Jargon box
Vibration isolation (soft mount)
Simple words: a soft layer between the frame and the flight controller that soaks up fast shaking. Analogy: the folded cloth under the glasses (a proposed F10 mapping, see NOTES). Precise: a compliant, damped support that, together with the mass it carries, forms a mass–spring–damper; above about √2 times its natural frequency it transmits less vibration than a rigid mount, below that it transmits as much or more. D1 R1
Natural frequency (of a mount)
Simple words: the frequency at which the board likes to bounce on its mount. Analogy: the rate at which the glasses sway on a soft cloth if you nudge the tray once. Precise: fn = (1/2π)·√(k/m) for stiffness k and supported mass m; excitation near fn is amplified. D1
Damping ratio (ζ)
Simple words: how quickly the bouncing dies away. Analogy: a thick, slightly sticky cloth that stops the swaying quickly. Precise: the ratio of the damping coefficient to its critical value; it limits the peak at resonance (to about 1/(2ζ) for small ζ) and slightly reduces isolation at high frequency. D1
Transmissibility
Simple words: how much of the frame's shaking reaches the board, as a ratio. Analogy: how much of the deck's hum still ripples the drinks. Precise: for base excitation of a mass–spring–damper, the ratio of the mass's amplitude to the base's amplitude, T(r) = √(1 + (2ζr)²) / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²), with r = f/fn. D1 R1
Clipping (sensor saturation)
Simple words: when the shaking is bigger than the sensor can measure, so its readings stick at the largest number. Analogy: a glass that jumps off the tray: the cloth cannot fix that. Precise: the sensor's output is limited to its full-scale range; samples beyond it are replaced by the limit value, which distorts the signal and, for asymmetric peaks, shifts its mean. D5 R2
Vibration level (vibration metric)
Simple words: one number per axis that says how strongly the sensor is shaking. Analogy: how big the ripples on the drinks are. Precise: in this chapter, the standard deviation of an accelerometer axis over a window (the vibration's RMS with the mean removed); PX4 and ArduPilot log their own vibration metrics and clipping counters, defined in their documentation. D2 D3
Blade-pass frequency
Simple words: how many times per second a propeller blade passes the same place. Analogy: the beat of the ship's engine felt through the deck. Precise: the rotation frequency multiplied by the number of blades; aerodynamic and structural excitation of a multirotor's frame appears at the rotation frequency (imbalance), the blade-pass frequency and their harmonics. D1 D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Spinning motors and propellers make a drone shake very fast. The flight controller's motion sensor feels that shaking on top of the slow tilts it is supposed to measure.

A soft mount works like a cloth under glasses. Fast shaking is soaked up; slow tilting still gets through. But the mount must be the right softness. If it is too soft, the board wobbles on it.

The worst case is shaking so strong that the sensor's numbers hit their limit. Then the sensor reports a wrong average, and the drone may think it is falling when it is not. That is why engineers measure vibration before every first flight.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

Where vibration comes from

The ship's engine shakes the deck at its own beat. On a multirotor the main sources are the rotating parts: a propeller or motor that is not balanced shakes the frame once per revolution; each blade passing an arm gives an excitation at the blade-pass frequency; bent shafts, worn bearings and loose parts add their own lines; and the frame's arms have resonances that amplify whatever excites them. D1 D2 D3 Because motor speed follows throttle, these lines move in frequency as the vehicle manoeuvres; in a spectrum of a hover they appear as sharp peaks at the rotation frequency, the blade-pass frequency and multiples. D4 HW302's "jumpy IMU" (F1-65) was the same phenomenon on a robot.

How a soft mount works

The cloth under the glasses is a spring with friction. A flight controller on a soft mount is modelled as a mass m on a spring k with damping c, shaken at its base by the frame: x″ = −2ζωn(x′ − y′) − ωn²(x − y), with ωn = 2πfn. D1 For a sinusoidal base motion the steady-state ratio of amplitudes is the transmissibility T(r). Listing 1 integrates the equation with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (MA302) and agrees with the formula to three decimals at all seven test frequencies: 3.480 at resonance, exactly 1.000 at r = √2, 0.381 at r = 2 and 0.044 at r = 7.6. R1 Three design rules follow. First, the mount isolates only above √2·fn, so fn must be well below the lowest strong vibration line. Second, the mount amplifies near fn, so nothing important may excite it there; in particular, the controller's own bandwidth must stay well below fn, or the board moving on its mount becomes part of the control loop. Third, damping lowers the resonance peak but costs some isolation at high frequency. D1 D2

Noise you can filter, clipping you cannot

A ripple on the drinks averages out; a glass that jumps off does not come back. Vibration that stays inside the sensor's range is an oscillation around the true value: a low-pass filter or an average removes it, as long as it was sampled without aliasing (F1-64). D4 Vibration that exceeds the range is cut off. Because the accelerometer's z axis carries +1 g in a hover, the positive peaks reach the limit first, and the average of what remains falls below 1 g. Listing 2 shows this with a 190 Hz vibration: up to 14.9 g amplitude the mean stays at 1.0000 g; at 16 g the mean is 0.9247 g, at 18 g it is 0.7002 g (29.25 % of samples clipped) and at 22 g 0.5189 g. R2 An estimator that integrates this biased acceleration believes the vehicle is accelerating downward and the height controller responds by climbing (or, in other geometries, descending): a vibration problem that looks like a control problem. D2 D3 No filter after the sensor can undo this, because the information is gone before the filter sees it.

Measuring vibration

To see the ripples you must look at the drinks, not at the waiter's face. Vibration is measured from the raw (unfiltered) accelerometer at a rate high enough to resolve the lines you care about, for a steady hover. Three quantities matter: the vibration level per axis (here the standard deviation over the window), the number of samples at the range limit, and the spectrum, which tells you which source is responsible. D2 D3 D4 Listing 3 computes all three from a CSV file; spectrum_check.py repeats the computation with numpy and gives the same numbers (the forensic lab). R4 R5

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Six degrees of freedom. A real mount lets the board translate in three axes and rotate about three; each mode has its own natural frequency, and coupling between them means a vertical excitation can rock the board. Rotational modes are the dangerous ones for a gyroscope. Symmetric placement of the mount points around the board's centre of mass reduces coupling. D1
  • Cables as springs. A stiff cable from the frame to the board is a second spring in parallel with the mount and can raise fn enough to defeat the isolation; flexible, looped cables preserve it. D1 D2
  • Adding mass. Since fn = √(k/m)/(2π), adding mass to the isolated part lowers fn without softer material; doubling m divides fn by √2. D1
  • Sampling and aliasing. If the IMU's internal sampling and filtering let vibration above half the output rate through, it folds down to a low frequency that no later filter can tell from real motion (F1-64). IMUs offer internal digital low-pass filters and output rates; flight stacks configure them and then apply their own low-pass and notch filters, including notch filters that follow the motor speed. D4 D5 D2 D3 Filters add delay to the rate loop, so the best filter is the vibration you never let in (DN301, F10-17).
  • Choosing the range. A larger accelerometer range clips later at the cost of coarser resolution (F1-65: one count = range / 32,768). Some IMUs sample internally faster than they output and report clipping that happened between output samples; whether the course's part does this is a datasheet question (D5).
  • Gyroscopes vibrate too. Rotational vibration and, for some MEMS designs, sensitivity to linear vibration (g-sensitivity) put noise and bias into the gyro; this matters most for the rate loop. D5

How the hardware actually does it

Inside the IMU, the MEMS structure itself is a tiny mass on springs with its own resonance far above the band of interest; vibration close to that resonance can disturb the measurement, which is why datasheets specify the mechanical environment the part tolerates. D5 The analog front end and converter sample at the internal rate; a digital filter and decimator produce the output rate; and the output register saturates at the configured full-scale range (F1-64, F1-65). D5 Outside, the mount is foam, gel or elastomer elements; their stiffness and damping depend on temperature and on how much they are compressed, so a mount tuned on a warm bench may behave differently on a cold morning. D1 Some flight controllers include an internal soft mount for the IMU, sometimes with a heater to keep the IMU's temperature stable. D2

Worked example

Task. A quadcopter's motors turn at 95 revolutions per second in hover with two-blade propellers. You may choose a mount with fn = 25 Hz or 60 Hz (ζ = 0.15 for both). The rate loop's bandwidth is far below both. Which mount, and how much of the strongest line reaches the IMU?

  1. Lines. Once per revolution: 95 Hz. Blade pass: 2 × 95 = 190 Hz (the strongest, in this model).
  2. Mount 25 Hz. r = 190/25 = 7.6: T = √(1 + (2·0.15·7.6)²) / √((1 − 7.6²)² + (2·0.15·7.6)²) = √(1 + 5.198) / √(56.76² + 5.198) = 2.490 / 56.81 = 0.044. At 95 Hz, r = 3.8: T = √(1 + 1.300)/√(13.44² + 1.300) = 1.517/13.49 = 0.112. Listing 1 prints 0.044 for 190 Hz. R1
  3. Mount 60 Hz. r = 190/60 = 3.17: T = √(1 + 0.902)/√(9.03² + 0.902) = 1.379/9.08 = 0.152. At 95 Hz, r = 1.58: T = √(1 + 0.225)/√(1.51² + 0.225) = 1.107/1.58 = 0.70.
  4. Decision. The 25 Hz mount passes 4.4 % of the 190 Hz line and 11 % of the 95 Hz line; the 60 Hz mount passes 15 % and 70 %. Choose 25 Hz, provided the controller does not excite 25 Hz and the cables do not stiffen it. With 18 g at the frame, the 25 Hz mount leaves 0.79 g at the IMU (the forensic lab's flight A shows 0.790 g at 190 Hz). R4

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • A cloth under glasses always helps a little; a mount can make things worse. Near its natural frequency a mount amplifies (3.480 times at 25 Hz in Listing 1), which a cloth under glasses never seems to do. R1
    • 

  • Mateo sees a glass jump; the flight controller does not. A clipped sample looks like a valid number. Unless the software counts samples at the limit, clipping is invisible, and its effect appears later as a wrong mean. R2
    • 

  • The deck's hum has one beat; a multirotor has many that move. Rotation and blade-pass lines change frequency with throttle, so a mount and filters must work over a range of frequencies, not one. D2
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: after a crash repair the vehicle climbs or sinks in altitude hold, with no other fault. Cause: accelerometer clipping from vibration (a damaged propeller, a hard-mounted board). How to find it: clipping counts and the z mean in a hover log (forensic lab). Fix: remove the source, restore the mount; verify with a new log.
    • 

  • Symptom: a new, softer mount makes the vehicle wobble slowly. Cause: fn too low, inside the controller's bandwidth. How to find it: a tap test of the mount (Lab) and the attitude oscillation frequency in a log. Fix: a stiffer mount or less mass on it.
    • 

  • Symptom: a mount that worked stops working after a cable was added. Cause: a stiff cable bridges the mount. How to find it: repeat the tap test with and without the cable. Fix: a flexible cable with a loop.
    • 

  • Symptom: a low-frequency wobble in the attitude estimate with no matching motion. Cause: vibration aliased by too low an IMU output rate or a missing anti-alias filter. How to find it: compare a high-rate raw log with the estimator's input. Fix: configure the IMU's rate and internal filter per its datasheet.
    • 

  • Symptom: high vibration on one axis only, at the rotation frequency. Cause: one unbalanced propeller or motor. How to find it: run motors one at a time (propellers removed for motor-only tests, F10-10) and compare spectra. Fix: balance or replace.
    •

Summary


    

  • Multirotor vibration comes from rotating parts: lines at the rotation frequency, the blade-pass frequency and harmonics, moving with throttle.
    • 

  • A soft mount is a mass–spring–damper: it amplifies near fn (3.480× at ζ = 0.15) and isolates only above √2·fn (0.044 at r = 7.6).
    • 

  • Choose fn well below the vibration lines and well above the control bandwidth; cables and temperature change it.
    • 

  • Vibration inside the range can be filtered; clipping cannot, and it shifts the mean (1 g became 0.70 g at 18 g of vibration).
    • 

  • Measure level, clipping and spectrum on raw high-rate data; the spectrum tells source from path.
    • 

  • Same lines, frequency-dependent change of amplitude → the path changed (the mount), not the source.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. flight_log.cpp wrote 2,000 samples at 1,000 Hz per flight: gravity (0, 0, 1) g plus a 95 Hz line (frame amplitude 1.0, 1.0, 1.5 g) and a 190 Hz line (6, 6, 18 g), each multiplied by the mount's transmissibility, plus 0.05 g noise, clipped at ±16 g. Flight A uses the soft mount (fn = 25 Hz, ζ = 0.15: T = 0.112 at 95 Hz, 0.044 at 190 Hz). Flight B uses a rigid mount (T = 1). The frame vibration is identical in both.


Observation. Vibration levels A → B: x 0.207 → 4.302 g, y 0.210 → 4.304 g, z 0.574 → 12.105 g. Samples at the limit: 0 in A; 513 of 2,000 on z in B (x and y 0). z mean: +1.000 g in A, +0.716 g in B. Peaks: 190 Hz at 0.790 g (A) and 17.047 g (B); 95 Hz at 0.170 g (A) and 1.077 g (B). C++ and numpy agree on every number. R4 R5


Root cause. The same two lines appear in both flights, so the source (motors and propellers) did not change. The ratio B/A is 17.047/0.790 = 21.6 at 190 Hz and 1.077/0.170 = 6.3 at 95 Hz: the loss of isolation is larger at the higher frequency, exactly what removing a soft mount does (1/0.044 = 22.7 and 1/0.112 = 8.9 from the formula; the measured B values are lower than the frame amplitudes because B clips). The flight controller was hard-mounted during the repair. A new source (damaged propeller, bent shaft) would add or strengthen lines, mainly at the rotation frequency, rather than scale the existing ones by a frequency-dependent factor. R3


The climb. In B the z axis clips on 513 samples, more at the top (+1 g offset), so the mean drops to 0.716 g: the estimator sees a constant downward acceleration of about 0.28 g, and altitude hold climbs to compensate, as Listing 2 predicts (0.70 g at 18 g of vibration).


Fix and verification. Restore the soft mount (with fn well below 95/√2 Hz, verified by a tap test), with flexible cables; check that no stiff part bridges it. A new hover log must show no samples at the limit, a z mean of 1.00 g, and the 190 Hz line reduced by about the factor of Figure 1. Also inspect the repaired arm for looseness, since the method hints' second hypothesis cannot be excluded by a log alone.


Prevention. A vibration check (the mini-project's tool) after every repair, before the first flight, with clipping as a hard failure.
