Story hook

Kwame carries a tray of six glasses across a busy hall. His eyes watch the people ahead, his inner ear feels every tilt, his wrist feels the weight shift, and his legs feel the floor. Many times a second his brain gathers these feelings, decides how much the tray is tipping, and sends small corrections down the nerves to the muscles of his arm. If his brain stops to think about something else for even a moment, the tray wobbles.

A drone has the same problem with no eyes on the tray. Its brain is a small circuit board called the flight controller. On that board, sensors feel the motion, a processor runs a loop that decides on corrections, and output pins send commands to the motors. This chapter opens the board, follows one measurement all the way to the motors, and shows what happens when the brain gets distracted.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the parts of a Pixhawk-class flight controller (processor, sensors, buses, ports, storage, power input) and what each one does, using the vocabulary of the Pixhawk standards.
  • Trace one sensor sample through the flight stack's data path (driver, estimator, rate controller, mixer, output) and compute the motor commands for a simple case.
  • Analyse a flight controller's task set with fixed-priority scheduling and predict when a non-preemptible task makes the rate loop miss cycles.
  • Check a wiring plan against a board's port table before connecting anything.
  • Diagnose a stuttering control loop from a timing log.
Jargon box
Flight controller (autopilot)
Simple words: the small computer board that keeps a drone stable and follows commands. Analogy: Kwame's brain with its senses and nerves (a proposed F10 mapping, see NOTES). Precise: an embedded board that combines a microcontroller running a real-time operating system and a flight stack, inertial and other sensors, interfaces to radios, GNSS, ESCs and payloads, logging storage and power input; "autopilot" is the name the Pixhawk standards and flight-stack documents use for this unit. D1 D2
Pixhawk standards
Simple words: shared rule books that say how a flight controller's connectors and modules should look, so parts from different makers fit together. Analogy: every tray in the hall having handles in the same place, so any waiter can pick up any tray. Precise: open hardware standards published by the Pixhawk Special Interest Group (coordinated by the Dronecode Foundation), including a connector standard (DS-009), an autopilot bus standard (DS-010), several autopilot standards (DS-011, DS-012, DS-016, DS-018, DS-020), a payload bus (DS-014), a radio interface (DS-017) and a versions document (DS-019). D1 C1
Flight stack
Simple words: the big program on the flight controller: drivers, estimator, controllers, logging and communication. Analogy: all the habits Kwame's brain uses while carrying. Precise: the firmware above the operating system that implements sensor drivers, state estimation, control, mixing, outputs, failsafes, logging and the ground-station link; PX4 (on NuttX) and ArduPilot (on ChibiOS, on STM32-based boards in its porting guide) are the two open flight stacks studied in this university. D2 D3 C1
Rate loop
Simple words: the fastest control loop, which keeps the drone's turning speeds where they should be. Analogy: the quick wrist corrections that stop the tray tipping. Precise: the innermost loop of the cascaded controller (F10-16): it compares estimated body rates with rate setpoints and outputs torque commands to the mixer, once per control period. D2 D3
Port (flight-controller connector)
Simple words: a labelled socket on the board for one kind of device. Analogy: the tray's handles, each shaped for one hand. Precise: a connector that carries one interface (UART, I2C, CAN, PWM or other output, RC input, power and sense lines) with defined pins, signal levels and supply; types and pinouts are set by the board's documentation and, for compliant boards, by the Pixhawk connector standard. D1 D5
Ground control station (GCS)
Simple words: the program on a computer or tablet that shows what the drone is doing and changes its settings. Analogy: the head waiter's desk where the plan for the evening is written. Precise: software that talks to the flight stack over USB or a telemetry radio, usually with MAVLink (F10-12), to display state, set parameters, calibrate sensors, test motors and plan missions. D2 D3
Skipped cycle (deadline miss)
Simple words: a moment when the loop should have run but did not. Analogy: Kwame looking away from the tray for a moment. Precise: a periodic job that has not finished (or here, not started) by its next release; the control law then acts on older data or not at all for that period. D4 R2
Release jitter (start lateness)
Simple words: how late a task starts compared with when it should start. Analogy: a correction that comes a little late, so the tray tips a bit further first. Precise: the variation of the delay between a periodic task's release time and the time it first runs; measured from timestamps, reported as a histogram or maximum. D4

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

A flight controller is a small board with a computer chip in the middle. Around the chip sit sensors that feel tilting, turning, height and direction. Along the edges sit sockets for the radio, the GPS, the motors and the battery.

The chip runs a loop. It reads the sensors, works out how the drone is tilted, decides how much each motor must speed up or slow down, and sends the commands. Then it starts again, hundreds of times every second.

The loop must never be late. If the chip spends too long on a slow job, such as writing a diary to its memory card, the motors keep their old commands. That is like Kwame looking away from his tray: a short look is fine, a long one spills the drinks.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The parts of a flight controller

Kwame's brain needs senses, nerves to the muscles, a memory and food. A flight controller has the same four groups. Processing: one microcontroller (some designs add a second one for outputs) running a real-time operating system and the flight stack. D2 D3 Sensing: one or more IMUs (accelerometer and gyroscope, F1-65), a barometer and often a magnetometer on the board, with more sensors (GNSS receiver, external compass) on cables; redundant IMUs let the flight stack compare them and keep flying if one fails. D2 D3 Interfaces: UART ports for telemetry radios and GNSS, I2C and CAN for external sensors and smart peripherals, an RC input, a set of outputs for ESCs and servos, USB for the ground control station, and a debug connector. D1 D5 Storage and power: an SD card or flash for logs and parameters, and a power port that brings in a regulated supply plus battery voltage and current measurements from the power module (F10-11). D2 D3

What the Pixhawk standards add

Without a standard, every board maker chooses its own connectors and pin orders, and every cable is a special case. The Pixhawk standards are open documents from the Pixhawk Special Interest Group, coordinated by the Dronecode Foundation. The guide's source registry lists a connector standard (DS-009), an autopilot bus standard (DS-010) that separates a plug-in autopilot module from the carrier board around it, a series of autopilot standards (DS-011, DS-012, DS-016, DS-018, DS-020), a payload bus (DS-014), a radio interface (DS-017) and a document that lists the standards' versions (DS-019). C1 D1 For you as an engineer the useful idea is this: if your board states compliance with a named standard, that standard tells you which connector type and pin order to expect for each port; if it does not, only the board's own documentation does. One sample through the data path

Follow one gyroscope sample. A driver reads it from the IMU over SPI or I2C (the bus driver of F3-37), converts it to rad/s and publishes it inside the flight stack; in PX4 this publication goes through uORB, the internal notice board studied in DN302 (F10-22). D2 The estimator turns raw samples into the vehicle's attitude and body rates (DN301). The rate loop compares the rates with the setpoints and produces three torque commands. The mixer of F10-02 turns thrust and torques into one command per motor. The output driver converts each command into the ESC protocol (F10-10). D2 D3 Listing 1 does this once with numbers: a body roll rate of +0.200 rad/s, minus a calibrated bias of 0.010, gives +0.190 rad/s; a proportional gain of 0.15 gives a roll torque command of −0.0285; the mixer lowers motors 1 and 4 (left side) to 0.4640 and 0.4790 and raises motors 2 and 3 (right side) to 0.5240 and 0.5330. R1

Time is a resource

All of this runs on one processor that also logs, talks to the ground station, reads the GNSS and checks the battery. The operating system decides who runs when. With the fixed-priority preemptive scheduling of OS305, the rate loop gets the highest priority so that it runs as soon as it is released. D4 That guarantee breaks when a lower-priority task holds the processor in a section that cannot be preempted: a long interrupt-disabled region, a held lock that the rate loop needs, or a driver that busy-waits for a slow device such as an SD card. D4 In Listing 2 the logger needs 2,500 µs for each block write. With the write locked (design A), the rate loop skips 100 of its 1,000 releases in one second; with the same work preemptible (design B), it skips none, and the total CPU load is 75.0 % in both cases. R2 Load alone does not tell you whether deadlines are met: blocking does.

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Blocking in response-time analysis. For fixed-priority scheduling, the worst-case response time Ri of task i satisfies Ri = Ci + Bi + Σj∈hp(i) ⌈Ri/Tj⌉ Cj, where Bi is the longest time a lower-priority task can block i (a non-preemptible section or a lock without priority inheritance). D4 For the rate loop (C = 250 µs, no higher-priority tasks) with B = 2,500 µs, R = 2,750 µs > T = 1,000 µs: unschedulable, whatever the CPU load. With B = 0, R = 250 µs. The simulator's measured worst response for completed jobs, 450 µs in design A, is smaller than 2,750 µs only because the simulator drops a job that is overtaken by its next release instead of letting it finish late (Listing 2's table explains this). R2
  • Where the time goes in a real stack. PX4 organises most drivers and controllers as modules scheduled on work queues, and ArduPilot organises its vehicle code around a main loop and a scheduler table with per-task time budgets; both document how to view per-task timing on a running board. D2 D3 The names of those tools and their output fields are in the unverified box of the lab; DN302 and DN303 study the two architectures properly.
  • Sensor timing. High-rate IMUs are usually read with a data-ready signal or a FIFO, so that sample times are known and none are lost even when the reading task is delayed. D2 A delayed read that drains a FIFO keeps the data but adds latency; a delayed read without a FIFO loses samples. Both show up in logs as gaps or bursts.
  • Redundancy. Two or three IMUs, sometimes of different types and on different buses, let the estimator detect a failed or saturated sensor and switch; the flight stacks document how they select and vote. D2 D3 Redundancy does not help against a common cause such as vibration that affects every IMU on the same board (F10-09).
  • Separation of autopilot and carrier. A plug-in autopilot module on a carrier board (the subject of the autopilot bus standard, DS-010) lets a vehicle maker design its own connector layout and power around a standard compute-and-sensor core. D1 C1 What the module/carrier interface carries is in D1, not here.

How the hardware actually does it

The microcontroller's peripherals do most of the timing-critical work. SPI and I2C controllers clock sensor data in, often with DMA so that the CPU only handles a complete buffer (F1-44 and the drivers of OS305). D2 Timer peripherals generate the output pulses or bit streams for the ESCs without the CPU toggling pins (F1-70, F10-10). UARTs with DMA receive RC and telemetry bytes in the background. An interrupt controller wakes the right driver when a sensor's data-ready line fires (F1-42). D2 D3 The SD card is the slowest device on the board: a write can take a long and variable time, which is why logging is buffered and done by a low-priority task, and why a driver that waits for the card with interrupts disabled is dangerous. D2 D4

Worked example

Task. A flight controller runs the five tasks of Listing 2. The logger's SD write is changed so that only its first b microseconds are non-preemptible. What is the largest b for which the rate loop never misses a release?

  1. Rate loop budget. Period T = 1,000 µs, cost C = 250 µs, nothing above it: R = 250 + B. For no miss, R ≤ T, so B ≤ 750 µs.
  2. Sensors task. It runs after the rate loop each period: R = 150 + 250 + B ≤ 1,000 µs, so B ≤ 600 µs. The sensors task also has a deadline, so the tighter limit wins: b ≤ 600 µs. D4
  3. Check with the simulator. In scratch copies of Listing 2 (runs made while writing this chapter, not part of the lab logs), taskSet(600), taskSet(700) and taskSet(900) give 0 misses for every task; taskSet(1000) gives 50 misses of the sensors task and taskSet(1100) 50 misses of the rate loop as well. Why does the simulator tolerate more than 600 µs? In its schedule the logger always starts 300 µs before a release (at 3,700 µs, Figure 2), which is not the worst phasing. The analysis covers every phasing, including a locked section that begins an instant before a release, so it gives the smaller, safe bound; a simulation shows only the schedules it happens to produce. Check yourself, question 6, asks you to repeat these runs. D4
  4. Engineering answer. Make each locked piece shorter than 600 µs with margin, for example by writing the log in smaller blocks or by letting DMA move the data while the CPU is free; then confirm with a timing log on the real board.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Kwame's brain works in parallel; the flight controller mostly does not. His senses and muscles run at the same time without competing for one brain. On a single-core microcontroller every task shares one processor, and a slow task can steal time from the rate loop, as Listing 2 shows. R2
    • 

  • Kwame notices when he looks away; the flight controller does not. A skipped cycle is invisible unless the software measures its own timing and logs it. The forensic lab below finds the problem only because the log records release and start times.
    • 

  • A waiter's nerves are fixed at birth; a flight controller's ports are configured. The same UART socket can be telemetry or GNSS depending on settings, and a wrong setting looks like a broken device. D2 D3
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: a device on a port shows no data. Cause: the port is not configured for that device, or TX and RX are crossed. How to find it: compare the port's configured function with the plan; check the cable's pin order against the connector documentation. Fix: configure the port; use the correct cable.
    • 

  • Symptom: a 5 V device on a 3.3 V port works for a while and then the port is dead. Cause: signal level above the port's rating. How to find it: Listing 3's level check, then the datasheets. Fix: a level shifter or a device with the right levels (F9-15, the wiring and power plan).
    • 

  • Symptom: the vehicle wobbles periodically, more when logging at a high rate. Cause: a long non-preemptible section in a lower-priority task. How to find it: a timing log of the rate loop (forensic lab). Fix: shorten the section, move the slow work to DMA or a lower-priority task with a queue.
    • 

  • Symptom: motor 3 does nothing in a motor test while motor 4 behaves oddly. Cause: two ESC signal wires on one output. How to find it: Listing 3's "port already full" and "unused" notes. Fix: rewire per the plan and repeat the motor-order test of F10-10.
    • 

  • Symptom: high CPU load reported, but you were told the system is fine "because it is under 100 %". Cause: load ignores blocking and release patterns. How to find it: response-time analysis with blocking (Layer 3). Fix: budget each task's worst case, not the average.

Forensic lab


Scenario: "The loop that stutters". Aiko's quadcopter hovers well on the bench simulator, but on the real vehicle the logs show small periodic jolts in roll and pitch. They began on the day she raised the logging rate. She enables the flight stack's timing log of the rate loop: for every cycle, when it was released and when it started.


Evidence pack. The first 30 ms of the timing log and a histogram of start lateness over one second. The data are synthetic: produced by the university's simulator (jitter_log.cpp with fcsim.hpp), not recorded on a vehicle; how is in the key.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. jitter_log.cpp runs the five-task set of Listing 2, design A (logger: period 20,000 µs, cost 2,500 µs, the whole cost non-preemptible) for one second in the simulator of fcsim.hpp, and prints the rate loop's release and first-start times.


Observation. (All values from R4.) R4 Cycles 4 and 5 are skipped, then 24 and 25: every 20 ms, two consecutive releases. The cycle after each gap starts 200 µs late (6,200 µs for the release at 6,000). Over one second 900 of 1,000 releases started: 850 on time and 50 late by 200–299 µs.


Root cause. A task with a 20 ms period holds the processor in a non-preemptible section. To skip the releases at 4 ms and 5 ms and delay the one at 6 ms by 200 µs, the section must have started before 4,000 µs and ended at 6,200 µs; in the simulation it ran from 3,700 to 6,200 µs (2,500 µs). The 20 ms task is the logger, whose rate was raised on the day the problem started. Hypotheses ruled out: an interrupt storm would not be so exactly periodic and would also slow the rest of each cycle; a slow sensor read would affect the sensors task every cycle, not every twentieth.


Fix and verification. Make the SD write non-blocking (DMA with a queue serviced by a low-priority task) or split it into pieces shorter than the worked example's 600 µs bound; the new timing log should show zero skipped cycles and lateness close to zero, as design B of Listing 2 does.


Prevention. A bench test that logs rate-loop timing at the highest planned logging rate and fails if any release is skipped or late beyond a set limit; a rule that any new non-preemptible section must be budgeted.
