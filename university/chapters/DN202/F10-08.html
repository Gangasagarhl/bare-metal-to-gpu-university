Story hook

Lina carries a tray of drinks from the kitchen up to the roof terrace. Her inner ear tells her when the tray tilts and when she turns. In the lift, her ears feel the pressure change and she knows she is going up. On the terrace she glances at the sunset to know which way she is facing, and she listens for her friends calling from their table to know where she is in the crowd.

Each sense answers one question, and each can be fooled. Her ears pop for weather changes too. The sunset is hidden behind a wall. Her friends' voices echo off the building. A drone's flight controller has the same four senses: an IMU for tilt and turning, a barometer for height, a magnetometer for heading and a GNSS receiver for position. This chapter shows what each measures, how each is calibrated, and how each lies.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what the IMU, barometer, magnetometer and GNSS receiver of a flight controller measure, and the main error sources of each.
  • Compute height from barometric pressure and the size of its temperature and weather errors.
  • Calibrate a magnetometer's hard-iron offset with a least-squares sphere fit and compute a tilt-compensated heading.
  • Convert GNSS latitude and longitude into local north and east metres and spot implausible jumps.
  • Diagnose magnetic interference from power-wiring current in a bench log, and choose a sensor placement that avoids it.
Jargon box
Barometer (pressure sensor)
Simple words: a sensor that measures air pressure; pressure drops as you go up. Analogy: Lina's ears popping in the lift (a proposed F10 mapping, see NOTES). Precise: a sensor that measures static air pressure (and usually its own temperature); a flight controller converts the pressure change since a reference into a height change with a model of the atmosphere. D1
Pressure height (barometric altitude)
Simple words: how high you are, worked out from how much the pressure has dropped. Analogy: counting floors by how much your ears popped. Precise: the height above a reference pressure p0 computed from the hydrostatic equation; with constant temperature T, h = (R·T / (M·g))·ln(p0/p); it changes when the weather changes p0. D1 D6
Hard-iron offset
Simple words: a constant magnetic field from the drone's own parts that adds to the Earth's field in every reading. Analogy: a lamp on the terrace that is always on your left, so you mistake its glow for the sunset. Precise: a field vector fixed in the body frame (from magnetised parts and steady currents) that shifts every magnetometer reading by the same vector; it moves the centre of the sphere traced by readings in all orientations away from the origin. D1 D2
Soft-iron distortion
Simple words: metal near the sensor that bends the Earth's field, differently in each direction. Analogy: a curved window that bends the sunset's light. Precise: distortion by magnetically soft material that is induced by the external field and depends on orientation; it turns the sphere of readings into an ellipsoid, corrected by a 3×3 matrix. D1
Tilt-compensated heading
Simple words: the compass direction worked out correctly even when the drone is tilted. Analogy: knowing which way you face even while leaning to put down a glass. Precise: the heading obtained after rotating the body-frame magnetic field into the horizontal plane using roll and pitch from the attitude estimate, then taking atan2 of the horizontal components. D1
Magnetic declination
Simple words: the angle between where a compass points and true north. Analogy: the sunset is not exactly in the west; how far off depends on where you are. Precise: the angle between magnetic north and true north at a location; it varies with place and slowly with time, and must be added to a magnetic heading to get a true heading. D1
GNSS (global navigation satellite system)
Simple words: satellites that let a receiver work out where it is on Earth. Analogy: finding your place in the crowd by hearing several friends call from known tables (a proposed F10 mapping). Precise: a system of satellites broadcasting timing signals; a receiver measures ranges to several satellites and solves for its position, velocity and clock offset; GPS is one such system. D1 D5
Fix (GNSS fix) and its accuracy estimate
Simple words: one position answer from the receiver, with its own guess of how good it is. Analogy: "I think I am near the window table, give or take two tables". Precise: a navigation solution reported by the receiver with a fix type (no fix, 2D, 3D and others the receiver documents), the number of satellites used and accuracy estimates; the estimator uses these to decide how much to trust the fix. D2 D4 D5
Sensor orientation (board rotation)
Simple words: telling the software which way the board or sensor is turned on the drone. Analogy: knowing whether you hold the tray with your left or right hand. Precise: a fixed rotation between a sensor's own axes and the vehicle's body axes, set in the flight stack's configuration; a wrong value makes every correction go the wrong way. D2 D3

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

The drone has four senses. The IMU feels tilting and turning, like your inner ear. The barometer feels air pressure, which gets lower as you climb, like your ears popping in a lift. The magnetometer is a compass. The GNSS receiver listens to satellites to find where on Earth it is.

Each sense has a weakness. The barometer cannot tell whether you climbed or the weather changed. The compass is confused by magnets and by electric wires nearby. GNSS signals are blocked by buildings and bounce off walls. So the drone checks the senses against each other and calibrates them before it flies.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

The IMU on a flight controller

Lina's inner ear is the sense she trusts most from moment to moment. The IMU plays that role: its accelerometer measures specific force and its gyroscope measures angular rate, as F1-65 derived. D1 What is new on a flight controller is how it is set up and calibrated. Orientation: the flight stack must know how the board (and each sensor on it) is rotated relative to the vehicle's body axes; the sensor orientation setting does this, and a wrong value makes every correction go the wrong way. D2 D3 Calibration: the gyroscope bias is estimated at rest; the accelerometer is calibrated by placing the vehicle on several faces so that gravity gives known values on each axis; a "level" calibration records which attitude counts as level. D2 D3 Bias changes with temperature, so some stacks support a temperature calibration over a warm-up. D2 Timing: IMUs are sampled fastest of the four sensors, and the sample time stamps matter for the estimator (Layer 3).

The barometer

Lina's ears pop because air pressure falls as she rises. For air in balance, pressure falls with height as dp/dh = −ρ·g, and for an ideal gas ρ = p·M/(R·T). D1 D6 With T held constant, integrating gives the pressure height h = (R·T/(M·g))·ln(p0/p), the formula in Listing 1. A flight controller stores p0 when it arms, so the result is height above the take-off point, not above the sea. D2 Listing 1's numbers show the scale of things: near p0 = 100,000 Pa at 15 °C, 1 Pa is 0.0843 m, so pressure noise of 2 Pa is 0.17 m of height noise; a 1,000 Pa drop is 84.77 m at 15 °C but 77.42 m at −10 °C and 90.65 m at 35 °C; and if the weather lowers the ground pressure by 100 Pa during a session, a vehicle sitting on the ground reads +8.44 m. R1 The barometer is therefore good at short-term height changes and poor at absolute height over long times; the estimator blends it with the accelerometer and GNSS (DN301). D1 Moving air also changes the static pressure at the sensor: wind over the board and the propellers' downwash can shift the reading. D2 D3

The constants in Listing 1 (R = 8.314462618 J/(mol K), M = 0.0289644 kg/mol, g = 9.80665 m/s²) were typed in from memory and must be checked against the cited references (D6) before reuse. Real flight stacks may use a standard-atmosphere model with a temperature lapse instead of the isothermal model; which one PX4 or ArduPilot uses, and the barometer protection they recommend (for example covering the sensor with open-cell foam against airflow, and against light for parts that are sensitive to it), must be read in their documentation (D2, D3) and the barometer's datasheet (D4). No barometer's noise, resolution or range is stated in this chapter; the "2 Pa" is an exercise value.

The magnetometer

Lina knows which way she faces from the sunset, unless a bright lamp on the terrace fools her. The magnetometer measures the local magnetic field vector in body axes. D1 Three things make the raw reading wrong. A hard-iron offset adds a constant body-fixed vector; a soft-iron distortion stretches and rotates the field; and currents in nearby wires add a field proportional to the current. D1 D2 D3 Calibration turns the vehicle through many orientations so that the readings cover a sphere; fitting the sphere's centre gives the hard-iron offset, and fitting an ellipsoid adds the soft-iron matrix. D1 Listing 2 fits a sphere to 300 noisy readings and recovers the offset as (+12.03, −7.00, +3.98) µT for a model offset of (+12, −7, +4) µT; uncalibrated, the heading errors are up to 33.5°, calibrated at most 0.1°. R2 Once calibrated, the field is rotated into the horizontal plane with roll and pitch from the IMU (tilt compensation) and the heading is atan2(−yh, xh); adding the local magnetic declination converts it to a true heading. D1 Current-induced fields cannot be removed by a one-time sphere fit, because they change with throttle: the cure is distance from the power wiring (an external compass, often on the GNSS mast) and, where the flight stack supports it, a compensation measured against current. D2 D3

GNSS

Lina finds her place in the crowd by hearing friends call from tables she knows. A GNSS receiver measures ranges to satellites whose positions it knows from their broadcasts, and solves for its position, velocity and clock offset; at least four satellites are needed for position and time. D1 D5 It reports a fix with a fix type, the number of satellites and its own accuracy estimates, usually over a UART protocol defined by the receiver's maker. D4 D2 For control, latitude and longitude are converted to metres in a local frame. Over short distances a flat-Earth approximation is adequate: north = Δlat·RE, east = Δlon·RE·cos(lat), angles in radians. D1 Listing 3 shows that a step of 10−7 degree is 1.11 cm north–south and 0.79 cm east–west at latitude 45°, and flags a fix that jumps 5.49 m in one second on a vehicle that is standing still. R3 Such jumps come from reflections (multipath), a changing set of satellites, or a partly blocked sky; the estimator must test each fix against its prediction before using it (DN301, DN302's forensic "Estimator rejected GPS"). D5 C1

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Sensor delays and time stamps. Each sensor's sample describes a past moment: IMU samples are close to "now", barometer readings are filtered inside the part, and GNSS fixes arrive after the receiver's processing and the serial transfer. Estimators keep a short history of states and fuse each measurement at the time it describes; this needs good time stamps and configured delays. D2 D3 F10-07's lesson applies: a late driver task adds delay that the estimator does not know about.
  • Accelerometer calibration as a fit. With the model measurement = S·(true) + b (S a scale-and-alignment matrix, b a bias), readings on six faces against ±1 g give enough equations to solve for b and the diagonal of S; more orientations allow a full S. D1 This is the same least-squares idea as Listing 2's sphere fit, applied to a different sensor.
  • Ellipsoid fit. Soft iron turns |m − c| = r into (m − c)TA(m − c) = 1 with A symmetric positive definite; fitting the ten coefficients of a general quadric by linear least squares and then extracting c and A is the standard extension of Listing 2. D1 A fit is only as good as its coverage: readings from only a few orientations leave the centre poorly determined. Listing 2 samples pitch within ±85° and every roll and yaw.
  • Least squares without pivoting. Listing 2 solves 4×4 normal equations by Gauss–Jordan elimination without pivoting. That is safe here because the matrix is symmetric positive definite when the samples cover the sphere; with poor coverage it becomes ill-conditioned, and a real implementation should use pivoting or a QR or Cholesky solver (MA302). D7
  • GNSS accuracy. The receiver's accuracy estimate depends on the satellite geometry (dilution of precision) and the range errors; multipath and partial sky blockage make errors larger and less Gaussian. D5 Differential and carrier-phase methods (RTK) reduce errors with a reference station; the receiver's documentation says what it supports. D5 D4 The antenna's position relative to the vehicle's centre is a configuration value the estimator needs (DN302's forensic).
  • Choosing the trusted compass. With an internal and an external magnetometer, flight stacks let you choose which ones are used and in which order; disabling an internal compass that sits next to high-current wiring is a common result of a bench test like this chapter's forensic lab. D2 D3

How the hardware actually does it

All four sensors are small chips or modules with their own processing. MEMS accelerometers and gyroscopes sense the motion of tiny proof masses (F1-65). D1 MEMS pressure sensors measure the deflection of a thin membrane over a sealed cavity, piezoresistively or capacitively, and include a temperature sensor because the reading depends on temperature. D1 Magnetometers on small boards use magnetoresistive or Hall-effect elements. D1 A GNSS module contains a radio front end, correlators that track each satellite's signal, and a processor that computes the fix and sends it over a UART; external GNSS modules for drones often include a magnetometer on I2C in the same housing, which puts the compass on the mast, away from the power wiring. D5 D2 On the flight controller, each sensor is read by a driver of the kind written in F3-37, over SPI or I2C, with the part's datasheet giving registers, ranges, data rates and filters. D4

Worked example

Task. A vehicle arms at p0 = 100,000 Pa on a day the flight controller assumes 15 °C. It climbs until the pressure is 99,000 Pa. The real air temperature is 35 °C. Then, back on the ground, the level vehicle faces east (90°) and the uncalibrated magnetometer reads m = (12, −27, 49) µT. Compute the indicated height, its error, and the raw heading.

  1. Indicated height. R·T/(M·g) at 288.15 K = 8.314462618 × 288.15 / (0.0289644 × 9.80665) = 8,434.7 m; ln(100,000/99,000) = 0.0100503; h = 84.77 m (Listing 1 prints 84.771 for "p0 − 1000 Pa"). R1
  2. True height. With T = 308.15 K the factor is 9,020.1 m and h = 90.65 m (Listing 1: 90.65 m). The barometer under-reads by 5.88 m, 6.5 % of the height: the ratio of the absolute temperatures, 308.15/288.15 = 1.069.
  3. Raw heading. Level, so heading = atan2(−my, mx) = atan2(27, 12) = 66.0°: 24° wrong (Listing 2's row "90 0 0" shows 66.0 and −24.0). R2
  4. Calibrated heading. Subtract the fitted offset (12.03, −7.00, 3.98): m′ = (−0.03, −20.00, 45.02); atan2(20.00, −0.03) = 90.1°.

The barometer error grows with height and temperature difference; the compass error does not shrink with averaging. Both need calibration or modelling, not filtering.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Lina's ears adapt; the barometer does not forget. Her ears stop feeling a pressure change after a while; the barometer keeps the reference p0 from arming, so a weather change of 100 Pa appears as 8.44 m of height. R1
    • 

  • The lamp and the sunset look different to Lina; to a magnetometer they are one sum. A magnetometer measures the vector sum of all fields at its position and cannot tell the Earth's field from a wire's field; only calibration, distance or a current-based model separates them. D1
    • 

  • Friends calling are heard at once; GNSS fixes arrive late. A GNSS fix describes where the vehicle was a moment ago, and the estimator must account for that delay. D2
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: the vehicle "climbs" several metres while standing on the ground during a long session. Cause: weather changed the ground pressure. How to find it: compare the barometer height with the start of the log at rest. Fix: re-arm (new p0) before each flight; rely on the estimator's fusion.
    • 

  • Symptom: heading is right in one direction and wrong by tens of degrees in others. Cause: uncalibrated hard iron. How to find it: turn the level vehicle through 360° and plot mx against my: an off-centre circle (Figure 2). Fix: a full compass calibration with good coverage.
    • 

  • Symptom: heading changes when the throttle changes. Cause: field from current in nearby wires. How to find it: the forensic lab's test. Fix: move the compass (external, on the mast); twist and route power wires away; use the stack's current compensation.
    • 

  • Symptom: after mounting the board rotated, every correction goes the wrong way. Cause: sensor orientation not set. How to find it: tilt the vehicle by hand (props off) and watch the attitude display. Fix: set the orientation, then redo the accelerometer calibration.
    • 

  • Symptom: position jumps of metres while stationary. Cause: multipath or poor sky view. How to find it: jump check (Listing 3) and the receiver's accuracy estimate. Fix: mount the antenna high with a clear sky view; test away from walls.
    •

Summary


    

  • A flight controller senses tilt and turning (IMU), height change (barometer), heading (magnetometer) and position (GNSS); each has its own error sources.
    • 

  • Pressure height h = (R·T/(M·g))·ln(p0/p) is relative to the arming point; 1 Pa ≈ 0.084 m near 100,000 Pa, temperature errors scale the result, and weather moves p0.
    • 

  • A hard-iron offset moves the sphere of magnetometer readings off the origin; a sphere fit found (+12.03, −7.00, +3.98) µT and cut heading errors from 33.5° to 0.1°.
    • 

  • Tilt compensation uses roll and pitch; declination converts magnetic to true heading.
    • 

  • Fields from current change with throttle and need distance (an external compass) or compensation, not a one-time calibration.
    • 

  • GNSS gives a global fix with an accuracy estimate and latency; convert to local metres and check plausibility.
    •

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. bench_log.cpp computes, at 10 Hz for 25 s, the body-frame Earth field of a level vehicle facing east, (0, −20, 45) µT, adds a field proportional to the current, k·I, with k = (0.55, −0.35, 0.40) µT/A at the internal compass and (0.02, −0.01, 0.01) µT/A at the external one, plus noise of 0.25 µT, and current steps of 5 A (0.3 to 20.3 A, noise 0.1 A). Nothing else differs between the compasses.


Observation. The excerpt (R4) already shows the trend; the fit (R5) quantifies it. R4 The internal heading falls from 89.70° (0.32 A) to 67.54° (20.29 A); the external heading stays within 89.05–90.15°. The fitted slopes are −1.102° per ampere (internal) and −0.054° per ampere (external), intercepts 88.86° and 90.09°. R5


Root cause. A magnetic field from the current in the power wiring near the flight controller adds to the internal compass's reading. The error is proportional to current (a slope, not a step), present at the internal compass and nearly absent at the mast. In flight, climbing raises the current, the heading estimate shifts by several degrees, and the controller "corrects" a turn that is not happening: the slow yaw Femi saw. A bad calibration would give a constant error at zero current; a mechanical shift would be a step; neither fits.


Fix and verification. Use the external compass for heading and disable (or lower the priority of) the internal one; route and twist the power wires away from the flight controller; where supported, apply the stack's current-based compensation. Repeat the same bench test: the slope of the compass used must be close to zero, for example below a threshold you justify from the heading accuracy you need (with 20 A and a 2° budget, below 0.1° per ampere).


Prevention. The compass-versus-current bench test, with the propellers removed and an electronic load (or the stack's documented procedure), after every change to the wiring or the compass mounting.
