Story hook

Yusuf carries a heavy tray with both hands. His brain decides "a little more lift on the left", and nerve signals travel down to the muscles of his left arm. Each muscle has its own small nerve controller that turns the brain's message into exactly the right squeeze. If one arm's nerves understood "a little more" as "a lot more", the tray would tip, however good his brain was.

On a drone, each motor has such a nerve controller: the ESC. The flight controller sends each ESC a command many times a second, in an agreed code. This chapter is about that code: old-style pulses whose width means throttle, digital frames with a checksum, and the calibration and testing that make four ESCs understand "a little more" the same way.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what an ESC does between the flight controller's command and the motor's phases, and what it can report back.
  • Compare pulse-width ESC protocols with digital ones (frame contents, checksum, update rate, calibration).
  • Encode and decode a digital ESC frame with a checksum and show which errors the checksum catches.
  • Run a supervised motor test with propellers removed to check motor order, direction and response.
  • Diagnose a mismatched ESC from a motor-test table.
Jargon box
ESC protocol (motor output protocol)
Simple words: the agreed code in which the flight controller tells an ESC how hard to drive its motor. Analogy: the nerve signal's code between brain and muscle (the registered F10 mapping "ESC = the nerve controller for each muscle"). Precise: the electrical and timing format of the signal on the ESC's input: a pulse whose width encodes throttle (several variants with different pulse lengths and rates), or a digital frame of bits with a value and a checksum; the flight stack's output driver and the ESC must be set to the same protocol. D1 D2
Pulse-width command (servo-style PWM)
Simple words: a repeating pulse whose length means "how much throttle". Analogy: squeezing a hand for a longer or shorter time to say "more" or "less". Precise: a pulse repeated at a frame rate, whose high time between two end points maps linearly to zero and full throttle; it is the same kind of signal as a hobby servo's command (F1-69), and the ESC must know or learn the end points. D1 D2
ESC calibration (end-point calibration)
Simple words: teaching an ESC which pulse lengths mean "stop" and "full". Analogy: agreeing with a friend how long a squeeze means "stop" and how long means "go". Precise: a procedure in which the ESC records the pulse widths it receives as its zero and full-throttle end points; ESCs on one vehicle must be calibrated to the same end points as the controller's output range. D1 D2 D3
Digital ESC protocol (DShot family)
Simple words: sending the throttle as a short binary number with a check code, instead of a pulse length. Analogy: saying a number out loud instead of squeezing longer or shorter. Precise: a protocol in which each command is a frame of bits (a throttle or command value, a telemetry-request bit and a checksum), each bit sent as a pulse of one of two widths in a fixed bit period; DShot is the family named in the PX4 and ArduPilot documentation, with a bidirectional variant in which the ESC replies on the same wire. D1 D2 C1
Checksum (frame check)
Simple words: a few extra bits computed from the message, so the receiver can tell if it arrived damaged. Analogy: repeating the last digit of a phone number back to check you heard it right. Precise: a short value computed from the data bits by a fixed rule (here the XOR of three 4-bit groups); the receiver recomputes it and rejects frames that do not match; it detects every single-bit error of this design but not every multi-bit error. R2
ESC telemetry
Simple words: information the ESC sends back, such as motor speed. Analogy: the muscle telling the brain how hard it is working. Precise: data returned by the ESC (for example electrical speed, and on some ESCs voltage, current and temperature), either on the signal wire in a bidirectional protocol or on a separate serial or CAN connection; motor-speed telemetry lets the flight stack place its vibration notch filters on the motors' frequencies. D1 D2
Motor test
Simple words: spinning each motor alone, from the ground station, to check it. Analogy: wiggling each finger once to check the nerves before lifting the tray. Precise: a ground-station function that commands one output at a time at a chosen level for a short time, used with propellers removed to verify motor order, direction of rotation and response before flight. D1 D2

Layer 1 — the kid explanation

Each motor on a drone has a small helper board called an ESC. The flight controller tells each ESC how fast to spin its motor, hundreds of times a second.

The old way is a pulse: a longer pulse means faster. Every ESC must agree on how long "stop" and "full" are. The newer way sends a small binary number with a check code, so a damaged message is noticed and ignored.

Before any flight, engineers test each motor alone with the propellers taken off. They check that the right motor spins, in the right direction, and that all four respond the same way.

Layer 2 — the student explanation

What the ESC does

Yusuf's nerve controllers turn a brain message into a muscle squeeze. An ESC turns a throttle command into three-phase currents for a brushless motor: its microcontroller decodes the input, decides the commutation steps from the rotor position (often sensed from back-EMF, without Hall sensors), and drives a three-phase bridge through gate drivers (F1-69, F1-70). D4 D3 It also has to start the motor from standstill, limit current and protect itself, and decide what to do if commands stop arriving; how it does each of these is set by its firmware and documented by its maker. D3

Pulse-width commands

A squeeze held longer means "more". In a pulse-width command, the flight controller's timer produces one pulse per frame, and the pulse's width between two end points means zero to full throttle. D1 D2 Listing 1 shows the two properties that matter. First, the ESC's end points must match the controller's output range: an ESC that learned 1,100–1,900 µs gives 0.000 throttle for commands up to 10 % and saturates at 1.000 from 90 %, while a matched ESC gives 0.050 at 5 % and 0.900 at 90 %. R1 That is what ESC calibration fixes. Second, the frame rate limits how fresh the command is: at 400 frames per second a frame lasts 2,500 µs and a 2,000 µs pulse leaves 500 µs of gap; at 500 frames per second there is no gap at all for a 2,000 µs pulse. R1 A timer counting in 1 µs steps gives 1,000 distinct throttle levels between 1,000 and 2,000 µs. R1 Shorter-pulse variants (the OneShot family named in the flight-stack documentation) scale the same idea down to send commands more often. D1 D2 C1

Digital frames

Saying a number aloud is more exact than squeezing longer. A digital ESC protocol sends each command as a frame of bits; each bit is one pulse in a fixed bit period, long for 1 and short for 0, so the ESC decodes it by timing each pulse's width. D1 D2 The frame carries the throttle value (or a command), a bit that asks the ESC for telemetry, and a checksum. Because the value is a number, there are no analog end points to calibrate, and timing errors of a few percent do not change it. D1 D2 This chapter teaches with the university's own frame, U-Shot: 16 bits, most significant first, an 11-bit value (0–47 reserved for commands, 48–2047 throttle), one telemetry bit and a 4-bit checksum that is the XOR of the three 4-bit groups above it. Listing 2 encodes 1046 as 0x82C6 and, with the telemetry bit, 0x82D7; at the exercise bit rate of 300,000 bits per second one frame takes 53.33 µs. All 16 single-bit errors are detected, but flipping bits 4 and 8 together passes the check and delivers value 1054 with the telemetry bit cleared. R2 A 4-bit check can only make a wrong frame unlikely, not impossible; the ESC must also ignore frames that fail it rather than act on them.

Not verified — check before relying on this

U-Shot is the university's invention, designed to have the same kinds of fields that the flight-stack documentation describes for DShot. Whether DShot's frame really has 16 bits split as 11 + 1 + 4, whether its checksum is this XOR of nibbles, which values are reserved for commands and what those commands do, its bit rates and the pulse-width ratios for 0 and 1, how the bidirectional variant inverts the signal and encodes the reply, and which ESCs and outputs support it, must be read in the PX4 and ArduPilot documentation (D1, D2) and the ESC's own documentation (D3). Likewise, the pulse widths 1,000–2,000 µs, the frame rates 50–500 Hz and the OneShot family's pulse lengths are not stated here as facts about any real ESC.

Order, direction and the motor test

Before lifting a heavy tray, Yusuf wiggles each finger. The mixer of F10-02 assumes a numbering of motors and a direction of rotation for each; if motor 3's signal goes to the wrong output, or a motor turns the wrong way, the controller's corrections push the vehicle the wrong way, usually violently at take-off. D1 D2 The motor test of the ground station spins one output at a time, with propellers removed, so you can check that the motor in the expected position turns, in the expected direction, and that all motors respond alike. D1 D2 Direction is changed by swapping any two of the three motor wires or, on many ESCs, by a setting. D4 D3

Layer 3 — the engineer's depth

  • Latency budget. The delay from the rate loop's decision to a change in motor torque includes waiting for the next output frame, the frame's own duration, the ESC's processing, and the motor's electrical and mechanical response (F10-05). With pulse-width commands, the frame period and pulse length dominate the first two; digital frames shorten both. The motor's mechanical time constant usually dominates the total. D1 D4
  • Generating bits with timers and DMA. A microcontroller produces a digital frame by loading a timer's compare register with the pulse width for each bit, usually by DMA from a buffer of 16 widths per output, as Listing 2's pulses function computes. D5 This uses timer and DMA channels, which is one reason a board supports digital protocols only on some outputs or groups of outputs; the board documentation says which (D1, D2).
  • Output groups. Outputs that share a timer must share its frame rate and often its protocol, so a servo that needs a low pulse rate and an ESC on a digital protocol cannot always sit on outputs of the same group. D1 D2
  • Armed, disarmed, idle. When disarmed, outputs send a value the ESC treats as stop; when armed, a flight stack may spin motors at a minimum value; and failsafes define what outputs do if the controller stops. Each is a configuration with documented names and defaults (D1, D2), tested on the bench before flight.
  • Telemetry and filters. Motor-speed telemetry, sent per motor, lets the flight stack centre notch filters on each motor's rotation frequency and its harmonics, which removes the vibration lines of F10-09 from the gyro with less delay than a low-pass filter. D1 D2 Electrical speed must be divided by the number of motor pole pairs to give mechanical speed. D4
  • CAN ESCs. ESCs on a CAN bus receive commands and send telemetry as bus messages, with addressing and error detection provided by CAN (F1-47); the flight stacks document the higher-level protocol they use on CAN. D1 D2

How the hardware actually does it

On the flight controller, each output pin is driven by a timer channel in output-compare or PWM mode (F1-48, F1-70): for a pulse-width command the compare value is the pulse width in timer ticks; for a digital frame a DMA stream reloads the compare value for each bit. D5 In the ESC, a small microcontroller measures the input pulses with a timer in capture mode, decodes the value, runs commutation and controls the bridge's MOSFETs through gate drivers with dead time (F1-70). D3 D4 For a bidirectional protocol the same pin switches from output to input after each frame to receive the reply, which needs pins and timers that support both directions. D1

Worked example

Task. Encode the U-Shot frame for value 1046 with the telemetry bit set, by hand, and check that it matches Listing 2.

  1. Data (12 bits). 1046 = 0b100_0001_0110 (1024 + 16 + 4 + 2). Shift left by one and add the telemetry bit: 0b1000_0010_1101 = 0x82D.
  2. Checksum. XOR of the three nibbles 0x8, 0x2 and 0xD: 1000 ⊕ 0010 = 1010; 1010 ⊕ 1101 = 0111 = 0x7.
  3. Frame. 0x82D shifted left by 4, plus 0x7: 0x82D7. Listing 2 prints "value 1046 with telemetry request -> 0x82D7". R2
  4. Pulses. Bit period 1/300,000 s = 3,333.3 ns. The first four bits 1, 0, 0, 0 are 2,333, 1,166, 1,166, 1,166 ns high (70 % and 35 %, rounded down), as printed. R2
  5. Why two flips pass. Bits 4 and 8 of the frame are bit 0 of the low data nibble and bit 0 of the middle data nibble. Flipping both changes two nibbles in the same bit position, and their XOR is unchanged. Any pair of flips in the same position of two different nibbles (or in a nibble and the checksum) passes this check.

Where the analogy breaks


    

  • Nerves do not need to be told what a signal means; ESCs do. An ESC on a pulse protocol interprets widths through its calibration; two ESCs given the same pulse can deliver different throttle (Listing 1: 0.050 and 0.000 at a 5 % command). R1
    • 

  • Yusuf's muscle always belongs to the arm it is in; an ESC belongs to whichever output it is plugged into. A swapped signal wire makes the "left arm" nerve drive a right-arm muscle, which a body never does; only a motor test reveals it.
    • 

  • A nerve signal is not checked; a digital frame is, but not perfectly. The checksum rejects all single-bit errors yet passes some double ones (bits 4 and 8 gave value 1054). R2
    • 




Common mistakes and debugging


    

  • Symptom: one motor starts later and reaches full speed earlier than the others. Cause: its ESC's end points differ. How to find it: a motor test table with speed telemetry (forensic lab). Fix: calibrate all ESCs to the controller's range, or use a digital protocol.
    • 

  • Symptom: the vehicle flips at take-off. Cause: wrong motor order or direction, or propellers on the wrong motors (DN201's forensic). How to find it: motor test with propellers removed, a tape flag on each motor bell to see direction. Fix: rewire outputs, swap two motor wires or change the ESC setting; recheck.
    • 

  • Symptom: motors twitch or stop randomly on a digital protocol. Cause: signal integrity (long or unshielded signal wires, missing ground return), or a protocol/rate the ESC does not support. How to find it: the ESC's error counters if it reports them; an oscilloscope on the signal. Fix: short twisted signal and ground pairs; a protocol and rate from the ESC's documentation.
    • 

  • Symptom: a servo on the same output group as the ESCs jitters or overheats. Cause: it receives the ESCs' faster frame rate or protocol. How to find it: the board's output-group table. Fix: move the servo to another group.
    • 

  • Symptom: motors spin when the vehicle is disarmed. Cause: the disarmed output value is above the ESC's stop end point. How to find it: props off, measure the disarmed output. Fix: set the disarmed value per the stack's documentation; recalibrate.
    •

Summary


    

  • An ESC decodes a command and drives a brushless motor through gate drivers and a three-phase bridge; it may report speed and other telemetry.
    • 

  • Pulse-width commands encode throttle in a pulse's width; ESCs must share the controller's end points (calibration), and the frame rate limits update speed.
    • 

  • Digital frames carry a value, a telemetry-request bit and a checksum; no calibration, and damaged frames are rejected.
    • 

  • The U-Shot checksum caught 16 of 16 single-bit errors but passed a double flip (1054 instead of 1046): checks reduce, not remove, risk.
    • 

  • A motor test with propellers removed verifies order, direction and response before any flight.
    • 

  • A dead band at the bottom and early saturation at the top of one motor's curve point at a mismatched ESC calibration.

Forensic lab answer key


How the evidence was made. motor_test.cpp maps each command to a pulse 1,000 + 10 × command µs, then to throttle with each ESC's end points: (1,000, 2,000) µs for motors 1, 2 and 4, and (1,100, 1,900) µs for motor 3. Speed = throttle × 900 rpm/V × 14.8 V with ±20 rpm noise; a motor below 4 % throttle does not turn. The injected fault is motor 3's calibration only.


Observation. At 10 % motor 3 is stopped (0 rpm) while the others turn at about 1,321–1,347 rpm; at 50 % all four agree (6,645–6,675 rpm); at 90 % motor 3 is at 13,325 rpm, at full speed, while the others are at 11,971–11,982 rpm; at 100 % all are at about 13,300–13,330 rpm. R3


Root cause. Motor 3's speed rises by about 1,665 rpm per 10 % (1,668 at 20 %, 3,345 at 30 %, 5,009 at 40 %) instead of about 1,333: a slope 1.25 times steeper, i.e. a range of 800 µs instead of 1,000 µs. Extrapolating to 0 rpm gives 10 % (1,100 µs); full speed is reached at 90 % (1,900 µs). Its ESC learned end points 1,100–1,900 µs, a different calibration from the other three. A weak motor or bad bearing would reduce speed at all commands, including 100 %, which is not the case.


The tilts. Motor 3 is rear-right (F10-02 numbering). At spool-up (low commands) it produces less thrust than the others, so the rear-right corner lags and the vehicle tilts towards it; above 50 % it produces more, so on hard climbs it pushes that corner up and the vehicle tilts the other way. The rate loop fights both, at the cost of control margin.


Fixes and verification. (1) Calibrate all four ESCs together to the controller's output range, following the ESC and flight-stack documentation, propellers removed. (2) Switch to a digital protocol the ESCs support, which removes end-point calibration. Verification: repeat the motor test; all four motors within the chosen tolerance at every step, all starting at the same command.


Prevention. A motor-test table with speed telemetry after any ESC replacement or protocol change, checked automatically by the mini-project's tool.
