Fact list (keyed to the chapter's claim tags, in chapter order)

  1. F1 (jargon) … armth into a needle position. Precise: a device that converts energy or a physical quantity from one form to another; a sensor uses a transducer to produce an electrical quantity (voltage, current, resistance or charge) that depends on the measured quantity. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  2. F2 (jargon) … ping the thermometer's glass and turning it so the needle can be read from the doorway. Precise: amplification, level shifting, filtering and buffering applied between a transducer and an ADC so that the signal fits the converter's input range and bandwidth. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  3. F3 (jargon) equal steps. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  4. F4 (jargon) − 1. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  5. F5 (jargon) for an ideal n-bit converter; also called the resolution in volts. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  6. F6 (jargon) … e and the voltage the code stands for. Analogy: the part of a degree Amara cannot write down. Precise: the difference between the input and the value represented by its code; for an ideal converter reported at mid-step it is at most half an LSB in magnitude. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  7. F7 (jargon) Simple words: how many readings are taken per second. Analogy: how often Amara walks to the cold room. Precise: the number of samples per second, in hertz (Hz); the sample period is Ts = 1 / fs. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  8. F8 (jargon) … our, she cannot see anything that swings faster than once an hour. Precise: fs / 2; a band-limited signal whose frequencies are all below it can be reconstructed from its samples (the sampling theorem), and components above it fold back to lower frequencies. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  9. F9 (jargon) … e because it was read too rarely. Analogy: the slow wave in Amara's log book that was never in the cold room. Precise: after sampling at fs, a sinusoid at f is indistinguishable from one at |f − k·fs| for any integer k; the alias seen is the one in 0 … fs/2. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  10. F10 (jargon) … Analogy: a thick case around the thermometer that makes the needle too slow to follow the quick swings. Precise: an analog low-pass filter placed before sampling that attenuates components above the Nyquist frequency enough that their aliases are negligible. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  11. F11 (jargon) … ds: a circuit that freezes the voltage for a moment so the ADC can measure it. Analogy: Amara taking a photo of the needle so she can read it calmly. Precise: a switch and capacitor that track the input and then hold its value constant during the conversion. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  12. F12 (fig1) … of the signal; dashed arrows are the sample clock and the reference voltage. Many sensor chips (the IMU of F1-65, for example) contain the whole chain and send you only the final code. The structure follows D1; the order "filter before sampling" follows D2. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  13. F13 (fig2) Figure 2. Aliasing, drawn from the same formula as Listing 2 (a 9 Hz sine with phase 0.3 rad, sampled at 10 Hz). The samples fit a 1 Hz wave exactly, so no program looking only at the samples can tell the two apart. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  14. F14 (l2-trans) … e. Some change a resistance (a thermistor with temperature, a strain gauge with stretch), some produce a voltage or current directly (a photodiode with light), some change a capacitance or a charge (the tiny moving plates inside the accelerometers of F1-65). — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  15. F15 (l2-trans) A resistance is turned into a voltage by a circuit such as the voltage divider of F1-04. Which quantity a sensor changes, how much per unit, and how linear that relation is are properties of the named part, so they come only from its datasheet (D3). — source: D3 kit MCU datasheet (not opened)
  16. F16 (l2-cond) Next, signal conditioning fits the signal to the converter: an amplifier makes a small signal bigger, a level shifter moves a signal that swings below 0 V into the converter's range, and a buffer stops the converter's input from loading the sensor. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  17. F17 (l2-cond) Then comes the anti-alias filter, an analog low-pass filter. It must sit before the sampler, because once a signal has been sampled, an alias is just a number like any other and no later program can remove it. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  18. F18 (l2-adc) The sample-and-hold freezes the input while the converter works. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  19. F19 (l2-adc) This is the model in Listing 1. With a pretend Vref = 3.0 V and n = 3 there are 8 codes and the step is 0.375 V; an input of 0.374 V gives code 0 and 0.376 V gives code 1, because the boundary is at exactly 0.375 V. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-64)
  20. F20 (l2-adc) If your program reports each code as the voltage in the middle of its step, (code + 0.5) × LSB, the quantisation error is never more than half a step: the 10-bit sweep in Listing 1 found a worst error of 0.001465 V, exactly half of its 2.930 mV step. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-64)
  21. F21 (l2-adc) Inputs outside the range are clamped: 3.20 V reads as the top code, 7, and the program cannot know how far above the range the input was. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-64)
  22. F22 (l2-adc) … verters are not ideal. Their datasheets give offset error, gain error, non-linearity, noise and the exact transfer function (some round instead of truncating, some report signed codes). Those numbers belong to the named part and come only from its datasheet. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  23. F23 (l2-sampling) Reading at a sample rate fs means taking one sample every Ts = 1/fs seconds. The sampling theorem says that a signal containing only frequencies below fs/2, the Nyquist frequency, is fully described by its samples. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  24. F24 (l2-sampling) A component above the Nyquist frequency is not lost; it is disguised. Its samples are identical to those of a component at |f − k·fs|, and the one you will see is the copy between 0 and fs/2. This is aliasing. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  25. F25 (l2-sampling) Listing 2 checks it: a 9 Hz sine sampled at 100 Hz shows 8.95 Hz by counting zero crossings (the right answer, within the counting method's resolution), but sampled at 10 Hz it shows exactly 1.00 Hz, as the formula |9 − 1 × 10| = 1 predicts. — source: R2 (local run, labs/F1-64)
  26. F26 (l2-sampling) The table at the end of its output shows the folding: at fs = 10 Hz, inputs of 1, 9, 11, 19 and 21 Hz all look like 1 Hz. — source: R2 (local run, labs/F1-64)
  27. F27 (layer3) … tween samples, the quantisation error behaves like a random value spread evenly over ±LSB/2. A uniform distribution of width LSB has standard deviation LSB/√12 (a result from probability, MA202), so each extra bit halves the error and its standard deviation. — source: D4 Blitzstein & Hwang (not opened)
  28. F28 (layer3) Resolution is not accuracy. Part B of Listing 1 shows that a 16-bit converter with a 3.0 V reference has steps of 0.046 mV. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-64)
  29. F29 (layer3) Whether the bottom bits mean anything depends on the reference's accuracy, the converter's noise and the wiring (F1-72), all of which come from the named parts' datasheets and from measurement. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  30. F30 (layer3) … designs sample several times faster than the highest frequency of interest and let the filter attenuate everything near and above fs/2. The exact choice is a trade-off between filter order, sample rate and how much aliased energy the application can accept. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  31. F31 (layer3) … ma, flash, among others) that trade speed, resolution and power; delta-sigma converters oversample and filter digitally, which moves part of the anti-alias job inside the chip. Which type a microcontroller or sensor uses, and its timing, is in its datasheet. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  32. F32 (layer3) Timing jitter. Sampling at uneven moments adds error proportional to how fast the signal is changing. A hardware timer that starts conversions (F1-48) gives evenly spaced samples; starting conversions from a busy software loop does not. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  33. F33 (hardware) … egins when it opens), and the conversion logic compares the held voltage against fractions of Vref to find the code; the result lands in a data register that your driver reads over the internal bus, by polling a "done" flag or in an interrupt (F1-41, F1-42). — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  34. F34 (hardware) Sensor chips that speak I2C or SPI (F1-47) contain this whole chain and deliver codes in registers, which is exactly how the IMU of F1-65 works. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  35. F35 (breaks) Amara can look twice; a sampler cannot. When the log looked strange, Amara walked in and watched the needle. A program has only the samples; once aliasing has happened there is nothing left to look at. That is why the filter must come before the sampler. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  36. F36 (breaks) Amara's rounding is to the nearest mark; many ADCs truncate. The ideal model of Listing 1 takes the floor, so the reported value is biased low by half a step unless the program reports mid-step. Which rule a real converter uses is in its datasheet. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-64)
  37. F37 (transition) … the idea of sampling and quantisation; F1-05 built RC circuits; HW204 shows how a processor reads a peripheral's registers. What carries over: voltages and dividers, the RC low-pass filter, binary codes and bit widths (MA102), and register reads over a bus. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §13.5/§13.7 (TOC only; text not opened)
  38. F38 (transition) What changes: time matters now: when you sample decides what you see, and a mistake made at sampling time cannot be repaired later in software; every number has a step size and an error; and real parts add errors listed in their datasheets. — source: D2 O&S 3rd ed. ch. 4 (contents only)
  39. F39 (lab) … use. Part B: low voltage only, from the kit's own supply; never connect anything to mains. L2 learners work with a supervising adult. Never put more than the voltage the microcontroller's datasheet allows on an ADC pin; when in doubt, ask before you connect. — source: S1 guide "Honesty first" (local)
