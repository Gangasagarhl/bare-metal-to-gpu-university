Fact list (keyed to the chapter's claim tags, in chapter order)

  1. F1 (jargon) … heel, turned by water pressure. Precise: a motor whose rotating winding (armature) is fed through brushes and a commutator that reverse the current in each coil as the rotor turns, producing torque in a fixed direction with a permanent-magnet or wound field. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  2. F2 (jargon) … es by itself, which pushes against the supply. Analogy: the turning wheel pushing back on the water. Precise: the electromotive force induced in the windings by rotation, e = Ke·ω, opposing the applied voltage; it is why current falls as the motor speeds up. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  3. F3 (jargon) … unit of speed. Analogy: how hard the wheel turns per bucket of water, and how hard it pushes back per turn. Precise: torque constant Kt (N·m per A) and back-EMF constant Ke (V per rad/s); in consistent SI units they are numerically equal for an ideal motor. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  4. F4 (jargon) … is held still. Analogy: the wheel stopped by sacks while the water roars through. Precise: at zero speed there is no back-EMF, so the current is V/R and the torque Kt·V/R, the largest values on the torque–speed line; the winding then dissipates V²/R as heat. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  5. F5 (jargon) … s you load it more. Analogy: the wheel's speed for each number of sacks. Precise: for a DC motor at fixed voltage, speed falls nearly linearly from the no-load speed to zero at stall torque; mechanical power, torque × speed, peaks near half the stall torque. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  6. F6 (jargon) … ts that a helper opens and closes in turn as the wheel moves. Precise: a permanent-magnet synchronous motor with stator windings (usually three phases) commutated electronically from rotor-position information (Hall sensors, back-EMF sensing, or an encoder). — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  7. F7 (jargon) … r deciding which jet to open by looking at marks on the wheel. Precise: in six-step (trapezoidal) commutation, three Hall sensors spaced 120 electrical degrees apart give six valid 3-bit patterns; each selects one phase driven high, one low and one floating. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  8. F8 (jargon) … the valve until a pointer matches the order slip. Precise: a motor, gearbox, position sensor (often a potentiometer) and controller in one housing, commanded by the width of a repeating pulse; the pulse-to-angle mapping and timing are given by its datasheet. — source: D4 servo datasheet (none in kit)
  9. F9 (fig1) Figure 1. Brushed DC motor model used by Listing 1 (D1). The back-EMF grows with speed and opposes the supply; the torque is proportional to current. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  10. F10 (fig2) Figure 2. Points from the run of Listing 1 (R1) for the pretend motor at 6 V. Speed falls linearly with load; mechanical power peaks at half the stall torque. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-69)
  11. F11 (l2-model) … e L; turning induces a back-EMF Ke·ω. Kirchhoff's voltage law around the loop (Figure 1) gives V = R·i + L·di/dt + Ke·ω. The current makes a torque Kt·i, which accelerates the rotor's inertia J against friction b·ω and the load: J·dω/dt = Kt·i − b·ω − Tload. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  12. F12 (l2-model) N·m·s). — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-69)
  13. F13 (l2-model) At a 6 V step, the current jumps to nearly 3 A within the first milliseconds (2.868 A at 10 ms), because the motor is still almost stopped. As speed rises, back-EMF rises and the current falls: 1.122 A at 200 ms, 0.077 A after 1 s at 5583 rpm. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-69)
  14. F14 (l2-model) Two time scales appear: the electrical one, L/R = 0.5 ms, and the mechanical one, about J·R/(KtKe) = 0.2 s. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  15. F15 (l2-model) No-load speed is proportional to voltage: 1861, 3722 and 5583 rpm at 2, 4 and 6 V. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-69)
  16. F16 (l2-ts) In steady state di/dt = 0 and dω/dt = 0. Eliminating i gives a straight line: speed falls from the no-load speed to zero at the stall torque Kt·V/R. For the pretend motor at 6 V, stall current is 3.00 A and stall torque 0.0300 N·m. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  17. F17 (l2-ts) Listing 1's loaded runs fall on that line (Figure 2): 4187 rpm at 0.0075 N·m, 2792 at 0.015, 1396 at 0.0225. Mechanical power, torque × speed, is largest at half the stall torque (4.385 W here) and zero at both ends. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-69)
  18. F18 (l2-ts) A gearbox trades speed for torque by its ratio, minus its own losses and friction. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  19. F19 (l2-bldc) A brushed motor's brushes rub and wear and spark. A brushless motor moves the magnets to the rotor and leaves the three-phase windings on the stator, so the switching has to be done electronically. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  20. F20 (l2-bldc) … ey give six valid patterns per electrical revolution, and each pattern selects which phase is driven high, which low, and which floats (six-step commutation). Listing 3 implements the pretend motor's table; a healthy sequence is 101, 100, 110, 010, 011, 001. — source: R3 (local run, labs/F1-69)
  21. F21 (l2-bldc) Patterns 000 and 111 never occur in a healthy motor, so seeing one means a sensor fault. — source: D5 BLDC/ESC documentation (not opened)
  22. F22 (l2-bldc) Sensorless drives estimate position from the back-EMF of the floating phase instead; more advanced drives (field-oriented control) use an encoder or an estimator and smooth sinusoidal currents. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  23. F23 (l2-servo) A hobby servo packs a small DC motor, a gearbox, a position sensor and a controller in one box. It is commanded by a repeating pulse whose width sets the target angle; Listing 2 uses the pretend mapping 1.0 ms → −90°, 1.5 ms → 0°, 2.0 ms → +90°. — source: D4 servo datasheet (none in kit)
  24. F24 (l2-servo) The internal controller drives the motor at a speed proportional to the error, limited to a top speed (300°/s pretend), and stops inside a small deadband: the arm reaches 89.0° for a 90° target and −44.0° for −45°. — source: R2 (local run, labs/F1-69)
  25. F25 (l2-servo) That 1° gap is the deadband, not a bug: the servo stops trying when it is "close enough". The real mapping, pulse repetition rate, speed and deadband are in the servo's datasheet. — source: D4 servo datasheet (none in kit)
  26. F26 (layer3) … r function. Neglecting L, the voltage-to-speed response is first order with time constant τm = J·R/(KtKe + R·b); with L it is second order, and the electrical pole is much faster here (0.5 ms against 0.2 s). Controllers in RB201 are tuned against this model. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  27. F27 (layer3) Efficiency and heat. Copper loss is i²R; at stall all input power becomes heat. Continuous ratings in datasheets are thermal limits; peak torque can be much higher for short times. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  28. F28 (layer3) Friction is not linear. The model uses viscous friction b·ω. Real gearboxes add static (breakaway) and Coulomb friction, which create a minimum voltage below which the motor will not start: the forensic lab models a breakaway torque. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  29. F29 (layer3) Electrical vs mechanical degrees. A BLDC with p pole pairs completes p electrical revolutions per mechanical one; Hall patterns repeat every electrical revolution. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  30. F30 (layer3) Parameter identification. R from a locked-rotor current at low voltage; Ke by spinning the motor and measuring its open-circuit voltage; J and b from a coast-down curve. Each is a measurement you can make on the real motor and compare with the datasheet. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  31. F31 (hardware) … the brushes change, reversing the current in each coil at the right moment. The brush contact is also an electrical noise source (sparks), which is why small capacitors are often fitted across motor terminals, as the kit's documentation will show if it does. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  32. F32 (hardware) In a BLDC the "commutator" is six transistors in a three-phase bridge (F1-70) and a controller that reads the Hall sensors, which are digital magnetic-field sensors mounted in the stator. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  33. F33 (hardware) A hobby servo's controller reads a potentiometer on the output shaft and drives its motor through a small H-bridge. — source: D4 servo datasheet (none in kit)
  34. F34 (hardware) … apolated; so its stall current is half the pretend 3.00 A of the Listing 1 motor, and its 330 rpm is an output-shaft speed behind the gearbox, not comparable with the pretend 5583 rpm of that ungeared model. It is driven by the kit's DRV8833 carrier (F1-70). — source: D3 Pololu 5190 product page
  35. F35 (hardware) … d to the bench, with fingers, hair and cables clear of the shaft before power is applied; no propellers. A stalled motor gets hot: do not hold a stalled motor at full voltage, and do not touch it after a stall test. L2 learners work with a supervising adult. — source: S1 guide (local)
  36. F36 (breaks) Water wheels have no inductance. A motor's current cannot change instantly (L·di/dt); switching it off suddenly causes a voltage spike, which F1-70 must handle. The water wheel has no such effect. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  37. F37 (breaks) The wheel's push-back is mechanical; back-EMF is electrical. Back-EMF is a voltage that exists even with nothing connected: spin a motor by hand and you can measure it. That is a generator, which the water wheel analogy only hints at. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  38. F38 (breaks) A stalled wheel only wastes water; a stalled motor heats up. The current at stall turns into heat in the winding (Layer 3). — source: R4 (local run, labs/F1-69)
  39. F39 (transition) … (rad/s and rpm); the encoder (F1-66) now measures the speed this chapter predicts. What changes: the robot now acts on the world: energy flows out, currents are amperes rather than microamperes, and mistakes produce heat and motion instead of wrong numbers. — source: D1 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  40. F40 (lab) Safety. Part B: see the safety box above. Clamp the motor; current-limit the supply; no propellers; supervised for L2. — source: S1 guide (local)
