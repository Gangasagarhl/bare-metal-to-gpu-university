Fact list (keyed to the chapter's claim tags, in chapter order)

  1. F1 (jargon) … mage. Analogy: the hole in Nadia's shutter and the picture on the wall. Precise: a projection in which a point (X, Y, Z) in the camera frame maps to image coordinates u = fx·X/Z + cx, v = fy·Y/Z + cy; real lenses add distortion that is calibrated separately. — source: D2 HZ 2nd ed. ch. 6 (contents only)
  2. F2 (jargon) … e image. Analogy: how far the wall is from the hole, and where on the wall the hole's straight-ahead line lands. Precise: the focal lengths fx, fy in pixels and the principal point (cx, cy), often written as the calibration matrix K; obtained by calibration. — source: D2 HZ 2nd ed. ch. 6 (contents only)
  3. F3 (jargon) … pixel. Analogy: Farid's squared paper, one square per pixel. Precise: an array of photodiodes that convert light into charge during the exposure; in a CMOS sensor, each pixel has its own readout transistors and rows are read through column circuits and ADCs. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  4. F4 (jargon) … a bucket stands in the rain, and how much it holds before it overflows. Precise: the exposure (integration) time is the interval during which a pixel accumulates photo-generated charge; the full-well capacity is the maximum charge before the pixel saturates. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  5. F5 (jargon) … Analogy: two buckets in the same steady rain never collect exactly the same number of drops. Precise: photon arrivals (and the electrons they produce) follow Poisson statistics, so a mean of N electrons has standard deviation √N and signal-to-noise ratio √N. — source: D3 AoE 3rd ed. §8.1 (TOC only)
  6. F6 (jargon) … colouring each square with only one of three crayons, in a fixed pattern. Precise: a mosaic of colour filters over the sensor; the common Bayer pattern repeats a 2 × 2 tile with one red, two green and one blue filter; full colour is estimated by demosaicing. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  7. F7 (jargon) … rs at every pixel from its neighbours. Analogy: filling in each square's missing crayons by looking at the squares around it. Precise: interpolation of a full RGB image from a colour-filter-array image; simple methods produce false colours along sharp edges. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  8. F8 (jargon) … us taking a photograph of the whole wall at once. Precise: with a rolling shutter, each row's exposure starts and ends a fixed row time after the previous row's, so moving objects are skewed; with a global shutter, all pixels expose during the same interval. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  9. F9 (fig1) Figure 1. The pinhole model (D2). The real image behind the pinhole is upside down; the formulas use a virtual plane in front of it, where the image is upright. Not to scale. — source: D2 HZ 2nd ed. ch. 6 (contents only)
  10. F10 (fig2) Figure 2. The Bayer pattern in the RGGB arrangement used by Listing 3 (other sensors start the tile on another colour; the datasheet says which, D4). Letters as well as fills mark the colours. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  11. F11 (fig3) Figure 3. Exposure windows per row. With a rolling shutter each row sees the scene a row time later than the one above; with a global shutter all rows see the same moment (D1). Bars show exposure; readout is not drawn. Not to scale. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  12. F12 (l2-pinhole) Nadia's hole sends each point's light along one straight line. With the camera frame's Z axis pointing forward (X right, Y down, a common computer-vision convention), similar triangles give the pinhole model: u = fx·X/Z + cx, v = fy·Y/Z + cy. — source: D2 HZ 2nd ed. ch. 6 (contents only)
  13. F13 (l2-pinhole) The intrinsic parameters fx, fy (focal lengths in pixels) and (cx, cy) (the principal point) are found by calibrating each camera; real lenses also bend straight lines (radial and tangential distortion), which calibration models and removes. — source: D2 HZ 2nd ed. ch. 6 (contents only)
  14. F14 (l2-pinhole) Listing 1 uses pretend values f = 600 px and principal point (320, 240) for a 640 × 480 image: a 1 m pole at 2 m is 300 px tall, at 4 m 150 px, at 8 m 75 px. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-68)
  15. F15 (l2-pinhole) At 1 m its top lands at v = −60, outside the image: the formula does not know about image borders. A point behind the camera (Z ≤ 0) has no image at all. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-68)
  16. F16 (l2-pinhole) Projection loses depth: every point on the ray X/Z = 0.2, Y/Z = 0 lands on pixel (440, 240). Going back from a pixel gives a ray, not a point; depth needs a second view (stereo, F1-67), a known size, or a depth sensor. — source: D2 HZ 2nd ed. ch. 6 (contents only)
  17. F17 (l2-pixel) Each pixel's photodiode turns arriving photons into electrons during the exposure time; the charge is converted to a voltage, amplified (the gain) and digitised by an ADC (F1-64). — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  18. F18 (l2-pixel) A pixel holds at most its full-well capacity; more light saturates it at the top code. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  19. F19 (l2-pixel) Light itself is noisy: photon arrivals follow Poisson statistics, so a pixel that collects N electrons on average varies with standard deviation √N, and its signal-to-noise ratio is N/√N = √N. — source: D3 AoE 3rd ed. §8.1 (TOC only)
  20. F20 (l2-pixel) Listing 2 tabulates this for a pretend pixel (full well 10,000 electrons, 10-bit output): 2 electrons per ms for 1 ms gives code 0 and SNR 1.4; 50 per ms for 40 ms gives code 204 and SNR 44.7; 1000 per ms for 40 ms saturates at 1023. — source: R2 (local run, labs/F1-68)
  21. F21 (l2-colour) Photodiodes measure intensity, not colour. A colour filter array puts one filter over each pixel; the Bayer pattern repeats R G / G B, with two greens per tile. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  22. F22 (l2-colour) … ates the missing two colours per pixel. Listing 3 uses the crudest method, one colour per 2 × 2 tile, on a scene with a red-white edge in the middle of a tile: the tile across the edge becomes (9, 4.5, 9), a magenta-like colour that is neither red nor white. — source: R3 (local run, labs/F1-68)
  23. F23 (l2-colour) Better algorithms use larger neighbourhoods and edge directions, but every method guesses, and sharp, high-contrast edges are where guesses fail. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  24. F24 (l2-shutter) Farid filled his paper row by row. Many CMOS sensors do the same: each row's exposure is shifted by a fixed row time from the previous row's (Figure 3), so the image is not one instant. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  25. F25 (l2-shutter) If an object moves horizontally at v pixels per second and the row time is tr, row r sees it shifted by v·tr·r pixels: vertical edges lean. A global shutter exposes all pixels during the same interval and has no such skew. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  26. F26 (l2-shutter) The row time and shutter type of a sensor are in its datasheet. — source: D4 image-sensor datasheet (none in kit)
  27. F27 (layer3) … ogeneous coordinates, projection is x ≃ K [R | t] X, where K holds the intrinsics and R, t the camera's pose (extrinsics). Calibration estimates K, distortion and, for multiple cameras, the relative poses; reprojection error in pixels is its quality measure. — source: D2 HZ 2nd ed. ch. 6 (contents only)
  28. F28 (layer3) … ates without light, growing with temperature and exposure), fixed-pattern noise and quantisation. At low light, read noise dominates; at high light, shot noise does. Values are per sensor and come from its datasheet or from measuring a dark and a flat frame. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  29. F29 (layer3) Dynamic range and HDR. The ratio between full well and the noise floor limits the range of brightness one exposure can capture; some sensors combine several exposures (HDR) at the cost of motion artefacts. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  30. F30 (layer3) Rolling shutter in geometry. For a rolling-shutter camera on a moving robot, each row has its own pose; visual odometry and SLAM either model this or prefer global-shutter sensors. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  31. F31 (layer3) … modules connect over parallel buses, serial camera interfaces (the MIPI Alliance's CSI-2 is common on SoCs) or USB. The curriculum notes that camera sensors are identified by a chip-ID register read over I2C, the control channel next to the image data path. — source: C1 curriculum (map)
  32. F32 (layer3) Timestamps. For fusion with an IMU (F1-65), you need the time at which each row (or the middle of the exposure) was captured, not when the frame arrived in memory; sensors and drivers differ in what they report. — source: D4 image-sensor datasheet (none in kit)
  33. F33 (hardware) … , its select line is enabled; every column's amplifier passes that row's pixel voltages to column circuits, which sample them (often twice, once after reset and once after transfer, and subtract: correlated double sampling) and convert them with column ADCs. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  34. F34 (hardware) Rows are selected one after another, which is the physical origin of the rolling shutter; a global-shutter pixel adds a storage element so that all pixels can stop exposing at once and wait to be read. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  35. F35 (hardware) The control side (exposure time, gain, region of interest, frame rate) is a set of registers written over I2C; the image data leave over a separate high-speed interface. — source: D4 image-sensor datasheet (none in kit)
  36. F36 (breaks) A pinhole is sharp at all distances; a lens is not. The tiny hole lets very little light in; cameras use lenses to collect more, which brings focus, depth of field and distortion that the pinhole model leaves out. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  37. F37 (breaks) Farid looks at the colours; the pixel cannot. A pixel counts electrons only; colour exists only because a filter was placed over it, and the other two colours at that pixel are guesses. — source: R3 (local run, labs/F1-68)
  38. F38 (breaks) Farid can be slow or quick; a sensor's row time is fixed. The skew of a rolling shutter is predictable from the row time and the motion, which is what makes it diagnosable (forensic lab). — source: R4 (local run, labs/F1-68)
  39. F39 (transition) Where you came from: F1-64 (one ADC, one signal), F1-67 (depth by triangulation). What carries over: the measurement chain, quantisation and saturation (now per pixel); triangulation (stereo needs this chapter's projection model); timing of each sample. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
  40. F40 (transition) What changes: millions of measurements per frame instead of one; geometry (where a point lands) matters as much as value; noise has a physical floor (shot noise) that no electronics remove; and a single image is not one instant unless the shutter is global. — source: D1 Szeliski 2nd ed. 2022 (pages only; text not opened)
