Fact list (keyed to the chapter's claim tags)

  1. F1 (jargon) a periodic digital signal whose edges (here the rising edges) trigger the flip-flops of a synchronous circuit. — source: D3 “Synchronous Sequential Logic”
  2. F2 (jargon) the duration T of one cycle of the clock, measured in seconds (often ns). — source: D3 “Setup Time Constraint”; W1
  3. F3 (jargon) the minimum time before the active clock edge during which D must be stable for the flip-flop to sample it reliably. — source: D3 “Timing”; D7 Timing requirements
  4. F4 (jargon) the minimum time after the active clock edge during which D must remain stable. — source: D3 “Timing”; D7 Timing requirements
  5. F5 (jargon) the delay from the active clock edge to the flip-flop's output Q becoming valid. — source: D3 “Timing”
  6. F6 (jargon) the path between two clocked elements with the longest total delay; it sets the minimum clock period (see also F1-15 and HW202 ). — source: D3 “Setup Time Constraint”
  7. F7 (jargon) clock period minus the time the path needs; negative slack means the path fails timing. — source: D6 §2.2.2; W1
  8. F8 (jargon) a control input that makes clocked elements update only on edges where it is 1, so slow parts of a design can share the main clock. — source: D5 §2.2.3.6
  9. F9 (jargon) the difference in arrival time of the same clock edge at two clocked elements, caused by the clock distribution wiring. — source: D3 “Clock Skew”
  10. F10 (jargon) Inside chips such as FPGAs, phase-locked loops (PLLs) are the recommended source of internally generated clocks. — source: D5 §2.2.3
  11. F11 (fig2) If the bars do not fit between two edges, the slack is negative and the path fails — source: D3 “Setup Time Constraint”; D6 §2.2.2
  12. F12 (layer2) It must then be stable at the capturing flip-flop's input for the setup time before the next edge — source: D3 “Setup Time Constraint”
  13. F13 (layer2) T ≥ t clock-to-Q + t logic + t setup — source: D3 “Setup Time Constraint”
  14. F14 (layer2) T ≥ t clock-to-Q + t logic + t setup The path with the largest sum is the critical path ; it sets the smallest period and therefore the highest frequency of the whole circuit — source: D3 “Setup Time Constraint”
  15. F15 (layer2) The fastest path from one flip-flop to the next must take longer than the hold time — source: D3 “Hold Time Constraint”
  16. F16 (layer2) Unlike the setup rule, the clock period does not appear in it, so slowing the clock does not fix a hold problem — source: D3 “Hold Time Constraint”
  17. F17 (layer2) Using such a bit as a clock for other flip-flops is possible, but it creates a second clock whose edges come a clock-to-Q delay after the main one, which brings back the problems of the ripple counter — source: D5 §2.2.3, §1.2.2.3
  18. F18 (layer2) The usual alternative is a clock enable : keep one clock everywhere, and generate a one-cycle pulse (here tick , every 5 cycles) that tells the slow part "this edge counts" — source: D5 §2.2.3.6
  19. F19 (layer3) If the capturing flip-flop's clock is late, the setup budget gains time but the hold budget loses it, and the other way round — source: D3 “Clock Skew”
  20. F20 (layer3) It works from delays, not from input values — source: D6 §1.1, §2.2.2
  21. F21 (layer3) This lowers the chance of failure but cannot make it zero — source: D3 “Synchronizers”
  22. F22 (layer3) Every clock edge charges and discharges wire and transistor capacitance, so a circuit's switching power grows with its clock frequency — source: D4 power consumption
  23. F23 (layer3) Where saving power is not the goal, a clock enable is the safer choice — source: D5 §2.2.3.5.1
  24. F24 (hardware) It is distributed through dedicated low-skew clock routing, and because the clock switches every cycle (its activity factor in the power formula is 1), the clock network contributes a share of the chip's switching power — source: D5 §2.2.3, §2.2.3.5.1; D4 power consumption
  25. F25 (hardware) HW202 shows pipelining (temporal parallelism), which splits a long job into stages separated by registers, so that less logic lies between two flip-flops — source: D3 “Parallelism”
  26. F26 (breaks) It stores something, maybe the old value, maybe the new one, maybe (for a while) neither: a metastable level — source: D3 “Metastability”
  27. F27 (breaks) Skew is the gap the analogy hides — source: D3 “Clock Skew”
  28. F28 (transition) What carries over: delays add along a path, exactly as in F1-15; the flip-flop's rule (store at the edge) is unchanged — source: D3 “Setup Time Constraint”
