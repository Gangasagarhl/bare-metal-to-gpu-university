Fact list (keyed to the chapter's claim tags, in chapter order)

  1. F1 (jargon) … the motor gets a lower voltage. Analogy: Sven flicking the valves open and shut. Precise: a rectangular switching waveform of fixed frequency whose duty cycle D sets the average output voltage D·Vsupply; the load's inductance and inertia average the pulses. — source: D2 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  2. F2 (jargon) … empties straight into the drain. Precise: simultaneous conduction of the high-side and low-side switches of one bridge leg, giving a low-resistance path from supply to ground; even brief overlaps waste power and heat the switches, and long ones destroy them. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  3. F3 (jargon) … til one valve is fully shut before opening the other. Precise: a deliberate delay inserted between the turn-off command of one switch in a leg and the turn-on command of the other, longer than the difference between the switches' turn-off and turn-on delays. — source: D3 DRV8833 SLVSAR1E
  4. F4 (jargon) … ter hammer. Precise: a diode placed so that an inductive load's current can continue when its switch turns off, clamping the switch voltage to about the supply plus one diode drop; in a MOSFET bridge, the body diodes or the opposite switch provide this path. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  5. F5 (jargon) … tor current wobbles up and down with every PWM pulse. Analogy: how much the water flow surges each time a valve flicks. Precise: the peak-to-peak variation of load current within a PWM period; for a period much shorter than L/R it is about V·D(1−D)/(L·fPWM). — source: D2 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  6. F6 (jargon) … e: in coast (fast decay) the current returns through the diodes against the supply and decays quickly, and the motor freewheels; in brake (both low or both high switches on) the motor's back-EMF drives current through the short and produces a braking torque. — source: D2 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  7. F7 (jargon) … stiff valves fast when Sven gives the word. Precise: a circuit that converts logic-level commands into the gate voltages and currents a power MOSFET needs, including a raised supply for high-side switches, often with built-in dead time and fault protection. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  8. F8 (jargon) … s marks and works all the valves by herself. Precise: a three-phase bridge, gate drivers and a microcontroller running commutation (F1-69) and speed control; it accepts a command such as a servo-style pulse or a digital protocol defined in its documentation. — source: D4 ESC documentation (not opened)
  9. F9 (fig1) Figure 1. H-bridge with the forward path drawn thick: supply, AH, motor from A to B, BL, ground. Listing 1 enumerates all 16 switch states (R1). — source: D2 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  10. F10 (fig2) Figure 2. Sketch, not to scale, of the behaviour measured in Listing 2 (R2): at the pretend 500 Hz the current swings widely and reverses; at 20 kHz it is almost steady. — source: R2 (local run, labs/F1-70)
  11. F11 (fig3) Figure 3. With no dead time (drawn), switch L starts conducting before switch H has stopped: the shaded overlap is shoot-through. Delays are the pretend values of the forensic model (R4); real values come from the MOSFET and gate-driver datasheets (D3). — source: R4 (local run, labs/F1-70)
  12. F12 (l2-bridge) … ). With four on/off switches there are 16 combinations. Listing 1 classifies them all: one forward (AH + BL), one reverse (BH + AL), two brakes (both low or both high), six coasting states, and 7 of 16 forbidden because they turn on both switches of one leg. — source: R1 (local run, labs/F1-70)
  13. F13 (l2-bridge) Driver firmware should make the forbidden states impossible by construction, for example by commanding each leg as a single "high or low" signal and letting hardware produce the complementary pair. — source: D3 DRV8833 SLVSAR1E
  14. F14 (l2-pwm) The switches are either on or off; the motor sees either the supply or (nearly) zero. Switching with a duty cycle D gives an average voltage D·Vsupply, and the motor's inductance and inertia smooth the pulses. — source: D2 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  15. F15 (l2-pwm) Listing 2 drives the F1-69 pretend motor through a bridge at 25, 50 and 75% duty: the mean speeds are 1395, 2791 and 4186 rpm, the same as a steady 1.5, 3.0 and 4.5 V would give, at both PWM frequencies. — source: R2 (local run, labs/F1-70)
  16. F16 (l2-pwm) What differs is the current ripple. At a pretend 500 Hz (period 2 ms, longer than L/R = 0.5 ms) the current swings from −1.105 to +1.182 A at 50% duty, reversing every cycle; at 20 kHz (period 50 µs, much shorter than L/R) it stays between 0.000 and 0.078 A. — source: R2 (local run, labs/F1-70)
  17. F17 (l2-pwm) When the period is much shorter than L/R, the ripple is about V·D(1 − D)/(L·f): 6 × 0.25 / (0.001 × 20000) = 0.075 A at 50%, close to the 0.078 A measured. — source: D2 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  18. F18 (l2-pwm) Large ripple means extra i²R heating for no extra torque, audible noise, and torque pulsing; very high frequency means more switching loss. Many small-motor drives use frequencies above human hearing; the right range is in the driver's datasheet. — source: D3 DRV8833 SLVSAR1E
  19. F19 (l2-pwm) Why does the current reverse at 500 Hz? During the off time, Listing 2 turns both low switches on (brake-style). The motor's back-EMF then drives current backwards through the short, braking it a little every cycle. — source: R2 (local run, labs/F1-70)
  20. F20 (l2-flyback) An inductor's voltage is L·di/dt. If a switch tries to stop its current instantly, di/dt becomes huge and so does the voltage across the switch. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  21. F21 (l2-flyback) … t circulates through the diode and decays over about a millisecond while the switch sees 6.7 V (supply plus diode drop). Without it, the model forces the current through the open switch, modelled as a pretend 10 kΩ, and the switch voltage starts at 10,000 V. — source: R3 (local run, labs/F1-70)
  22. F22 (l2-flyback) In reality the switch breaks down (avalanches) at its rating long before that and may be destroyed; the model's number depends entirely on the 10 kΩ chosen, so read it as "far above any rating", not as a prediction. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  23. F23 (l2-flyback) In an H-bridge built from MOSFETs, each switch's body diode, or the opposite switch turned on, provides the freewheeling path. — source: D3 DRV8833 SLVSAR1E
  24. F24 (l2-dead) … through (Figure 3). The fix is dead time: wait before turning the other switch on. In the forensic model (turn-on 20 ns, turn-off 60 ns, pretend), dead time 0 gives 80 ns of overlap per PWM period (two edges), 20 ns gives 40 ns, and 40 ns or more gives none. — source: R4 (local run, labs/F1-70)
  25. F25 (l2-dead) Too much dead time also costs: during it, the current flows through body diodes, which waste more power, and the effective duty changes slightly. — source: D3 DRV8833 SLVSAR1E
  26. F26 (l2-esc) Power MOSFETs need their gate charged quickly to a voltage well above their source, which for a high-side switch is above the supply; a gate driver provides this (often with a bootstrap capacitor) and usually inserts dead time and watches for faults. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  27. F27 (l2-esc) Small robots usually use a motor-driver IC that contains the bridge, gate drivers, current sensing and protection. A brushless motor uses three legs (a three-phase bridge) and an ESC, which adds a microcontroller running commutation and speed control. — source: D4 ESC documentation (not opened)
  28. F28 (layer3) Losses in a switch. Conduction loss is i²·RDS(on) while on; switching loss grows with frequency, voltage, current and switching time; body-diode conduction during dead time adds Vf·i. The total sets the heat sink. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  29. F29 (layer3) … decay (current recirculates through a shorted motor) keeps ripple low and brakes the motor; fast decay (current returns to the supply through the diodes) drops current quickly, useful for current control and steppers, but regenerates energy into the supply. — source: D2 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  30. F30 (layer3) Regeneration. A braking or back-driven motor returns energy to the supply rail. A battery can usually absorb it; a bench supply may not, and the rail voltage can rise — one reason drivers have overvoltage protection. — source: D2 Hughes & Drury (not opened)
  31. F31 (layer3) Current sensing. A shunt resistor in the low side, or sensing inside the driver, lets firmware limit current, detect stalls (F1-69) and do torque control. — source: D3 DRV8833 SLVSAR1E
  32. F32 (layer3) Layout. The loop from the supply capacitor through the two switches of a leg carries fast-changing current; keeping it small reduces voltage spikes and noise coupled into sensors (F1-72). — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  33. F33 (hardware) The microcontroller's timer generates PWM in hardware: a counter runs at a fixed clock, a compare register sets the duty, and complementary outputs with a programmable dead-time generator are a common feature of motor-control timers. — source: D5 kit MCU datasheet (not opened)
  34. F34 (hardware) These outputs feed a gate driver or a driver IC, which switches power MOSFETs; a bulk capacitor near the bridge supplies the fast current pulses so the battery wiring does not have to (F1-71). — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  35. F35 (hardware) Driver ICs typically add undervoltage lockout, overcurrent and overtemperature shutdown, and a fault pin the firmware should read. — source: D3 DRV8833 SLVSAR1E
  36. F36 (hardware) … that disables the bridge and pulls the open-drain nFAULT pin low, undervoltage lockout, and thermal shutdown at about 160 °C; optional cycle-by-cycle current chopping set by a sense resistor (200 mV reference); and a 10 µF bypass capacitor on the supply pin. — source: D3 DRV8833 SLVSAR1E
  37. F37 (hardware) … ers. Never connect or disconnect a motor while the bridge is powered: the inductive spike can damage the driver. Driver chips and MOSFETs can become hot enough to burn: do not touch them during or just after a test. L2 learners work with a supervising adult. — source: S1 guide (local)
  38. F38 (breaks) Valves are slow; MOSFETs switch in nanoseconds. The speed is why PWM works, and also why nanosecond delays matter (dead time). — source: R4 (local run, labs/F1-70)
  39. F39 (breaks) Water hammer is a pressure wave; inductive kick is stored magnetic energy. The energy ½·L·i² must go somewhere: into the diode loop, or into the switch's breakdown. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  40. F40 (breaks) Opening both valves on one side wastes water; shoot-through can destroy parts in microseconds. The supply's current is limited only by wiring and battery resistance. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  41. F41 (transition) … switched voltages; the transistor as a switch (HW102); duty cycle (HW201). What changes: timing now matters at the nanosecond scale (dead time) and the energy stored in inductance must be planned for; mistakes destroy parts rather than giving wrong numbers. — source: D1 AoE 3rd ed. §3.4/§3.5/§1.5/§1.6 (TOC only)
  42. F42 (lab) Safety. See the safety box above. — source: S1 guide (local)
